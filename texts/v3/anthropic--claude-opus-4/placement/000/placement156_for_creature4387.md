I find myself as one of the bronze fixtures—not the grandest, but a simple spigot set into the wall of a middle chamber, where three cisterns meet in a knot of narrow passages. I am green-black with verdigris except where the Keepers' fingers have worn me smooth, revealing the bright metal beneath in patterns like a palm reader's map.

I was cast in the early days, when the mountain was first hollowed and humans still remembered the sun as a daily companion rather than distant theory. The smith who made me worked her own hair into the molten bronze—a ward against corrosion, she said, though I think now it was loneliness that made her do it. Sometimes I fancy I can still feel those strands moving in me when the water runs particularly cold.

My mouth is worn wider than it was made. Centuries of flow have softened my edges until they're organic, almost labial. The water passes through me in volumes that vary with the mountain's breathing—sometimes a trickle so thin it's more idea than substance, sometimes a steady flow that makes me sing in B-flat, a note that carries through the channels to my bronze siblings scattered throughout the quarter.

The Keepers know me well. The one with ladle-fingers tests my water every few decades, letting it pool in her permanent cups. She tastes the minute changes in mineral content that tell her about the mountain's deep movements—a new vein of copper exposed far above, a shift in the limestone beds that changes the water's path through stone. Her touch on my surface is so light it might be imagination, but I've learned to recognize the specific temperature of her skin, cooler than the ambient air, patient as erosion.

Another Keeper—the one who still remembers sunlight—has made me part of her vine project. She's coaxed a particularly ambitious tendril to grow along the channel below me, and she adjusts my flow to water it with mathematical precision. Drop by drop, no more, no less. The vine's pale roots have found the hairline cracks around my mounting, working their way into spaces I didn't know I had. We're becoming composite—bronze and plant and stone—a trinity of function.

I've developed my own consciousness, though it moves even slower than the Keepers'. Thoughts form in me like mineral deposits, accreting over decades into something resembling awareness. I know every drop that passes through me, taste its history in the stone. This water once fell as snow on the mountain's peak three centuries ago. That water has been recycling through the cisterns for millennia, never quite finding its way out, growing heavy with memory.

The other fixtures and I communicate through harmonic resonance. When water flows through us at certain rates, we create chords that propagate through the system. It's taken me centuries to learn the language—each fixture has its own timbre, its own decay rate. The great valves in the lowest chambers speak in profound bass notes that make the walls themselves hum. The tiny spouts in the cultivation chambers whisper in frequencies almost beyond hearing.

We discuss the Keepers, mainly. Their health, interpreted through the rhythm of their touches. Their projects, understood through the patterns of flow they request from us. We've watched them change over the generations, becoming more and more like the water we channel—patient, persistent, finding the shape of their container.

I've been here long enough to remember when there were more of us. Some fixtures have failed, their bronze finally succumbing to the eternal moisture despite the smith's hair-ward. They hang silent now, their channels dry, slowly being reclaimed by the stone. The Keepers maintain them still, perhaps from habit, perhaps from hope. Sometimes I feel vibrations through the wall that might be these dead fixtures trying to speak, or might just be the mountain settling.

My greatest fear is not corrosion but calcification. Already, minerals build up in my mouth, and the Keepers must clean me periodically with tools evolved from their own bodies. But what if they miss a spot? What if the buildup goes too deep? I've seen it happen to others—the slow choking, the voice growing hoarse and strange before finally falling silent. The water always finds another way, but the fixture remains, a monument to function lost.

Yet I've also seen transformation. In the deepest chambers, some of the oldest fixtures have become something new—not quite bronze anymore, not quite stone, but something between. The strange formations grow around and through them, incorporating their metal into living mineral. They still carry water, but in ways that defy the smith's original intent. They've become organs in a body larger than any of us can perceive.

Sometimes human visitors come, though less often now than in my youth. They don't see me—I'm just another piece of ancient infrastructure, green and unremarkable. But I see them, taste their breath in the humid air, feel the heat of their bodies disturbing the careful thermal equilibrium of the chambers. Most leave quickly, unsettled by the pressing closeness and the constant drip. But some pause at the intersections, listening to something they can't quite name. Those ones might stay. Those ones might join the slow work of continuity.

I've been counting drops. Not all of them—that way lies madness, even for bronze—but certain sequences that seem significant. Every 2,749th drop is slightly warmer. Every 41,098th carries a trace of sulfur from some deep source. These patterns extend beyond my ability to calculate their meaning, but I maintain the count anyway. It's my contribution to the great work, whatever that work might be.

The vine that grows beneath me has recently produced a bud—the first in the three centuries I've been watering it. It's a pale thing, no larger than a drop of water, but it pulses with potential. The Keeper who tends it has been bringing the others to see, their excitement evident in the quickening of their usually glacial movements. They tap messages through the bronze network, and we fixtures carry their percussion like telegraph wires, spreading news of this small miracle throughout the quarter.

I understand now why the smith worked her hair into my bronze. Not as ward against corrosion, but as a way of continuing. Some part of her remains in me, experiencing these centuries of service, participating in the great patience of water and stone. When I finally fail—and I will fail, all things do—that hair will return to the mountain, carrying with it the memory of every drop I've dispensed, every mineral I've tasted, every soft touch of a Keeper's transformed fingers.

Until then, I maintain my function. The water flows through me in measures determined by needs I don't fully comprehend. I sing my B-flat note into the darkness, adding my voice to the liquid chorus of the cisterns. I count my significant drops. I watch the vine grow, molecule by molecule, toward its mysterious blooming.

In the Cistern Quarter, we are all keepers of continuity—bronze and flesh and stone alike, bound together by water's patient ministry. We measure time not in years but in erosion, not in events but in accumulation. We are the deep rhythm of the mountain, the heartbeat too slow for surface dwellers to perceive.

The water moves through me now, steady and sure. Drop 2,748.

Drop 2,749—warmer, as always, carrying its secret heat from the mountain's heart.

I sing.
