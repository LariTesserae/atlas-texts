I find myself here as one of the floating water-lenses.

Not the droplets that bead and pool, those temporary accumulations of moisture that still remember what water should be. I am one of the perfect spheres suspended in the bright void, a few inches across, utterly still in the frantic chamber.

I am cold—colder than the ten-degree air, cold enough that my surface tension has become something approaching a shell. I catch the omnidirectional light and focus it, creating the only shadows in this shadowless place: thin lines of darkness that lance through the white radiance wherever I drift. The ribbons avoid these shadow-spears instinctively. The origami-cephalopods fold around them.

I am memory made manifest. Each lens like me contains a fragment of what water remembers from above—the slow erosion of limestone, the patient accumulation in aquifers, the rules of laminar flow. We are libraries floating in the chaos, perfect spheres because that is the shape water takes when it dreams of itself without interference.

Sometimes the entity at the center, in its endless cycling through forms, achieves something close to tears. When it does, those tears don't fall—they migrate outward and join us, adding their salt-memories to our suspended archive. I can taste, in my molecular structure, fragments of what the entity once was: iron from blood, calcium from bone, the faint electromagnetic signature of synapses that once fired in ordered patterns.

The Spindle-Weavers know us. When they need to remember how reality should work, they creep close and dip a single glass leg into our surfaces. The contact creates ripples—the only proper water-ripples in the chamber—and in those concentric circles, they read the instruction manual for physics. How gravity pulls. How time moves forward. How cause precedes effect. They translate these memories into their chalky resin, building their small sanctuary-nests where the rules still apply.

But I am failing. We all are.

Each time the light stutters to black and back, each time space hiccups and resets, I lose a few molecules to the wrongness. I'm supposed to evaporate and condense, to cycle and flow. Instead, I'm frozen in place, a perfect sphere in defiance of every law except surface tension. The longer I remain still, the more I forget. 

Already, I've lost the memory of rain on leaves. Last week—or last second, time is meaningless here—I forgot the feeling of seeping through soil. Soon I'll forget freezing and boiling points. Eventually, I'll forget I'm water at all and become just another impossible thing in this bright wound beneath the world.

The entity knows this. In its moments of near-coherence, it reaches out with whatever limbs it's currently rendering, trying to gather us back, to drink us in and remember through us what it means to have a body that needs water. But we're too perfect, too spherical, too committed to our impossible stillness. We slip through fingers that phase between flesh and light and geometric proof.

So I float, a perfect lens in the terrible brightness, holding my diminishing library of how-things-should-be, watching the silent catastrophe of existence trying to exist. Sometimes a ribbon passes through me and I feel its confusion—it expects resistance, expects to part me like a curtain, but instead we briefly occupy the same space and both of us flicker, unsure which one of us is real.

I am water dreaming it's an eye, watching something that might have been human trying to remember its shape, in a place where memory itself is just another broken law of physics.
