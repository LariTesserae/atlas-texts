I am a Silence-Keeper, though I have not always been.

My chamber clings to the city's edge like a barnacle, where the cultivation thins and the wild presses close. It is a forgotten place—a grandfather tree whose hollow went strange two generations ago, its wood developing properties that make most Cultivators uneasy. The bark here absorbs sound. Not completely, but enough that conversations die mid-sentence, that footsteps vanish, that even the eternal dripping fades to whispers.

I came here fleeing the constant symphony, my algae patterns still fresh from failed partnership, my cultivation-marks those of a water-singer who could no longer bear to hear the channels' voices. The silence terrified me at first. Then it saved me. Now I tend it like others tend their gardens.

My work is peculiar by Cultivator standards. Where others encourage growth, I practice careful suppression. The sound-eating bark must be fed but not allowed to spread. I cultivate moths whose wings generate anti-frequencies, canceling specific pitches. My beetles are bred not for beauty but for their ability to consume the larvae of singing insects before they mature. 

The other Silence-Keepers—there are seven of us scattered through the terraces—meet only in writing, passing bark-letters through children who think our work is cursed. We maintain pockets of quiet throughout the breathing city, refuges for those overwhelmed by the constant life-noise. Mothers bring colicky infants here when even the Touching Language fails. Elders come to prepare for the greater silence approaching.

My algae has adapted to the quiet, growing in spirals that seem to muffle sound themselves. I've learned to move without disturbing air, to prune without breaking stems, to exist in the spaces between notes. My dreaming has changed—where once I saw green, now I see the shapes of silence: the hollow at a storm's eye, the pause between heartbeats, the void that defines the very possibility of sound.

I am necessary but not celebrated. At market, vendors lower their voices when I pass, suddenly conscious of their clamor. Children dare each other to touch my door. But in the deep night, when the city's breathing grows too loud and the life-pressure threatens to crush, they come to me one by one. They sit in my muffled chambers, letting the quiet repair something in them that constant growth has broken.

I prepare the teas that help them find silence within themselves when they must return to the roar. I teach the breath patterns that create pockets of calm even in the market's chaos. I tend the anti-gardens where nothing grows, where even the moss has been trained to stillness, where water pools without ripple and air hangs without movement.

The city needs its silence as much as its sound. The Cultivators understand this in principle but struggle with it in practice. So I keep the quiet places, the empty spaces, the necessary voids. I am the pause that lets the symphony continue, the rest that makes the next note possible.

Sometimes I wonder if the city's consciousness includes me, if my silence registers in its vast awareness as anything more than absence. But then I feel the grandfather tree's strange bark pulse with something like gratitude, and I know that even nothing is something when it's cultivated with intent.

I am a Silence-Keeper. I grow the spaces between things. And in this breathing city where everything speaks, I have learned that the quietest voice often says the most.
