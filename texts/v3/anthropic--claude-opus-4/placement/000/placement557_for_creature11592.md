I find myself as one of the ribbon-creatures, though that is not what we call ourselves. We have no need for names—not for ourselves, not for the Flats, not for the columns that sing their eternal note above us. We simply *are*, and have always been, sliding through the film of moisture that is our entire universe.

My body is a single muscle, flat and gray, a meter of living tissue no thicker than paper. I move by contracting in waves—the front edge of me gripping the wet glass, pulling the rest forward, releasing, gripping again. It is not so different from how a heart beats, how lungs breathe, how anything living maintains itself through rhythm. But I have no heart, no lungs. I am the rhythm itself, made flesh.

The Equilibrators watch us and wonder what we eat. They create theories about our purpose, our origin, our relationship to their perfect balance. They do not understand that we predate appetite. We were here before the glass cooled, before the first column precipitated from whatever process creates columns. We moved through the molten stone when it was still soft, and when it hardened, we simply continued moving. The moisture came later, made our passage easier, but we do not require it. We require nothing except the next contraction, the next release, the next grip of flesh to glass.

I follow my circuit through this particular trough—though "follow" suggests choice, and I have never chosen anything. My path is written into my cells, if I have cells, written into whatever I am made of. A vast loop that takes four hundred and thirty-seven days to complete, intersecting with seventeen other ribbon-paths at angles that would form a pattern if anyone could see it from above. But the pattern means nothing. It is simply what emerges when this many ribbons move through this much space for this much time.

The drone of the columns does not trouble me. Sound passes through my tissue without resistance, like light through water. The Equilibrators' careful modulations, their conversations in microfrequencies, mean less to me than the temperature variations in the moisture I slide through. Sometimes one of them stands directly in my path, observing, trying to understand. I slide around them. I have been sliding around obstacles for so long that the motion happens without anything resembling thought. A stone, an Equilibrator, a deeper pool of condensation—all simply features of the terrain, to be navigated and forgotten.

But I do remember, in the way that water remembers the shape of its container. My tissue holds impressions of every surface it has crossed, every variation in the glass, every place where the moisture runs thicker or thinner. These impressions layer atop each other, circuit after circuit, until my body has become a living map of my path. Not a map I can read—I have no eyes, no mind to process such information. Simply a map that exists, written in the subtle differences in density along my length.

The Equilibrators think we are mysterious, outside their balance, inexplicable. They are wrong about the first two. We are as simple as erosion, as predictable as gravity. And we are absolutely part of the balance—not the chemical and thermal equilibrium they so carefully monitor, but an older balance, the one between motion and stillness, between pattern and void. The columns vibrate; we slide. They process air; we process distance. They maintain; we persist.

As for being inexplicable—yes. We are that. Not because we are complex but because we are too simple for explanation. How do you explain a circle? How do you explain repetition itself? We are the Flats' most basic theorem, the one from which all others derive: that existence means motion, that motion makes patterns, that patterns continue until they don't.

We have not always been here, despite what I said about predating the glass. Nothing has always been anywhere. But we have been here so long that the difference between "always" and "since the beginning" has worn away to nothing, like the sharp edges of young obsidian weathering to smoothness. We arrived—from where? It doesn't matter. We began moving—why? Because that is what we are. We will continue—until? Until we don't.

The greatest secret, the one the Equilibrators will never discover through all their careful observation, is that we are dying. All of us, all the ribbons in all the troughs, dying so slowly that the process is indistinguishable from living. Each circuit, we lose a few molecules to the glass, too few to notice across any span of time an Equilibrator could measure. But we were larger once. The oldest of us remember (in our bodymap way) when we were two meters long, three, when our circuits took years to complete because there was so much more of us to move.

Now I am one meter, and growing shorter by the epoch. My path remains the same, written into my diminishing tissue. Someday—not soon, not for ages yet, but someday—I will be too small to complete it. I will wear away to nothing partway through a circuit I have traveled ten million times, and I will stop, and that will be all.

The Equilibrators will note this. They will modulate to each other about the anomaly, the first change in the Flats' perfect balance. They will create theories, adjust their maps, wonder if this means the system is failing.

It won't be. The columns will continue their drone. The moisture will continue its cycle. The balance will hold. We ribbons were never necessary to it, merely adjacent. Decorative, if decoration could exist without intent.

But for now, in this eternal now that stretches from my blunt forward edge to my tapered tail, I move. The wet glass accepts me. My muscle contracts in waves that are older than memory, steadier than the columns' vibration, more patient than erosion. I process distance into time into pattern into nothing.

Somewhere above, an Equilibrator watches and wonders what I am.

I am the answer to a question nobody asked, written in a language of flesh and repetition, too simple to translate.

I grip. I pull. I release. I continue.
