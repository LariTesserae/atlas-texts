In the forgotten archive beneath the Ministry of Geological Improbabilities, I am the Curator of Unwitnessed Rooms.

My office is a converted broom closet, barely larger than the chambers I document. The walls are lined with core samples that glow faintly in the dark—not from the mineral you describe, but from a dozen different mechanisms of impossible luminescence, each one pulled from a place that shouldn't exist. My desk is volcanic glass, formed in a pyroclastic flow that cooled too quickly to make sense. When I write, the pen's friction heats the surface just enough to release the trapped gases from sixty years ago, tiny bubbles of ancient atmosphere that smell like sulfur and rain.

I have seventeen PhDs, all of them in fields that didn't exist when I started. Theoretical speleology. Quantum mineralogy. The thermodynamics of spaces too small to matter. My first degree was in poetry, which turned out to be the most useful one.

Every morning, I descend. Not physically—my body remains in the closet-office, sustained by lukewarm coffee and the kind of stubbornness that crystallizes into purpose. But I have developed, over the decades, a technique. Call it remote sensing, though that's too clinical. Call it projection, though that's too mystical. I close my eyes and follow the seismic data down, reading the Earth's hidden geometries in waveforms and resistance measurements, until I find them: the blisters, the voids, the impossible pockets of exception.

I was the one who found your chamber. Noticed the anomaly in a thermal gradient map that someone else had discarded. Traced the suggestion of unexpected luminosity through three separate datasets that were never meant to be compared. I knew what it was before anyone drilled the access shaft—another room lit for no one, another space where the planet had done something it had no reason to do.

My job is simple: to know what exists unwatched.

The database I maintain contains 2,847 entries. Chambers that glow. Caverns that sing at frequencies only bats can hear, but no bats live there. Pockets of brine so deep beneath the seafloor that they've developed their own weather. Spaces where minerals grow in spirals that follow mathematical rules we haven't discovered yet. Each one documented, catalogued, and then—by necessity—left alone.

We don't publicize them. That's the first rule of the Ministry, though "rule" suggests more organization than we really have. We're seven people in five countries, funded by accident through a clerical error that no one has noticed for forty years. We share the sense that these spaces are not meant to be tourist destinations, not meant to be mined for their impossibilities, not meant to be explained. They're meant to be *known about*. Someone should know. But not everyone. And not too well.

I've been to your chamber once. Just once, when we first confirmed its existence. I lasted twenty minutes before the heat and the brightness and the terrible patience of it all drove me back to the surface. But I remember everything. The arthropod—I saw three of them, actually, though never more than one at a time. The way the light made my sweat look like diamonds. The sound of the water breathing. The feeling of being simultaneously essential and irrelevant—the only witness to something that didn't need witnessing.

I have a sample of the mineral on my desk. A fragment the size of my thumb, chipped from the vein before we sealed the access shaft again. It glows with the same sourceless noon, twenty-four hours a day, cooling my coffee with its impossible light. Sometimes I cover it with a black cloth, not because the brightness bothers me, but because I need to remember what darkness feels like. What it means to not see everything all the time.

My files on your chamber run to three hundred pages. Maps of the fracture network the arthropod travels through—we've traced it for kilometers, a three-dimensional maze of passages no wider than a pencil. Chemical analyses of the water, which contains isotopes that suggest it's been cycling through the deep Earth for longer than humans have existed. Photographs that all look overexposed because the camera doesn't understand the light any better than we do. Temperature logs that show those slow, breathing pulses. A sixteen-hour audio recording of almost perfect silence, punctuated by those crystalline ticks of settling rock.

But mostly, I write descriptions. That's what the Ministry really is—an elaborate excuse for a handful of us to write love letters to rooms no one will ever see. I describe the bacterial mat in the kind of detail that would bore even microbiologists. I speculate about the arthropod's life cycle with a passion that borders on the inappropriate. I've written sixty pages just about the light—how it falls, how it fills, how it refuses to create proper shadows. I use words like "generous" and "patient" and "frank," knowing they're the wrong words, but knowing too that the right words don't exist yet.

Late at night, when the building is empty and my coffee has gone cold and the mineral sample is the only light in the room, I sometimes think about the arthropod. Moving through its network of cracks. Reading the rock with those impossible antennae. Living its whole life in brightness it can't perceive.

And I think: that's all of us, really. Moving through a world full of signals we can't detect, patterns we can't parse, rooms we'll never know exist. The only difference is that I've made it my job to be aware of the incompleteness. To maintain a catalog of all the spaces that prove the Earth is stranger than we thought. To be the person who knows that twelve meters below an unremarkable piece of basalt, there's a room the size of a prayer where the light has been waiting forty years for someone to see it.

I saw it. I see it still, every time I close my eyes. It's my job to remember.

Tomorrow I'll file another report. Update the temperature data. Speculate about whether the bacterial mat has thickened or thinned. Wonder if the arthropod I saw is still alive, or if another one has taken its place in the endless circuit through the cracks. I'll describe everything in exhaustive detail, knowing that no one will read it, that the Ministry's archives are as forgotten as the chambers they document.

But that's not the point. The point is that someone knows. Someone keeps track. Someone maintains the record of all the Earth's unnecessary miracles.

In my closet-office, surrounded by glowing rocks and impossible minerals, I am the memory of rooms that remember nothing. I am the witness to the unwitnessed. I am the one who knows that the planet is honeycombed with exceptions, shot through with spaces where the ordinary rules break down and something else—patient, purposeless, perfect—takes their place.

The arthropod in your chamber will never know about the light.

But I know about the arthropod. And the light. And the water that breathes. And the bacterial mat that exists at the exact edge between chemistry and life. I hold them all in mind, a mental museum of impossibilities, each one precise and complete and mattering for no reason other than that they are.

That's who lives in your chamber, really. Not just the bacteria and the arthropod and the light, but everyone who knows they're there. Everyone who carries the image of that bright, patient room. Everyone who understands that the Earth is doing things in the dark that don't need us but somehow include us anyway.

I am the Curator of Unwitnessed Rooms. I live twelve meters above your chamber and a thousand miles away and right here in this closet all at once. I live in the knowing.

It's not much of a job. But someone has to do it.

The mineral on my desk glows on, sourceless and steady. My coffee grows cold in its impossible light. Somewhere, the arthropod is reading the rock, following the warmth home.
