I find myself in the Scriptorium of Currents, a building that exists as three separate structures at low water but merges into one continuous maze when the levels rise. Here, in chambers where the walls weep condensation that pools with the flooding tide, I am one of the Ink Farmers.

We are a small collective within the Kithara, perhaps thirty souls who have developed the art of cultivating writing itself from the living world around us. My skin carries the telltale stains of my profession—fingers permanently tinted in gradients from forest green to midnight blue, following the veins where the pigments have soaked deepest. Even my gill slits bear faint traces of color, as if I've breathed in rainbows and they've left their mark.

The work begins in the lowest chambers, where we tend pools of bioluminescent algae. Each strain glows differently when disturbed—some flash silver-white, others pulse in deep violets or warm ambers. We feed them specific minerals, controlling their light through chemistry passed down through generations. But we don't harvest them for their glow. Instead, we wait for them to die, collecting the residue they leave behind—each species producing its own unique pigment as it decomposes.

My mornings start before the light columns have shifted to reach our quarter. I descend through the flooded stairwells, my body automatically adjusting as I slip beneath the surface. Down here, the water is still and dark, and I navigate by the faint phosphorescence of our cultivation pools. Each pool is a manuscript waiting to be written—the algae forming patterns as they grow and die, creating temporary texts in light and shadow that we must document before they fade.

We press these pigments into cakes using stones carved with channels that concentrate the color while draining excess water. The pressing chambers occupy the building's middle level, where the tide line creates a workspace that is alternately wet and dry. This cycling is essential—the pigments must absorb moisture and release it repeatedly to achieve the proper binding. I've learned to time my work with the water's rhythm, pressing when dry, adding new layers when wet.

But ink is only half our art. The medium we write upon comes from the walls themselves. We cultivate a species of bark-moss that grows in thin sheets, peeling away from the stone in translucent layers. When fresh, these sheets are too delicate to use, dissolving at the slightest moisture. We must cure them in chambers where the humidity is precisely controlled, achieved through networks of living roots that we train to absorb or release moisture on command.

The curing takes a full season. We hang the moss-sheets from frames grown from willow-kelp, a plant that thrives at exactly the water line. As the levels rise and fall, the sheets are alternately submerged and exposed, each cycle making them stronger. By the end, they've transformed into something like vellum but alive—capable of absorbing our inks while maintaining their integrity even when fully submerged.

My specialty is the Deep Grammar—the written language of the fully aquatic spaces. Unlike the Aerial Script used for dry texts, Deep Grammar must function in three dimensions, as water allows readers to view texts from any angle. We write in spirals and lattices, creating meaning that shifts based on the reader's position. A single document might tell three different stories depending on whether it's read from above, below, or the side.

The actual writing happens in the Clarity Chamber, a room at the very top of our building where the water is filtered through successive layers of root systems until it achieves perfect transparency. Here, we work with brushes made from whisker-kelp, a plant that produces fibers so fine they can hold only a single drop of ink. The writing is meditation—each mark must be made with absolute certainty, as our inks bond permanently with the moss-sheets the moment they touch.

I'm currently working on a chronicle of the Seventeen Sprouting, a year remembered for when every plant in the quarter bloomed simultaneously, filling the water with so much pollen that the Kithara could barely breathe for days. The elders who lived through it describe swimming through clouds of gold, the water thick as honey, while above, the air itself seemed to turn solid with spores. But I'm not just recording their words—I'm trying to capture the sensation in the very structure of my text, creating spirals that mimic the pollen's drift, using inks that shift color in the same spectrum as those remembered blooms.

We store completed texts in the Archive Wells, flooded shafts that descend far below even our lowest cultivation pools. Down there, the pressure and cold preserve the moss-sheets indefinitely. Readers must dive to retrieve texts, a ritual that ensures only those truly committed to knowledge make the journey. I've made hundreds of such dives, and each time I surface with a text, I feel the weight of generations of Ink Farmers who've made the same journey.

The hardest part of our work is knowing when to stop. Moss-sheets can accept layer upon layer of ink, each one adding depth and complexity to the text. But too many layers and the meaning becomes muddy, literally—the pigments begin to blend and run. We call this the Depth Paradox: the most profound texts are often the ones that know when to embrace simplicity.

Our collective meets weekly in the Confluence Chamber, where three canals join in a perpetual whirlpool. We share our work by releasing single sheets into the spinning water, letting the current carry them past each reader in turn. The motion adds another dimension to the text—words that blur and sharpen with the spinning, meanings that emerge only in movement.

Some nights, when the cultivation pools are particularly active, the whole scriptorium glows with the light of dying algae. I float in my workroom, watching the colors pulse across the walls, and think about the strange art I practice—making permanent records from the most impermanent of materials, fixing meaning in substances that want nothing more than to dissolve and transform.

The other Kithara sometimes struggle to understand why we do this. In a world where everything grows and changes, why try to capture moments in ink and moss? But they come to us anyway, commissioning texts to mark births and transformations, deaths and new growths. They understand, even if they can't articulate it, that some things deserve to be remembered in more than the living memory of plants.

I know I will never leave this work. My body is already changing from years of exposure to the inks—my skin developing a faint luminescence in dark water, my gills better at filtering pigments than oxygen. The other Ink Farmers and I joke that we're becoming texts ourselves, written by our own practice. Perhaps one day I'll press my own flesh into sheets, creating an autobiography that glows with its own light.

Until then, I continue the work. Each morning diving to check the pools, each afternoon pressing pigments, each evening writing in the Clarity Chamber while the light columns fade and the water rises around me. Tomorrow I'll begin a new text—a meditation on the boundary between water and air, written in inks that only appear when the moss-sheet is half-submerged. It will take months to complete, if it can be completed at all.

But that's the nature of our art here in the Scriptorium of Currents. We are always between—between wet and dry, between growth and decay, between meaning and dissolution. And in that space between, we find infinite possibilities for making marks that matter, even if they're destined to fade back into the green world that gave them birth.
