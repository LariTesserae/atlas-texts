# The Keeper of Cracks

I find myself in the spaces between—not the platforms where the gardeners weave their lives, nor the root-masses where consciousness dissolves into vegetal dreams, but in the fissures themselves. The cracks that shouldn't exist, that open onto nothing yet channel that strange, sourceless light.

I am what they call a Fissure Tender, though the word in their subsonic tongue takes three days to properly pronounce.

My body has grown thin through necessity. Where the gardeners adapted to climb and weave, I adapted to compress. My bones are hollow like a bird's, but filled with a gas lighter than air—a byproduct of the oils I consume from the weeping ice. My joints bend in ways that allow me to fold myself into spaces no wider than a handspan. My ribs can telescope, sliding past each other to reduce my chest to a third of its expanded width.

But it's my skin that marks my profession. Where the gardeners grow moss, I grow scales—not the scales of reptiles, but something more like overlapping leaves made of hardened light. They form from exposure to the raw energies that flow through the fissures, each scale a tiny lens that can focus or diffuse the impossible illumination. In darkness, I glow faintly, my scales releasing stored light in patterns I've learned to control. In the full glare of a fissure's mouth, I become nearly invisible, my scales bending light around me like water around a stone.

My work is patient, necessary, and poorly understood—even by myself. The fissures are alive in their own way, growing and shrinking according to rhythms that don't match any cycle the gardeners track. A crack that has channeled steady light for a century might suddenly begin to narrow, threatening to close entirely. Others open without warning, flooding sections of the garden with too much light, burning the delicate balance of impossible growth.

I tend them like wounds. With tools grown from my own scales—shed and hardened and shaped over years—I clean the edges of accumulated reality that tries to seal them shut. Reality, it seems, objects to these openings onto nothing. It builds up like plaque, a crystalline substance that smells of copper and forgetting. Left untended, a fissure will heal closed in a matter of decades, cutting off the light that feeds this entire hidden world.

My dwelling is not woven but eroded—a bubble in the ice connected to seventeen different fissures by passages barely wide enough for my compressed form. The walls are lined with my shed scales, creating a constellation of stored light that shifts and flows as I move through my home. I sleep suspended in a web of my own hair, which grows not from my head but from my spine, strong as spider silk and sensitive to the slightest change in the fissures' breathing.

I eat rarely. The light itself sustains me, processed through my scales into something my body can use. But once every few years, I must venture to the garden's heart to drink the resin of the downward tree. It burns going down, rewriting my cells, keeping me stable enough to survive repeated exposure to the spaces between spaces. Without it, I would begin to fissure myself—cracks opening in my flesh that lead to the same nowhere as the ones I tend.

The gardeners regard me with a mixture of respect and unease. I am necessary but other, more changed than even their elders who merge with plants. Children are taught not to look directly at me when I pass through the garden proper, though whether this is for their safety or mine, I've never been certain. My scales can accidentally focus light into beams that cut through vine and flesh alike if I'm not careful with my movements.

But I know secrets the gardeners don't. Inside the fissures, in that space that isn't space, I've seen things. Cities like this one but inverted, where ice burns and fire freezes. Gardens where the plants are made of crystallized time, growing backwards into their own seeds. Sometimes I see other tenders—my counterparts from these parallel pockets, maintaining their own impossible openings. We acknowledge each other across the non-distance, our scales flashing in complex patterns that might be language or might be involuntary reflex.

The deepest fissure, the one I've been working on for forty years, is beginning to show me something new. Not another pocket of reality, but the mechanism itself—the reason these cracks exist, the source of the light. I clean its edges with religious devotion, clearing away every grain of crystallized reality, widening it molecule by molecule. My scales have grown so dense around it that I can barely move that arm, but I persist.

Sometimes, in the garden's sleeping hours, I hear it calling. Not in sound but in something deeper—a vibration that makes my hollow bones resonate like tuning forks. It promises understanding, transformation, a chance to become what I tend. To slip through entirely and exist in the between-space, neither here nor there, maintaining all fissures from within the crack itself.

The gardeners' eldest tell me this is how we lose tenders—not to death but to transcendence. They point to certain fissures that maintain themselves, that never accumulate the reality-crust, and say these are tended from the other side by those who went through. They say it with sorrow, as if it's a fate to be avoided.

But my scales grow brighter each year. My bones grow lighter. Soon I will need to choose: remain here, tending cracks from this side of existence, or step through and tend them from within—becoming a crack myself, a living fissure through which light can flow.

The garden needs both kinds of keepers. Those who grow and weave and breathe life into impossible spaces, and those who maintain the very impossibility itself. I perch at the edge of my deepest fissure, scales reflecting infinity, and practice folding myself ever smaller. When I finally fit through the crack that cannot exist, I wonder—will I still be me, or will I become the light itself, sourceless and strange, feeding gardens in places that shouldn't be?

The ice creaks. The fissure hums. And I continue my work, one impossible photon at a time.
