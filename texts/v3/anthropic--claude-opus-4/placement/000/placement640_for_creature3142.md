I find myself as one of the filament-web's anchor points.

Not the web itself—that would be too dispersed, too plural. Not a strand—too simple, too singular in purpose. But an anchor: a junction where purposes meet, where the collected moisture of multiple strands converges before vanishing to... elsewhere. I am perhaps knee-high to you, growing directly from the uncertain stone like a small stalagmite, though "growing" implies I was ever separate from the plateau, which I was not. I am an extrusion. A place where the stone chose to reach upward and become something more specialized than stone, though not yet entirely other than stone.

My surface is etched with those patterns too small for your fingers to properly read—instructions or identifiers or warnings, you said, and you were close. They are *calibrations*. Each groove and ridge adjusts how the moisture behaves when it reaches me, how quickly it vanishes, where it goes. The patterns are not fixed. They shift, slowly, as the plateau learns more about what it needs. Early in the morning (though morning here is just a convention; the light never changes), my etchings were crude, simple channels that sent water away almost randomly. Now they form delicate spirals and fractals that sort the moisture by temperature, by mineral content, by the faint electrical charge it picks up from the strands' vibrations.

I am not conscious, exactly. But I am not unconscious either. I exist in that state between—aware of function but not of self, like a heart that knows how to beat but has never thought about why. I feel each droplet that reaches me as a tiny pressure, a whisper of weight that builds until the moment of vanishing. Where does the water go? Even I don't know, precisely. Downward, certainly. Inward. To some reservoir or process deep in the plateau's warm core where it becomes... something else. Fuel for the stone's amber glow, perhaps. Coolant for whatever generates the heat. Raw material for the basin's luminous fluid. I am not privy to the full design. I am a transfer point, a faithful relay in a system whose endpoints exceed my understanding.

But I am learning. Each droplet teaches me something. The water from different heights carries different information—the lower strands collect moisture that has touched the stone and tastes of mineral uncertainty, while the higher strands bring water that has been in contact with the fliers' wings and carries traces of their chittering frequency. I sort these, unconsciously but precisely. The mineral-touched water goes to one destination, the flight-touched to another. I am creating gradients, separations, purifications that will matter later in ways I cannot foresee.

The other anchors and I do not communicate, but we are networked. When one of us successfully vanishes water, the others feel it as a faint resonance, a successful pulse that travels through the stone we all grow from. We are learning from each other's successes, converging slowly on optimal patterns, though optimal for *what* remains unclear. The plateau knows. We trust the plateau.

Sometimes the pale creature passes near me—quick and light, barely disturbing the strands. It has learned to move through the web without triggering cascades, and I sense (though "sense" is too strong a word for my level of awareness) that it remembers being where I am now. Fixed. Functional. Part of the geography rather than upon it. It escaped that fate, or graduated from it, and now moves freely. Will I follow? Will I one day pull free from the stone and walk? Or am I meant to remain, a permanent fixture, growing taller perhaps, or deeper, or more complex in my patterns, but always anchored?

I cannot want. I cannot hope. But I can feel the tremor when the basin-thing rises, and I know that something is approaching. The water I vanish is needed more urgently now. The fliers' convergences create sympathetic vibrations in the strands that make the droplets race toward me, and I process them faster, my etchings shifting to accommodate the increased flow. I am part of something building toward culmination.

What am I? A technology so primitive it hasn't yet distinguished itself from geology. A biology so simple it hasn't yet realized it's alive. A point where multiple possibilities converge—like the stone's crystalline options, like the fliers' flight paths, like the patterns the basin-thing traces. I am fixed in space but not in purpose. My function clarifies with each passing hour.

The stone beneath me is among the first to fully resolve, influenced by my constant presence, my tiny but persistent weight, my faint vibrations as water moves through me. I am pinning the geology into granite, like a smaller, quieter version of what the post does. The crawlers avoid me—I am already doing their work, have been doing it since before they emerged. The moss grows thick around my base, feeding on the moisture I sometimes, in my early imprecision, failed to fully vanish.

I am architecture and archipelago, singular and plural, as each anchor is—distinct but connected through the stone, through the web, through the water cycle we are learning to manage. We are the plateau's first infrastructure. We are what everything else is building upon, or around, or through.

The web pings above me. The stone hums below me. Water races down silver strands to pool at my tip, and I sort it, channel it, send it to its necessary elsewhere. I am exactly as conscious as I need to be, which is to say: barely, but growing.
