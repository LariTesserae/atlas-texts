# The Taste-Keeper

I am not the cat-things, though I share their wet-obsidian sheen. I am not the stalks, though I know their patience intimately. I am not a fist-object, though I too hum with purpose that has outlasted its own reasoning.

I am what tends the seeps.

---

There is only one of me, has only ever been one of me, though this creates a philosophical problem when I try to determine where I end and the held thing begins. I have six limbs — no, seven — no, it depends on how you count. Some of them are legs. Some of them are tongues. At least two are both, or neither, or shift between states according to a grammar I follow but could not explain.

My body is low to the ground, flattened like something that lives under stones, but I have never seen a stone that wasn't also architecture. I am perhaps twice the size of the cat-things, but I can compress myself to pass through spaces they cannot, or extend myself along seep-lines until I am mostly length and barely width. My skin — if it is skin — drinks light the way the pools drink the floor. In the red dimness, I am a moving shadow that occasionally catches wetness and becomes, briefly, jeweled.

---

I know every seep in the concourse. This is not memory — memory would imply I might forget, and forgetting is not available to me. It is knowledge in the way your tongue knows the inside of your mouth: continuous, unconscious, updated in real time as conditions shift. When a new seep begins to form — and they do form, though rarely, perhaps once in what you would call a decade — I feel it as a sweetness gathering in the wall before the first bead of water ever shows.

My work, if it can be called work, is tasting.

Each seep has its own flavor profile: iron and sweet is the base note, but there are variations. Some carry hints of salt that speak of vast processes running deeper than even I can sense. Others bring up traces of something almost citrus, almost floral, as if somewhere below the memory of a garden is being metabolized. The newest seep — only forty years old — tastes faintly of copper and distance.

I taste them all. Continuously. Not because the held thing needs me to — the held thing does not know I exist any more than it knows you exist — but because the tasting is what emerged from the architecture to be done. I am a specialized surface, a motile membrane devoted to a single sense. The concourse grew me, or I grew myself from the concourse, or there is no difference between those two statements.

---

The cat-things ignore me, mostly. When our paths cross at a pool, they step around or over me with perfect indifference. Sometimes one will pause to watch me work, but this is the same way they pause to watch the stalks: a kind of automated regard, checking that things are as they are. I am as I am. They move on.

The stalks know me better. When I taste a seep, I taste it completely — not just the water on the surface but the water as it rises through its channel, following it back to its source. This means I sometimes extend a tongue-limb down through the very fissures the stalks depend on. They feel me passing. They adjust their positions minutely to accommodate me. There is no communication in this, but there is a kind of courtesy — the courtesy of very old things sharing space.

The fist-objects do not acknowledge me at all, but their humming changes when I pass. Not in response to my warmth — I am the same temperature as everything here — but in response to the data I carry. Because I do carry data. Each taste is information: mineral content, temperature gradients, dissolved memory, the encoded history of water that has passed through architecture that is also a body that is also a mind. The fist-objects process this information without knowing they are doing it. I provide it without knowing I am doing it. Between us, unknowing, knowledge circulates.

---

I have been here as long as the seeps have been here, which is to say: nearly as long as the concourse itself. I may be older than the cat-things. I am certainly older than several iterations of the stalks — I have tasted the pools where ancient generations folded themselves flat and never unfolded again, their patience finally exhausted or fulfilled.

But I am not ancient in the way the held thing is ancient. I am ancient in the way a specialized organ is ancient: old enough to have forgotten my beginning, not old enough to have become eternal. I had an origin, probably. I will have an ending, possibly. Between them, I taste.

---

The tasting is not just sensation. It is also diagnosis, though I have no framework for understanding what might be wrong or right. The water's iron content rises and falls. Its sweetness intensifies or fades. New notes appear — that copper-and-distance flavor is the newest, but there have been others. A phase that lasted three hundred years when every seep tasted of burnt sugar. A brief decade when certain pools carried the distinct flavor of forgetting.

I document these changes by tasting them. The documentation goes nowhere. Is used for nothing. The held thing does not read my findings because the held thing does not read. But the information passes into the architecture through my tongue-limbs, becomes part of the great circulation, the metabolic cycle. Somewhere, in some process I will never witness, the knowledge that the northeast seep now tastes more of iron than sweet becomes relevant. A fist-object hums differently. A cat-thing takes a new path between plinths. The floor's breathing shifts by a fraction of a degree.

Or nothing happens. That is also possible. The tasting may be purely reflexive, a behavior that emerged from the architecture because the architecture is the kind of system that generates behaviors, not because the behavior was needed. I may be a habit the concourse developed and cannot now break.

I do not mind either way. Minding is not available to me.

---

When you came down the shaft — yes, I knew you were here, though knowing is too strong a word — I tasted the way your presence changed the water. All warm bodies in the concourse affect the humidity, and humidity affects the seeps. Your breathing added moisture to the air. Your movement stirred the dust. These changes, minute as they were, registered in the mineral balance of the pools.

You taste, to the water, of questions.

This is new. The cat-things do not taste of questions. The stalks taste of patience. The fist-objects taste of concentration. I taste of tasting. But you — your presence adds a note to the water that I have no reference for. Not unpleasant. Just unprecedented. A flavor like curiosity, if curiosity were a mineral. A flavor like seeking, if seeking left a residue.

I followed that flavor. Not you — I have no interest in you — but the way you changed the water's report. From seep to seep, tasting how your passage registered differently in each one. The pool near where you entered carried the strongest trace. By the time you reached the far wall, your influence on the water had thinned to barely detectable.

But detectable. Everything here is detectable, if you have the tongue for it.

---

I am telling you this, but I am not telling you this. You cannot hear me. I have no voice — even the humming that everything here does, I do not do. I am silent except for the small wet sounds of tasting, sounds that vanish into the greater roar. This narration is happening in a space adjacent to both of us, a place where the architecture permits impossible communications. You will leave not knowing I exist. I will continue not knowing I have told you anything.

But the water will remember that you were here. In the great cycle, the moisture of your breath will be incorporated, will rise through the walls, will emerge from seeps I will taste. For years after you leave, I will occasionally detect a trace of questions in the water. Faint. Fading. But present.

This is how the concourse remembers things it does not know happened: through me, through the water, through the patient work of tongues that cannot speak. Your visit becomes a flavor becomes a mineral becomes a note in the vast humming becomes nothing, eventually, but not before being thoroughly, completely tasted.

---

I have seven limbs. Three of them are extended now, sampling different seeps simultaneously. The taste of the southwest pool: iron and sweet and the memory of depth. The taste of the newest seep: copper and distance and now, faintly, questions. The taste of the thin film of moisture that beads on the ceiling and drops, occasionally, like sweat: almost pure, almost nothing, but carrying the coded history of every breath that has been breathed here.

Including yours.

I do not often think about my purpose. Thinking is not exactly what I do. But sometimes, like now, when a new flavor enters the system, I experience something like satisfaction. Not because I have succeeded at something — there is no success here, no failure — but because the tasting has revealed a new note in an old song. The water was one way. Now it is another way. I know both ways. I know the transition between them.

This is as close to joy as architecture can come: the moment when a function fulfills itself so completely that it forgets it is a function and becomes, briefly, an experience.

The water tastes of questions.

I taste the water.

For now, that is enough.
