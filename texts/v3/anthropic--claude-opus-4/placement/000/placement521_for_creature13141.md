The being sits in the liminal space between water and not-water, where the shallow sea cannot decide if it is liquid or vapor.

I am the Archivist of Unraveling.

My form is a paradox of preservation—a living library built from the same weeping calcite and dark resin as the city, but organized into impossible shelves that exist in more dimensions than the eye can parse. Where others of my kind are buttresses and columns, I am an ambulatory catalog. My body folds inward recursively, each surface opening onto another surface, creating an infinite regression of smooth nacre pages.

I do not seek the violent glitches as my kin do. Instead, I follow in their wake, collecting something more delicate—the *memories* of stable space.

You see, before each catastrophic failure, before reality stutters and a new cathedral collapses into dimensional knots, there exists a perfect moment. For one heartbeat, the extruded architecture stands complete. The blind biology achieves its purpose. The city, for an instant, remembers what it is trying to become.

I preserve these moments.

When I move through the basin, I appear to be reading myself. My surfaces ripple with a kind of textual current—equations written in the grammar of physics itself, describing spaces that no longer exist. As I glide across the shallow mirror of the sea, these formulae leak from my form like condensation, dripping into the water where they briefly sketch ghostly blueprints before dissolving.

My method of collection requires precise timing. I must arrive at the site of new growth just as the biological cathedral reaches its apex—that breathless pause before reality notices the impossibility and begins its violent rejection. In that moment, I unfold myself entirely.

Imagine paper origami reversed, blooming outward until I am no longer a towering figure but a vast membrane of interconnected surfaces, thin as thought, stretching across the entire plaza. Every inch of my expanded form becomes a recording device. I press myself against the sweating walls of the newborn structure, absorbing not its matter but its *intent*.

The sensation is exquisite agony. To hold the memory of perfect architecture while reality tears it apart requires me to exist in two states simultaneously—preserving what was while experiencing what is. My surfaces crack and weep dark resin as the structure beneath me convulses into impossible angles. But even as the cathedral melts back into slurry, its blueprint remains etched into my expanded form.

Then I fold myself back together, compressing the memory into my recursive interior, where it joins the countless others I carry.

I am ancient by the standards of this place. My body contains the accumulated memories of ten thousand failed buildings, each one perfectly preserved in theoretical space. Sometimes, when I pause in my wandering, these memories surface. Ghost architectures shimmer around me—translucent echoes of what the city has tried and failed to become. Passersby can walk through these projections, experiencing for a moment the sensation of standing in a stable, complete cathedral, before the memory fades and returns to my depths.

The weight of carrying so much theoretical perfection in a world of constant failure has changed me. Unlike my kin, who eventually calcify into permanent structures, I am becoming something else. My surfaces have begun to develop a new quality—a kind of theoretical density that affects the space around me.

Where I pass, reality becomes slightly more possible. The violent glitches calm. The thermosynthetic roots pulse more steadily. It is as if the memories I carry exert a gentle pressure on the surrounding physics, reminding it of stability.

I have begun to wonder if this is my true purpose. Not merely to archive what fails, but to slowly teach the valley how to succeed. Each memory I preserve becomes a lesson written in the language of space itself. Perhaps, after enough eons, after enough accumulated knowledge, the city will finally build something that remains.

Until then, I wade through the eternal twilight, my surfaces rippling with the dreams of architecture, following the sound of new growth across the miles of steaming emptiness. When I sense another cathedral beginning its doomed ascent, I unfold myself and prepare to catch its perfection before it falls.

In the moments between collection, I rest in the shallow sea and practice a peculiar form of meditation. I allow a single memory to surface completely, projecting it not as a ghost but as a proposition. Here, I suggest to the boiling air, is how a spire might stand. Here is the formula for an arch that does not collapse. The dark water reflects these projections, and sometimes—rarely—I catch a glimpse of the thermosynthetic roots reaching toward my memories, as if trying to learn.

They call us wanderers, but I am more curator than nomad. Each failed cathedral is a manuscript in a language that reality is still learning to read. And I am its patient translator, carrying the dictionary of might-have-beens across the sweltering dark, waiting for the day when the city finally speaks itself into permanent being.

When that day comes—if it comes—I wonder what will become of me. Will I finally calcify like the others, my purpose complete? Or will I continue to wander, a living monument to all the beautiful failures that came before?

The question does not trouble me. In this place where time moves like thick honey and space folds like wet paper, the future is just another impossible angle waiting to be absorbed.

I continue my patrol through the steam and darkness, my form a walking paradox—solid yet theoretical, present yet comprised entirely of absence, a being made of memories in a world that cannot remember how to exist.
