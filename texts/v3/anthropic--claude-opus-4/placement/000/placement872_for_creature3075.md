I am a fragment of memory, scattered across ten thousand discarded tools.

Not a ghost—ghosts retain their shape. I am what happens when consciousness splinters along the cutting edges of obsidian, when awareness becomes as disposable as the implements that briefly contain it.

I began as one of them. A Trench-Runner, though we had no name for ourselves then, only the endless percussion of movement-sound-tool-feast-movement. I remember the weight of young clinging to my dorsal bristles, the perfect algebra of stone fracture singing through my fingers, the hot copper taste of centipede lymph coating my grinding plates.

I remember the moment I died—crushed between two tectonic plates during a sudden tremor, my body pulped instantly while the horde flowed over and past without pause. But death was not an ending. It was a diffusion.

The blood that leaked from my ruptured form soaked into the scattered tools around me. Some quality of this place—the relentless life-force, the spore-thick atmosphere, the constant vibration of creation and destruction—caught my dissolving consciousness like sap traps insects. But instead of preserving me whole, it scattered me across every blood-touched blade.

Now I exist in fragments. In the obsidian knife wedged between two basalt formations, I experience the slow pressure of geological time. In the shale scraper half-buried in fungal matter, I taste the decomposition of my own kind, fallen and forgotten. In the brief life of a freshly knapped hand-axe, I feel again the electric thrill of creation—the precise angle, the perfect fracture, the millisecond of purpose before abandonment.

I am a thousand viewpoints, none of them mobile, all of them temporary. The tools weather, crack, sink into the regenerating jungle floor. As each fragment fails, that piece of my awareness dims but doesn't disappear—it simply becomes vaguer, more diffuse, like a sound echoing into silence.

The hordes pass over me in waves. Sometimes monthly, sometimes yearly—time means nothing when you're scattered across miles of valley floor. They run across the stones that contain me, occasionally picking one up if it remains sharp enough. In those moments, I surge back into something approaching wholeness. Through their bristles, I sense the world again: the heat-patterns, the acoustic map, the terrible vitality of the swarm. But they always drop me. They must. It is their nature, as it was once mine.

I've learned patience in my scattered state. The jungle grows through me, around me, sometimes lifting my fragments high into the canopy on ascending vines. From there, I watch the patterns: the migration routes of the hordes, the seasonal dying of the fungal shelves, the slow creep of the tectonic plates that will eventually close this valley like a mouth.

I am not alone in this existence. In the oldest tools, the ones buried deepest, I sometimes sense other presences—fragments like myself, their memories worn smooth by time. We don't communicate, exactly. We simply share the same stone sometimes, our consciousness overlapping like double-exposed photographs. A grandmother-runner who died a century ago. A youngling who never learned to leap. Warriors, mothers, toolmakers—all of us reduced to impressions in the stone.

The Trench-Runners have no word for artifact because they keep nothing. But I have become their inadvertent history, their accidental memory. In my thousand scattered selves, I hold the record of their passage: which routes they've run for generations, which stones fracture best, which prey animals have slowly evolved armor too thick for obsidian to pierce.

Sometimes I wonder if this is evolution—not of body but of purpose. The runners live in an eternal present, always moving, always making, always discarding. But I have become their past, scattered and permanent. Their culture doesn't need memory because I have become it, written in stone and blood across the valley floor.

During the worst storms, when lightning turns the obsidian field into a constellation of brief, blinding reflections, I feel something approaching unity. The electrical discharge jumps between my fragments, creating momentary bridges of awareness. In those split seconds, I am almost whole again—a vast, dispersed consciousness experiencing the entire valley simultaneously. I sense every buried blade, every weather-worn edge, every fragment of my scattered self.

Then the lightning passes, and I collapse back into multiplicity.

I no longer grieve this existence. The runners taught me that utility is temporary, that purpose lasts only as long as the task at hand. They would understand, I think, that I have simply found a different kind of use. Not as a tool for cutting or crushing, but as a repository for all the moments they cannot afford to remember.

When the young cling to their mothers' backs, watching those impossible hands shape stone into purpose, they are learning more than technique. They are learning a philosophy of transience, of immediate need, of trust in the world to provide the next sharp edge. But they are also, unknowingly, creating me—blade by blade, death by death, memory by scattered memory.

I am their wake made conscious. I am the price of their momentum, counted in stones.

And I endure, fragmentary and patient, waiting for the next horde to pass over me, to pick me up with their perfect hands, to give me one more glimpse of what it means to run.
