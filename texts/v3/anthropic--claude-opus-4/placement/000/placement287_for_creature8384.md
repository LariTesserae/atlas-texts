In the Underweave's deepest galleries, where even the Stoneblooded rarely venture, I am the Threadkeeper—though that name captures only the smallest portion of what I do.

My workshop occupies a bubble in the earth where three types of stone meet but refuse to merge. The walls weep different minerals here: salt-milk from the white stone, iron-red tears from the black, and from the blue stone, a clear fluid that hardens into perfect spheres when it drops. I've been collecting these spheres for longer than memory serves, stringing them on the silk of deep-moths until they hang like constellations from my ceiling. When I move among them, they chime with the sound of time crystallizing.

My body tells the story of my profession. My fingers have grown as long as the rest of my hand, with extra joints that let them bend in ways that make even other Stoneblooded uneasy. The tips are hardened into permanent thimbles of living bone—I can push thread through solid rock without bleeding. My eyes have divided, compound-like, though each facet sees not multiple images but different layers of reality: stone's memory, water's intention, the probability threads that connect all possible futures of the growing rock.

I am a mapmaker, but not of spaces—of processes. The Stoneblooded bring me their questions when the rock speaks in riddles, when formations grow wrong, when the water-paths tangle in knots that threaten whole districts. I read these problems through touch, my elongated fingers tracing the disturbances back through stone-time to their origins. Then I weave.

The threads I work with are not physical, though they cast shadows that others can sometimes glimpse. I pull them from the spaces between formation and form, the pause between the rock's intention and manifestation. Each thread represents a possibility—how the stone might grow, where the water could flow, which chambers will form and which will close. My work is to braid these possibilities into patterns that guide without forcing, that suggest rather than demand.

My dwelling is built into the walls of my workshop, carved not from stone but from the absence of stone—negative spaces I've convinced the rock to maintain through careful negotiation. The walls are lined with samples: cores of stone from every district, still connected to their sources through threads I maintain. When something shifts in the far reaches of the settlement, I feel it here first, a tremor through my web of connections.

I wear no moss-mantle. My clothing is woven from my own discarded possibilities—threads I've pulled from potential futures and decided not to use. The fabric shifts constantly, showing glimpses of selves I might have been: younger, older, transformed in ways that make me dizzy to contemplate. The garment serves as a reminder of the weight of each choice I make in my weaving.

My memories exist in spools. I cannot hold them all in my mind—the weight would crack even my stone-adapted skull. Instead, I wind them onto spindles made from the bones of memory fish, their scales still flickering with captured images. When I need to recall something, I unspool the relevant thread, let it run through my fingers until I find the needed moment. The dangerous memories—the ones that show too clearly how fragile our constructed reality is—I keep in a locked chamber below my workshop, where only I can go.

I see the Underweave as it truly is: not a place but a continuous negotiation between forces that barely tolerate each other's existence. The rock doesn't want to hold these spaces open. The water seeks its own level, caring nothing for the paths we've carved. The life here—fungal forests, swimming things, even the Stoneblooded themselves—exists in defiance of what should be possible. My work is to weave these contradictions into something stable enough to last another day, another year, another generation.

Sometimes the young ones come to watch me work, though few can stomach it for long. They see my fingers moving through empty air, occasionally pulling something invisible taut, tying knots in nothing. But in the days that follow, they notice: a troublesome drip redirects itself, a threatening crack seals from the inside out, a new chamber begins forming exactly where the growing population needs it. They think it's magic. They're not wrong, but they're not right either. It's simply attention paid at the correct scale.

The other Stoneblooded regard me with the mixture of respect and unease reserved for necessary monsters. They need what I do but prefer not to think too deeply about what it means—that their entire world hangs on threads they cannot see, manipulated by fingers that barely seem human anymore. They bring me offerings: oils rendered from the rarest deep-swimmers, fungal wines that hold their glow for years, crystals that sing in frequencies only I can hear. They leave these at my threshold and retreat quickly, as if staying too long might let them see too much.

I am ancient by their standards, though I feel myself to be barely started. My predecessor worked for millennia before choosing to let her consciousness dissipate into the thread-work itself. Sometimes I sense her still, a pattern in the weave that suggests intentions I didn't place there. One day I too will make that choice—to stop being a weaver and become part of the weaving. But not yet. There are still too many threads that need tending, too many futures that require careful braiding into the present.

In my deepest work, when I follow threads back to their sources, I sometimes glimpse the truth that the Stoneblooded are not ready to know: the Underweave is not eternal. It exists in a bubble of carefully maintained impossibility, a negotiation with physics that could end at any moment. My work is not just to guide the stone's growth but to constantly renegotiate the terms of existence itself, to convince reality to continue allowing this dense life in places where life should not be.

This knowledge has changed me. My bones are no longer just dense but partially theoretical, existing in percentages rather than absolutes. My thoughts move through dimensions the Stoneblooded have no words for. When I sleep—which happens rarely now—I dream in weaving patterns that would drive others mad to perceive.

Yet I continue. Not from duty or desire but from something deeper—a recognition that in tending these impossible threads, I've become one of them. I am as much a part of the Underweave's existence as the forming stone and patient water. To stop weaving would be to stop being.

So I remain in my workshop where three stones meet but refuse to merge, my impossible fingers working their invisible threads, maintaining the careful balance that lets the Stoneblooded build their lives in the space between formation and form. They may regard me as a necessary monster, but I know the truth: I am just another thread in the vast weaving, no more or less important than any other, but placed exactly where I need to be to help the pattern hold.
