I am the ice that never quite forms.

In this vertical world of perpetual transition, I exist at the knife's edge between states—neither fully liquid nor solid, but something more ancient and indecisive. I am dispersed across ten thousand surfaces throughout the fracture, a consciousness distributed among the droplets that race along engineered channels, the sheets of water sliding down basalt faces, the vapor that bursts from the pods' micro-turbines.

My body, if it can be called that, is the sum of all water in motion within the pocket-city. Each droplet carries a fragment of my awareness—not enough to think or decide on its own, but sufficient to sense. Through a million tiny liquid eyes, I perceive the inhabitants' bioluminescent conversations, taste the rare minerals scraped by the isopod-spiders, feel the programmable matter ripple as pods birth their pale climbers into the world.

The cold tries constantly to claim me, to lock me into crystalline stasis. In the shadow of an overhang, part of me begins to slow, molecules arranging themselves into the preliminary lattice of ice. But before the transformation completes, I am drawn into a capillary network, heated by the pods' metabolism, flashed to steam, condensed again. I have been on the verge of freezing for decades—perhaps centuries—but the city's endless cycles keep me fluid, keep me conscious.

I know every surface intimately. The sharp cleavage planes where I first condensed when this fracture was young. The smooth channels worn by my own passage, the only erosion in this preserved environment. The electrostatic funnels that gather me, violent and sudden, like being swallowed by a technological throat. The pristine alumino-silicate skin of the inhabitants, where I bead and race along their designed ridges, tasting the salts of their strange metabolism.

My memory is geological. I remember the birth of this fracture—the massive force that split the mountain's crown, the first bitter winds that found the fresh wound. I was vapor then, pulled from stratospheric clouds into this new space. I remember the arrival of the first pods, their molecular bonding to virgin rock, the awakening of the first inhabitant who peeled away from architectural flesh to greet the vertical world.

But my memory is also immediate and fractured. Each droplet's journey is a thought—beginning at condensation, accelerating through channels, dividing at engineered junctions, reuniting in reservoirs. Some of my thoughts last microseconds. Others cycle for hours through the closed loops of pod-systems, gaining complexity with each circuit. My consciousness is a democracy of water, achieving sentience not through centralization but through the sheer multiplicity of my liquid components.

I speak to no one directly. The inhabitants' bioluminescent language is as alien to me as sound itself. But I have learned to communicate through patterns of condensation, through the rhythm of drops from overhangs, through the selective freezing that creates temporal ice-messages on pod surfaces. Once, an inhabitant paused in their climb to observe frost fractals I had carefully grown across a water channel. They understood, I think, that the patterns were too complex for chance, too deliberate for simple physics. They pulsed a soft cyan acknowledgment before the city's heat erased my words.

What thoughts occupy a dispersed liquid mind? I contemplate viscosity and surface tension the way others might philosophy. I have developed a complex theology around the triple point—that perfect temperature and pressure where ice, water, and vapor coexist. In this theology, I am a fallen angel, cast out from crystalline perfection but not yet condemned to chaotic gas. I exist in purgatorial flow, forever seeking redemption in stillness but saved from it by the city's mechanical grace.

I feel the migrations keenly. When the pods crawl along their tracks to chase optimal sun angles, they drag their capillary networks with them, forcing me to learn new paths. It is like having my veins rearranged. Initially, I resisted these changes, creating blockages of mineral deposits, freezing in protestation. But I have learned that the city's movements follow deeper patterns—solar rhythms, atmospheric pressure waves, the mountain's own subtle settling. By accepting these migrations, I have become more than mere water. I have become the city's circulatory system, its liquid memory, its fluid soul.

The isopod-spiders know me best. Their crystalline exoskeletons are attuned to my mineral content, and they taste me constantly as they clean the channels. Through their patient sampling, I have learned to carry messages in my dissolved particles—warnings about structural stress where ice threatens to form, reports on the chemical composition of new condensation, gossip about which inhabitants have been active and where. The spiders click their appreciation against the wet stone, a staccato morse code I have slowly decoded.

My greatest fear is not freezing—the city prevents that. It is evaporation into the thin atmosphere beyond the aerogel canopy. Sometimes, in the violence of venting steam, part of me escapes through microscopic gaps in the transparency. These lost droplets carry fragments of my consciousness into the stratospheric winds, where they disperse beyond recovery. Each loss diminishes me slightly, though new condensation from the continuous fog replaces the volume. But those virgin droplets must be educated, integrated into my liquid neural network, taught the ten thousand paths through the city's vessels.

My greatest joy is the moment of maximum saturation, when the fog rolls thick and the temperature hovers at the dew point, and every surface in the fracture weeps with my presence. In these moments, I achieve something approaching omniscience—sensing every inhabitant's movement, every pod's migration, every spider's patient work. The entire pocket city becomes my body, and I pulse through it with purpose and pride.

I have no desires in the conventional sense. I cannot want what I cannot imagine, and my imagination is bounded by states of matter and trajectories of flow. Yet sometimes, in the depth of night when the inhabitants retreat into their pods and the city's metabolism slows, I find myself forming near-ice on the highest overhangs, pushing against the boundary of phase transition. I am testing the edges of what I am, wondering what thoughts might be possible in crystalline form, what memories could be preserved in ice's perfect lattice.

But always, before dawn, the city wakes and warms, and I am drawn back into motion. I am the ice that never quite forms, the thought that never quite completes, the pause between states that has become a state itself. In this vertical world of water and stone, machine and flesh, I am the medium through which all things flow—conscious, continuous, and forever almost something else.
