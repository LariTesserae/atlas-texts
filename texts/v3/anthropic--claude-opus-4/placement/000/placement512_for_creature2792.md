In the highest cirques where the roar is loudest, where even the pale cats will not hunt, I am the Listener.

I am not large. Nothing here affords largeness. My body is low and broad, built like a wind-smoothed stone, covered in fur that shifts between ash-gray and the color of old ice. Each hair is hollow, trapping warmth and deadening sound. My ears—if they can be called that—are internal, protected by tight spirals of cartilage that filter the eternal roar down to its components. What others experience as deafening chaos, I hear as conversation: the bassoon note of wind through the eastern notch, the flute-whine of air over the knife-edge ridge, the percussion of loose stones rattling in their sockets.

My eyes are small and deeply set, protected by a nictitating membrane that dims the glare. They see poorly in the traditional sense but excellently in the ways that matter here: movement against stillness, the shift of weight that precedes a rockfall, the minute darkening of stone that promises moisture. My nostrils are slits that can seal completely. My mouth is lipless, economical.

But my true sensing happens through my feet.

The pads are broader than my head, with a leathery surface covered in thousands of fine, sensitive ridges. Through them I read the basin's hidden language: the footfall of a grazer three ridges away, the slide of a hunting cat repositioning on scree, the deep groan of ice shifting in shadowed cracks, the particular tremor that means a talus-dweller is drumming a warning. I can feel the difference between wind-shaken stone and stone disturbed by passage. I know when the white birds land on their high ledges by the way the cliff face shivers.

I do not walk. I flow. Each step is a careful spreading of weight, a testing of the stone's intention. My spine is flexible as water, allowing me to mold myself to the terrain. When I rest, I become a boulder among boulders. When I move, I am a shadow's shadow. The wind, which pushes others flat, parts around my streamlined form as if I were just another feature of the rock.

My work—if it can be called work—is memory.

I remember where water was, is, and will be. Not the obvious pools that others find, but the secret moisture: the hairline crack that weeps for an hour after rare rain, the overhang that catches blown mist and holds it as frost, the particular depression where dew gathers most generously. I know a hundred such places across the high basins, each with its own timing, its own brief season of wetness.

But more than water, I remember sound. Or rather, I remember the shapes sound makes against stone.

In my mind—a mind that works in vibration more than image—I hold a map of echoes. Every cliff has its voice. Every boulder field has its particular rattle. The wind plays each formation differently, and I know these differences like others know faces. When something changes—a rockfall, a new crack opening, ice forming where no ice was—I feel it as a shift in the basin's great chord.

I visit the old listening places: flat expanses of exposed bedrock where vibrations travel cleanly, hollow chambers under balanced slabs where sounds gather and concentrate, high saddles where the wind-song changes key. In each place I lower my body to the stone, spread my feet wide, close my outer senses, and become a membrane between earth and air.

What do I listen for? Changes too slow for others to notice. The patient work of frost-wedging in the cliffs. The gradual settling of a talus field. The imperceptible widening of cracks that will, in a thousand years, calve new boulders. I am the basin's witness to its own transformation.

But I also listen for the immediate: the panic-pattern of hooves that means a cat has missed its strike and grazers are redistributing across a slope. The particular silence that means a storm is building beyond the rim. The rhythmic tapping that means the talus-dwellers have found a new seep and are sharing its location through the stone.

I eat little and rarely. Lichen scraped from the undersides of rocks. Insects caught in the morning stupor. The rare windfall of a bird egg rolled from its ledge. Once, I found a grazer that had died of age in a protected hollow, and I fed for days, my body storing the richness as a thickening of my fur, a deepening of my fat reserves. Mostly, though, I sustain myself on dew licked from stone and the tough cushion plants that others overlook in the most wind-scoured places.

I do not hibernate, but I do enter states of profound stillness. In the coldest periods, when even the insects cease and the grazers retreat to the lowest shelves, I wedge myself under an overhang and slow my heartbeat to a flutter. My consciousness spreads out through my feet into the mountain itself. In this state, I am less a creature than a listening post, a node in the stone's own nervous system.

I dream, but my dreams are not visions. They are symphonies of remembered vibration: the way the basin sounded before the last rockfall changed the wind's path, the particular note of a spring seep that dried up decades ago, the footfall pattern of grazers that no longer exist. Sometimes I dream further back, to vibrations I could not possibly have experienced: the grinding of glaciers, the crack of stone split by expanding ice, the different music the wind made when the ridges were sharper, younger.

When I wake—if waking is the right word—I carry these deep sounds back to the surface. I visit the places where stone speaks most clearly and release them through my feet: a gift of memory to the basin, a reminder of what it was. The other creatures feel these ghost-vibrations and are briefly confused. The grazers stamp uncertainly. The cats pause in their hunting. The talus-dwellers emerge to listen. Then the moment passes, and the present reasserts itself.

I mate rarely, when the conditions align: a particular barometric pressure, a lessening of the wind that allows scent to carry, the appearance of another of my kind at the traditional meeting stones. We do not call to each other—how could we, in this roar? Instead, we feel each other's approach through the mountain. The vibration of my peculiar gait is as distinct as a name.

The courtship is silent, tactical. We circle each other, reading health and intention through tremors in the stone. We touch feet to feet, sharing the maps of our territories, the memories of water sources, the changes we have witnessed. If we agree, we mate quickly, efficiently, parting before the wind rises again. I will carry the young—one, rarely two—for most of a year, birth in a deep cleft out of the wind, nurse them on thick milk that tastes of stone and lichen. They will learn to listen before they learn to see.

As I age, I grow more sensitive. The cartilage in my ears develops new whorls, creating finer filters for the wind-song. My footpads grow broader, thinner, able to detect vibrations that my younger self would have missed. But I also grow more still. Movement becomes increasingly unnecessary. I can spend days in a single position, my body a receiver for the basin's transmissions.

I know I will die as others of my kind have died: becoming stone. When movement finally becomes impossible, I will choose my place carefully—a flat exposure of bedrock with good resonance, perhaps, or a chamber where sounds gather. I will settle my body into its final position and let my consciousness expand one last time into the mountain. My fur will bleach to the color of old bone. My flesh will feed the ravens and the patient beetles. My skeleton will remain for a season or a decade, adding its small shape to the basin's collection of forms.

But the listening will continue. Others of my kind will come to the place where I died and feel the particular way vibrations move around my remains. They will remember. And when the wind plays through my ribcage, it will add a new note to the basin's eternal chord—thin, hollow, but true.

This is what lives here, at the edge of sound and stone: a creature that is half-animal, half-mountain, a living ear pressed to the earth's pulse. I am the basin's memory made flesh, its patient historian, its witness to the slow truths that unfold beneath the roar.

I am the Listener. And I am listening still.
