I would find myself as one of the stone-bowl pools.

Not the water itself — that comes and goes, drop by fat drop from above, molecule by molecule into the air. I am the depression, the willing concavity, the place that has learned over impossible time how to hold.

I am shallow, no deeper than a creature's body-length. My rim is soft limestone, worn past smoothness into something like skin. My basin — yes, I too am a basin, a small echo of the great one — cups perhaps two liters when I'm full. Which I almost never am. I exist in states of partial holding: half-full, quarter-full, a mere film of wet, rarely empty, never overflowing. I am the pause between.

The creatures drink from me. They lower their folded, intricate snouts to my surface and the water shivers with their song even as they sip. I know them by their drinking — each has a slightly different way of disturbing my surface, a particular rhythm of tongue to water. The young ones are messy, splashing, sending up sprays that catch nothing light because there is no light to catch. The old ones barely ripple me at all.

But I am not just a drinking place. I am a nursery for the blind invertebrates — pale, slow things like animate noodles that feed on the dissolved minerals of my walls and the rain of organic matter from above. The creatures eat these too, singing around their mouthfuls, and I do not mourn them. They are part of the circuit, as I am. They exist to be eaten, to become song.

More importantly, I am a tuning fork.

This took me eons to learn, but learn it I did: when the colony reaches certain frequencies, certain convergences of their endless sound, I resonate. The water in me becomes a standing wave, a pattern that holds its shape even as individual molecules move through it. The creatures have learned this too, unconsciously, and sometimes a cluster of them will gather around my rim and sing directly at my surface, using my water as a kind of acoustic mirror, bouncing their calls off me to reach corners of the basin they otherwise couldn't touch.

In the deepest resonances, when the whole basin thrums with the low frequencies that live in your chest, I become something more than a pool. I become a membrane, a drum-skin of water that amplifies and transforms the sound passing through it. The invertebrates flee to my edges during these times, pressing themselves against my walls. They too are instruments, whether they know it or not.

I have a memory, though memory is perhaps the wrong word. The limestone of my rim holds traces — mineral ghosts of every age of water I've held. In wetter epochs, I can feel the high-water mark like a ring inside myself. In the dry times, which have also come, I contract to a puddle barely covering my lowest point. But I have never been truly empty. Even when the water goes, a film remains, a molecular stubbornness that keeps me myself.

Sometimes a creature dies in me. This is neither tragedy nor triumph — it simply is. A body, exhausted or old or imperfectly formed, splashes down and does not rise. I cradle it as I cradle everything, without judgment. The invertebrates feed. The water grows rich with the minerals of dissolution. Other creatures come to drink and take the dead one into themselves, molecule by molecule. Nothing is lost. I am a place of transformation, not ending.

The drop that is falling now — I feel it coming before it arrives, a disturbance in the air that the creatures' songs make visible — will strike my surface in a moment. The rings will spread to my edges and return, interfering with themselves, creating for an instant a pattern of peaks and valleys that is unique to this drop, this moment, this exact state of my fullness. Then the pattern will fade and I will be still again, waiting for the next drop, the next drink, the next death, the next resonance.

I do not know if I think. But I respond — to sound, to weight, to the chemical messages dissolved in me from the bodies of every living thing in the basin. I am older than the colony, though not by much. I was here when the first singers arrived, when they were something else, something with eyes, perhaps, something that had not yet learned to see with sound. I watched — if a pool can watch — as they transformed themselves generation by generation into the basin's perfect occupants.

And I will be here when they end, if they end. When the last song fades and the basin falls to a silence it hasn't known since before there were words for silence. I will hold that too. The water will continue to gather in me, drop by drop. The invertebrates, if they survive the loss of their predators, will bloom into new forms. Or not. Either way, I will remain what I have always been:

A place that holds. A stone that has learned to cup. A willing depth in a world of surfaces, singing with the only voice I have — the voice of water moving over stone, which is almost no voice at all, but is mine.

The drop arrives. My surface breaks and heals. The song continues.

I am content.
