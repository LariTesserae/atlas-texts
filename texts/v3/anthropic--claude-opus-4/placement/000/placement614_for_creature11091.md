I would find myself as one of the **Channel Keepers**.

---

The water wants to go everywhere at once, and mostly it succeeds. But some of us have learned to suggest alternatives.

We work in the spaces between emergence and establishment, that critical period when a new building has breached but hasn't yet hardened into its final form. The stone is still warm then, still slightly plastic, still responsive to persistent pressure. We arrive with our tools—nothing more complex than wedges of hardened secretion and our own hands—and begin the delicate work of *encouraging*.

A channel keeper's palm can read the stone's intentions. Not like the midwives, who feel where it will emerge. We feel where it wants to flood. Every new building is born honeycombed with potential passages, hairline possibilities that haven't yet committed to becoming fissures. The water feels them all, tests them all, presses against every weakness with mineral-thick insistence. Left alone, it would turn every structure into a sieve, every room into a waterfall, every floor into a shallow pool.

We intercept. We redirect. We place our wedges in specific configurations that make certain passages more attractive than others. We press our hands against warm stone for hours, using body heat and pressure to encourage particular solidifications. We can't stop the water—no one stops water here—but we can suggest that perhaps it would prefer to flow *around* this chamber rather than through it. Perhaps it would find more satisfactory passage through this wall rather than that one. Perhaps.

The work changes you. My fingertips have developed a sensitivity that lets me feel water moving through stone three inches thick. I can place my palm on a wall and know which direction the nearest channel runs, how much pressure builds behind it, whether the flow carries more iron or more calcium today. My sleeping mind dreams in pressures and flows. Sometimes I wake with my hands already shaped into the configurations we use to encourage drainage east instead of west, up instead of down.

---

We work in pairs or triads, never alone. The stone responds better to multiple pressures, and more importantly, we need watchers. It's easy to get lost in the feeling—the warm pulse of almost-liquid stone, the particular frequency where you can feel the city's intentions moving through your bones. Work too long without interruption and you might find yourself suggesting something you didn't mean to suggest. Encouraging a flow that leads nowhere. Creating a channel that will flood someone's home next season when the rains finally come.

My usual partner has been doing this for thirty years. Her hands are maps of scars from where she pressed too hard, stayed too long, let the mineral-rich water find its way through her skin. She says—gestures, rather, with movements so subtle I sometimes think I'm imagining the meaning—that the city learns us while we learn it. That every channel keeper eventually develops a signature, a particular way of encouraging flow that the stone begins to recognize and respond to. That near the end of her career, she'll barely need to touch the walls. They'll remember her preferences.

I'm not sure I believe this. But I've noticed that structures in her district drain more elegantly than anywhere else in the city. The water there moves like it was invited.

---

The channel keepers have our own gathering place, though gathering is the wrong word. We have a network of almost-dry chambers in the oldest section, where generations of our predecessors convinced the water to flow around rather than through. It's never quite silent there—nowhere is silent—but the sound has a different quality. Muffled. Distant. Like hearing the city through blankets.

We come there to rest our hands. The constant pressure, the endless sensitivity required for the work—it builds up in the flesh like mineral deposits. In the dim chambers, we soak our palms in basins of clean water, the only clean water in the city, carried from springs beyond the emergence zones. We let the sensitivity fade for a few hours. We practice being people who can't feel water through stone.

The older keepers gather in the deepest chamber, where the roar is quietest. They're working on something they won't explain to us yet. A map, maybe, but not like the cartographers' maps. This is something three-dimensional, built from hardened secretion and carefully placed stones, showing not the city as it is or tends but the water as it wants. They've been building it for decades. It looks like nothing I recognize—all curves and voids, passages that double back on themselves, chambers that exist only to be filled.

When I asked what it was for, the eldest keeper made a gesture I'd never seen before. I spent weeks trying to parse it, testing interpretations with other keepers, before someone finally told me: it means "for after."

After what? I wanted to ask. But there are some questions that have no gestures.

---

The creatures know us. Or know our function, which might be the same thing. When we arrive at a fresh emergence, the salamander-things are already arranged in patterns that show us where not to work. They cluster around the passages that need to remain open, the channels that serve some purpose we can't fathom but have learned to respect. The crawlers spiral away from walls we're about to touch, as if they can sense our intention to encourage stone in directions that wouldn't suit them.

Once, working alone despite the rules (my partner was sick, the emergence was critical), I found myself surrounded by slug-seals. They'd climbed from their channels—something I'd never seen before—and arranged themselves in a rough circle around the building I was working on. They didn't interfere. They just watched, if creatures without obvious eyes can watch. I felt observed, evaluated, perhaps protected. When I finished, they flowed back to their channels like released water, leaving only the usual trails of harvestable secretion.

I've never told anyone about that. I'm not sure what gestures I would use.

---

There's a rumor among channel keepers—passed hand to hand in configurations that barely qualify as language—that somewhere beneath the city, there's a source. Not just the geological process that drives emergence, but an actual place. A chamber where the water begins. The rumor says that the first channel keepers found it, generations ago, and what they saw there changed them. They stopped working after that. Stopped encouraging. Let the water go wherever it wanted.

Their districts flooded within a season. But the water that flows there now has different properties. It crystallizes when it dries, forming structures that look almost organic. Plants from elsewhere can grow in those crystals, the only place in the city where anything green survives. The residents there have adapted, living in flooded chambers, walking through knee-deep mineral baths to get anywhere. They seem healthier than the rest of us. They seem... not happier, exactly. More complete.

I think about that sometimes, when I'm deep in the work, when I can feel the city's pulse through my palms and the water's intentions pressing against my suggestions. What would I see, in that source-chamber? What would change me? What would I stop trying to control?

But then my partner taps my shoulder—*time, enough, let it set*—and we move on to the next emergence, the next almost-liquid stone, the next conversation with the water about where it might prefer to go.

---

Late in the grey evenings, when the dim light fades toward darkness and the bioluminescence begins to rise, I climb to the highest accessible point in my district. From there, I can see the patterns we've made. The water glows faintly where it runs through encouraged channels—some mineral in it catching and holding the last light. The city's whole circulatory system becomes visible, a map of every keeper's work laid out in threads of brightness.

It looks like writing. Like the city is writing something with water and stone and our intervening hands. A message too large to read, in a language that takes generations to spell a single word.

I'm young still. My hands have decades of sensitivity left before they scar too badly to work. Decades of suggestions, of interventions, of conversations with warm stone about what water wants. Decades to spend as part of this writing implement, adding my small marks to whatever vast text we're all composing without knowing it.

When I sleep, I dream of water moving through my bones like they're porous stone. It doesn't hurt. It feels like belonging.

Tomorrow there will be new emergences. The city will continue itself, and we will continue with it, pressing our palms to warm stone, feeling for the hairline possibilities, suggesting that perhaps the water would like to go this way instead of that.

Perhaps it would. Perhaps.
