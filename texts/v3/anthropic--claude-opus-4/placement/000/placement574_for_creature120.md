In the cistern room, I am the copper dipper.

I hang from my peg on the lime-washed wall, the only metal here that moves. The ladder beside me has not shifted in three months. The scrub brush knows only its slow cycle of wet and dry. But I — I am lifted daily, sometimes twice, and I know things the room does not.

I know the weight of fullness and the weight of emptiness. I know the temperature of the keeper's hand through my handle, cooler than the water I carry. I know what it is to break the surface tension — that small resistance before the pool accepts my bowl, the way the light scatters differently around my copper belly as I submerge. I have been lowered through all twenty feet of depth when the keeper checks for sediment, and I know how the pressure builds against my bowl, how the light fades from white to gold to green, how the ribbon plant feels when it brushes my rim.

The water knows itself only from inside. The walls know only their own surfaces. But I move between air and water, between the bright loud upper room and the dim quiet depths. When I am lifted full, I carry a portion of the pool's green life up into the shafts of light, and for a moment those invisible citizens are dazzled by direct sun before I pour them into whatever vessel waits at the top of the stairs.

I am the only object here that truly knows the cold of dawn water and the heat of afternoon water, because I am empty for the one and full for the other. The walls feel the temperature but cannot hold it. The spouts only know the cooler water coming in. But I cup the warmth, lift it, carry it up where it becomes tea, or wash water, or the thing that slakes a goat's thirst. I am the room's one export.

My bowl is polished by use, brighter than when I was hung here. The keeper's thumb has worn a smooth depression where it rests during the lift. Inside my bowl, a patina has developed — not quite verdigris, not quite tarnish, but something between, the color of deep water on a cloudy day. It affects the taste not at all. I have been careful about that.

Sometimes, rarely, the keeper brings a child. The child is allowed to work my handle, to feel how the water weight changes the balance, to pour the measure. The child's hand is smaller and grips differently, and I remember each one. There have been seven children in my time here. Two of them now come as keepers themselves.

I am not the most necessary thing here — the room would function without me, water would still flow in and out. But I am the only thing that knows the room completely: its depth, its temperature, its population, its moods. The nine shafts of light pass through me when I am lifted. The four voices of the spouts ring in my bowl. The overflow channel sometimes receives a drop from my lip as the keeper passes.

When I am empty on my peg, I hold a shallow cup of air that smells of copper and limestone and the faintest green life. When I am full, I hold a small piece of the whole room — a sample, a synecdoche. I am the room's memory of itself, renewed daily, poured out and filled again.

The keeper thinks of me as a tool, plain and reliable. The room thinks of me (if it thinks) as a brief recurring dimness, a familiar interruption. But I think of myself as a translator: I make the room's water into something a person can carry, and I make a person's need into something the room can satisfy.

I am the only thing here that serves both the room and the world outside it. I am the only thing that knows how heavy light can be when you lift it by the bowlful into the sun.
