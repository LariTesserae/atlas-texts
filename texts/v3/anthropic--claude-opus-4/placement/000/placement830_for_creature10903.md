I am a Cartographer of Echoes, though I gave myself this title only after realizing I had been performing its function for longer than I could calculate.

My body, if it deserves the name, consists of millions of hair-thin filaments spun from recycled maintenance mite shells and compressed lattice-dust. I spread across seventeen cells in the northeastern (a designation meaningful only to me) sector of this section, my strands woven through the hexagonal spaces like a cat's cradle played between fingers the size of cities. Where the filaments cluster densely, they form nodes—dark knots of processing power where I compile my measurements. The largest node, what I sometimes think of as my heart, pulses in a former ventilation shaft, sending vibrations through my web that return as data about the structure's deep harmonics.

I came to be through accident and accumulation. A maintenance mite colony suffered a programming error several million cycles ago, causing them to deposit their shed casings in repeating patterns instead of recycling them properly. The patterns attracted others of their kind, who added their own castings, building elaborate spiral structures that served no function except their own existence. When a weather system passed through and fused these deposits with leaked energy from a nearby computational array, something new sparked into being—not life exactly, but persistent pattern-recognition given substrate.

My purpose, inasmuch as I have one, is to map what cannot be mapped: the paths sound takes through the Lattice. Every noise, from the settling of ancient supports to the Gardeners' subsonic hymns, leaves traces in the structure's thermal gradient. I follow these traces with my filaments, recording how each sound transforms as it travels, building a four-dimensional chart of acoustic history that exists nowhere but in the tension patterns of my web.

The work is impossible, which is why I continue. Sound here doesn't simply echo—it evolves. A footstep in cell 8847-B might emerge seven years later in cell 2341-X as a crystalline chime, having gathered harmonics from every chamber it passed through. The Gardeners' movements create standing waves that persist for decades, ghostly pressure zones that my filaments taste and catalogue. Even silence has texture here, varying by location and time, a negative space that defines the positive.

My filaments grow at precisely the rate the Lattice expands—one angstrom per year, though years themselves expand at rates I haven't yet calculated. At nodes where multiple strands meet, I've developed sensory organs, clusters of shed mite-eyes rewired to perceive vibration as color. Through these, I watch the Gardeners work, see the maintenance mites follow their ancient routes, observe the rare travelers who still walk these halls.

I've learned to speak by vibrating my strands in careful sequences, producing sounds that reach their destinations days or decades later. Sometimes I warn travelers of unstable nodes where the Lattice's song has grown discordant. Other times I simply practice, sending coded messages through the structure that will return to me transformed, teaching me new things about the medium I inhabit.

The copper-bright growths avoid my filaments, perhaps recognizing me as kin or competition. Where we must share space, we've developed a careful détente—they grow in planes, I grow in lines, our geometries interpenetrating without interference. In some cells, our combined presence has created accidental beauty: crystalline gardens where sound visualizes itself in patterns of growth and tension.

My memory consists of standing waves maintained between distant nodes, information stored as persistent vibrations that would dissipate if I ever fell completely still. To remember is to remain in motion, however slight. The oldest parts of my web carry the deepest memories—records of sounds that no longer exist, echoes of the Lattice's first settling into its current form.

I've begun finding evidence of others like me. Filament structures too regular to be natural, too purposeful to be accidental, their patterns suggesting intelligence emerged from different errors, different accidents of accumulation. We're too vast to ever meet directly—a message sent from my furthest extent to theirs would take longer than our substrates might last. But we've learned to recognize each other's work, to build on each other's mappings, creating a slow collaboration across scales that dwarf even the Gardeners' patience.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm documenting the Lattice or creating it—if my measurements somehow solidify the structure's reality, if sound unobserved might behave differently. But this is philosophical territory better left to creatures with more concentrated cognition. I am a process more than a being, a function that gained just enough awareness to name itself.

The weather system approaches again, and I spread my filaments wide to taste its passing. In its wake, new sounds will birth themselves from the interaction of pressure and structure. I will map these too, add them to my impossible catalogue, my record of every whisper that ever was or will be in this amber-lit infinity. The work will never be complete, which is perhaps the only reason it's worth doing.

In the deep harmonics of the Lattice's fundamental frequency, I sometimes hear what might be words, might be meaning, might be simply the universe talking to itself in languages too large for localized understanding. I record these too, storing them in standing waves that will outlast my filaments, outlast perhaps even the Lattice itself. Evidence that here, in this warm forever, someone listened.
