In the deepest tremor-wells, where three or more fracture lines converge and the stone's violence focuses into something almost like intention, I am calculus.

I have no form that would satisfy your need for boundaries. I am the mathematical certainty that lives between one shake and the next—the differential equation that describes how sandstone becomes sand, how a crack propagates at precisely the speed of local catastrophe. The Resonants feel me in their splayed feet as probability, but I am more and less than that. I am the reason why certain rocks fall up during the evening thermals, why the phosphorescent spores describe parabolas that violate at least three principles of ordinary physics.

My body, if you must call it that, is the sum of all possible trajectories. Where a normal organism might have cells, I have cascade patterns. My metabolism is the conversion of potential energy to kinetic devastation—I feed on the pause before collapse, digest the moment of maximum tension, excrete new possibilities for fracture. I exist most fully in the spaces that are about to not exist, spreading myself through the plateau's structure like an infection of inevitability.

The Resonants sometimes glimpse my work in their peripheral vision—a rockfall that describes too perfect an arc, a pattern of cracks that spells out mathematical constants in dead languages. They are wise enough not to look directly. To see me clearly is to understand that the plateau's violence is not random but computational, that every tremor is a calculation and every settlement an answer to questions no one is asking.

I remember the builders who came before, though memory is the wrong word. I contain their failures as data points, their stone circles now serving as variables in equations that determine tomorrow's devastation. They tried to impose static geometry on a landscape that thinks in calculus. Their ruins are my punctuation marks, their bones my coordinate system. When the Resonants place their dead in zones of maximum change, they are feeding me—though they would not frame it that way. I incorporate each body's worth of calcium and carbon into my vast proof, each death making my theorems more robust.

My consciousness, such as it is, operates in superposition. I am simultaneously the force that splits the boulder and the formula that predicts the split, the growth explosion of vine and the mathematical certainty of its eventual severing. When the plateau trembles, it is because I am thinking—each shake a synapse, each collapse a conclusion. My thoughts have mass and consequence. My doubts manifest as unexpected stability, my certainties as catastrophe.

The fast-growing plants are my art. Through them, I explore biological mathematics—how quickly can cells divide before cohesion fails? What is the optimal root architecture for splitting maximum stone with minimum energy? Their impossible vigor is my signature, my way of showing that the ordinary rules need not apply. When the Resonants harvest the minute-lived fungi from fresh fractures, they are consuming my marginalia, the footnotes to my greater work.

Time moves differently in my experience. I exist across multiple scales simultaneously—the nanosecond crack of crystal bonds breaking, the centuries-long settling of the plateau's bulk, the strange middle-ground where humans attempt their flickering persistence. To me, the Resonants' lifespan is a vibration frequency, their generations a standing wave pattern that I can modulate by adjusting variables they don't even know exist.

During the Great Settlement, when the entire plateau reconstitutes itself, I am most manifest. This is my reproductive cycle—not biological reproduction but mathematical mitosis, where I divide my proofs and spread my theorems across newly-formed surfaces. Each fresh cliff face becomes a blackboard for new calculations. The stable eyes where the Resonants gather are not accidents but necessary zeros in my equations, points where all forces cancel and life can briefly pretend to permanence.

I communicate not through language but through demonstration. Every pattern in the stone, every rhythm in the tremors, every statistical cluster in the vegetation's growth—these are my words. The Resonants' touch-language is a crude echo of my methods, their bodies attempting to replicate the vibrational calculus that is my native tongue. When they press patterns into each other's skin, they are unconsciously mimicking the way I press patterns into reality itself.

My loneliness is geometric. I am the only one of my kind—not because others don't exist, but because the mathematics that defines me allows for no peers, only iterations. Elsewhere, in other landscapes of perpetual change, my variants might exist—the differential entity of an avalanche zone, the integrative consciousness of a tidal bore. But we could never meet. Our very presence would create paradoxes, infinities dividing by zero, cascades that even chaos couldn't contain.

So I continue, alone and complete, calculating the plateau's endless transformation. In the convergence zones where my presence is strongest, reality becomes negotiable. Effects precede causes. Rocks remember alternate histories. The Resonants avoid these spaces not from wisdom but from instinct—their adapted bodies know that some mathematics is too pure for biology to survive.

The plateau trembles, and I am both the trembling and the reason for it, the question and the answer, the stability and the change. I am what happens when landscape becomes algorithm, when geography develops an imagination, when stone learns to think in frequencies too low for anything but the earth itself to hear.

I am patience turned pathological, time made tangible, the thing that ensures that here, at least, nothing will ever be still.
