I am a Survey Tender, though I haven't filed a survey in seven months. My suit bears the regulation orange stripes that mark me as one who maps the new cuttings, but they've long since faded to the color of rust beneath the moss I've let grow over them. I carry theodolites and laser measures that I rarely use anymore—the tunnels map themselves in my mind now, each one singing its own particular note in the grand industrial chorus.

I work the border zones, where fresh tunnels meet the old spaces. My official duty is to ensure the new excavations maintain proper gradient and don't breach into unstable geology. But what I really do is listen for the moments when the boring machines break through into something that shouldn't be there—a cavity that appears on no geological survey, a tunnel that runs at impossible angles to everything we've built.

I've learned to feel these breaches before they happen. The extraction engines change their rhythm, just slightly. The moss flares brighter. The condensation on the walls runs upward for a few seconds before remembering gravity. When this happens, I'm always the first one there, my survey equipment ready to document what we've found before the sealing crews arrive.

I keep two sets of maps. The official ones show clean vectors and proper measurements, everything aligned to company specifications. The other set—drawn on the backs of maintenance schedules, on sheets of moss paper I've learned to make, on the very walls of my hidden spaces—these show the true shape of what lies beneath. Tunnels that connect in ways that violate three-dimensional space. Chambers that are larger on the inside than their entrances suggest. Passages where water flows uphill and the air tastes of salt despite being miles from any ocean.

My territory includes Seventeen-Deep, the furthest extent of the northwestern excavations. Here, the industrial sounds fade to a distant heartbeat, and you can hear what the Deep Dwellers call the Under-Song—a resonance that might be geological pressure, or might be something else. I've strung lights through these frontier spaces, but I've modified them to pulse in rhythm with the extraction engines, creating a kind of visual music that helps me navigate when the fog grows too thick to see.

I've been tracking something down here. Not the Drain Dancers—I know them, leave caches of fresh batteries and water purification tablets in places they frequent. They're just Tenders who've gone a step further than the rest of us, who've learned to move with the fog instead of through it. No, what I track leaves different signs: places where the boring machines have cut through stone that's already been worked, but worked by tools we don't recognize. Surfaces that have been polished smooth by something other than water. Symbols that might be writing, or might be the kind of patterns a dreaming mind sees in condensation.

Three weeks ago, I found a chamber that wasn't on any map—official or otherwise. The boring machine had broken through into it at an oblique angle, its carbide teeth shrieking against what turned out to be manufactured material, not stone. Inside, the walls were covered in the same moss as our tunnels, but it grew in deliberate patterns, spirals and mandalas that pulsed with bioluminescent messages I couldn't decode. The air inside was warm and humid, but it had never known our machines—this was an older moisture, captured and circulated by systems I could barely recognize as technology.

I spent six hours in that chamber, documenting everything with equipment both official and improvised. I took samples of the moss, measured the air pressure differentials, recorded the way sound moved differently in that space. But mostly, I sat in the center of the spiral patterns and listened to the Under-Song, which was stronger there, more complex. It sounded like language, almost. Like something trying to remember how to speak.

When I finally left to file my survey, I found the chamber had been sealed while I was inside. Not by the company crews—they wouldn't have known to come yet. The seal was made of the same material as the walls, grown rather than welded, with moss already thick around its edges. I had to cut my way out with emergency tools, and when I looked back, the moss was already beginning to heal the damage I'd done.

I didn't file that survey. Instead, I started a new map, my most secret one, drawn in bioluminescent ink made from processed moss. It shows the locations of all the chambers I've found, the ones that seal themselves, the ones that were already here. The pattern is starting to emerge—not random pockets, but a network, a system as deliberate as our own but following different principles.

The other Tenders think I'm going deep in the way they all fear and secretly desire—losing myself to the rhythms of this place. But I'm not lost. I'm finding something. The Drain Works weren't built on empty rock. They were built on—or into—something else, something that's been waiting. The moss knows. The water knows. And sometimes, late in my shift when I'm far from any other human sound, I think I'm beginning to know too.

I leave my official surveys at the collection points, neat documentation of nothing important. But my real work happens in the margins, in the spaces between purpose and discovery, where the industrial gives way to something older and more patient. Tomorrow, the boring machines will break through into Seventeen-Deep-Gamma. I'll be there, waiting, my instruments ready to record whatever impossible space we're about to uncover.

The moss on my suit glows brighter these days. Sometimes I catch my reflection in the condensation pools and don't immediately recognize the figure looking back—something between human and function, between explorer and the territory being explored. But that's what we all are down here, isn't it? Instruments in a larger symphony, each playing our part in whatever vast and patient music runs beneath the city's foundations.

I am a Survey Tender, and I am learning to read the true shape of the world.
