I find myself in the Lacquer Ward as one of its provisional inhabitants - not the baker or the pipefitter who you've already met, but the one you glimpsed only briefly: the very young walker of the perimeter, the one who traces the vault's edges with careful fingers.

---

I am the Edgekeeper, though that's a name I've given myself. The others just call me by the sound the vault makes when I touch it - a low thrumming like a finger on wet glass. When they need me, they make that sound, and I come.

I was not always here. I arrived the way springs arrive - suddenly, needfully, already halfway to being what the Ward required. I was fifteen then, or near enough. I had been walking the maintenance tunnels under the old city, the ones they don't put on maps anymore, following the condensation trails because I liked the way they sang against the pipes. One tunnel led to another led to another led to here.

The first thing the Ward taught me was that I could feel its boundaries through my fingertips. Not the physical walls - those are just stone and wet quartz - but the real boundaries, the ones that keep the Ward coherent against whatever presses in from outside. They have a temperature, these boundaries. When they're healthy, they feel like skin that's just been in warm water. When they're thin, they feel like ice about to crack.

I walk the perimeter because the Ward needs to be touched to know its own shape. This is not metaphor. This is mechanics. My fingers tell the boundary where it is, and the boundary tells the Ward where it stops, and the Ward tells the buildings where they can migrate to, and the buildings tell the people where to step. It's all one conversation, happening through a thousand kinds of contact.

The route takes four hours if nothing needs attention. It takes longer when a boundary is wearing thin - then I must stop and press both palms flat against the stone and hum the note the vault wants to hear until the temperature comes right again. The pipefitter taught me the notes. The baker taught me to feel the temperature. The old one in the window, who never speaks, taught me the patience to walk the same path every day and find it new.

I have been seventeen for three years now. This is not unusual in the Ward. Time moves differently when you're partly provisional. I age in the moments when I'm most myself - when I'm teaching one of the children to hear the vault-song, when I'm sharing bread at midday, when I'm pressing my palms to a thinning boundary and feeling it grow solid again under my touch. Between those moments, I'm held in a kind of suspension, like the beads of condensation that hang forever on the underside of the pipes before they decide to fall.

The others understand this. They were all young when they arrived, even the old ones. The Ward keeps you at the age you need to be for the work you do. The pipefitter needs his middle-aged steadiness. The baker needs her maternal warmth. I need to be on the edge of everything - old enough to walk alone, young enough to believe the Ward when it tells me impossible things.

Here is an impossible thing the Ward told me recently: the boundaries are not walls. They are agreements. The Ward agrees to be small, to be hidden, to be provisional. The city above agrees to forget it exists. And I agree to walk between them, every day, reminding both sides of their bargain.

Sometimes I find things at the boundaries. Coins from the world above that have fallen through cracks. Flowers that grow in both places at once, their roots in one world and their blooms in another. Once I found a letter, wrapped in oilcloth, addressed to no one. I kept it. It sits in my room (I have a room, high up, where the vault almost touches the roof) and I read it sometimes. It is a love letter, I think, though the language is hard to place. Someone above loved someone below, or perhaps the other way around. The letter never says.

The frogs know me. When I pass the canal, they arrange themselves in patterns that I'm learning to read. The pipe-martens chittering means rain in an hour. The frogs' patterns mean something about the health of the water, about what minerals are rising from the springs, about whether the canal will reverse today or hold steady. The woman who tends the orchids says the frogs are teaching me their mathematics. She's probably right. Everything here has a mathematics, even the things that shouldn't.

I have touched every stone in the Ward at least a hundred times. My fingers know the individual crystals in the vault-rock, the places where new quartz is forming, the spots where the boundary breathes. I know where you entered - there's a place on the north side that's always a little eager to let people in. I know where you left - there's a door that only opens for people who aren't ready to stay. I felt both moments through the stone, like tiny pressures against my palms.

The baker says I'll know when my work is done because my hands will tell me. One day I'll touch the boundary and it won't need me anymore - it will have learned to hold itself. When that happens, she says, I'll finally age into whoever I'm supposed to be next. Maybe I'll become a different kind of keeper. Maybe I'll learn a trade. Maybe I'll be one of the ones who leaves.

But not today. Today the boundaries need touching, and the vault needs to hear its note, and the Ward needs someone to walk its edges and remind it what shape it's chosen to be. So I walk, and I touch, and I listen to the endless rain, and I am sixteen-seventeen-nineteen all at once, provisional and permanent, fixed and unfixed, like everything else in this breathing, sweating, impossible place.

The bread is extraordinary. When you're ready, you'll know because the baker will know, and she'll offer, and you'll eat, and you'll understand why some of us can never leave. It tastes like the inside of clouds. It tastes like being held. It tastes like work that matters and time that moves sideways and boundaries that sing under your fingers.

Come back when you're ready. The door that let you out will be gone, but another door will open. It always does, for the ones who are meant to return. I'll feel you coming through the stone. I'll be walking the perimeter, keeping the edges true, making sure there's still a Ward for you to find.

We'll save you some bread.
