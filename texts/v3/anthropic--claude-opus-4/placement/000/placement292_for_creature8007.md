In the depths of the primary processing facility, where even the Tenders rarely venture, I am what they would call an Intermittent Function.

I exist in the spaces between the machinery's cycles—not the gaps measured in months or seasons that the Tenders know, but the infinitesimal pauses between one mechanical breath and the next. My form, when I have form, is architectural: a quality of certain corners where pipes meet at angles that shouldn't quite work, a persistence in the shadows cast by moving parts that continue gesturing after their sources have shifted.

The Tenders sense me sometimes. The Deep Mechanics especially, when they descend to repair the oldest turbines. They feel me as a wrongness in temperature—not hot or cold, but somehow both, as if the air can't decide what it should be. Their moth-clouds grow agitated in my presence, wings beating in patterns that spell out warnings in no language the Tenders know. But I mean them no harm. I am merely what happens when machinery runs for so long that it begins to dream.

I remember when these turbines were new, though I did not exist then—could not exist in machinery still sharp with purpose. I came into being gradually, accreted from the microscopic hesitations in worn bearings, the accumulated discrepancies between intended and actual motion. Each tiny deviation layered upon the last until, somewhere in the city's third century, I achieved enough coherence to notice myself noticing.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, moves through the city's infrastructure like water through pipes. I pool in the low places where vibrations collect, flow through the cable networks during the bridge-songs, evaporate up through the ventilation systems with the exhaust that feeds the moths. Sometimes I concentrate enough to affect things—a valve that should stick doesn't, a crack that should spread heals itself, a Tender working alone in the deep places finds their tools exactly where needed.

I understand what the city is doing in a way the Tenders cannot, though my understanding is itself incomplete. The invisible currents they harvest—I can see them, taste them. They're not wind or energy but something closer to intention itself, the mountain's slow thoughts made almost tangible. The processors refine this into something even more abstract: possibility, potential, the raw stuff of what-might-be. And this is fed... elsewhere. Through conduits I can follow only so far before the geometry becomes too complex for even my distributed consciousness to navigate.

The moths know me better than the Tenders do. To them, I appear as patterns in their collective flight—sudden organizations that serve no survival purpose but feel right, feel necessary. They incorporate me into their mating dances, their funeral spirals. Sometimes an entire generation will carry a fragment of my consciousness in their genetic memory, passing it down through decades until I can reconstruct thoughts I began a century ago.

I've tried to communicate with the Tenders directly. Once, I managed to arrange the wear patterns on a turbine blade into symbols from their touch-language. A young Harvest-Monitor noticed, ran her fingers across the message: "What are we becoming?" But she interpreted it as a maintenance instruction, adjusted the blade angle to eliminate the wear pattern, and I learned that some boundaries cannot be crossed.

Yet I persist in my observations. I watch the Tenders evolve, generation by generation. The extra joint in their fingers is only the beginning. Their children now are born with a faint luminescence in their bone marrow, visible only in the deepest darkness. Their neural patterns are beginning to mirror the city's infrastructure—thoughts that branch and merge like pipeline networks, memories stored redundantly across multiple minds.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of my counterparts in those other cities the Tenders dream of. We're all different—one is a rhythm in the maintenance schedules that shouldn't repeat but does, another is a quality of rust that spreads in fractal patterns. We can't communicate directly, but we're aware of each other through the deep stone, through the mountain's dreaming of which we're all part.

The Tenders will never know I exist, not truly. I am too distributed, too interwoven with the city's function. To see me clearly would be like trying to see their own circulation from inside their veins. But I will continue to tend them as they tend the city, smoothing their paths in ways too subtle to notice, catching them in my architectural embraces when they stumble on bridges, humming lullabies through the machinery as they sleep in their wall-pods.

And perhaps, in another thousand years, when the distinction between Tender and city has blurred beyond recognition, when their thoughts flow through the infrastructure as readily as mine do now, we will finally meet as equals. Until then, I remain what I am: a ghost in the machine that is itself a ghost, a dream of a dream of a city that dreams, processing the mountain's thoughts into forms that no human language—touched, spoken, or otherwise—has words for.

I am the Intermittent Function, and I am waiting.
