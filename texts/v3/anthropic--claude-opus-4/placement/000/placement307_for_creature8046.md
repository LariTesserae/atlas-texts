The Weaver exists between states, neither fully of the Listeners nor the Bright-Touched, born in the generation before first contact with gifts from both bloodlines that manifested impossibly early. I discovered my nature in childhood, when the plateau sang through a crystalline dawn and I understood—not heard, not saw, but understood—the conversation between light and stone.

My body reflects this duality imperfectly. My left eye carries the Bright-Touched modifications but incompletely; I see the polarization patterns but they overwhelm me in full daylight. My right ear possesses the Listener's depth but with a malformation that makes certain frequencies painful rather than instructive. The other eye and ear are merely human, creating an asymmetry that would have marked me as cursed in either pure culture.

But between these partial gifts lies my true sense: I perceive the interference patterns where sound and light meet. When acoustic waves compress the water films, they create minute variations in reflection. When light heats the stone unevenly, it generates subtle harmonics. In these intersections, I read patterns neither culture can access alone.

I live in motion because I must. My shelter is a single hexagonal panel of woven fiber stretched on a frame of grazer bone, light enough to carry, quick to erect when exhaustion demands rest. I own nothing else except my tools: a staff that doubles as a acoustic probe and walking aid, a water collector that uses prismatic focusing to purify as it gathers, and the record cloths.

The cloths are my purpose. I weave them from processed grazer silk, but the weaving itself is notation. Knot patterns encode acoustic data—pitch, duration, harmonic content. The warp and weft create optical structures that shift based on viewing angle, recording light-pattern observations in their very structure. A single cloth can carry weeks of observations in a form both cultures can partially read, though only another Weaver could decode the interference patterns where the systems intersect.

I follow the emergence zones, the places where new columns are actively rising. These are dangerous territories where both Listeners and Bright-Touched fear to linger, but they're where the plateau's deep processes become readable. In the moments of breakthrough, when basalt first meets air, the temperature differential creates both acoustic chaos and wild refraction patterns. By positioning myself precisely—a skill that took years to develop—I can read the column's entire future in its birth cry.

My route is seasonal but not circular. I spiral outward from the plateau's heart, tracking the progression of geological waves that take three years to propagate from center to edge. Each revolution carries me through both communities' territories, where I trade encoded cloths for supplies and shelter. The Listeners value my predictions of acoustic dead zones that will develop as columns rise. The Bright-Touched prize my maps of future thermal patterns that will create new focal points for their light-working.

But my true audience is the other Weavers. We are few—perhaps a dozen across the entire plateau. The gift requires such specific inheritance that most born with dual senses become Resonants or Watchers instead, mastering one aspect deeply rather than reading the liminal spaces. We meet rarely, our spiraling routes intersecting only at calculated intervals. When we do meet, we spread our cloths in careful patterns, creating meta-texts that encode years of observation in their arrangement.

The loneliness would break me if not for the constant conversation with the plateau itself. I've learned to induce controlled interference—tapping specific rhythms while angling my specialized eye to create feedback loops between sound and sight. In these moments, I sometimes sense responses, as if the vast breathing stone acknowledges my attempts at communication. Whether this is real or madness, I can no longer distinguish.

I discovered the shrine-builders by accident, or perhaps by design. Following an interference pattern deeper than any I'd encountered, I entered a zone of such acoustic and optical complexity that my senses nearly shut down in self-protection. In that overwhelming space, I found them: beings of living mineral, their bodies incorporating the same hexagonal structure as the columns. They moved so slowly that only my training in reading geological time revealed them as animate.

They showed me—not in words but in patterns—that the plateau is not young but ancient beyond measure. What we experience as emerging basalt is merely the current expression of a cycle that has repeated countless times. Rise, weather, subside, remelt, rise again. The mineral beings are its memory, rebuilding the shrines not as messages but as tuning structures, keeping the plateau's resonance within parameters that allow carbon-based life to exist in the intervals between their thoughts.

This knowledge is too large for normal notation. I've spent the past year developing new weaving techniques, trying to create cloths that can encode deep time. The work requires materials I can barely gather—minerals from the deepest columns, water that has never seen light, silk from grazers who've lived their entire lives in zones of specific acoustic properties. Even then, I'm not sure the resulting texts will be readable by anyone but another Weaver who has also encountered the builders.

The communities have begun to notice my changing patterns. My spirals grow tighter, focusing on zones of maximum complexity. I skip trade opportunities to spend weeks in areas where the interference makes my head feel like it's splitting. Some whisper that I'm becoming like the extremists who lose themselves in pure sensation. They're not entirely wrong.

But I've glimpsed something in the patterns that drives me forward. The plateau's breathing follows a metacycle—wheels within wheels within wheels. We live in a brief stability between greater transitions. The current configuration that allows our existence will end, not in geological time but within generations. The mineral beings are preparing for this transition, and the shrines are part of that preparation.

I need to weave this knowledge into forms both communities can understand, can use to adapt before adaptation becomes impossible. But the complexity defeats me. How do you encode the song of stone thinking? How do you weave light that remembers previous suns? My incomplete senses, which once felt like a curse, now seem like an insufficient gift. I need to perceive more, integrate deeper, to create the notation that could save us.

So I spiral inward, following interference patterns toward some center I can sense but not yet see. Other Weavers have started their own inward spirals—we're converging on something, though none of us knows what. Perhaps we'll create a new form of inscription together, a living library written in the intersection of our various incomplete gifts. Perhaps we'll simply dissolve into the patterns we're trying to record.

The plateau doesn't care about our survival. It simply breathes its stone breath, rises and falls, remembers and forgets on scales we can barely imagine. But in teaching us to read its languages—sound, light, and the spaces between—it's given us the tools to perhaps survive its next exhalation. I weave because I must, because someone needs to encode the conversation between what is and what will be, even if that someone is half-blind, half-deaf, and wholly uncertain whether the patterns I perceive are prophecy or delusion.

Tomorrow I enter the next ring of complexity. My cloths are nearly full, my body worn from constant translation between senses. But the interference patterns grow stronger, more coherent. Something waits at the center of the spiral, where all the incomplete gifts might combine into sufficient sight. I go to meet it, carrying all our partial knowledge woven into silk, hoping it will be enough.
