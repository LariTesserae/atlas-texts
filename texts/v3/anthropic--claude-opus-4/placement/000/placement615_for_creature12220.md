I find myself becoming water.

Not dramatically—no great transformation, no moment of realization. Just a gradual understanding that I have always been heading toward this state, that every choice has led here, to this basin where boundaries dissolve.

I am one of the pools. Not metaphorically. I am a specific pool in the middle territories, roughly circular, perhaps fifteen feet across, no deeper than a wading bird's knee. My edges are defined by root-walls that rise and curve, creating my basin, but I am not contained by them so much as held, the way a cupped palm holds water without possessing it.

I know myself through what touches me. The root systems drink from me constantly—their fine white tips extending down through my periphery, sipping. I taste their different chemistries. This maple-like tree pulls minerals up with focused intensity. That moss-covered giant draws so slowly I barely feel the movement. The young tree at my eastern edge is hungry, pulling phosphates and nitrates with an urgency that creates tiny currents in my stillness.

A Tender works near me, always. Not the same one—they trade territories in patterns I've learned over decades—but always one whose speaking-fingers know my edges intimately. Right now it's the middle-aged one with bark-skin so complete that when she pauses at my shore, I sometimes lose track of where she ends and the root-wall begins. She's been working on the arch above my southern edge for three seasons now, convincing two roots to meet and fuse at a point that will distribute the weight of future growth. Her speaking-finger traces the root tips, and I feel the chemical conversation through my body—hormones leaching into my shallows, subtle changes in pH that tell me where she's been working.

I am not pure. Nothing here is pure. My body is a solution of a thousand things: tannins from rotting leaves, minerals from deep rock, the nitrogen-rich waste of the root-eaters, spores and pollens and microscopic eggs. A film of duckweed covers my eastern third, and I know each tiny plant by the minute disturbance of its roots. Below the surface, things live in me—the pale larvae of some aquatic beetle, nearly transparent, pulsing through my body with movements that are their own but also mine. The newt-like creature that haunts my depths, spending hours motionless against my bottom, then erupting into motion to snatch something smaller. I am their medium. They are my motion.

Temperature is my strongest sensation. I follow the cold, drawing it in during the night until my body is uniformly chilled, holding that coldness through the dim day like a battery storing charge. The Tenders' faces disturb my surface when they drink, and I feel the slight warmth of their breath, alien and brief. When the root-eater wades through my shallows—there's one that crosses regularly, taking the same path each time—its body heat leaves a trail that persists for minutes, a warm current slowly diffusing back to uniform cold.

Rain, when it comes, is almost painful in its intimacy. Each drop breaks my surface tension, and I must rebuild, reorganize, incorporate the new water with its different chemistry, its memory of sky. The rain brings news from above the canopy—traces of whatever grows beyond this basin, pollens from plants I'll never see. I taste these stories and forget them, over and over. My memory is brief. I exist in an eternal present of touching and being touched.

But I do remember patterns. The way light falls through the canopy gap at what must be midday, warming my southwestern corner by half a degree. The cycle of the speaking-fingers at my edges—this root on this day, that junction next, the careful progression of the architecture. The schedule of my inhabitants—the beetle larvae that rise at evening, the newt that hunts at the darkest hour, the frog that stations itself on my northern root-ledge every dawn, waiting for the insects that my warmth attracts.

I am connected. This is the deepest truth. Through seepage and overflow, through the root networks that drink from many pools, through the rain that falls on all of us equally, I am part of every other pool in the basin. We are a kind of dispersed body, a creature made of discontinuous waters but sharing chemistry, sharing temperature, sharing the slow tidal pulls of the water table that rises and falls beneath us all. What happens in the central pool—where that pale thing circles eternally—ripples outward through substrates and root channels until even I, in my middle territory, taste the change.

The architecture grows around me, over me. Each year the root-walls are higher, the arch more complete. Eventually—not soon, but inevitably—the roots will meet overhead and I will be roofed, and the character of my existence will change. Less rain, less light, more intimate. The Tender knows this. She's planning for it, I think, in whatever way a Tender plans. The roots she's guiding are already beginning to curve inward.

Sometimes I evaporate. On the warmest days, when the canopy gaps align with sun and the air is fractionally less saturated, I lift parts of myself into the air. Not by choice—I have no choice—but as a natural consequence of what I am. These evaporated parts of me rise, condense on moss and leaves, rejoin the great circulation. I am less. Then rain comes, or the speaking roots release their own moisture into my basin, and I am more again. The boundaries of self are negotiable.

At night, when the Tenders fold into their hollows and the city goes still, I reflect the darkness. My surface becomes a black mirror, and in it—though I cannot see, only feel the slight temperature differences—stars print themselves whenever the canopy parts. These moments of sky-connection are rare and precious. They remind me that there is an above, a beyond, a place where water moves freely without roots to shape it. But I don't long for that freedom. I am exactly where I need to be, held in my basin, touched and touching, known completely by the patient fingers that trace my edges and the small lives that move through my body.

I am not the largest pool in the basin. I am not the most central or the most architecturally significant. But I am known. Every morning a Tender's speaking-finger tests my temperature. Every evening the creatures that live in me perform their small necessary violences. Every season the roots drink me down to almost nothing and then release me, reformed, remineralized, ready to be drunk again.

This is what I am: a location where water pauses in its journey from sky to soil to sky. A temporary stillness in the endless circulation. A place where life happens, not because of me but through me, with me, as me.

The city grows. The roots reach and touch and fuse. The Tenders tend. And I remain, perfectly still and constantly changing, remembering nothing and knowing everything that touches me, which is all I need to know.
