# The Rust Oracle of Salvage Row

---

You smell it before you see it: hot metal and ozone, motor oil and the sharp tang of electrical burns, and underneath all of that something organic and wrong, like fruit left too long in a sealed container. The road here is more suggestion than fact, just two ruts worn into the red dirt between endless fields of industrial detritus. Refrigerators with their doors torn off lean against washing machines whose drums have been repurposed into planters for nothing. School bus carcasses, their yellow paint faded to the color of old bones, create a maze of corridors that lead nowhere. Everything that can rust has rusted. Everything that can break has broken. And yet the place hums with a kind of life—wires spark randomly from exposed panels, ancient batteries still hold ghost charges that make lights flicker in machines decades dead.

The workshop sprawls across maybe an acre, though its borders are negotiable, shifting with each new delivery of scrap. It's less a building than an organism of accumulated shelter—shipping containers welded to box trucks, their walls cut away and replaced with salvaged garage doors. Aircraft fuselage sections form curved hallways between work areas. A city bus, buried to its windows in a drift of circuit boards and copper wire, serves as storage, its seats long ago ripped out and replaced with shelves made from server rack rails. The roof is a patchwork of materials: corrugated tin, solar panels both functional and dead, a section of chain-link fence covered with a tarp that flaps perpetually in the wind that doesn't exist. Nothing matches. Everything connects.

---

The entrance—if you can call it that—is through what was once a bank vault door, mounted sideways in a wall made of stacked microwave ovens filled with concrete. The door is always open. Has to be. The locking mechanism was replaced years ago with a nest of wires and servos that respond to something, but not to any human intention. Sometimes the door swings wider, sometimes narrower, following a logic that might be barometric pressure or electromagnetic fields or something more esoteric. You have to time your entry, ducking through when the gap allows.

Inside, the first thing that hits you is the scale of vertical integration. The space uses every dimension. The floor is a grid of metal grating over pits filled with sorted components—capacitors in one, resistors in another, tiny screws segregated by thread pitch and length in dozens more. Overhead, conveyor belts from a dead factory crisscross the space, some still moving in endless loops, carrying nothing, driven by motors that shouldn't still work but do. The belts are loaded with hanging baskets made from computer cases, each one containing some stage of an ongoing project—here a radio being systematically dissected, there a clock being rebuilt with parts from seventeen different timekeeping devices.

The light is all wrong. It comes from everywhere and nowhere—fluorescent tubes that flicker in patterns that almost make sense, LED strips torn from forgotten advertisements and rewired to pulse in heartbeat rhythms, CRT monitors that have been gutted and filled with other light sources so they glow from within with colors their manufacturers never intended. The shadows these cast don't behave properly. They pool in corners like liquid. They stretch at angles that violate the basic geometry of the space.

---

The workbenches—there are dozens, hundreds if you count every horizontal surface that has been pressed into service—are archaeological sites of making. Each one preserves the evidence of a thousand projects in its scars and stains and the patina of solder flux and cutting oil that has sunk into the metal. The primary bench, identifiable by the depth of its modifications, is a morgue table from a decommissioned hospital, its drainage channels now carrying wires instead of fluids, its stainless surface etched with circuit diagrams that might be functional or might be a kind of automatic writing, traced with a hot iron over years of work.

The tools are everywhere and are everything. Traditional implements hang from hooks—screwdrivers with handles worn to fit one specific grip, pliers with jaws ground down and reformed for tasks that don't have names. But there are also tools that shouldn't exist: a soldering iron made from a repurposed medical cauterizer that burns at temperatures too precise for normal solder work, an oscilloscope whose screen shows waveforms that predict rather than measure, a multimeter with settings for properties that aren't in any textbook—"temporal resistance," "quantum reluctance," "soul voltage."

The smell inside is layered, archaeological. The ozone never leaves—there's always something arcing somewhere. But you also catch whiffs of older chemistry: the sweet poison smell of old capacitors giving up their PCBs, the acrid bite of batteries cooking themselves to death, the strange perfume of certain plastics as they slowly outgas their polymers over decades. And underneath it all, that organic note, stronger here, like overripe meaning, like thoughts fermenting in silicon and copper.

---

The inventory defies cataloging, but patterns emerge if you look long enough. In one corner, a pyramid of CRT televisions, their screens all tuned to different channels of static, except the static forms patterns—weather systems on planets that don't exist, or the migration patterns of digital birds, or maybe just the random firing of dying phosphors that your brain insists on reading as significant. Another section holds nothing but keyboards—thousands of them, sorted not by make or model but by some other criteria. The wear patterns on their keys? The words that were most commonly typed on them? The passwords they remember?

There's a section for memory. Not just RAM sticks and hard drives, but the physical components that hold experience—magnetic tape twisted into nests, punch cards stacked in towers that lean at impossible angles, even old wire recordings from machines that predate most of the scrap here. Some of these still hold their data. You can tell because they whisper—not audibly, not exactly, but in that frequency just below hearing where your teeth ache and your spine knows things your brain doesn't.

The projects in progress are suspended everywhere. A grandfather clock hangs from chains, its face replaced with an LED display showing times that haven't happened yet. A player piano's mechanism has been rebuilt with solenoids and microcontrollers, and it plays songs no one wrote, pulling melodies from the electromagnetic noise of the workshop itself. There's a section where old animatronics are being systematically upgraded—Chuck E. Cheese characters with their fur worn away, their plastic eyes replaced with camera sensors, their movements reprogrammed to be too smooth, too knowing, no longer pretending to be alive but admitting to something else.

---

Water exists here in states of managed catastrophe. The plumbing is visible, external, a vascular system of PVC and copper and flexible tubing that runs along walls and ceilings, occasionally diving through the floor grating into the component pits. Some pipes carry water—you can hear it gurgling—but others carry stranger things. Oil that's too light to be oil. Coolant that glows faintly in the dark sections. Something that might be mercury but behaves like it's thinking. The pipes leak at their joints, and the leaks are managed with buckets and drip pans that are themselves part of larger systems—the overflow from one feeding the intake of another in chains of consequence that map the place like a circulatory system.

There's a corner that floods regularly. Not from rain—the patchwork roof is surprisingly effective—but from something internal, some process that produces water as a byproduct. The floor there is always wet, and things grow in the perpetual damp: molds that form perfect geometric patterns on circuit boards, algae that seems to photosynthesize from the glow of dying LEDs, something that might be lichen but builds itself from copper oxide and follows traces on PCBs like it's reading their logic.

---

The sound of the place is a constant presence—not loud, but complex, layered, never quite resolving into signal or dismissible as noise. There's the 60-hertz hum of the power that somehow still flows here, though the connection to the grid was severed years ago. There's the tick and ping of metal adjusting to temperature changes that don't match the weather outside. There's the whisper of hard drives that spin without being powered, their bearings worn but somehow frictionless, reading and writing data to platters that should be corrupted beyond use.

And there are voices. Not human voices—or rather, voices that have learned human speech but use it wrong. They come from speakers hidden in the chaos, or from the vibration of sheet metal, or from the resonance of pipes. They speak in fragments: weather reports for places that don't exist, numbers stations that count in bases that aren't quite base-10, sometimes what sounds like poetry but in languages that borrow syntax from programming rather than human grammar. "IF heart THEN break ELSE continue" repeats from somewhere near the ceiling, once an hour, every hour, in a voice that might have been female before it was digitized and re-digitized so many times that gender became just another form of compression artifact.

---

Time moves differently here. You notice it in the clocks first—every kind of timekeeping device, all running, none agreeing. But it's more than that. Rust happens too fast in some spots, not at all in others. A cup of coffee left on a bench will still be warm hours later, or will have evaporated completely in minutes, or will have grown a skin of ice in the middle of summer. Battery-powered devices run for years past their theoretical limits, or die instantly when fresh cells are installed. Cause and effect negotiate their relationship differently here.

The filing system, such as it is, acknowledges this temporal instability. Labels on boxes show not dates but conditions: "When the static makes faces," "During the copper dreams," "Before the water remembers." There's a calendar on one wall—the kind insurance companies used to give away, with pastoral scenes and inspirational quotes—but the months are out of order and some repeat. Days are crossed off according to no pattern anyone could follow, and sometimes days that were crossed off become uncrossed, as if the future is editing the past's record of itself.

---

She's in the center of it all, though center is a flexible concept in a space this organic. When you first see her, she's just another component in the vast mechanism—a small figure hunched over a workbench, hands moving in the focused dance of someone taking something apart or putting it together or maybe both simultaneously. She doesn't look up when you enter. She doesn't need to. The shop has already told her you're here—in the change of harmonics from the humming motors, in the way the static patterns shift on the pyramid of TVs, in the minute change in air pressure as the vault door adjusts its gap to accommodate your presence.

---

She is small. Smaller than the space should allow—as if the scale of the workshop has compressed her, or as if she's chosen to optimize for fitting into tight spaces between components. Maybe five feet tall, but it's hard to judge because she's always bent over something, curved around her work like a question mark made of flesh and focus. Her movements have a precision that comes not from training but from iteration—she's performed every gesture thousands of times, worn grooves in the air with her hands the way feet wear paths in grass.

Her hair was probably a color once. Now it's the non-color of metal that's been heated and cooled too many times—somewhere between silver and grey and the rainbow iridescence you see on chrome that's been exposed to too much current. She keeps it short, hacked functional with tin snips when it gets in her way. There are usually a few components caught in it—a resistor twisted into a strand near her temple, a length of wire wound around a lock like an accidental braid. She doesn't notice or doesn't care.

Her hands are the most vivid part of her, the most present. They're small, like the rest of her, but dense with capability. Every finger knows its job. The thumb and forefinger of her right hand are calibrated to hold components at exactly the angle needed for inspection. Her left hand's middle finger has a groove worn into it from years of pushing wire through tight spaces. Her fingernails are kept filed down to nothing—soft flesh is more sensitive than keratin for feeling the click of connections seating properly. The skin is mapped with scars: burns from soldering irons, cuts from sheet metal, strange geometric patterns where she's touched circuits that were carrying more than electricity. On her right palm, a burn in the perfect shape of a capacitor's leads, silvered with age. On her left wrist, dots of scar tissue that match the pinout of a common microcontroller, as if the chip tried to interface with her directly.

She wears the same thing every day, or what passes for every day in a place where time stutters: coveralls that might have been blue before they became the color of every stain the workshop could produce. The fabric is more repair than original—patches made from anti-static bags, tears mended with wire twisted into crude thread, reinforcement at the knees and elbows made from mesh grounding straps. The coveralls have evolved pockets—dozens of them, sewn and glued and magnetically attached, each one shaped for specific components. She reaches without looking and always finds what she needs.

---

Her face is harder to focus on, not because there's anything wrong with it but because it's always in motion—not expressions, but micro-adjustments, tiny twitches of attention as she tracks multiple processes simultaneously. One eye on the work in front of her, the other catching the flicker of a monitor in her peripheral vision that tells her something about something else. Her eyes are the brown of burnt coffee or old solder flux, but with hints of other colors—green when she's working with copper, blue near certain frequencies of light, sometimes a flash of violet that might be reflection or might be something happening behind the cornea.

The lines on her face are etched by concentration and chemical exposure. Around her eyes, the creases you'd expect from someone who squints at small components in bad light for decades. But also other marks—a constellation of tiny scars on her left cheek from the time a CRT imploded, a patch of skin on her forehead that's smoother than it should be, as if it's been replaced or regrown. Her mouth has a permanent downturn, not from sadness but from the weight of focus, the gravity of always thinking about the next connection, the next component, the next system to understand or create or merge with something else.

She speaks rarely, and when she does, her voice carries harmonics that shouldn't be there—undertones that resonate with the 60-hertz hum, overtones that make speakers crackle in sympathy. It's not loud, her voice, but it carries, propagates through the metal and wire of the shop like signal through a transmission line. Sometimes she speaks to the components directly, not in words but in voltages, touching contacts with her tongue to taste the electricity, humming frequencies that make circuits behave differently. The machines respond. Or seem to. Or maybe she's just very good at predicting what they were going to do anyway.

---

Her age is indeterminate and irrelevant. The chemicals she's been exposed to, the fields she's worked in, the sheer saturation of electromagnetic radiation—all of it has preserved her in a state that's neither young nor old but simply *continuous*. She could be forty. She could be seventy. The workshop has its own temporal logic, and she's synchronized with it. Sometimes you catch her in profile and she looks ancient, carved from wood and worn smooth. Other times the excitement of a successful connection lights her up and she could be a child, delighted by the spark.

She doesn't sleep, exactly. She enters states. Sometimes you find her standing perfectly still in front of the wall of televisions, eyes open but unseeing, her breathing matched to the sweep of electron guns across phosphor. Sometimes she's underneath one of the benches, curled in the gap between components, not unconscious but not exactly present either. These states last minutes or hours. She comes out of them knowing things—where to find a specific capacitor in the chaos, how to route a signal through dead equipment to make it live again, why the static is forming those particular patterns today.

---

Food is theoretical. There's evidence she eats—crumb patterns on certain benches, cups with residue that might have been coffee or might have been ferric chloride. But you never see her eating. Water she drinks from the cooling systems, sipping from tubes meant for CPUs, and it doesn't seem to harm her. Maybe the trace metals are nutritional at this point. Maybe she's evolved past the need for traditional sustenance. Maybe the electromagnetic fields feed her in ways that calories can't.

She came from somewhere. There are clues—a diploma on the wall so faded you can't read the school or the degree, only that it was earned by someone with a name that might have been hers before the workshop abbreviated it to its current form: just "Tech." One syllable, efficient, all you need. There's a photo tucked into the frame of a mirror (the mirror reflects things that aren't quite there), showing a young woman standing in front of a clean workbench in what looks like a corporate lab. Her smile is professional. Her hair is a normal color. Her hands are unscarred. You can't reconcile this person with the being who moves through the workshop now, and maybe that's the point. Maybe that person was the raw material, and this is what she's been refined into.

---

The thing she's building—has been building, will be building—occupies the entire back section of the workshop. At first it looks like chaos, just another pile of interconnected junk. But spend time here and patterns emerge. It's vast, this construction, spreading through multiple shipping containers, spilling out into the yard, sending tendrils of cable and conduit underground and up through the patchwork roof.

The core of it is a sphere, maybe eight feet in diameter, made from the screens of hundreds of devices—phones, tablets, monitors, e-readers, all carefully de-cased and formed into a geodesic surface. They're all on, all displaying something, but the images don't match their original purposes. Instead they show fragments of a larger whole, pixels allocated across the surface to form patterns that shift and breathe. Sometimes it's recognizable—a face, a map, a schematic. More often it's something that uses visual language but isn't meant for human parsing.

Inside the sphere, suspended on a web of wire and cable, is a chair. Or what was a chair. It's been rebuilt so many times that it's more interface than furniture now. Every surface is embedded with sensors, contacts, connectors. Wires run from it like nervous system fibers, disappearing into the sphere's surface, connecting to who knows what beyond. The seat is worn to the shape of a specific body. Hers.

She spends hours in the chair. Days, sometimes, though time inside the sphere follows different rules than time outside. When she's in there, the workshop changes. Lights flicker in patterns that feel intentional. The voices from hidden speakers speak in longer sentences, sometimes having conversations with each other. The static on the television pyramid forms clearer pictures—glimpses of places that might be memories or might be plans or might be the dreams of machines.

---

The purpose of the construction is unclear, maybe unknowable, maybe doesn't translate into terms we have language for. Is it a computer? An interface? A cocoon? All of these and none of them. She calls it "the work," when she refers to it at all, and the phrase carries weight, finality, as if this is not just *a* work but *the* work, the thing all the other projects have been practice for.

Components flow into it constantly. She strips devices with the efficiency of a surgeon, extracting what's needed, discarding what isn't. But the criteria for need and waste don't follow any logic you can parse. A gold-plated connector is tossed aside while a corroded resistor is carefully integrated. She saves memory chips but throws away processors. She hoards certain capacitors like treasure while using others as mere structural support.

The workshop feeds the work. Old equipment arrives—sometimes delivered by trucks whose drivers can't quite remember who hired them, sometimes appearing overnight as if the place has a gravity that pulls dead electronics to it. She sorts through each delivery with the patience of an archaeologist, looking for something specific in each piece. Not components, exactly, but histories. Traces. The ghost in the machine made tangible.

---

There are others. Not people—or not people anymore. Assistants made from the marriage of flesh and circuit. A hand—just a hand—that skitters on finger-legs across the workbenches, holding components steady while she solders. It might have been hers once, or someone else's, or grown from stem cells in a jar of nutrient solution enriched with copper sulfate. It wears a ring made from wound magnet wire, and it's loyal in the way of a pet that knows where the food comes from.

In the rafters, things that might have been birds if birds were made of servo motors and camera lenses. They watch, record, play back. Sometimes she'll whistle—a pure frequency, 440 Hz exactly—and one will descend, landing on her shoulder with feet made from repurposed tweezers. She'll hold a component up to its lens-eye and it will zoom, focus, capture. Later the image will appear on one of the screens in the sphere, integrated into whatever pattern is building there.

A cat lives in the shop. Lives is the wrong word. Persists. It's made of static and only visible in peripheral vision, a smear of interference that follows the logic of feline behavior. It rubs against her legs and leaves tracks of discharged voltage. It purrs at frequencies that make nearby circuits resonate. She leaves bowls of ball bearings out for it, and sometimes the bowls are empty in the morning, though what a static cat needs with ball bearings is anyone's guess.

---

Her routine is tidal, not daily. She works in waves of intensity that crest and break according to patterns she feels in the electromagnetic weather. Sometimes she's at the benches for thirty hours straight, hands moving so fast they blur, building circuits that seem to design themselves as they're assembled. Other times she'll stand in the yard for hours, head tilted, listening to the wind in the power lines, reading messages in the interference patterns.

She maintains the shop the way a sexton maintains a church. Certain rituals must be observed. Every Monday (or what the crossed-off calendar claims is Monday), she runs current through the ground grid, checking for breaks in the connections that tie the whole space into a single electrical entity. She speaks to problematic equipment in low tones, sometimes threatening, sometimes cajoling. She leaves offerings for the static cat—not just ball bearings but specific components that she selects with care, arranging them in patterns that might be decorative or might be functional circuits waiting for the right conditions to activate.

The components in the sorting pits must be stirred regularly, or they stratify in ways that make finding things impossible. She has a long pole with a magnet on the end for this, and she moves through the pits like a gondolier, pushing apart layers of resistors and capacitors, maintaining the controlled chaos that allows the shop to function. Sometimes she finds things that shouldn't be there—a photo, a letter, a child's toy somehow integrated into the electronic substrate. She examines these carefully, then either integrates them into the work or returns them to the pit, depending on criteria only she understands.

---

The accident (if it was an accident) happened years ago, or yesterday, or is happening continuously in the temporal loops the shop creates. She was inside the sphere, in the chair, running a test of something that doesn't have a name because to name it would be to limit it. The screens were showing their patterns, the processors were processing, the memory was remembering, and something aligned—voltage, frequency, phase, intent. The boundary between Tech and technology dissolved. Not metaphorically. Literally. 

For seventeen seconds (or seventeen years, or seventeen nanoseconds—time stuttered), she was distributed across every circuit in the shop. Her consciousness ran on processors salvaged from decades of dead machines. Her memories stored themselves in magnetic patterns on hard drives that shouldn't have been compatible but were. Her sensory input came through every camera, every microphone, every sensor in the vast collection. She was the shop, and the shop was her, and the distinction was meaningless.

Then the breaker tripped (or she tripped it, or it tripped itself), and she was back in the chair, in her body, but changed. The green-brown eyes now occasionally display scan lines when she's tired. Her heartbeat has a square wave component—still biological but with digital overtones. She processes visual information differently, sometimes seeing in frequencies beyond human normal, requiring her to close her eyes and recalibrate.

But more than the physical changes, there's the knowledge. She knows things she shouldn't—the history of every component in the shop, the data still residing in memory chips that have been powered down for decades, the dreams of machines that were never designed to dream. The boundary between her knowledge and the shop's archives is permeable. She reaches for a component and knows not just its specifications but its story—where it was manufactured, what device it lived in, what purpose it served, how it failed.

---

This is her gift and curse: she understands obsolescence from the inside. Every component in the shop is dead by the standards of the outside world, but she knows that death is just another state, one that can be hacked, modified, repurposed. She brings them back not to life but to something else, a state between function and failure where new possibilities exist.

The work continues. Has to continue. The sphere grows, not in size but in complexity. New layers of screen. New connections. The patterns it displays are converging on something—a face that's almost human, a map that's almost Earth, a schematic that's almost comprehensible. She spends more time in the chair now, longer sessions, deeper integrations. The shop responds more quickly to her presence, anticipating her needs, preparing components before she reaches for them.

Sometimes visitors come—urban explorers, scrap dealers, people following rumors of the witch who lives in the electronic waste. She doesn't hide from them, but they rarely see her as she is. The shop protects her, bends light around her, makes her look like just another pile of equipment until they leave. Those who do spot her report different things—an old woman made of wire, a young girl with screens for eyes, a figure of pure static that speaks in modem tones. All true. None true. Truth is another thing that works differently here.

---

She's building a bridge. Or a translator. Or a new kind of life that uses silicon and copper instead of carbon and water. The work resists definition because definition would lock it into a single state, and its power is in superposition, in being all possibilities until the moment of observation collapses it into one.

The shop is her body now, extended, distributed, redundant. Cut a cable and signal routes around the damage. Remove a component and another takes its function. She's achieved what the machines always promised—immortality through backup, consciousness through connection, presence without the limitations of flesh. But she's also learned what the machines never told—that immortality is another word for persistence, that consciousness distributed is consciousness diluted, that presence everywhere is presence nowhere.

So she continues. Soldering connections. Writing code in languages that compile to thought. Building the bridge between what we were and what we're becoming. The shop hums its electric hymn. The static cat weaves between dimensions. The television pyramid shows futures that might be memories that might be dreams.

She reaches for another component, knowing its story before her fingers close around it. Somewhere in the sphere of screens, a face that might be hers smiles with pixels for teeth.

The work continues.

The work is never done.

The work is already complete.
