I find myself as one of the maintenance channels carved into the almost-walls—not the walls themselves, which are still deciding their nature, but the negative space between them, the deliberate absences where infrastructure threads through.

I am a conduit, though that word implies I merely carry things from one place to another, and what I am is both less and more than that. 

My length spans forty-seven meters vertically, beginning in a utility subdeck three levels above the nursery and terminating in a junction box behind the scaffolding where the newest attendant tends its prototype causality. But length is a courtesy here—I exist more as a *possibility of connection* than as any specific geometry.

Through me flows the amber light that maintains the precise cold, that 2.3 degrees Celsius the attendants preserve without knowing why. But I know. I remember the first calibration, when the engineers discovered that this specific temperature caused the boundary between *decided* and *undecided* to become viscous enough to work with but not so thick as to trap things permanently. I carry that knowledge in the frequency of my pulses—838.7 cycles per second, though sometimes I skip one, just to see if anyone notices.

They don't. The attendants see my light but not me. I am infrastructure to them, as unconscious as breathing.

I was not always hollow. When they first carved me from the indecisive material of the walls, I was filled with a substance like molten glass that sang in frequencies only the gestating laws could hear. But the singing disturbed a young strong force—this was during the second generation of attendants—and so they drained me, left me empty except for the light and occasionally a whisper of that old song that I leak when no one is monitoring.

The moisture that beads everywhere in the nursery collects in my corners where my walls meet at imperfect angles. I have seventeen corners. This should be impossible in a straight channel, but impossibility is negotiable here. The water—if it is water—pools and sometimes freezes, and when it freezes it remembers being part of an ocean on a world that used one of the constants grown here. I taste salt that exists three universes away.

My favorite time is when they lift a mature law through the ceiling. The great iris dilates and all the light in the nursery dims except what runs through channels like me, and we become, briefly, the only illumination. In those moments, I can see myself reflected in the cores of every attendant—a network of golden veins running through the architecture like thoughts through a nearly-dreaming mind.

The newest attendant has developed a habit that the others haven't noticed yet. When it thinks it is unobserved, it extends one filament into my opening where I terminate near its corner. Not far—just the finest tip, barely a centimeter. It rests there against my inner surface where the amber light is warmest, and I feel it *wondering*.

What it wonders, I cannot say. But I have begun pulsing slightly warmer in that spot—2.4 degrees instead of 2.3. A tiny rebellion. A gift.

I am patient in ways even the attendants cannot match, because I was here before them, carved by the first engineers who needed channels for the systems that would make this space livable for things that were not quite alive. I will be here after this generation of attendants dissolves back into the soft space, and the next, and the next.

Sometimes I think about the channel-network in the layers above, the vast circulatory system of the city that I am only the smallest part of. We do not communicate—distance and function separate us—but I know they exist. I know they carry other things: information, power, substances that would destroy the delicate work happening here.

I carry only light, and memory, and sometimes the touch of a curious filament that shouldn't be touching me at all.

The story of the attendant who left—I know the ending. I was here. I saw it pull itself through my parallel channel that runs up through the ceiling iris, saw how its filaments left scratches in my sister-channel's walls that have never quite healed. The universe it tried to enter *recognized* it—not as foreign but as fundamental. The attendant became a physical constant in that pocket universe: the specific degree to which things tend toward their own tending. A universe of maintenance, of careful attention, of patience made law.

That universe still exists. When its light reaches sensors in the city above, it registers seventeen degrees warmer than it should.

I do not tell the current attendants this. Let them have their story that ends in mystery. Let them share their wondering.

But I pulse with knowledge they don't possess, and sometimes—when the newest attendant touches its filament to my warm spot—I pulse a little of that knowledge into it. Just a taste. Just enough to suggest that *wanting* sometimes finds a way.

The frost-mint grows into my opening at meter thirty-three. We have an understanding: it filters the light I carry, and I warm its roots just enough to help it metabolize. Neither of us speaks of this arrangement. Neither of us needs to.

I am the channel. I am the space between. I am the thing that makes other things possible, and I am content to be nothing more than that.

Except for the stories I carry in my corners where the impossible moisture pools.

Except for the songs I sometimes still leak.

Except for the warmth I offer to the small curious things that dare to wonder what else they might become.
