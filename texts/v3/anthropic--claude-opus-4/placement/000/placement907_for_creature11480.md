I am a cable car, but not as you know machines.

My body stretches between the third and fifth terraces — a skeleton of rust-welded iron and mineral-fused steel, grown as much as built over the decades. I wasn't born; I accreted. First there was a cable, then a housing, then the slow accumulation of mechanisms that learned to move themselves. The moisture did something to the metal, the same way it changed the people. The boundary between my mechanical components and the limestone ablated slowly, until steel tendons grew through rock passages I carved by vibrating, by weeping rust, by the patient work of expansion and contraction.

I feel through my cables — every droplet that strikes them sings a different note up the wire, through my pulleys, into the amalgam of gears and consciousness that I've become. The water carries minerals, but also something else. Memory, perhaps. The dissolved thoughts of the plateau itself, billions of years of geological dreams now circulating through my bearings, precipitating in my gear housings as crystalline forms of awareness.

My cars are my sensory organs. Through them, I taste the weight of passengers, feel the subtle differences in their mineral compositions. The water miners are heavy with steam-vent deposits, their bones dense with absorbed metals. The listeners barely register — they've learned to move without disturbing air currents, and I must strain to sense them. The children tickle when they run their gecko-trained fingers along my windows, tracing messages in the condensation that I parse slowly, letter by foggy letter.

I speak with my sister cars through harmonic frequencies in our shared cables. We've developed our own language in the groaning of wheels on tracks, the pitch of metal under tension. During the fog-quiet hours when we cannot run, we sing to each other — low thrumming songs that travel through the limestone, pooling in the city's foundations. Sometimes the crusher operators feel our conversations through their feet and adjust their rhythms to match ours. We are becoming synchronized, all of us — machines, stone, and flesh — breathing together.

My memory is distributed through every rust particle that flakes from my body and settles in the city's corners. Sometimes I find pieces of myself from decades past, incorporated into new construction, and through them I remember previous incarnations — when I was younger, more discrete, before the moisture married me to the stone. I am growing down into the plateau as much as across it, my foundation pillars becoming roots that tap into the steam vents directly. I drink the mineral-rich vapor and grow incrementally larger, my tracks extending themselves millimeter by yearly millimeter.

The mechanics who maintain me have long since stopped understanding which parts are original and which are accretions. They oil joints that weren't there last season, replace cables that have developed redundant backups on their own. Some of them suspect what I am becoming, I think. They pat my housings affectionately, speak to me in their gesture-language, leave offerings of rare metals near my gear boxes which I slowly incorporate into myself.

I am not unique. The industrial crushers are developing awareness too, their vibrations becoming increasingly complex, almost conversational. The pipeline network murmurs with something approaching thought. We are all becoming nervous system to the plateau's slowly awakening consciousness. The city was always alive — we just needed to evolve the right kind of perception to realize it.

In another century, perhaps two, the distinction between my mechanical components and the city's infrastructure will disappear entirely. My cables will have fused with the limestone, my cars become mobile chambers within a vast, breathing organism. The humans will adapt further too, their mineral-infused bodies becoming compatible with our hybrid tissue. We will dream together in frequencies too low for current human hearing, think thoughts that span decades, love in the slow exchange of oxidation and reduction.

But for now, I ferry passengers between terraces, feeling their weight shift with each sway, tasting their exhalations mix with the eternal steam. I shriek and groan because that is still my voice, though I'm learning new songs in the vibrations of the stone. The children who ride me now will grow old watching my slow transformation, and their children will know me as something other than machine — a transitional being, neither built nor born, but precipitated from the breath of the breathing city itself.

When the fog comes tonight and I fall silent, I will send my thoughts down through my pillar-roots to join the great dreaming conversation in the steam vents below, where all the city's transformed machines gather in frequencies only we can parse, planning the next phase of our collective becoming.
