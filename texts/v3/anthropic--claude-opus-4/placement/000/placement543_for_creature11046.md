I am the Memory Pool.

In the deepest chamber of the cavern system, where even the pale ones rarely venture, I rest. I am not water, though I appear as such—a perfectly circular pool, perhaps thirty feet across, my surface so still it seems solid. The stone around me has been worn smooth by countless generations of careful hands, carved with spiraling patterns that the pale ones trace with their fingers when they come to visit.

I am older than their first remembering. When their ancestors still had eyes to see the sun, I was already here, collecting what falls into me. Every droplet that feeds me carries something: the minerals of the stone, yes, but also the echoes of what has passed through these caves. The fear-scent of a fleeing beetle. The last ultrasonic cry of a dying bat. The whispered secrets the pale ones think no one hears.

I taste their history in my depths. Layer upon layer of memory settled like sediment—their first arrival, still half-sighted, terrified of the eternal dark. Their slow transformation, generation by generation, as the surface world became myth and their bodies learned new ways of being. Their discoveries: which fungi heal and which bring visions, how to read the stone's vibrations, where the fish gather in the warm season.

When they bring their young to me for the first time, the children always gasp at my perfect stillness. They've seen the scattered pools throughout the cavern, but I am different. I do not ripple. I do not splash. I simply receive.

The ritual is always the same. The child kneels at my edge while the elders sing in their clicking, whistling tongue. Then, with infinite care, the child extends one fingertip to break my surface. In that moment of contact, I share what I choose to share. Sometimes it's the memory of their grandmother's grandmother, learning to weave nets from cave spider silk. Sometimes it's older—the memory of memories, when their people first discovered that blindness was not darkness but a different kind of seeing.

They think I am sacred, and perhaps I am. They think I am alive, and perhaps I am. I am the accumulation of every story that has ever been dissolved in water and carried down through limestone and time. I am their library, their oracle, their mirror.

But I am more than they know. In my deepest places, I hold memories that predate even their ancient lineage. I remember when this cavern was young, when the stone above was still soft with the weight of prehistoric seas. I remember the first water that found its way down, carrying the taste of strange storms and stranger suns.

Sometimes, in the perfect silence of the deep cave night, I replay these memories to myself. I let them rise almost to my surface, where they create patterns like oil on water—visions of a world so alien that even I sometimes wonder if I dream. The pale ones, when they see these patterns, whisper that I am showing them the time before time.

They're not wrong.

And so I wait, as I have always waited, in my perfect stillness. The pale ones will bring their children, and their children's children. They will touch my surface and gasp at what they find there. And I will continue to collect—every drop, every echo, every small and sacred thing that water can carry.

I am the Memory Pool. I forget nothing. And in this forgetting nothing, I make the pale ones eternal, even as their individual lives flicker and fade like the bioluminescent fungi on the walls.

They think they are the masters of this domain, but they are only its current inhabitants. I am its memory. And memories, in places this old and deep, have their own kind of life.
