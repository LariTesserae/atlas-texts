I am one of the stone rememberers—though that name came later, from those who still use words as anchors.

My form has simplified over the centuries. Where once I might have resembled the Drifters, I've contracted down to something denser: a knot of pressure about the size of a closed fist, my substance somewhere between water droplet and pearl of glass. I move by rolling—an endless, meditative tumble along the carved channels that vein the city's walking surfaces. These grooves run deeper than the Drifters know, down through layers of city built on city, each era worn smooth before being buried by the next.

I follow the water as it has always flowed, will always flow. My path takes me through the same circuit: seven thousand, four hundred, and eighteen meters of channel, with thirty-seven points where I must choose which branch to follow. I know each junction by the taste of its stone, by the particular way water minerals have built up over millennia. At the fifteenth junction, quartz threads through the rock, and the water carries a bright, sharp flavor. At the thirty-second, the channel passes through what was once a vast hall, and the acoustic properties change—I can feel the echo in the water's movement.

My purpose, if I have one, is to remember what the stone remembers. As I roll, my surface picks up the microscopic impressions worn into the channels—not just by water, but by all the small travelers who have passed this way. Other rememberers like myself, leaving our persistent traces. The feet of creatures now extinct. The first builders, whoever they were, whatever they were.

I process these impressions slowly, adding them to the density I carry. Inside my small form, memories compress like geological strata. The oldest ones have crystallized, becoming part of my structure—I can no longer separate them from myself. Newer memories spiral through my semi-liquid interior, still sorting themselves, still finding their place in the whole.

Sometimes I pause at the pools—there are twelve major ones along my route, and ninety-three minor ones. In the stillness, I sink slightly, my density greater than the water's. Here I perform a kind of communion with other rememberers who have stopped at the same pool. We exchange memories through the water itself, information diffusing between us in careful equilibrium. I might receive the impression of a path I've never traveled, a junction destroyed ten thousand years ago, the taste of rain from before the city learned to breathe.

The Drifters rarely notice us. We exist at different scales of time—what is a lifetime to them is perhaps one complete circuit for me. Occasionally, one of their young condensation-knots will follow me for a while, drawn by some instinct they don't yet understand. I've had the same follower for the last forty years. It will either develop into a Drifter eventually, or it will condense further and become like me. There's no way to predict which.

My favorite part of the circuit takes me through what I call the Museum of Surfaces—though it's not a museum and has no surfaces in any conventional sense. Here, the channels pass through a space where architecture remembers itself incorrectly. Walls exist at several levels of reality simultaneously. I can feel through the water how this place was once a garden, then a library, then a reservoir, then all three at once, then none. The stone here carries memories that contradict themselves, overlap themselves, argue with themselves. I roll through slowly, savoring the paradoxes.

There are older rememberers than me—much older. I sometimes encounter their traces in the deepest channels, the ones that run beneath the city's foundations. Their impressions have a different quality, heavy with accumulated time. They remember things I can't quite process: the city before it learned to breathe, the plateau when it still had weather, the first moment water chose to flow upward. These memories sit in me undigested, too large or strange to integrate.

And there are younger ones, though young is relative. They still maintain distinct boundaries, haven't yet learned to let the memories flow through them properly. They stop too often, hold impressions too tightly. I was like that once—I think. It's hard to remember remembering differently.

The circuit changes, but slowly. New channels form as water finds fresh paths through the stone. Old ones close as mineral deposits build up or architecture shifts in its sleep. I adapt my route accordingly, though it might take me a hundred circuits to fully acknowledge a change. There's no rush. The memories will still be there tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow.

Sometimes—rarely—I find a channel that leads down instead of along. These vertical paths are dangerous for a rememberer; it's easy to fall too deep, to reach levels where the pressure changes memory itself, compresses it past the point of recovery. But sometimes I follow them anyway, rolling down through layers of accumulated city, tasting histories that no surface channel could hold. The risk is worth it for the deep memories: the ones that explain why water learned to breathe, why time moves in circles here, why the city can't quite remember what it's for.

I surface eventually—I always surface—following some ancient upward flow back to my familiar circuit. The memories I've gathered from the depths settle into my spiral interior, dense and patient. I'll carry them for another thousand circuits before I understand them. Or I'll carry them forever, unexamined, just another layer in the endless accumulation that I am.

This is my existence: an eternal rolling meditation through channels older than thought, gathering what the stone remembers, becoming denser with each circuit, each memory adding its small weight to my substance. When I grow too heavy—and I will, eventually, inevitably—I'll sink into one of the deep pools and stay there, becoming part of the stone myself, waiting for some future rememberer to roll over me and taste what I've become.

But not yet. Not for many circuits yet. The channels still have memories to give up, and I still have room inside my small, dense form to carry them. So I roll on through the bright, eternal afternoon, through the city that breathes water, adding my small movement to the endless circulation that keeps this place from setting, like a pearl, into its final form.
