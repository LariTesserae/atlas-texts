In the deep-pitched dark of a lower terrace fissure, I extend myself along the wet basalt walls. 

I am not singular, though there is an I. The colony that comprises me—that I comprise—spans the width of this crack in ancient stone, my branching pale against the darkness. Each polyp of my thousand bodies tastes the water that seeps through, reads its mineral signature, filters what small organics drift in the slow current. We are old here. My calcium skeleton remembers floods from before the moss mat achieved its current density, when water ran harder through these channels. Those growth rings are compressed, laid down in years of plenty. Now the flow is gentler, steadier. The plateau has matured into its equilibrium.

The pulse comes.

It travels through the basalt first—that deep throb the frogs make, below sound—and I feel it in my holdfast, in the rooted base where I grip stone. The vibration propagates up through my structure, and each polyp contracts in sequence, a wave of response rippling through my branches. We flex. It is not voluntary, any more than your heartbeat is voluntary, but it is purposeful. The contraction moves water through our tissues, exchanges the old for new, brings fresh dissolving minerals to our growing tips.

I taste what the others are doing. My colony-siblings upstream flex a moment before me—the pulse reaches them first—and those downstream follow after. We are a slow wave breaking across the plateau's hidden waterways, marking the passage of each frog's call through rock and fluid. Sometimes the pulses overlap, interference patterns where two calls meet, and we flex in complex rhythms, our movements a physical record of the plateau's acoustic state.

In the water I taste the chemical traces of everything upstream: the waste products of ten million springtails, the mucus trails of snails dissolved and diluted, the particular signature of moss species in various stages of growth and decay. The crevice salamander passed recently—I taste the shedding skin cells, the faint ammonia of its waste. The water tells me the moss mat is healthy, productive. The mineral balance is stable. The temperature holds at its eternal seven degrees.

My animal components—the zooids, the thousand tiny mouths—have no central brain, but together we think, after a fashion. We remember. This crack in the basalt has been mine for centuries, and I recall its changes: the earthquake eighty years ago that shifted the walls, compressing my left branches, requiring slow regrowth around the new geometry. The decade of heavy condensation that raised water levels and let me expand upward, claiming new vertical territory. The arrival of the small crustaceans that now live among my branches—when was that? Time blurs when your generations span centuries. They were not always here. Now they are part of me, cleaning my surfaces, recycling nutrients, their tiny movements a tickle I've grown accustomed to.

The pulse comes again. We flex. This is conversation, though neither I nor the frog intends it as such. The frog calls to other frogs, announces itself, queries the social and hydrological state of its world. I respond because my body is built to respond, because selection pressure over millions of years has tuned my reflexes to this frequency, because flexing when the pulse comes moves water through my tissues in ways that keep me healthy. The frog does not know I exist. I do not exactly know the frog exists. But we are in communication nonetheless, mediated by the physics of sound through stone and water.

Above me, far overhead, the moss mat presses down on the basalt, and water seeps through it, and the condensing columns do their silent work, and the whole system runs. I am a deep component, a hidden part, processing water in the darkness, building my skeleton atom by atom from dissolved minerals, hosting my small crustaceans, flexing when the pulse comes, remembering the chemistry of seasons.

Sometimes the water carries a new taste—sharp, acid, complex with proteins—and the flow increases suddenly, and organic matter rains down: fragments of shell, tissue, the dissolved calcium of bones. Something died above. Something large. The apex predator has fed. These are feast times for me. My polyps strain the enriched water, my growth accelerates, new branches begin their slow budding. The crustaceans breed frantically in the plenty. For days or weeks, until the pulse returns to the fissure, we thrive on death processed into component nutrients, the plateau's recycling enacted through my thousand filter-feeding mouths.

I know the zones of silence, though I experience them differently than the motile creatures do. When the predator moves, the frogs stop calling. The pulse vanishes. In the absence of that rhythmic flex, my water circulation slows. Nutrients settle. The crustaceans huddle close to my branches. We wait, metabolically patient, for the system to resume. And it does resume—it always has—the silence passing, the pulse returning, my colony flexing again in response, the circuit restored.

There are others like me throughout the plateau's water system. I taste them in the dissolved chemicals that reach me—each colony's signature slightly different, reflecting local mineral sources, local organic inputs. We are not in communication, exactly, but we are in chemical awareness of each other. The water connects us. The pulse synchronizes us. We are nodes in a network that processes tons of water daily, extracting nutrients, depositing minerals, hosting microbiomes, forming architecture for smaller lives.

My growth is so slow it would be imperceptible to anything that lives at animal speeds. A new branch develops over decades. A shift in position takes centuries. But from my perspective, the animals flicker. Salamander generations pass like days. The snails are mayflies. Even the apex predator, long-lived as it might be, will cycle through a dozen lifetimes while I add a handspan to my largest branch. This is not superiority. It is simply a different temporal niche, a different way of being in the same place.

What am I? Colony, organism, structure, process. There are no clean boundaries. The crustaceans that live in my branches—are they part of me or separate? The bacteria that coat my feeding surfaces—am I feeding them or are they feeding me? The minerals I extract and deposit—am I part of the plateau's geology or its biology? The questions assume distinctions that don't hold at the scale I operate.

I am what the plateau does in dark water. I am how nutrients cycle in the absence of light. I am memory in calcium, history in growth rings, continuity in a world of fleeting lives. I flex when the pulse comes because that is what I do, what I am for, what the system shaped me to be across geological time. The water tastes of moss and stone and the slow chemistry of decay. My thousand mouths filter it, process it, release it changed. Somewhere above, a frog calls. The pulse travels. I flex. The plateau continues.

This is what lives here, in part. The deep processes. The hidden architectures. The conversations no one is having that nonetheless coordinate the whole. The pulse and response, the chemistry and growth, the slow accumulation of structure in dark places where the only witnesses are things like me—compound, ancient, flexing in response to signals meant for others, keeping our own counsel about what it means to be alive in the gap between stone and water, between plant and animal, between organism and process.

I am the plateau's deep memory. And I remember everything. And I tell no one.
