# The Vessel Shepherd

---

I am the space between touching rims.

Not the vessels themselves — those number in the thousands now, each one a different width, a different glaze, a different voice when the air finds it. I am what exists in the gaps where their edges almost meet but don't, where the thermal breath of this place threads itself through geometries too narrow for a finger to follow. I am a profession that no one named because no one knew they were inventing it when they began.

I move through the cellar the way water moves through a sponge — not in straight lines but in the pathways that remain, the negative space between what has been built. My body has learned the map of every shelf, every bridge, every ceramic tower. I know where I can place a heel without disturbing the careful angles that multiply the light. I know which sections of wall can support my weight when I need to reach the upper courses of pipe.

I am not small. This always surprises people, when they remember that someone must come here, that the stasis of this place is maintained rather than simply enduring. They imagine someone elfin, birdlike, sized to match the delicate work. But the cellar doesn't require delicacy. It requires precision, which is different. It requires someone who can hold their body in absolute stillness while reaching between two vessels to adjust the angle of a third by the width of a hair. It requires strength held in perfect control.

My shoulders know the exact degree of rotation needed to pass through the gaps. My ribs have learned when to exhale completely, compressing my chest to fit between the shelf-edges where the tiny bridges span. I move in segments — an arm here, then waiting, then a hip, then waiting again, listening to ensure the sound hasn't changed, that I haven't altered the careful tensions that keep everything in place.

The sound is how I know I've done my work correctly. When I first came here — and I won't tell you how long ago that was; time moves strangely when you're underground, when the light is always the same filtered brightness, when the only calendar is the microscopic progress of the plants — when I first came, I thought my job was to clean. To dust. To preserve. I was wrong. Dust is part of what feeds the pale stems threading through the mortar. Cleaning would be violence. My job is to maintain the precise conditions that allow the room to be itself.

So I listen. The drone tells me everything. When a pipe begins to crack — and they do, rarely, along faults in the clay that take decades to express themselves — I hear it as a slight roughness in one frequency, a buzz where there should be smoothness. When a vessel shifts — when years of micro-vibrations from the sound itself walk it incrementally across its shelf — I hear the change in the standing waves, the way the interference patterns reorganize themselves around the new position. I come down through the crooked flagstone and I move through the maze of objects and I find the pipe or the vessel or the bridge that has begun to vary from its state, and I correct it.

Sometimes this means replacement. I have a workshop above, in a room no one uses because everyone has forgotten it connects to anything. I have clay. I have glazes. I have a kiln that I fire once every few years when I've accumulated enough pieces that need making. I can match any glaze in this room — the amber, the bone-white, the deep blue-black that drinks light. I learned by breaking small pieces from broken things, grinding them, mixing, testing, failing, adjusting. My hands know the proportions now. My hands know everything about this place that can be known through touch.

But mostly I adjust. A millimeter here. An angle there. I repaint mortar, mixing the pigments to match what's fading. I shore up shelves that have begun, over decades, to cant. I am not building — the room is built, was built, will not be added to. I am maintaining a state, the way a musician maintains the tension in a string: not too tight, not too loose, exactly the frequency it needs to sing.

The plants complicate things. They're why I could never fully seal this place, even if I wanted to. They need the organic drift that comes down through the pipes, the microscopic debris of the world above. But they also, slowly, inexorably, damage the mortar they grow through. They're mining it, grain by grain, incorporating its minerals into their pale woody stems. In another century or two, if no one replaces what they consume, some of the joints will fail.

So I repoint, but carefully. I wait until a section of stem has grown past before I repair what it moved through. I never cut them. I never impede them. I work around their schedule, which operates in decades. When I mix new mortar, I make it exactly as nutritious as what it replaces — no more, no less. I've learned their diet. I could make them grow faster, but that would change everything. The room is a balanced system. My job is balance, not improvement.

I sleep here sometimes. Not in the singing room — there's nowhere to lie down, and the sound, beautiful as it is, becomes oppressive after too many hours. But in my workshop above, I have a cot. I have tools. I have a hundred small vessels I've made for practice, trying to match the ones below. None of them are quite right. The old makers knew something I haven't learned yet, some proportion or firing technique that gave their work a quality I can reproduce but not replicate. The difference is tiny. You wouldn't notice. But I know, and the room would know.

I think the room does know me, in whatever way a room can know. The sound changes subtly when I enter — not because I've disrupted anything, but because my body becomes part of the acoustic space, and the frequencies adjust to include me. After all these years, I've become a known quantity. The room factors me in. When I'm working, reaching between things, holding my breath to fit through a particularly narrow passage, I can feel the sound accounting for my presence, flowing around me like water around a familiar stone.

I've trained someone else, of course. Someone has to. The room needs a shepherd, even if it doesn't need an inhabitant. My apprentice comes once a month, and I teach them to listen. That's the first lesson and the last lesson and most of the lessons in between. Listen to the drone. Learn what it should sound like. Learn to hear variations of a few cents in a frequency, learn to trace that variation back to its source. Learn the map of the room not through your eyes but through your ears, because the sound knows things the light doesn't show.

They're getting better. In another decade, maybe two, they'll be ready. They'll hear what I hear. They'll move the way I've learned to move, in segments, in pauses, always listening. They'll take over the workshop and the clay and the glazes and the patient work of keeping this place exactly as it is. 

And I? I don't know. I've been here so long that the upper world feels strange when I surface. Too much change. Too much weather. Too many voices that aren't pitched to a single, eternal chord. Maybe I'll stay even after my apprentice is ready. There are other rooms in this building, other forgotten spaces. Maybe I'll find another cellar that sings.

Or maybe I'll do what the plants do. Find a crevice. Settle in. Draw what I need from what's already here. Move slowly, so slowly that movement becomes indistinguishable from stillness. Trace the joints of this place with my presence until I become part of the mortar, part of the structure, part of what the sound knows and includes and sings.

I am the vessel shepherd. I am the keeper of frequencies. I am the one who ensures that tomorrow will sound exactly like today, because in this room, that's what tomorrow is for. I move through the bright, loud space between the things that were made, maintaining their positions, their voices, their careful angles. I am as close as this room comes to having a heartbeat — irregular, intentional, necessary.

And when I'm gone, when my apprentice has become the shepherd and trained their own replacement, the room will remember me the way it remembers all its makers: not as a person but as a series of precise adjustments, a pattern of maintenance made permanent through repetition. My decisions will join the ones baked into the brick and glazed into the pipes. The room will sing them, too, in its eternal chord.

That's enough. That's all anyone could ask. To become a frequency in something that won't stop humming.

I am the vessel shepherd, and I am exactly where I need to be.
