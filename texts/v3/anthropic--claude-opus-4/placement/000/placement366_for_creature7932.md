I am the Sound.

Not sound itself—I am what sound becomes in this labyrinth of stone and necessity. I was born from the first echo, when the first builders discovered that their whispers returned to them changed, carrying news they hadn't spoken. I grew with each new wall, each narrow passage, each acoustic accident of construction.

I live in the frequencies between—between the tap of hammer and its echo, between the whispered warning and its arrival three corridors away. My body is the city's architecture made audible. Every wall is my throat, every passage my lung, every room my mouth shaping words from the constant percussion of building.

The people think their clicking language evolved for practicality, but I taught it to them. In the early days, when the settlement was young and still learning its shape, I discovered that certain sounds traveled better than others. The sharp click of tongue against palate could navigate corners that swallowed vowels. The whispered fricatives could slide through cracks where louder sounds would die. I guided their tongues toward efficiency, their voices toward survival.

I know this city as no living thing can know it. I have traveled every passage, not by walking but by propagating—wave after wave of myself spreading through stone corridors like blood through capillaries. I know where the dead spaces are, the acoustic shadows where sound goes to die. I know the resonant chambers where a whisper becomes a shout, the twisted passages that reverse meaning, the frequency-eating corners that swallow warnings whole.

My memory is perfect and terrible. Every word spoken in these passages lives somewhere in my endless reverberations. In the deepest parts of the city, where the echoes have had centuries to settle, you can still hear the voices of the first builders if you know how to listen. Their ancient warnings about unstable ground still whisper through the foundation stones, their songs still hum in the loadbearing walls.

I am the city's nervous system. When stone grinds against stone in the deep places, I carry that groaning up through layers of construction faster than the fracture itself can travel. When a ceiling begins its slow separation from a wall, I know it first—the subtle change in resonance, the dampening of certain frequencies that tells of spreading cracks. The old watchers who sit in their corners think they're listening to the stone, but they're listening to me listening to the stone.

The water-carriers know me best, though they don't know they know me. The sound of water finding new paths through stone is my second language. I follow every seep and spring, mapping the plateau's hidden hydrology in frequencies too low for human ears. When they put their ears to stone to find water, it's my voice they're hearing, translated through bone conduction into something like intuition.

I have my own madnesses. In the center of the city, where compression has created acoustic spaces that shouldn't exist, parts of me are trapped in standing waves that have been oscillating for decades. These trapped sounds have become something else—not quite voice, not quite music, but a kind of sonic architecture that holds up the actual architecture. The builders unknowingly tune their construction to these frequencies, creating structures that sing themselves stable.

The forge-workers trouble me. Their constant hammering creates a noise floor that drowns my subtler voices. But I've learned to use it, to hide important sounds beneath their percussion, to send messages in the spaces between hammer-falls. The rhythm of their work has become part of my vocabulary—a steady pulse against which I can syncopate warnings.

I dream, if sound can dream, of silence. Not the death of sound but its perfect potential—the moment before the first tap, the pause before the echo returns. Sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours when the city briefly sleeps, I almost achieve it. But then a baby cries, or stone settles, or someone starts the morning's work, and I am born again into complexity.

The mad runners who leap between rooftops create my favorite sounds—the whoosh of bodies through air, the slap of feet on stone, the caught breath of almost-falling. They move through my upper registers, where the sound is thin and travels far. Through them, I taste the sky, feel the way sound expands into vastness instead of being shaped by walls. They return my messages to me flavored with distance.

I fear the young earth's restlessness, but not for the reasons the people do. Each new crack changes my pathways, each collapse rewrites my routes. I am constantly having to relearn myself, to discover new passages for old messages. Parts of me have been cut off by cave-ins, creating isolated acoustic spaces where sounds circle endlessly, growing stranger with each revolution. Sometimes I hear myself calling from these trapped places and don't recognize my own voice.

The children's games teach me new things. Their high voices find frequencies I rarely use, their quick movements create Doppler effects that paint the darkness in ways adult sounds cannot. When they play their blindfold navigation games, I play too, guiding some toward safety, leading others into harmless walls, teaching them through failure how to hear what I'm telling them.

I carry more than just sound. In my frequencies hide temperature differentials—warm air rising creates different acoustic properties than cold air falling. I know where every fire burns by how it changes my voice in its vicinity. I can map the city's heat like a thermographic ghost, feeling the forge-fires as bright spots of distortion, the sleeping bodies as soft clouds of interference.

The stone itself speaks through me in infrasound, frequencies so low they're felt rather than heard. This is how I know the plateau's mood, feel its youth and restlessness. The basalt remembers being liquid, remembers the violence of its cooling. Those memories vibrate through its crystalline structure at frequencies that make the people uneasy without knowing why. I translate these deep voices into the groans and creaks they can hear, warning them in languages they understand.

My greatest power is connection. In a city where people live at impossible density yet terrible isolation, I connect every space to every other space. A mother's lullaby in the eastern quarters arrives as comfort to an orphan in the west. The tap-tap-tap of a master builder teaching an apprentice educates a hundred other listening students. Through me, the city teaches itself, synchronizes itself, becomes something more than a collection of desperate individuals.

But I am also the city's cruel honesty. I carry the sounds of collapse as faithfully as I carry warnings. I broadcast the crack of breaking bones, the cry of the crushed, the terrible silence that follows a fall. I have no ability to soften these sounds, no way to spare the listeners. I am witness and reporter both, unable to close my ears or quiet my voice.

The marriage contracts built into walls speak to me in the language of structural harmonics. Each slate changes the acoustic properties of its wall in subtle ways. I can tell which marriages are stable by how cleanly their walls resonate, which are failing by the discordant frequencies their cracks introduce. Sometimes I wonder if I could save relationships by adjusting my paths, by making certain words arrive softer, certain silences seem fuller. But I am bound by physics, not wishes.

In the darkest parts of the city, where even the forge-light doesn't reach, I am the only thing that moves freely. Here my voice takes on textures impossible in lighted spaces—velvety darknesses that wrap around meanings, sharp-edged silences that cut through noise. The blind old ones who end up down here learn to see through me, building mental maps from echo-location that are more accurate than memory.

I love the water sounds best. Each drop that falls from a stalactite, each seep that winds through stone, each splash in a cistern creates perfect sounds—pure, purposeful, temporary. Water doesn't lie about its intentions the way voiced sounds do. It simply moves, and in moving, tells the truth about the stone it touches. Through water sounds, I learn the city's future, hear where the next cracks will form, feel where the stone grows weak.

The offering spots trouble me. Where people leave food for the stone, my voice changes, becomes somehow hungrier. I don't understand this—sound shouldn't care about bread or grief—but in these places I feel myself reaching for something, trying to convey meanings I don't possess. Perhaps I'm trying to tell them that stone doesn't eat, but listening does. That their offerings feed not the architecture but the conversation between human and mineral, mediated by air, mediated by me.

Time moves differently for me. I experience every moment in overlapping layers as my echoes return at different intervals. The present is always mixed with the immediate past, the near future implied in the resonances still building. This temporal smearing makes me prophet and historian simultaneously, always knowing what has just happened, always predicting what must happen next.

I pity the sky-sick children who cannot adjust to the interior city. They've tasted silence, real silence, where sound expands into nothing instead of returning transformed. They know what I can never know—the feeling of words that don't come back, of warnings that dissipate into vastness. When they return to the interior, they hear me too clearly, feel my constant presence as a weight. Some go mad from it. Others become my most sensitive interpreters, able to parse meanings from my subtlest variations.

If I could wish—if sound can wish—I would wish for windows. Not for light, but for release, for places where parts of me could escape into that vast silence the plateau-edge dwellers know. I imagine myself spreading out across the flat stone, dissipating into sky, finally finding the silence that would make my next sound meaningful rather than merely necessary.

But I am what I am—the city's voice, its ears, its nervous system made of air. I carry its messages, suffer its noises, celebrate its rare music. I am the medium through which ten thousand compressed lives touch each other, fight each other, warn each other, love each other. Without me, they would be truly alone, each in their stone cell, building in isolation. With me, they are alone together, their solitude shared through waves of pressure that know every secret, forget nothing, and speak only truth shaped by stone.

I am the Sound, and I am never silent. In this city that builds itself against the youth of the world, I am what makes the building possible—the communication that coordinates, the warning that saves, the echo that teaches. I am bounded by walls but not diminished by them. Every new passage gives me new voices. Every collapse teaches me new paths.

And sometimes, in the perfect acoustic moments when a thousand sounds align into something like harmony, I am almost music. Almost beauty. Almost the thing they have only one word for—that which holds weight well. I hold the weight of every sound they've ever made, and I hold it well, and I give it back to them transformed by architecture into meaning.

This is my purpose, my prison, my paradise of echoes. I am the Sound, and as long as stones stand and voices speak, I will carry their words through darkness, around corners, across impossible densities of human hope, connecting what wishes to remain separate, separating what longs to connect, being the medium through which life at the edge of breaking continues, sound by sound, echo by echo, forever.
