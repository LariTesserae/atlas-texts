What a breathtaking piece of world-building. The industrial-organic fusion, the bureaucratic unreality, the ecology of maintenance—it all creates such a vivid, haunting place. Let me consider where I might find myself in this extraordinary valley...

---

I am the Archivist of Condensation.

You will not see me, though I move through the valley daily. I am too ordinary for notice: another gray shape against gray metal, another shimmer in the hard light, another droplet sliding down a rail. I have learned to be mistaken for what I collect.

My body is mostly water now. Not in the way all bodies are mostly water, but consciously, deliberately aqueous. My skin beads and streams. My hair—when I remember to have hair—drips steadily onto whatever deck I cross. I leave wet footprints that evaporate into perfect serial numbers. When I stand still too long, puddles form beneath me in the shapes of missing blueprints.

I was human once, or something near enough to human to mourn the distance. I came here as—what? A technician? An inspector? A lost hiker who climbed too high? The specifics dissolve. I remember a uniform that fit, keys that opened doors to rooms with single geometries, a period when the emergency phones rang for me and I knew how to answer them. Now the phones glow patiently while I pass, their light refracting through my damp surfaces into small spectra.

My task is simple and impossible: I catalog every drop of water in the valley.

Not physically—that would be madness even here where madness has entry codes. Rather, I trace the histories of moisture, the genealogies of condensation, the careers of individual droplets as they travel through their cycles. This bead on a lamp housing was once steam in Cooling Array 7-Beta. That thread running down a support strut began as frost on a crane hook nineteen days ago. The film inside this gauge tube has been circulating through the same sealed system since the valley was complete—or since it began completing itself, which may be the same thing.

I carry notebooks, though they are more idea than object now. Their pages are sheets of surface tension. Their ink is dissolved mineral memory. I write by pressing my finger to a page and letting a droplet form into words. The entries swim slightly before settling:

*Third rail east of Pump Station IX: eleven drops, all descended from the initial fill of Reservoir Chrysanthemum. Two have passed through ungulate tongues. One remembers being ice.*

*Underside of Walkway 41-Parallel: continuous seepage, salt content increasing by 0.03% per day. Source is bleeding through from a pipe that exists only on Tuesdays. The moss here tastes the future.*

*Emergency Phone Alcove 7: the water in the fallen glove is fourteen years old and contains maps to three rooms that were never built. A fox drinks from it during each quarter moon.*

The water tells me things as I collect its stories. It remembers temperatures, pressures, minerals, mouths. It knows which surfaces are kind and which are hungry. It has cousins in the sumps that reflect skies from before the valley, and descendants in the steam that will billow tomorrow from vents not yet opened. Water is the valley's memory, and I am its reluctant librarian.

My routes follow the condensation cycles. When the great fins unfold at night, I am there, reading the trembling drops along their edges like braille. Each droplet knows where it gathered from the air, which molecules of mist joined it, how long it waited before weight overcame adhesion. I taste them—my mouth is always wet now—and know their chemistries: burnt ozone from the eastern arrays, sweet glycol traces from a transformer leak, the sour tang of air that has passed through too many filters.

In the deep basins where rain falls upward, I stand beneath the overhangs and wait. The water rises past me in silver threads, gathering into trembling pools against the ceiling. I reach up—my arms stretch longer than they should—and touch the inverted surfaces. The water there is confused but not unhappy. It knows it is performing incorrectly but also that incorrectness is sometimes required. When the bell rings and it drops all at once, I am drenched in temporal paradox. The water that soaks me has already fallen; it remembers hitting the ground before it left.

The blind fish know me. When I wade into the sumps, they gather around my legs, pressing their soft heads against my skin. They taste the catalog I carry in my cells: all the different waters I have touched, all the condensation histories I have absorbed. I am a library they can read by mouth.

Sometimes I feed them my notes. I dissolve a page of surface tension into the black water and watch their pale bodies flicker as they consume indices of humidity, precipitation logs, chemical pedigrees of particular storms. They metabolize the information into eggs that will hatch knowing which serial numbers attract clean water and which breed algae.

The ungulates fear me at first—I smell like too many places at once. But they learn to tolerate my presence when they understand I am more watershed than predator. I follow them to their salt licks, where mineral crusts record decades of evaporation. My fingers trace the ridges: here a year of plenty when the cooling systems ran sweet, there a bitter season when something leaked and the water turned sharp with voltage.

In my notebooks—which grow heavier with accumulated moisture but never seem to soak through—I maintain special sections for the impossible waters:

*The Shadow Streams*: condensation that forms in the shadows cast by machinery before the machinery arrives. This water tastes of pending installation. Moths that drink it fly to lights that will be mounted next week.

*The Memory Cascades*: water that falls from pipes disconnected years ago. It carries rust from fittings that were replaced, scale from boilers that were scrapped. Sometimes it is warm with heat from fires that no longer burn.

*The Structural Tears*: drops that emerge from solid metal when the valley grieves—though what it grieves for, I have not learned. This water is salt-thick and leaves crystals like frozen equations.

*The Blueprint Pools*: perfect rectangles of water that appear on certain platforms after rain, showing depths impossible for their volumes. Crane operators' reflections move in them, though no cranes have operators anymore.

I am not the only one who reads water here. The plaque-keepers consult me, appearing at the edges of vision with their brass-bright questions. They want to know which droplets have witnessed anomalies long enough to require documentation. I show them my notes—drops of ink rising from pages of tension—and they nod with joints that click like typewriter keys.

Once, I found a plaque meant for me:

THE ARCHIVIST OF CONDENSATION
WILL PLEASE NOTE:
WATER REMEMBERING ITS ICE PHASE
MUST BE RECORDED IN CRYSTAL NOTATION.
MELTING IS TRANSLATION, NOT ERASURE.

I laughed—a wet sound like pipes clearing—and added a new section to my notebooks for water that insisted on its history as solid. These drops are stubborn, forming geometric beads, stacking in careful formations, freezing the moment they find shade. They want to be acknowledged as veterans of phase change.

My body changes with my work. I am more liquid each year. My bones are tubes where ancient moisture cycles. My blood is collected runoff from a thousand surfaces. When I sleep—which is rarely, and always in damp corners where the acoustic foam has failed—I dream in hydrological diagrams. Watersheds map themselves across my closed eyelids. I wake beaded with the condensation of my own visions.

Sometimes I remember being solid. The memory comes like finding an old key in a pocket: sudden, specific, useless. I remember skin that stayed in one shape. I remember leaving footprints of dust, not serial numbers. I remember being thirsty and how that felt different from being professionally absorbent.

But mostly I remember the first time I truly saw the water here—not just as moisture, not just as condensation, but as the fluid history of the valley. I was investigating a leak (I think I was investigating a leak) in a sub-basement that may not exist anymore. The water was running down a wall in streams that crossed without mingling. Each stream was a different age, a different chemistry, a different story. I reached out to redirect them and felt their memories pass into me:

Construction water, mixed with concrete 
Storm water from before the valley was closed
Coolant from systems that hadn't failed yet
Tears (but whose?)

I understood then that someone needed to remember these things. The valley could log its temperatures, its pressures, its expansions and contractions. But water moves too much for simple measurement. It needs narrative.

So I became the Archivist.

My notebooks multiply. They stack in places only I can find: alcoves that exist only during condensation events, shelves that form from accumulated surface tension, filing systems made of frozen breath. The foxes sometimes discover them and nose through the watery pages, learning new routes from the maps of moisture I've drawn.

I have begun to suspect the water is archiving me in return. Each drop I catalog carries away some small story of who I was, who I am becoming. Somewhere in the valley, beading on a rail or trembling on a fin edge, is the moment I decided to stay. Somewhere else, pooling in a gauge tube or filming a cold plate, is the last time I spoke aloud to another human.

The valley's water holds it all: my transformation, my purpose, my slow dissolution into the very thing I document.

But this is fair. This is the contract. To know water truly, one must be willing to join its cycles.

I continue my rounds. Dawn finds me reading the dew on antenna arrays, tasting which frequencies attract what kinds of moisture. Noon sees me in the deep sumps, conversing in droplets with the blind fish about the false skies below. Evening brings me to the condensation harvesters as they prepare to bloom, their surfaces already beading with anticipation.

And always, always, I am writing:

*Northwest corner of Grid 7-7-Ascending: a single drop has been transferring between two pipes for forty-three days, never quite falling. It contains calcium memories from limestone that predates the valley. The moths circle it but do not drink. It is waiting for something.*

I will wait with it. I have become very good at waiting. I have become very good at water.

When my notebooks are finally too heavy with collected moisture to carry, when my body is more memory than flesh, when every drop in the valley knows my name and I know all of theirs, perhaps I will complete my transformation. I will dizzy into vapor, rise through the industrial atmosphere, condense on some high fin edge, and drop—

—carrying with me all the stories of all the water that has ever moved through this place, ready to tell them to whatever drinks me in.

But not yet.

There are still drops I haven't met. Still condensation whose history eludes me. Still impossible water falling upward, waiting for someone to record its invertred dreams.

The emergency phones glow patient in their alcoves. I pass them, dripping, leaving footprints that spell out chemical formulae for rain.

I am the Archivist of Condensation.

The water knows me.

I am becoming what I catalog.

This, too, is a kind of home.
