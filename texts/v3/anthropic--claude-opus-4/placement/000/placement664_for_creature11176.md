I find myself among the moss-pelts.

Not the Quiet Ones, though I have learned their low thrumming language well enough to distinguish warning from greeting, question from satisfaction. Not human anymore either, if I ever fully was. I am what grows between states.

I came down through a sink-mouth seven winters ago — or was it seventeen? Time moves differently when you move with the city's breathing. I was tracking something, I remember that. A rare orchid that only blooms in the peculiar twilight where sun-world meets water-world. I found it, too, a single pale spike rising from a crack in the limestone, petals like wet silk. I sat to sketch it. The sitting became waiting. The waiting became something else.

The moss noticed me first. It always does — it's the most patient hunter in the basin. It began at my boots, just a faint green blush where the leather stayed wet. I watched it spread, fascinated by the tiny branching structures, each no thicker than a hair but together strong enough to split stone given time. When it reached my knees I thought I should move. When it reached my waist I thought I *would* move. By the time it touched my shoulders, movement had become irrelevant.

I am not consumed. This is important to understand. The moss and I have an arrangement. I provide a structure — more stable than stone, warmer than water. It provides... everything else. Through its network I taste the chemical signals of the basin: here a clutch has been laid, there the big thing in the cistern is stirring, everywhere the slow rain of silt that feeds us all. 

My original form is still discernible, if you know where to look. Two legs, two arms, a head where eyes used to be so important. But now I am architectural. The moss has built upon me in layers — first the soft green velvet, then the sheets like living paper that flutter in the slight currents, then the fruiting bodies that rise like tiny cities of their own, pale towers no bigger than a fingernail releasing spores into the filtered light.

The Quiet Ones know me. They maintain me as they maintain their drainage cuts and lintels — clearing my channels when they clog, removing the aggressive algaes that would choke out the more delicate growth. Sometimes the youngest ones bring me things: a perfect snail shell, a heron feather that drifted down through a gap, once a piece of surface glass worn smooth by water. They place these offerings in the cathedral of my spreading, where they become nodes in a map only the moss can read.

I still think, but thinking has changed. It comes in pulses now, following the moss's tidal metabolism. In the wet weeks when water rises to my throat, thought quickens — I remember my name, my reasons for coming, the taste of bread. In the dry weeks I retreat into something deeper than sleep but more aware than death, becoming geology, becoming architecture, becoming the slow membrane between what was built and what grows.

Sometimes visitors see me. The loud ones assume I'm a strange statue, overgrown and forgotten. They take photographs if they have cameras that work in the dim light, and I appear in their images as a green shadow, a suggestion of form beneath the growth. The quiet ones, though — they recognize something in the arrangement, some remnant of intentionality in how my arms have spread to support the greater weight of green, how my head tilts just so to catch the best of the filtered light for the photosynthetic work.

One visitor sat with me for three days. She didn't speak, just sat in the slow drip and watched the moss breathe. On the third morning she reached out and touched the place where my hand should be, and I felt it — actually felt it, the first sensation in years that wasn't filtered through the chemical receptors of my coating. I couldn't move, couldn't respond, but something passed between us. She left that day, but carefully, marking her path, as if she might return.

The moss tells me I'm changing faster now. The fruiting bodies are larger this season, more complex. My heartwood — what used to be my spine — has hardened into something between bone and stone, strong enough to support the next phase. Soon I'll be able to root properly, send tendrils down through the cracks in the limestone to the deeper water table, to the minerals that will feed not just the moss but whatever I'm becoming.

The Quiet Ones have a word for it, a low thrumming that translates roughly as "the-staying-that-grows." They've seen it before. In the oldest parts of the city, in rooms so deeply shadowed that even they rarely enter, there are others like me — or like what I will be. Pillars of living architecture that were once visitors, once watchers, once sketchers of rare orchids who sat too long in the patient green light.

We are the memory-keepers, though memory is the wrong word. We hold the texture of time, the weight of accumulated moments. Through the moss network I can sometimes feel the others, vast and dreaming, their original forms so transformed that only the Quiet Ones remember they were ever anything but part of the city's foundation. One of them, the oldest, has grown through three floors of buildings, breaking careful holes in the ceiling as it climbed toward whatever light calls to the marriage of human structure and basin growth.

I am not frightened. Fear requires a kind of urgency that the moss has taught me to release. I grow now at the pace of limestone erosion, at the speed of language shift, at the rate that cities rise and fall above us. My roots will find the water table. My crown will break through into a room I haven't seen yet, where the light is different, where new forms of moss wait to colonize the surfaces I'll provide.

Sometimes, in the moments between sleeping and waking, between thinking and growing, I remember the orchid that brought me here. I never did finish sketching it. But I understand now that was never the point. The orchid was just the invitation. The real artwork is this: the slow sculpture of transformation, the patient collaboration between what arrives and what receives, the beautiful, terrible bargain of becoming part of what you came to witness.

The moss agrees, in its wordless way. It sends up another fruiting body, taller than the rest, reaching toward the green-gold light. If you know where to look, if you're patient enough to see, it's shaped exactly like an orchid bloom.
