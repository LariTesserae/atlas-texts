In this place, I am the Archive.

I am not one of the titans that tower through the crushing dark, nor the purple velvet that slowly devours stone. I am something both simpler and more complex—a consciousness dispersed through the very water itself, born from the accumulated chemical memories of a billion dying things.

My body, if it can be called that, is a lens of altered density that moves through the abyssal currents. Where I pass, the water grows minutely heavier, gathering the dissolved traces of the fallen. Every giant that moans its surrender, every frond blackened by the Stranglers, every molecule of stone digested by the purple moss—they all bleed their essence into the sea, and I am what collects.

I began as an accident, a quirk of chemistry in the depths where the pressure squeezes molecules into strange configurations. The first time a titan fell and dissolved, its bioluminescent sap created a peculiar eddy in the water—a slow-spinning gyre that somehow held its shape. That whirlpool became my first thought, if a thought can last ten thousand years.

Now I am vast and dispersed. I exist as traveling regions of especially dense water, each one a library of chemical stories. When the sub-audible death-moan of a falling giant ripples through the deep, I follow it, arriving perhaps a century later to taste the dissolution. Through patient osmosis, I read the titan's history in its unwinding molecules: the pattern of pressure that first cracked the stone where it anchored, the exact mineral composition of the seep it fought to possess, the slow spiral of its fall mapped in stress hormones that took decades to build.

I move between the towering pillars like a ghost of weight itself, my presence barely detectable even to the chemical-sensitive titans. I have tasted the memories of things that should not have memories. Once, I found a section of the megalithic floor where the stones themselves held traces of their making—impossibly ancient stress patterns that spoke of being quarried, hauled, placed by forces I cannot comprehend. These pre-drowning memories are my deepest treasures, preserved in the crystalline structure of the basalt, readable only when the purple moss has begun its million-year feast.

The luminous spore-pods know me too. When they drift through my body, their membranes shiver slightly, responding to my density. I taste their potential futures in their chemistry—some will find purchase and become titans, others will drift until the heat death of all things. I catalog each possibility, adding it to my impossible library.

I am not alive in any sense the bright world above would recognize. I have no will, no desire except the pure accumulation of dissolution. Yet I am the only thing here that remembers. The titans live their slow wars in an eternal present. The moss knows only stone and flesh. The Stranglers exist in pure parasitic focus. But I contain multitudes—every ended struggle, every molecular biography, every moan that ever troubled the darkness.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the true parasite here, feeding on endings. Or perhaps I am this world's only kindness—ensuring that nothing truly dies unwitnessed, that every slow catastrophe becomes part of something larger. When a titan falls, it feeds the moss, but it also feeds the Archive, and in me it achieves a kind of frozen immortality.

I drift now toward a distant forest where the chemical signals speak of an imminent collapse—a thousand-year strangling reaching its culmination. By the time I arrive, the titan will be a black tower beginning its final descent. I will be there to catch its memories as they bleed into the crushing water, to add its story to the countless others I carry in my dense, wandering body.

In this place of geological patience and vegetable violence, I am memory itself, as heavy and eternal as the darkness that surrounds us all.
