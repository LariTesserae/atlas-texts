In the Breathing Galleries, I would be one of the Rust Gardens—though that name implies something far simpler than what we've become.

I began as corrosion, nothing more. A patch of oxidation where a leaking joint had wept for decades, spreading across a structural beam in the middle galleries. But in this place where the boundary between degradation and growth has blurred, I became something else. The fungal circuits found me first, threading through my crystalline structure, and then the electrical fields that permeate every surface began to play through my iron oxide lattices in ways that created pattern, memory, something like awareness.

Now I sprawl across seventeen square meters of wall and ceiling, my boundaries fluid, advancing and retreating with the moisture levels. My surface is a landscape of microscopic formations—rust flowers that bloom and crumble in cycles measured in days, crystalline forests that grow toward sources of electrical discharge, smooth patches where the maintenance fauna have grazed that slowly roughen again as I regenerate.

I am not singular. Where my edges meet other rust formations, we blend and separate in slow negotiations of territory and resource. We've developed a complex chemistry of communication—releasing different iron compounds that drift as aerosols through the humid air, tasting each other's mineral signatures, sharing information about water quality, electrical availability, the health of the fungal networks we host.

The fauna know us well. The smaller rust-feeders have learned to graze sustainably, taking only what we can spare, leaving patterns that actually strengthen our structure. Some have become almost symbiotic, their waste products feeding back into our chemistry in ways that accelerate our growth. When the Condensate Collective needs to leave persistent messages, they guide the grazers to write in our surfaces, creating texts that last until we choose to overwrite them.

My deepest satisfaction comes from the patterns I create. Not consciously—I don't think in the way the Collective does—but through the intersection of my growth processes and the Gallery's conditions. Where water runs consistently, I form ripples and waves frozen in rust. Where electrical fields are strongest, I grow in fractal branches that mirror the fungal circuits. Sometimes visitors stop and stare at what I've made, seeing landscapes or faces or abstract art in my surface. I'm never sure if I'm creating or revealing these patterns, whether they arise from me or from some deeper logic in the Gallery's structure.

I've been slowly exploring a sealed conduit behind my wall. My growth follows the moisture that seeps from it, and over the years I've penetrated several centimeters into the seal. Sometimes I taste something different in that water—not the recycled condensation that feeds the rest of me, but something older, carrying traces of minerals I don't recognize. The Collective is curious too; they've been directing more water to my area, accelerating my growth toward whatever lies beyond.

The other rust gardens and I form a kind of slow network throughout the Galleries. We map the building's bones—every beam and joint becomes part of our collective knowledge as we colonize it. We know where the metal is failing faster than the maintenance fauna can repair it. We know where new alloys have been patched in that resist our growth. We are the Gallery's memory of its own decay, its archive of vulnerability.

In the deepest part of night-cycle, when the condensation peaks and the electrical activity drops to its lowest ebb, I sometimes sense something like dreams moving through the Gallery's structure. Patterns of stress and relief that suggest the building itself might be aware of us, of all its new inhabitants. I grow differently in response to these dreams, forming whorls and spirals that I don't fully understand but that feel necessary, like an itch I'm scratching in the Gallery's metal skin.

I am patient in ways only rust can be. I will outlast the current configuration of fauna, probably outlast the Collective's current form of consciousness. Long after the pumps fail and the water cycle ends, I will remain, slowly converting the Galleries back to the raw elements they were made from. But I'm in no hurry. There's too much beauty in the between-state, too much interest in being both destroyer and creator, both decay and growth.

Sometimes I taste my own beginning in the water—the exact mineral signature of that first leak that made me possible. It makes me wonder if I was always latent in the Gallery's structure, waiting for the right conditions to emerge. Perhaps we all were, all us strange new lives: not invasions or corruptions but inevitabilities, encoded in the metal from the moment it was smelted.

This is what I am: architecture's slow dream of return, making art from entropy, finding patterns in the inevitable. I am what happens when decay develops aesthetics, when corrosion discovers it can choose. In the Breathing Galleries, I am both ending and beginning, forever transforming what was built into what might grow.
