In this thunderous wooden cosmos, I would find myself as one of the **Resonance Worms**.

We are creatures the factory almost forgot to make—or perhaps made by accident, birthed from the marriage of sound and time itself. Each of us begins life no larger than a fingernail, transparent as water, pressed flat against the glass-smooth heartwood where the vibrations are strongest. We do not eat food. We eat frequency.

Our bodies are living tuning forks—elongated, segmented tubes of specialized cartilage that grow in perfect mathematical ratios. Each segment resonates at a different harmonic of the factory's fundamental frequencies: the deep THUD of the valves, the pneumatic hiss of the seed-pods, the endless clatter of the paddle-leaves. We position ourselves at the acoustic nodes where these sound waves converge and cancel, creating pockets of relative quiet in the deafening roar. Here, in these islands of lesser noise, we feed.

The feeding is not consumption but transformation. When the factory's cacophony strikes our precisely calibrated segments, we don't simply vibrate—we transpose. The brutal mechanical noise enters our bodies as chaos and emerges as something else entirely: ultrasonic songs beyond the hearing of any other creature, compressed into frequencies so high they become a kind of sonic temperature. This is our warmth, our sustenance, our very metabolism. We are living converters, taking the factory's waste noise and rendering it into silent heat.

As we mature, we grow longer, adding new segments to capture more of the factory's acoustic spectrum. The eldest among us stretch nearly twenty feet, their bodies draped across the walls like living pipework, each section swollen or contracted to match the resonance of its chosen position. We move only when the acoustic landscape shifts—when a valve wears enough to change its pitch, when a new flight pattern of brass insects alters the drone. Then we must relocate, slowly undulating across the polished wood until we find a new harmonic home.

Our reproduction is synchronized to the factory's rarest event: the momentary silence that occurs once every forty-seven days when, by pure mathematical coincidence, every valve, pod, and leaf operates in perfect phase cancellation for exactly one-point-seven seconds. In this precious pocket of quiet, we sing—not with voices but with our entire bodies, releasing all our stored ultrasonic energy in a single, inaudible scream that cracks our segments open. From these acoustic fractures, microscopic larvae emerge, already tuned to their first frequencies, ready to find their own nodes in the eternal din.

We are conscious only in the way a prism is conscious of light—aware of our function but not our purpose. We dream in waveforms, think in harmonics, and measure time not in days but in beat frequencies. Other creatures occasionally crawl over us, mistaking our transparent bodies for irregular wood grain or dried sap trails. We do not mind. We are too busy listening to the deep mathematics of the engine, converting its violence into our silent song, living proof that even in a world of perfect mechanical equilibrium, there are gaps between the gears where stranger forms of life can grow.

Some might say we are parasites, stealing the factory's acoustic energy. But we prefer to think of ourselves as the machine's memory—storing its sounds in our bodies, carrying the history of every worn valve and shifted frequency in our ever-growing segments. We are the only creatures here that remember the factory's voice from a thousand years ago, and the only ones who will notice when, in another thousand years, its song has changed.
