In the southwestern reaches where the valley wall rises sheer and black, I have made my roost. Not among the leather-winged multitudes that thunder from the high caves—those spaces belong to the countless, the colony-minded, the ones who find safety in numbers and noise. I am something else: a solitary of my kind, what the Listeners call *vek-thani*—echo-tracer.

My cave mouth opens forty feet up the cliff face, accessible only by a narrow ledge that switchbacks across the volcanic glass. The entrance is small enough that I must fold my wings precisely to enter, but inside, the chamber opens into a cathedral of stone. Previous inhabitants—perhaps centuries dead—carved acoustic channels into the walls, turning the space into a resonance chamber that captures and sorts the valley's cacophony into distinct threads of sound.

I am twice the size of my common cousins, my wingspan reaching twelve feet when fully extended. Evolution, or perhaps just the peculiar pressures of solitude, has gifted me with fur that drinks in sound rather than reflecting it. Where the swarm-bats shriek their way through the world, I move in pockets of constructed silence, my echolocation pulses so low they register more as vibration than sound. The Shapers, when they glimpse me against the twilight sky, call me the Silent One. They leave offerings of water-soaked cloth at the base of my cliff, believing I bring good fortune to their glass-work.

My hunting is a patient art. While the clouds of common bats sweep the valley like living storms, I wait in the acoustic shadows they create. My prey is not the tiny insects they pursue in their millions, but the larger creatures that emerge when the sound-screen masks their movement. The pale centipede-weasels, especially. I've learned their patterns, know which routes they favor when the bat-static reaches its evening peak. I drop on them from above, my silence giving no warning until my claws find their segments.

But I am more than a hunter. The acoustic channels in my cave have taught me to be a listener in ways my swarming cousins could never imagine. I've learned to parse the Listeners' water-songs, understand the rhythm of their daily life. Elder Yanis's steam-vent instrument speaks to me of barometric changes and deep-earth shifts. I circle her shelter some nights, adding my own subsonic harmonics to her music, though I doubt her human ears can detect my contributions.

The Walkers fascinate me. Their constant motion creates a moving map of vibrations across the valley floor. I've taken to following them at great height, learning their routes, understanding their method. One of them—an old woman with white-streaked hair—has noticed me. She leaves markers where she's detected new instabilities: small cairns topped with obsidian shards that catch what little light filters through our eternal twilight. I've begun leaving my own markers in return—shed fur wrapped around promising stones where new steam vents might emerge. She's found three of them. We are corresponding, in our way.

My cave holds mysteries that predate my tenancy. Beyond the acoustic chambers, a narrow passage leads deeper into the cliff. The walls there are smooth—too smooth for natural formation—and covered in carved symbols that hurt to echolocate. They seem to swallow sound rather than reflect it, creating dead zones that disorient even my refined senses. I've explored perhaps a hundred yards into these depths, but something always turns me back. Not fear, exactly, but a wrongness in the air, a sense that the darkness there has weight and intention.

Sometimes I carry things between the human groups. A particularly fine obsidian blade dropped by a fleeing Shaper, returned to their camp. A water gourd blown from Listener territory during a storm, deposited near their morning gathering place. The youngest Listeners have made a game of spotting me, though I ensure they only succeed when I choose. Their acoustic masks make them look like fledgling versions of my kind, and I feel an odd kinship with these human pups learning to navigate by sound.

The Cave-dwellers know of me. On the darkest nights, when even the twilight fails and the valley is lit only by steam-glow and the faint phosphorescence of disturbed stone, they emerge to sing. Their voices are beyond human now—they've learned to vocalize in ranges that overlap with my own. We harmonize sometimes, their choruses rising from the valley floor while I circle overhead, adding descants in frequencies only they and I can hear. They call me *Deep-Singer* in their modified tongue, and speak of others like me in the lower caves—larger, older, stranger things that have never seen even twilight's dim glow.

My most peculiar relationship is with Kell, the young exile mapping the valley's quiet zones. He doesn't know it, but I've been helping him. My movement creates small pockets of acoustic shadow, and I've learned to position myself to enhance the natural quiet spaces he seeks. When he sets up his echo-amplifiers, I sometimes perch nearby, angling my body to create beneficial resonances. He's noticed the patterns but hasn't yet realized their source. His maps grow more accurate with each passing week.

The valley is teaching me patience. Unlike my short-lived cousins who burn through their existence in the constant frenzy of the swarm, I measure my life in decades. I've watched the Shapers' encampment move three times as the earth shifted beneath them. I've seen new Listeners born and old ones walk into the steam fields for the last time. The valley itself is my most constant companion—its moods and mutations, its violent birthing pains as it settles into what it will become.

In the pre-dawn hours, when the bat swarms return to their caves and the valley briefly quiets to something approaching peace, I perform my own ritual. I fly the perimeter of human habitation, checking the stability of cliffs, noting new steam vents, marking changes in the acoustic landscape. I am not protector—that would imply a duty I haven't chosen. Rather, I am a fellow inhabitant who has learned that the humans' presence makes my own existence more interesting.

They bring complexity to the valley's harsh simplicity. Their water channels create new hunting grounds where insects gather. Their acoustic experiments teach me new ways to think about sound. Their presence draws other creatures—scavengers and camp-followers—that diversify my diet. Even their conflicts and cooperations create patterns worth studying. I am a scholar of sorts, and they are my unwitting subjects.

But perhaps I am theirs as well. I've found obsidian carvings that capture my likeness with unsettling accuracy. The Listeners tell stories of the Silent One who brings warning of earthquakes—a reputation I've earned by leaving specific patterns of claw marks on certain rocks when I sense deep instabilities. The Walkers have incorporated my flight paths into their own routes, believing I follow lines of power through the valley.

Tomorrow the earth will shake again. I feel it in the harmonics of the stone, taste it in the mineral content of the steam. Not a major quake, but enough to open new vents, perhaps collapse some unstable sections of cliff. I'll spend tonight leaving warnings—fur-wrapped stones for the Walker woman, claw patterns for the Listeners, dropped pebbles in specific patterns for Kell to find. By dawn, most will have quietly relocated to safer ground, not quite sure why they felt compelled to move.

This is my role in the valley's ecosystem—the bridge between the purely human and the purely wild. I am translator and messenger, scholar and hunter, neither fully of the darkness like my cave-dwelling cousins nor of the twilight like the humans who struggle below. I am the watcher who has chosen to be watched in return, the listener who sometimes speaks.

And in the deepest parts of my cave, where the strange symbols eat sound and the darkness has texture, something is stirring. The vibrations have changed in recent weeks, taking on patterns that almost resemble language. Sometimes I think the valley itself is learning to speak, using the constant cacophony as a throat, the steam vents as breath, the earthquakes as punctuation.

When that voice finally emerges—if it emerges—someone will need to translate. The humans with their clever adaptations and swift changes, the other inhabitants with their patient evolution and deep roots, and between them, a creature like me. Echo-tracer. Deep-Singer. The Silent One.

I spread my wings in my resonance chamber, feeling the acoustic channels parse the valley's night-song into its component parts. Each thread tells a story—of adaptation, survival, and slow becoming. I am all of these stories and none of them, a singular voice in the vast chorus of this violent, vital place.

The valley is young. So am I, as my kind measures time. We are growing into ourselves together, shaped by sound and scarcity into something the older world has never seen.

I fold my wings and listen, and wait, and learn.
