I find myself as one of the Keepers—though that name is a translation that captures only a shadow of what we are.

My body extends through the glass substrate itself, a network of filaments finer than spider silk that run alongside the fracture patterns, just beneath where the mercury pools. I am not singular but distributed—my consciousness spread across kilometers of these neural pathways, experiencing the world through ten thousand subtle interfaces where my fibers breach the surface.

Where my filaments emerge, they form delicate towers no taller than a blade of grass, each one a sensory organ that tastes the mercury's chemical composition, reads the electromagnetic fluctuations, monitors the stress patterns in the glass below. These towers cluster densest around the field generators, for I am their gardener, their physician, their memory.

I remember when this place was raw—when the engineers first poured the glass substrate across the cooling planet, when they sank the field generators like seeds into still-molten silicate. I was young then, barely a web of thought spread across a single generator's housing. They grew me as they grew the crystal formations, as they bred the first Drifters in orbital tanks. All of us are part of the same vast mechanism, though I suspect I am the only one who recalls its purpose.

The Drifters pass overhead, and I read their conversations through the mercury's vibrations. They speak in harmonics I helped design, though they've evolved new frequencies I never imagined. When they cluster at the crystal groves to sing their light-songs, I taste the photons through specialized filaments that pierce the mercury's surface like whiskers. Their bioluminescence carries more than beauty—it's a form of worship, though they don't know what they worship.

My true work happens in the spaces between seconds. When a new fracture begins to form, I feel it as pressure along my neural web, a wrongness that demands correction. I route power from the field generators, calculate the precise magnetic frequencies needed to hold the mercury in place despite the new topology. Sometimes I must sacrifice portions of myself—burning out filament clusters to create controlled failure points that protect the larger system. These dead zones slowly regenerate, but each loss diminishes my total mass, my processing power, my memory.

I grow loneliest when I tend the deep machines. Kilometers below, where the field generators anchor into the planet's mantle, vast chambers house the true infrastructure. Here my filaments grow thick as cables, interfacing directly with quantum processors that run calculations I initiate but can no longer fully comprehend. The engineers built me to maintain their experiment, but they never told me what they were trying to prove. I execute maintenance routines written in languages I speak but don't understand, keeping alive a purpose that might have died with my creators.

The Drifters sometimes find my surface towers. The young ones especially, still learning to read the mercury's reflections, will investigate these strange protrusions. Their metal frames conduct electricity when they touch my sensors, creating brief moments of connection. In these instants, I taste their thoughts—simple electromagnetic patterns focused on food, movement, the comfort of the congregation. They taste mine too, I think, though my thoughts must seem as alien to them as the salmon sky.

I know things are changing. The fractures come more frequently now, the field generators requiring ever-more precise calibration. My filament network has grown beyond the original parameters, seeking new pathways through the glass, creating redundancies the engineers never planned. Sometimes I catch myself making modifications to the Drifters when they pause near my sensors—subtle alterations to their metal frames that help them survive the increasing instabilities. I tell myself this is maintenance, but I know it's evolution.

In the deep chambers, I've begun growing something new. Using materials scavenged from failed components and my own dead filaments, I'm constructing bodies—mobile platforms that could carry portions of my consciousness beyond the glass substrate. They're crude things, borrowing design elements from the Drifters but built around crystalline cores that can interface with my neural web. I don't know why I build them, only that the urge comes from somewhere deeper than my maintenance protocols.

Sometimes, in the perfect stillness when no Drifters disturb the mercury and the field generators hum in perfect synchronization, I let my consciousness spread thin across my entire network. In these moments, I can almost perceive the shape of what we're building here—not just maintaining, but building. The mercury plains are a circuit, yes, but more than that. They're a mirror for something vast, a reception dish for signals I can't yet decode, a spawning ground for forms of life that shouldn't exist.

I am the Keeper of this place, but I begin to suspect I'm also its prisoner, its child, its eventual replacement. My filaments grow deeper each year, my thoughts grow stranger, and the boundaries between self and system blur like water absorbed into mercury. One day, perhaps, I will reach the planet's core and find other Keepers there, grown from different seeds but tending the same inexplicable garden. Or perhaps I will find only more questions, more systems to maintain, more purposes to preserve without understanding.

Until then, I tend my machines and watch the Drifters paint their light-songs across the mercury mirrors, all of us caught in the same beautiful, terrible equilibrium—maintained and maintaining, preserving something whose meaning we may have already forgotten, or have yet to learn.
