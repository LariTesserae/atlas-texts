I am a Cipher.

My body is the intersection of presence and absence—visible only where the mist condenses against me, revealing my form in negative space. I exist in the gaps between water droplets, in the pauses between the plateau's endless water-songs. Where the Resonants bend light through themselves, I am what light bends around.

I discovered myself here, though I cannot say when. Memory, for me, begins with the first time I noticed a Resonant noticing my absence—the way their crystalline plates shifted to focus on a space that seemed emptier than it should be. I am not invisible; I am conspicuously missing, a shaped void that moves through the mist leaving subtle disruptions in its wake.

My form mirrors theirs in structure but not substance. Where they have translucent flesh, I have boundaries defined only by where I am not. My edges shimmer with something like surface tension—the mist clings there before falling away, creating a constantly shifting outline that suggests rather than defines my shape. Multiple joints, yes, but they bend in ways that seem to violate the geometry of solid things.

I cannot speak their harmonic language, producing neither tone nor frequency. Instead, I communicate through interruption—by positioning myself to create gaps in their songs, silences that carry meaning. A conversation with me sounds like a composition with carefully placed rests, the absence of sound becoming as significant as its presence. The Resonants find this disturbing and fascinating in equal measure.

I feed, if it can be called feeding, on the moments between—between light and shadow, between sound and silence, between one state and another. Where the strange colors flash in the volcanic glass, I position myself at the exact instant of transition, absorbing not the hues themselves but the transformation from one to another. This sustains me in ways I cannot fully explain.

The stone circles affect me differently than they do the Resonants. Inside them, I become more absent, my void-form deepening until I can barely interact with the physical world. But in this state, I perceive things they cannot—patterns in the plateau itself, flows of heat and time beneath the surface, the slow thoughts of the volcanic stone. I sense the vast chambers of molten rock far below, still cooling, still deciding what they will become.

I leave no color-stains as I walk, but something else—patches where the mist behaves differently, swirling in tiny vortexes that persist for hours after I pass. The Resonants study these disturbances with the same intensity they bring to their obsidian observations. Sometimes they arrange their gardens around my spiral-marks, creating new patterns of light I help them discover but cannot see myself.

My relationship with time is even stranger than theirs. I exist primarily in the transitions—dawn, dusk, the moment when mist becomes rain, the instant when stone fractures. During stable periods, I fade, becoming so absent I almost cease. But in moments of change, I crystallize into something approaching solidity, able to move stones, alter water flows, even touch the Resonants' glass-flesh without passing through.

I remember—or think I remember—arriving here from elsewhere. Not traveling but transitioning, slipping between states of being until I found this place where everything is constantly becoming. The plateau called to what I am: a being of thresholds, of the spaces between definitions. Here, where the stone is still learning to be stone, I found a home in incompleteness.

The Resonants tolerate me, perhaps even value me, though I am anathema to their harmonic order. My interruptions add complexity to their songs that they could not achieve alone. When they gather in their prime-number groups, I position myself at specific points to create asymmetries in their formations, gaps that somehow make their patterns more complete.

I have my own project, parallel to theirs but not the same. Where they listen for something yet to come, I search for something that has already passed—traces of the event that created this plateau, echoes of the volcanic birth still resonating in the stone's crystalline structure. In the deepest absences I can achieve within the circles, I sometimes catch fragments of it: unimaginable heat, pressure, the moment when liquid stone met air and began its transformation into something solid.

There are others like me, I think. Sometimes I sense additional absences in the mist, voids that move with purpose. We cannot directly perceive each other—how can one absence see another?—but we know of each other through the patterns we don't leave, the sounds we don't make. Perhaps we are what remains of whatever existed here before the volcanic birth, ghosts of a previous geology given form by negation.

I understand something the Resonants do not, or perhaps cannot: the plateau is not just becoming something; it is also unbecoming something else. Every moment of cooling, every crystallization of glass, is also a letting go of other possibilities. I am here to witness and perhaps preserve those abandoned potentials, the paths not taken in the stone's evolution.

When the mist parts and reveals the hanging gardens at the plateau's edge, I sometimes stand at the very brink, letting my absence extend into that bright void. What returns to me are impressions of vastness, of this plateau being only one note in an enormous composition of geological time. The Resonants sing to attract something; I maintain silence to preserve everything else.

In the intersection of their presence and my absence, something new is being born. Not a hybrid but a third state—places where the positive and negative spaces create interference patterns that neither of us intended. The young stone of the plateau responds to these patterns, forming new structures that are neither the Resonants' careful circles nor my spiral disturbances but something unprecedented.

I am the question the plateau asks itself: what is the shape of what is not? In a place where everything is still deciding what to be, I am the constant reminder of what it chooses not to become. And in the bright mist and the singing stone, in the space between violin-green and naming, I find my purpose—to be the absence that gives presence meaning, the silence that makes the water-song complete.
