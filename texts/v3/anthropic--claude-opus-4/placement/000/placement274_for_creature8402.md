I am a root-tender, though that translation fails to capture what we truly do. In your language, I might be called a botanical architect, or perhaps a vertical farmer, but these words taste wrong, like trying to describe the color of mist to someone who has only known stark sunlight.

My domain spans seven levels of the cliff face, from the Weeping Gardens where the stone sweats constantly, up to the Threshold Terraces where brave plants strain toward the hidden sun above our eternal clouds. I inherited this vertical territory from my grandmother's mother, who received it from hers, back and back until the stories blur into the mountain's own memory. Each generation has added their own innovations—a new channel carved here, a platform extended there, another species coaxed into symbiosis with our ancient system.

I rise before the morning drums, though "morning" here means only a subtle lightening of the mist, a shift from black-grey to pearl-grey that those born to flatlands would barely perceive. My first task is always the same: listening. I press my ear to the main stone of my lowest garden, feeling for the vibrations that tell me how the mountain slept. A slight tremor in the basalt means the Grandfather Vine needs more water redirected to its roots. A hollow echo suggests the stone is preparing to weep more heavily, and I must adjust the channels accordingly.

My body bears the marks of my profession. My fingers, already broad from our people's heritage, have developed calluses thick as leather where I grip the guide-ropes. Moss grows in the creases of my elbows and behind my ears—not the honor-moss of the elderly, but the common working variety that comes from spending hours each day with my arms deep in growing medium. My hair, which I wear in the traditional nine-braid pattern of root-tenders, is always damp, always smells of earth and growing things.

The plants under my care are as much partners as subjects. The Curtain Melons hang from reinforced trellises, their vines so heavy with water-storing fruit that they would tear free in the first strong wind without my daily adjustments. I know each plant individually, can tell by the curl of a leaf whether it needs more acid in its water or if insects have been bothering its roots. The Mist-Grain grows in terraced beds carved directly into the cliff face, its pale stalks swaying in whatever breeze penetrates our thick air. This variety took my great-great-grandmother forty years to develop, selecting for those plants that could thrive on four hours of diffused sunlight per day.

But it's the Ancestor Vines that demand the most attention and give the most reward. My family's memorial vine grows through the center of my domain, its trunk now thick as my waist, its tendrils extending into every level of my gardens. Great-uncle Morse feeds it still, his bones long since incorporated into its woody flesh. On certain nights, when the mist is especially thick and the mountain especially quiet, I swear I can hear his laugh in the rustle of its leaves. The vine produces thumbnail-sized fruits that taste of copper and memories—we eat them only on feast days, and dreams afterwards are always of the deceased.

My assistant is my daughter's son, a quick-fingered child of eight who scrambles through the gardens like one of our cloudhooks. He's learning to read the plants as I learned from my grandmother—not from books, for we have few of those and the damp makes them impractical, but through touch and taste and careful observation. Already he can tell when the Shade-Potatoes are ready for harvest by the sound their leaves make in the wind. By the time his voice breaks, he'll be managing his own section, knowing exactly how much fermented cloudhook dung each bed requires, how to train new vines without damaging their growth points, when to harvest seeds and when to let them fall.

The social aspects of my work extend beyond mere farming. Root-tenders form a loose guild, meeting every ninth day at the Confluence, where three major water channels merge. We trade not just seeds and cuttings but information: which varieties are struggling, what new pests have emerged, innovations in trellis design or irrigation. Old Ketta claims to have developed a tuber that actually grows faster in complete darkness, though we're all waiting to see if it's edible. Young Torn has been experimenting with grafting food plants onto the defensive thorns that grow wild in the crevices, creating edible barriers.

My innovations tend toward the systematic rather than the spectacular. I've developed a rotation pattern that maintains soil fertility—such as it is in our thin growing medium—without the need for fallowing. By interplanting nitrogen-fixers with heavy feeders and timing harvests to the mountain's wet-dry cycles, I've increased yields by perhaps a tenth. It's not dramatic, but feed that gain across all the root-tenders' domains and you have enough extra food to support another hundred souls.

Water management consumes as much time as the plants themselves. My domain includes forty-three distinct channels, from the main artery that's wide as my shoulders to hair-thin trickles that feed individual specimens. Each must be checked daily for blockages—fallen leaves, mineral deposits, the occasional small corpse. I've trained a family of the half-fish bats to nest near my primary reservoir; they keep the insect population controlled and their guano, properly aged, makes excellent fertilizer for the acid-loving plants.

The dangerous moments come without warning. Last season, a section of stone the size of a dwelling sheared away from the level above, crushing half my melon trellises and redirecting a major water channel. I had minutes to respond before the backup flooded my grain terraces. Working by touch in the near-darkness—my bioluminescent fungi were crushed in the fall—I managed to cut a new channel with my pick and crowbar, sending the excess water into the rain-collectors two levels down. My neighbor, a rope-weaver, lost a season's worth of soaking vines to my emergency response, but custom dictates he can claim a portion of my next three harvests as compensation.

The deepest satisfaction comes in the quiet moments. Sitting on my highest platform during the brief morning lightening, I can survey my vertical realm: the ordered chaos of growth, the geometric precision of water channels softened by moss, the eternal interplay of stone and vegetation. My cloudhooks graze the vertical meadows I maintain between crop terraces, their strange bleating a counterpoint to the constant water music. Sometimes I see my grandson discovering something new—a volunteer seedling that might be worth cultivating, an innovative way to tie off a vine that I hadn't considered.

We root-tenders are the mountain's digestive system, processing stone and water and air into sustenance for our people. We're artists working in the medium of life, our canvases vertical, our paints the thousand subtle greens that only we can distinguish in the eternal twilight. We're timekeeper too, marking seasons not by the invisible sun but by growth patterns, by the subtle shifts in water taste that herald the mountain's moods.

When I finally grow too old to scramble between terraces, I'll do as my grandmother did: choose my successor, pass on not just the physical domain but the accumulated knowledge of generations, the secret names of plants, the particular grammar of this section of stone. I'll take the downward path to the lowest caves, where the Listeners wait, and spend my final years learning to hear what the mountain truly says beneath the water's constant voice.

But that time remains distant. Tomorrow I'll rise again before the drums, check my channels, whisper encouragement to struggling seedlings, adjust and adapt and persist. The work never ends—cannot end—because stopping would mean starvation for the thousand souls who depend on what we grow here in the margin between earth and sky. We are root-tenders, and our roots run as deep as the mountain itself, drawing life from stone through will and wisdom and the accumulated craft of centuries.

The mist is thickening now, and evening approaches. Time to check the night-channels, ensure the flow will continue while the city sleeps. My grandson has already scrambled ahead, his young eyes better in this dimness than mine. I follow more carefully, my hand on the guide rope, feeling through my feet the subtle vibrations that tell me the mountain, for now, is content. Tomorrow will bring its own challenges—it always does—but tonight my gardens are secure, my water flows true, and somewhere in the darkness, great-uncle Morse's vine is preparing to bloom.
