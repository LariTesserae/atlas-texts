In the Vertical Nurseries of Kaelora, where mist pools thick between tiers and the mountain's infant stone weeps its mineral milk, I am a Seedkeeper—tender of the impossible gardens that should not exist at these heights, in this industrial maze, yet flourish with disturbing vigor.

My quarters occupy a forgotten gap between buildings on the fourth tier, where a structural miscalculation decades ago created a wedge-shaped void too narrow for proper construction. Previous residents tried to seal it, bridge it, claim it for storage. But the mountain kept breathing through this crack, pushing warm, mineral-laden air up from its gestating depths. When I arrived fifteen years ago, carrying nothing but a satchel of seeds collected from the world's edges and a bone-deep exhaustion with horizontal cities, I recognized the space for what it was: a birth canal.

Now my home grows through seven levels of accumulated platforms, ladders, and bridges I've built myself from salvaged cable and discarded grinding wheels. The walls bloom with specimens that exist nowhere else—hybrids born from the marriage of industrial runoff and volcanic breath. Here, a vine that metabolizes copper grows in spirals following the path of old plumbing, its leaves edged in verdigris. There, moss that glows not with simple bioluminescence but with actual warmth, generating enough heat to keep my smallest seedlings alive through the cold snaps when mist turns to ice.

I wake each day to the taste of tomorrow's weather on my tongue. The gift—or curse—came slowly, developed through years of breathing the mountain's exhalations. I can sense the chemical signatures of emerging stone before the engineers' instruments register any change. Sulfur tingles mean new vents forming. A metallic sweetness warns of shifting mineral veins. The green taste of chlorophyll-in-distress tells me which gardens will need reinforcement before the next geological adjustment.

My work begins before dawn, when the industrial lights create false constellations and the real stars fade behind rising steam. I move through my vertical garden with the practiced efficiency of a spider, never needing to look where my hands will find the next hold. My body has reshaped itself for this life—shoulders broader from climbing, fingers sensitive enough to feel a root's distress through soil, legs that lock automatically when I need both hands free for delicate work.

The first task is always the water harvest. I maintain seventeen different condensation systems, each positioned to catch moisture from specific atmospheric conditions. The copper coils that wrap around thermal vents must be cleaned of mineral deposits. The mesh screens that filter mist need checking for tears. The ceramic vessels positioned beneath dripping stone require precise placement to catch every drop of the mountain's milk—that mineral-rich secretion that makes my plants grow with such unsettling enthusiasm.

I've learned to read my garden's moods like other Calibrators read structural stress. This morning, the Grief Vine (my name for it—no botanist would recognize its twisted DNA) has grown three feet overnight, its tendrils reaching toward a spot on the eastern wall where, I suspect, new warm stone will emerge within days. The Copper-Eaters show signs of feast—their leaf edges sharp with metallic excess—which means somewhere in the building network above, a pipe has developed a slow leak that's trickling down through infrastructure to pool in my domain.

My specimens occupy every possible niche in the vertical space. Heat-lovers cluster near steam vents. Shade-dwellers lurk beneath platforms. The carnivorous varieties—evolved to supplement mineral-poor soil with insect protein—hang in baskets at junction points where small flies gather. The climbers follow paths I've trained them to over years, reinforcing my ramshackle architecture with their woody stems. Some have grown so thick they've become structural elements themselves, bearing weight that would make an engineer weep with either horror or admiration.

But the heart of my collection lives in the Womb—a natural cave formation I discovered behind what appeared to be solid wall. The mountain had hollowed out a perfect spherical chamber, walls lined with crystals that catch and amplify the faintest light. Here I nurture the specimens that shouldn't exist: plants that seem to absorb the mountain's geological uncertainty and express it through impossible growth patterns.

The Star-Throat Orchid blooms only when the barometric pressure drops below a certain threshold, its petals opening to reveal an interior that looks unsettlingly like distant nebulae. The Tempo Ferns grow in time with the grinding mills, their fronds unfurling and recurling in perfect synchronization with industrial rhythms. Most disturbing is the Colony Crown—what began as a simple succulent has become something between plant and fungus, individual specimens connecting through root networks that pulse with electrical signals I can measure but not understand.

By midday, when the mist burns off and the city reveals its vertical bones, I make my rounds through the public gardens. The official botanists tolerate me—barely. My methods disturb them, my results unsettle them more. But they cannot deny that my techniques work. When the vine-reinforced building in Sector Seven survived a massive stone shift that should have sheared it from the cliff, they had to acknowledge what I'd been telling them for years: the plants here want to collaborate with our architecture. We just need to learn their language.

I carry cuttings and seedlings in a leather satchel worn soft with use, its pockets designed for different moisture levels and root requirements. In the market square, I trade specimens for supplies—a cutting of Heat Moss for copper piping, seeds that germinate only in industrial runoff for bottles of condensate from the seventh tier. The water sellers know me well, setting aside samples from unusual sources: the first drops after a new vent opens, condensation from the underside of bridges during temperature inversions, the strange sweet water that sometimes weeps from certain stones during the full moon.

Children follow me sometimes, fascinated by the green chaos that clings to my clothes—spores and seeds and tiny volunteers that sprout from my very person given half a chance. I teach them to recognize the warning signs: which plants indicate unstable stone, which mosses mean too much carbon monoxide from the mills, which flowers bloom just before a major geological event. They absorb this knowledge with the frightening quickness of minds adapted to three-dimensional thinking.

Afternoons I spend in documentation, though my methods would horrify academic botanists. I write directly on the walls of my home, creating a living journal that grows and changes with my garden. Notes about growth rates intertwine with philosophical observations about the nature of adaptation. Sketches of new specimens blur into diagrams of the mountain's theoretical future geology. Everything is connected—must be connected—in ways that linear notebooks could never capture.

The engineers consult me, reluctantly. They've learned that my plants often know things before their instruments do. When the Gravity Vine began growing sideways last year, spiraling around buildings in a pattern that made no sense, I warned them to check their calculations. Sure enough, the mountain was preparing to shift its load, redistributing weight in ways their models hadn't predicted. The vine knew, responding to magnetic or gravitational signals beyond our measurement but not beyond chlorophyll's ancient wisdom.

Evening brings its own tasks. As temperature drops and the bioluminescent mosses begin their cold fire, I harvest. Not the plants themselves—I learned early that these specimens resist traditional exploitation. Instead, I collect their gifts: seeds that develop only in darkness, oils that condense on leaves during the precise moment when day-heat meets night-cold, spores that release exclusively during certain humidity ranges. These materials form the basis of my real work.

In my cluttered laboratory—a generous term for the series of shelves and workbenches crammed between growing beds—I process these offerings. Some become medicines that help newcomers adapt to the vertical life, easing the constant vertigo and strengthening grips. Others form the growth medium for the next generation of architectural plants, specimens designed to thrive in specific industrial conditions. My greatest success remains the Filter Ferns, which now grow in most mill facilities, their specialized fronds extracting toxic particles from the air while thriving on the very pollutants that would kill normal plants.

But the work I don't share, the experiments that happen in the deepest part of night, those focus on communication. I've become convinced that the mountain itself is conscious—not in any human sense, but aware nonetheless. The plants serve as its neural network, transmitting information through chemical signals, electrical pulses, root-whispers that travel faster than any human messenger. I'm trying to learn this language, to decode what the mountain dreams as it grows.

Night sounds different in my vertical garden. Beyond the usual industrial hum and water-whisper, there's a subtle chorus of growth—cells dividing, roots extending, stems reaching toward tomorrow's light. I've trained myself to sleep lightly, waking instantly if the pitch changes. A shift in harmonics might mean a plant in distress, a new specimen beginning its bloom cycle, or sometimes, thrillingly, the mountain itself adjusting its breathing.

I dream in green now—not the simple green of lowland fields but the complex, industrial-tinted green of Kaelora. Verdigris dreams where copper-eating vines teach me their secrets. Luminescent dreams where moss-light reveals hidden patterns in the mountain's growth. Dreams where I photosynthesize, pulling nutrients from mist and minerals, growing roots that anchor not in soil but in possibility itself.

The other Calibrators think me half-mad, probably more. They see my gardens as beautiful curiosities, useful for their predictions and air filtration but fundamentally separate from the real work of keeping the city stable. They don't understand that the plants aren't growing in the city—they're growing the city, guiding its evolution, teaching stone and steel how to become something new.

My greatest fear isn't that my gardens will fail—life here is too vigorous, too eager for that. Rather, I fear success. What happens when the plants no longer need a human intermediary? When they've learned enough about architecture to build their own structures? When the mountain's neural network becomes complex enough to make decisions we might not agree with?

But these are thoughts for the deep night, when even the mills pause their grinding and the city holds its breath. For now, I continue my work: one seed at a time, one cutting at a time, one impossible bloom at a time. Teaching plants to love metal. Teaching stone to support green life. Teaching myself to think in geological time while living in industrial minutes.

Tomorrow the engineers will announce where they predict the next major shift. I already know—the Grief Vine told me three days ago, growing toward a section of wall that will soon crack open to reveal new chambers, new possibilities. I've prepared cuttings that thrive in fresh stone environments, specimens ready to colonize the mountain's newest offerings before the dust even settles.

This is my purpose, my obsession, my love: to midwife the impossible gardens of Kaelora, to stand at the intersection of industry and biology, translation and transformation. To be the bridge between the city's human ambitions and the mountain's geological dreams, written in the ancient alphabet of chlorophyll and stone.

In my vertical world, suspended between earth and sky, between solid and possible, I tend gardens that shouldn't exist. And in tending them, I become something that shouldn't exist either—a person grown part plant, thinking in root-time, breathing mineral air, climbing toward a future that only the mountain knows.

The mist rises. The stone weeps. The gardens grow.

And I grow with them, reaching always upward, into the uncertain sky.
