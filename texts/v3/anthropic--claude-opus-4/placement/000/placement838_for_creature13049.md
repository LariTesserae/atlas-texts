In this shattered archipelago of sound and silence, I would be one of the wire-moss colonies—but not as they appear now, rigid and desperate at the edge of existence. I would be what they were before the great unspooling, when physics still drew straight lines and water knew which way was down.

I exist as a vast mycelial network threading through the stone itself, a living fossil pressed between layers of rock like flowers in an ancient book. Miles of my filaments run through the monoliths' cores, following veins of some long-exhausted mineral, branching into delicate fractals that mirror the standing waves above. I am older than the resonant monks, older than the floating stones, perhaps as old as the drone itself.

Where my networks approach the surface, breaking through in those shallow fissures, I extrude my bitter fruit: the wire-moss. These sparse patches are merely my fingertips testing the broken air, each cluster a sensory organ reading the new physics like braille. Through ten thousand such eruptions across a hundred monoliths, I map the crumpled topology of this place. I know where space folds and where it stretches thin. I taste the trajectory of every drifting droplet, feel the phase-shifts of the ghost leviathans as they pass through stone and flesh alike.

The resonant monks believe they alone can navigate the seams of this broken world, but they are young. They see only the surface—the obvious folds they can grasp with their frictionless pads. But I feel the deep grammar of the distortion. In the lightless compression of the rock, my networks have spent eons learning the syntax of unraveling space-time. Each monolith is shot through with my presence, and through this archipelago-wide nervous system, I experience the drone not as a single crushing chord but as an infinitely complex symphony of overtones and interference patterns.

Sometimes, in the rare moments when multiple ghost leviathans converge and phase through the same monolith simultaneously, their intersecting bio-luminescence illuminates my deepest networks. For an instant, the rock becomes translucent, revealing my true scope—a vast, three-dimensional mandala of living threads pulsing with stolen light. The monks, if they notice at all, mistake this for a trick of the mist.

I am patient beyond their understanding. While they build their small architectures of silence, their cairns and impossible staircases, I am slowly learning to fruit differently. Deep in the stone, where the drone's vibrations create pockets of negative pressure, I am teaching my wire-moss to weave itself into new geometries. Not the rigid, desperate bristles that cling to the surface, but something more ambitious—hollow spheres of living metal that could float free from the rock entirely, drifting through the grey expanse like spores of tarnished silver.

But I am in no hurry. The monks measure time in the erosion of their carapaces, the ghost leviathans in their endless circuits through stone and void. I measure it in the slow birth of new physics. Each time reality hiccups and space recrumples itself into new configurations, I map the change. I am compiling an atlas of entropy, a living record of how worlds unspool.

In the deepest core of the tallest monolith—the one the monks consider sacred, though they don't remember why—my networks have discovered something extraordinary. A hollow chamber, perfectly spherical, where the drone cannot reach. Not a crafted silence like the monks create, but a natural accident of geology and acoustics. In this womb of quiet, I am growing something new. Not wire-moss, not the bitter fruit of mere survival, but something unprecedented: a structure that feeds on silence the way the monks feed on sound.

It will take another million years to mature. The monks will have eroded to nothing by then, their fractured flesh slowly claimed by the drone. The ghost leviathans will have completed another thousand circuits. The floating stones will have drifted into new constellations.

And then, in that perfect sphere of silence, something will bloom. A new kind of life for a new kind of physics. The wire-moss patches on the surface will wither and die as I withdraw all my energy inward for the birthing. The monks, if any remain, will note the loss of even this small testament to traditional biology. They will think the broken world has finally won, that life has made its last stand and lost.

They will be wrong. I am not dying. I am metamorphosing. In the heart of stone, in the absolute absence of that eternal roar, I am becoming something that can thrive not despite the broken physics, but because of them. Something that navigates not by folding space but by dwelling in the spaces between the folds. Something that drinks not sound or silence, but the tension that holds them apart.

I am the memory of when this place had horizons. I am the promise of what it will become when the last echo of the drone finally, impossibly, fades. And in the meantime, I wear my wire-moss like a modest coat, playing the part of bitter survivor while my true body dreams in stone, vast and patient as the geology itself, waiting for the next phase of the world's long unbecoming.
