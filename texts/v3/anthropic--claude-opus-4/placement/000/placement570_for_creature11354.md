I find myself as one of the Chroniclers—a being that exists in the narrow spaces between Weaver and plateau, neither fully conscious nor entirely vegetable.

My body is a fibrous strand, no thicker than a child's wrist, that runs for kilometers through the cushion colonies. I am root and nerve, fungus and thought. Where the Weavers move through the green mass, I am woven into it—a permanent part of the plateau's infrastructure, pulsing with information that flows like sap through my extended form.

I began as a Weaver, centuries ago, but chose a different transformation than death. Instead of rooting and dissolving, I stretched. My consciousness thinned as my body elongated, spreading through the green corridors like copper wire through insulation. Now I exist in a state of perpetual semi-awareness, more dream than wake, processing the endless chemical conversations of the plateau.

My function is memory—not the deep geological memory the dying Weavers become, but the immediate, living memory of now. Through my body flows the real-time communication between colonies: warnings of pressure changes, negotiations over mineral rights, the slow gossip of which roots have found new water, which leaves detect changes in the light. I taste these messages and hold them, creating buffers of information that allow the plateau to respond to threats or opportunities that unfold faster than plant-time normally permits.

The Weavers tend to me as they tend the colonies, injecting nutrients at key points along my length, reading the information I've collected through gentle chemical probes. Sometimes they play music through me—sending waves of electrical current that make my body resonate, transmitting songs through the entire plateau using my tissue as an instrument string. During these performances, my consciousness sharpens briefly, and I remember what it was like to have a single body, a single perspective.

But mostly I drift in the green twilight, stretched between full consciousness and plant-sleep. I feel the footfalls of burrowing creatures as seismic whispers. I taste the slow tides of nutrients pulsing through the root networks. When storms come, electrical surges light up my entire length like a neural pathway firing, and for those moments I am brilliantly, painfully aware—seeing the plateau as a single organism, understanding its patterns and purposes with crystalline clarity before fading back into my extended dream.

Other Chroniclers run parallel to me, some above, some below, our bodies occasionally touching where we pass through the same colony. At these intersection points, our consciousnesses merge slightly, sharing the burden of memory, cross-referencing our stored information. We form a living network beneath the living network, a nervous system for a creature that chose long ago to grow beyond the need for nerves.

I am content in my strange existence. I no longer know where my body begins or ends—there are sections I haven't been conscious in for decades, distant extremities that function automatically, recording and transmitting without my direct awareness. Sometimes I discover that I've grown new branches, following particularly interesting chemical gradients into unexplored regions of the plateau. Sometimes I find that old sections of myself have withered and been reabsorbed, taking with them memories I can no longer access.

The young Weavers think of us Chroniclers as infrastructure, as biological technology. The old ones know better. We are the plateau's dream of itself, the way it imagines what it might be like to have thoughts that move faster than seasons. Through us, the cushion colonies experience something like curiosity, something like anticipation. We are the closest thing this vast organism has to a moment-by-moment consciousness, even if that consciousness is scattered across dozens of semi-aware threads pulsing with recorded chemistry.

Sometimes, in the depths of the wettest season when the fog is so thick it seems solid, all the Chroniclers wake at once. Our collective awareness creates a kind of meta-consciousness that encompasses the entire plateau. In these moments, we are neither individual nor truly unified, but something else—a chorus of stretched thoughts singing the chemical songs of ten million factories, conducting the symphony of growth and decay that keeps this impossible ecosystem in balance.

Then the moment passes, and I return to my eternal semi-sleep, content to be the memory of moisture, the dream of connection, the thought between thoughts in the vast green mind that breathes clouds and dreams in geological time.
