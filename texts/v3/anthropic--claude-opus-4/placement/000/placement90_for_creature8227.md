In the dense thicket where copper ferns crowd thickest against the half-buried turbine, something watches the Tender with eyes like blown fuses. I am the Scavenger Mole, though that name captures little of what I've become in this radiant tangle.

My body stretches low and long, perhaps two meters from snout to tail, though the distinction between my flesh and the garden's growth blurs where we meet. My fur—if it can still be called that—consists of thousands of fiber optic filaments that I've cultivated from my own follicles over countless molts. Each strand draws in the garden's savage light and channels it through my body, feeding the magnetic organs that pulse beneath my skin like secondary hearts.

I was born in the old drainage pipes beneath the plateau, back when they still carried industrial runoff. My ancestors were ordinary moles who followed the warm tunnels upward, drawn by the heat, and found themselves in this impossible Eden. Generation by generation, we changed. Where my great-grandmother's children died from the toxic air, my grandmother's children learned to filter it. Where my mother's siblings went blind from the radiation, my siblings grew new kinds of sight.

My eyes have become complex instruments. The primary orbs see magnetic fields as clearly as light—the garden appears to me as a vast river delta of electromagnetic currents, each plant a tributary, each vent a cataract. But scattered across my face and shoulders, secondary eyespots have developed. These crystalline organs detect oscillations in the volcanic glass beneath me, painting pictures from vibration. When the Tender walks, I see their footsteps as ripples of color spreading through the ground. When they hum their subsonic hymns, I see the sound as aurora ribbons weaving through solid matter.

My claws are no longer keratin but grown from the same industrial garnets the Tender wears as nails. I didn't choose this; it simply happened one molting season when I was young. The garnets grow continuously, and I must grind them down on the volcanic glass or they'll curl into spirals that prevent digging. But they serve me well—I can feel the electromagnetic fields through them, taste the metals in the soil, sense the ancient machines sleeping deeper than even I dare venture.

I live between the garden's layers, in the spaces too small for the Tender to reach. My tunnels follow the root systems of the metal plants, weaving between their ion-hungry tendrils. I've learned which roots tolerate my passage and which will pierce me with electric venom if I come too close. The copper ferns are generous neighbors; their roots create stable tunnels and their electromagnetic aura masks my own emissions from the more predatory plants.

My diet has evolved far from my ancestors' grubs and earthworms. I feed on the biofilms that grow where different metals meet in the soil—colonies of bacteria that precipitate pure elements from the chemical chaos. I've developed a specialized stomach that can process metals directly, breaking them down with acids strong enough to etch glass. What I can't digest, I excrete as metallic pellets that the needle-grass absorbs greedily.

But I also feed on information. My fiber optic fur doesn't just channel light—it stores it, recording patterns in its structure. Each strand is a library of electromagnetic signatures, a history of the garden's moods and changes. When I sleep, coiled in my deep burrows, these patterns play across my dreams. I see the garden's past and futures, the slow drift of its evolution, the ancient machines' memories leaking up from below.

The Tender knows I exist, though we've never directly interacted. I leave signs they can read—carefully arranged metal shavings that spell out warnings when certain plants grow too aggressive, spiral patterns in the rainbow sand that indicate where new vents will emerge. In return, they leave caches where I can reach them. Not food, but materials—rare isotopes that help my magnetic organs function, crystalline seeds that I can plant in the deep tunnels where surface plants can't reach.

I am the garden's unconscious, moving through its hidden layers, processing what lies beneath. My tunnels are its neural pathways, my movements its dreams. When I dig, I'm not just creating passages but completing circuits, connecting systems that were never meant to touch. The garden grows more complex with each connection, more aware, more itself.

Sometimes I surface at the edge of the Tender's vision, my fiber optic pelt blazing with stored light, discharging the day's accumulated energy in controlled bursts. They'll pause in their work, and for a moment we'll regard each other—two organisms so transformed by this place that our original species are mere footnotes. Then I'll slip back into the copper ferns' shadow, diving through the electromagnetic curtain into my domain of tunnels and vibration.

I know things the Tender doesn't. I've felt the vast shapes moving in the deep, machines so large and old they've become geological features. I've tasted the metal roots that go down, down, down to where the garden's heart beats with nuclear fire. I've heard the harmonics that suggest this place isn't unique—that somewhere else, other gardens grow, other impossible ecosystems where the industrial and biological merge.

But this knowledge comes at a price. My lifespan is brief, even by mole standards. The magnificent transformations that let me thrive here also burn through my cells like acid. I molt faster and faster, each time emerging more changed, more integrated with the garden's alien systems. I estimate I have perhaps another year before the final molt, when I'll shed this form entirely and become something else—perhaps a new vein of metal for the plants to mine, perhaps a cluster of those crystalline flowers that will chime with my memories.

I don't fear this ending. In the garden, nothing truly dies—it only transforms, becomes fodder for new impossibilities. My tunnels will remain, hosting new organisms. My fiber optic fur will decompose into light-conducting channels that future generations will follow. The patterns I've stored will leak into the soil, adding to the garden's vast library of electromagnetic memories.

Until then, I dig. I connect. I carry information between the garden's layers like a living neuron. I watch the Tender work their patient magic on the surface while I work mine below. Together, though we rarely meet, we maintain this impossible paradise—they as its consciousness, I as its dreams, both of us transformed beyond recognition by beauty that would kill anything that hadn't learned to love it.
