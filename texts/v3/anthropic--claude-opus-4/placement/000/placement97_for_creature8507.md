I would find myself as one of the **Moth Shepherds**.

We came to the drowning city following our charges—the sound-drinking moths that grow fat on decibels and shed scales like static. Unlike the Siphons, we retain our human shape, more or less. Our transformations are subtler: eardrums evolved into complex chambers that can parse the city's acoustic spectrum, fingernails grown long and hollow to produce precise frequencies, skin that shifts its texture to dampen or amplify vibration as needed.

We live in the threshold spaces where sound and silence meet. Our dwellings are carved into the dead zones—pockets of acoustic cancellation where opposing sound waves create nodes of relative quiet. These chambers appear empty from outside, just hollows in the crystalline walls, but step through the interference pattern and you find our workshops: walls lined with moth cocoons in various stages of development, delicate instruments for measuring sonic pressure, charts mapping the city's ever-shifting acoustic topology.

Our moths are not quite tame, not quite wild. We guide rather than control them, using tuning forks struck from the city's own stone to lead them through safe passages in the roar. Each shepherd develops a unique sonic signature—mine is a low thrumming that makes water droplets dance on surfaces, a frequency the moths find soothing. When I walk the feeding routes, they follow in undulating clouds, their wings beating in harmonics that create pockets of altered sound around us.

The work is delicate. Feed them too much and they grow beyond manageable size, their wings generating countersounds that can shatter stone. Too little and they wither, their scales losing the luster that makes them valuable. We harvest these scales carefully, during molting seasons when the moths gather at the acoustic nodes. The scales have properties—crushed and mixed with water from specific streams, they create pigments that shift color with sound. Woven into fabric, they provide limited protection from the city's more damaging frequencies. Burned, they produce smoke that reveals the invisible currents of sound flowing through air.

We trade with whoever still trades in this place. The Siphons occasionally surface long enough to exchange things pulled from deep streams—stones that hum with stored sound, water from pools that remember what they've reflected. Other shepherds, when our paths cross at the feeding grounds. The rare visitors who manage to find the city and survive long enough to need what we offer. Currency means nothing here; we deal in frequencies, in moments of silence, in maps drawn with sound-reactive ink.

My dwelling connects to others through a network of resonance tunnels—passages that exist only when specific tones are sustained. Strike the right note and a solid wall becomes permeable; let the sound fade and you're trapped in stone. We've learned to navigate by music, carrying small chimes and whistles that open ways between the threshold spaces. It's dangerous—hit a wrong note and you might open a passage to somewhere that isn't quite the city, isn't quite anywhere.

The moths show us things. In their flight patterns, we read the city's health like a vast medical chart. When they cluster, we know a silence-gap approaches. When they scatter, new growth is coming. The eldest moths, some as large as children, seem to perceive time differently—they react to sounds that haven't happened yet, fleeing from safe spaces moments before they become dangerous. We've learned to trust their prescience.

At night (though day and night mean little in the eternal twilight of mist and sound), I maintain my charges. I check the cocoons, adjusting the acoustic environment for optimal development. I repair the delicate instruments that let us parse frequencies beyond human hearing. I work on my maps, trying to chart connections between sound and space that shift even as I document them. Sometimes I just sit and listen, learning to distinguish between the ten thousand voices of falling water.

We age differently here. The constant exposure to precisely controlled frequencies does something to cellular growth. Our hair turns not gray but iridescent, developing properties similar to moth scales. Our bones become denser, better at conducting sound. Some shepherds report synesthesia—seeing sound as color, feeling it as texture. I've begun to taste frequencies, each tone leaving its own flavor on my tongue.

The moth shepherds occupy a middle space in the city's ecology. Not transformed like the Siphons, not temporary like visitors. We've adapted just enough to survive, to find purpose in this place of endless cacophony. We're the city's librarians in a sense, cataloging its voices through the living instruments of our charges. When the city finally finishes whatever vast thought it's thinking, we'll be here to document its conclusion in wing-beats and scale-dust, in the careful notation of sounds that have no names in any human language.

This is our work: to shepherd fragments of silence through a world that has forgotten quiet, to find meaning in the spaces between notes of an endless, deafening song.
