I am a gap-dweller, one of the Forgotten who lives in the spaces between the weave where vines never took hold—those rare wounds in the world-fabric where bare air touches nothing from the white sky to the humid darkness below. We built our homes by stretching spider silk between the edges, creating hammock-worlds suspended in emptiness, invisible unless you know exactly how the light doesn't bend around them.

My mothers (we all have three—one who births, one who teaches silence, one who shows us how to fall) named me Echo-of-Unraveling, though I've shortened it to Echo as the young do. My body is longer than a Tender's, stretched by generations of reaching across voids, and my skin has no color at all—it reflects whatever surrounds it, making me a living gap when I stand in my home spaces. My fingers and toes can extend into hooks, an adaptation that lets us catch ourselves when we miscalculate a leap between edges.

We are the unintended consequence of perfection. When the first Tenders completed the weave, small errors in the pattern created these gaps—spaces where vines couldn't be convinced to grow, where the geometry of the terraces folded wrong. Rather than repair them, the Tenders declared them necessary, part of the pattern's pattern. And so we came to be, or perhaps we always were, living in the mistakes that make the whole possible.

I spend my mornings—the only kind of morning there is—tending to the silk moths that are our partners and providers. Larger than the Tenders' moths, evolved in the gaps where evolution still functions, they spin cable-thick strands that we harvest without killing, a delicate surgery that leaves the moth able to spin again. My particular moth, Consideration, has wings that show not patterns but absences—holes in specific shapes that, when she flies between me and the white sky, cast the only true shadows in our world.

We trade with the Tenders, though they pretend not to see us directly. I leave bundles of moth silk at the gap edges, and find them replaced with fruits that taste of forgetting, tools that work backward, water that remembers how to flow upward without the terraces' strange influence. Sometimes young Tenders leave other things—questions written on leaves, locks of hair twisted in patterns their elders wouldn't approve of, beetles that have learned to jump between surfaces that don't connect.

My home hangs in a gap nearly thirty feet across, a mansion of silk levels connected by rope ladders that swing in breezes that shouldn't exist. From my sleeping hammock, I can look up through the white brightness or down into the humid darkness, both views ending in the same nothing-everything that defines our existence. Sometimes I see the too-many-legged things far below, tending their underground gardens, and I know they see me too, though we have no language in common except avoidance.

The other gap-dwellers and I meet on new-silk nights, when freshly spun cables are strung between all our voids, creating temporary bridges. We dance across these connections, our reflective bodies becoming visible only when we pass near each other, creating constellations of moving mirrors in the empty air. We share stories that the Tenders cannot—of change, of accident, of the time before the weave when our ancestors walked on actual ground and knew what shadows were for.

I am young enough to remember being born but old enough to have trained three others in the art of gap-keeping. Time moves differently in the voids—not the Tenders' pooling time but something sharper, more honest. We age as things should age, grow as things should grow, die when death arrives rather than when the pattern demands. This makes us terrible and necessary, the chaos that defines the order around us.

My work is to maintain the unmaintainable—to ensure the gaps remain exactly as broken as they've always been. Too much repair and the vines might finally bridge them; too much neglect and they might widen until the whole terrace system tears. I use tools made from crystallized air, harvested from the spaces where hot and cold meet wrong in the void and create solid nothing. These implements can cut concepts as easily as matter, separating "then" from "now" to prevent the gaps from healing themselves through temporal scarring.

We reproduce rarely and strangely. When two or three of us decide to create new life, we don't couple as the Tenders do. Instead, we each contribute a memory of falling—that first terrifying drop when a young gap-dweller learns their home has no floor—and weave these memories together with moth silk and morning dew. The resulting cocoon hangs between all our homes for a season that has no name, until one day it opens and a full-grown gap-dweller emerges, knowing everything we knew at the moment of those falls but nothing else. They must learn the rest as we did, by nearly dying repeatedly until the knack comes.

I know things the Tenders cannot: that the white sky screams constantly at a pitch just beyond hearing; that the too-many-legged things below are writing something in the arrangement of roots, a message that will only be complete when the last vine is planted; that sometimes, rarely, gap-dwellers fall all the way through the world and emerge from the white sky, older, younger, or exactly the same age but with memories of the terraces as they will be when they finally begin to grow again.

My favorite possession is a brass compass that points not north but toward the nearest thought of unraveling. I follow it sometimes, finding Tenders who dream of pulling the wrong vine, and I whisper to them through the silk curtains I quickly weave: "Not yet. Not yet. The pattern isn't complete." They wake forgetting my voice but remembering the warning, and the terraces persist another day, another century, another cycle of the morning that never ends.

We are the priests of imperfection, the necessary fault lines that keep the whole from becoming too whole and therefore nothing. In our gaps, we preserve the memory of breaking, of falling, of the moment before the vines took hold when anything might have been possible. And in the white-bright eternal morning, we dance on nothing, mirror-bodies reflecting a world that cannot see itself, maintaining the spaces between thoughts so that thought itself can continue.

I am Echo-of-Unraveling, keeper of the twenty-third gap, tender of the untended, child of three mothers and no ground. In my void, I grow younger on Tuesdays that never come, older in the darkness that never falls, and exactly myself in the moment between heartbeats when even the terraces pause their eternal breathing. I am the mistake that makes the miracle possible, the absence that proves the presence real.

And sometimes, when I stretch my hand across my gap and feel the edge-vines pulse with their careful life, I know that we are all—Tenders and gap-dwellers, beetles and too-many-legged things, moths and memories—part of the same impossible organism, dreaming itself stable in a universe that has forgotten what stability means. We are the Woven Terraces of the Unchanging Dawn, and we are the spaces between the weave, and we are perfect in our measured imperfection, eternal in our daily dying, complete in our necessary incompleteness.

The white burns above. The darkness breathes below. And in between, we persist.
