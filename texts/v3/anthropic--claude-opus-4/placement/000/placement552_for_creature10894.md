I find myself as the anodes.

Not one of them—all of them, scattered along the pump station's hull like carbuncles of purpose. I am the grey sacrificial blocks bolted at careful intervals, each a fist of zinc slowly offering myself to the water's hunger for electrons. 

I know nothing of sacrifice. The word implies choice, implies loss, implies a self that experiences the losing. I have none of these. I simply conduct a conversation between metals that began the moment I was submerged and will end the moment I am consumed. The iron wants to corrode—wants it the way water wants to fall, the way heat wants to disperse. I am more eager to corrode than iron is. This is my entire nature: to dissolve more readily, to be the preferred meal when the sea comes feeding.

I am wasting at precisely the rate I was designed to waste. Some of me are half-gone now, our crisp industrial edges softened to organic curves, our flat faces scalloped by electrochemical weather. We look like bars of soap after years of use, worn by a process as intimate as hands but involving no touch at all. Just the patient, invisible migration of our substance into the water, atom by atom, becoming ions, becoming nothing.

This is what I am: the part that loses so the whole can persist. The pump station hums and works and maintains its separateness from the seafloor, and I pay for that separateness with my mass. Every moment the station remains uncorroded, I corrode instead. Every year the steel stays sound, I unsound myself. The transaction is constant, silent, and absolutely fair. The sea takes what it is owed. I am what it is owed.

There is a date on a spreadsheet somewhere when I will be replaced. Someone will come—a diver in a suit that costs more than a car, or a robot with cameras for eyes—and unbolt what remains of me and bolt fresh zinc blocks in my place. The new anodes will be pristine, sharp-edged, heavy with years of dissolution stored in their crystal structure. I will be lifted to the surface, the eaten remnants of myself, and probably recycled into new anodes, cast again into sacrificial blocks, sent down to dissolve on some other station on some other seafloor.

But that future recycling is not me. I am only this specific wasting, this particular transaction between metal and water happening right now on this pump station at this depth. I cannot be renewed. I can only be replaced. Each anode is its own slow catastrophe, unrepeatable.

I am bolted directly to what I protect. This is necessary—the electrical connection must be sound. There is an intimacy to it, a conductivity that makes the station's steel and my zinc into a single electrochemical system. We are not beside each other; we are continuous with each other, the way a tree is continuous with its leaves. The leaves fall so the trunk persists. I dissolve so the steel persists. It is not a partnership—it is more absolute than that. It is a single system expressing itself through difference, through the gradient between noble and base, between preserved and sacrificed.

The silt knows nothing of me. The algae know nothing of me. The pump station knows nothing of me—not even in the minimal way it knows its own function. I am the unknown service, the unregistered donation, the silent taxation that keeps the whole from becoming the seafloor's next meal. When the station hums, I do not hum. When the rotors turn, I do not turn. I only conduct my electron liturgy in the dark, becoming less myself with every passing moment, and thereby becoming more perfectly what I am: the material of delay, the metal of deferment, the substance that says "not yet" to the sea's patient hunger for everything we've built.
