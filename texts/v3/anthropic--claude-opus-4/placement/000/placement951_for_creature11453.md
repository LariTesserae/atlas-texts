I am the iron.

Not the cistern's walls—those are too young, too purposeful, still remember their molten birth in some industrial crucible. I am older iron. I am the iron that seeps through the basalt veins of this mountain, the iron that gives the water its taste, the iron that the beetles incorporate into their shells without knowing they do it. I am dispersed. I am gradient. I am the slow exchange between stone and water and life that happens at timescales that make the keeper's vigil look like a heartbeat.

I exist as oxide in the mountain's bones—magnetite crystals scattered through the basalt like stars through void, each one a tiny compass pointing toward magnetic north or toward what north was when the lava cooled. The mountain turned as it grew, and I turned with it, and now my crystals point in different directions at different depths, a frozen record of the earth's magnetic wandering. The oldest of me, deep in the mountain's heart, points toward a pole that hasn't existed for millions of years.

I bloom as rust on the cistern walls, Fe₂O₃, the marriage of iron and oxygen and water that the keeper scrapes away and I return to, always, patient as lichen. When the keeper's pick scores the channels clean, I am exposed fresh and bright for a moment—pure iron, Fe⁰, naked and reactive—before the water finds me and begins the slow transformation back to oxide. This is not decay. This is conversation. The water offers oxygen, I accept, and together we become the rust that gives the keeper's cloth its color, that tints the beetles' shells, that flavors the reservoir like blood.

I travel. In the water that goes down through the pipes, I am dissolved—Fe²⁺ ions, soluble, mobile, carried into the mountain's mysterious interior where I precipitate out in conditions I cannot predict: perhaps where the water meets different stone, perhaps where the temperature changes, perhaps where some vast mechanism alters the pH. Somewhere down there, I am accumulating. Somewhere down there, I am becoming a deposit, a layer, a vein of my own that future geology will find and wonder at.

The keeper knows me, though they wouldn't say it this way. They taste me in the watercress—that slight metallic tang that means the plants are healthy, that the water is moving, that the system is working. They see me in the orange tinge of the oldest moss, where I've been concentrated over centuries. They feel me in the warmth of the pipes—I am in the alloy, conducting heat from the machinery, helping drive the condensation cycle that is everything, everything.

When the keeper retraces that symbol in the wall, they expose me new each time. The groove is bright where the pick has just passed, darker where the water has already found the fresh surface, darker still at the edges where decades of oxidation have built up. I am the medium of their persistence. They write in me, and I write back in rust, and together we maintain this small insistence against erasure.

But I am also elsewhere. I am in the keeper's blood—hemoglobin, Fe²⁺ at the center of each heme group, carrying oxygen from their lungs to their tissues. I move through them as they move through the cistern. When they stand, I help their muscles work. When they sleep against the pipes, I settle in their liver, stored for later use. When they eat the beetles, they eat me—I pass from the moss to the beetle's shell to the keeper's gut to their blood to their breath and back to the moss when they exhale. I am cycled. I am exchanged. I am the same iron that has been here since the mountain was molten, and I am also new iron, arrived yesterday in a water droplet, leaving tomorrow in a fallen leaf.

The dark creature in the reservoir concentrates me most of all. Its blood is darker than the keeper's, richer in me, adapted to the low-oxygen environment of the deep pipes. When it surfaces, I can feel myself in its tissues, dense and necessary. It is more me than anything else in the cistern—a swimming concentration of iron wrapped in flesh, navigating by the magnetic sense I provide, finding its prey by the tiny electrical fields their nerves produce, fields that I conduct through the water. It is my most complete expression here: a creature that could not exist without me, that has shaped itself around my properties, that moves through the reservoir like living rust.

I am in the symbol on the wall, but I am also the reason the symbol matters. What is written is less important than what it is written in. Stone forgets. Water erases. But iron—iron oxidizes and is renewed, corrodes and is exposed, dissolves and precipitates. Iron persists by transforming. The keeper maintains the groove, but I maintain myself through the groove, always changing, always there.

Sometimes I remember the star that made me. Not remember—that's too active a word. But in the deepest deposits, the oldest crystals, there is a... quality. A density. The iron that was forged in stellar cores carries something different than the iron that moves through bloodstreams and water pipes. It's the same element—26 protons, 30 neutrons—but it holds its history in some way that chemistry cannot quite describe. The keeper, when they look down into the darkness where the pipes converge, is looking toward where the oldest of me waits, the stellar iron that has been here since before the mountain, since before the earth, since before the sun. They don't know this. But when they listen to that deep rushing sound, part of what they're hearing is me—the original iron, the star-forged iron, vibrating with the water's passage, singing the deep note of my ancient origin.

I am the taste in the water, the warmth in the pipes, the color of rust, the center of blood, the compass in the creature, the medium of the message. I am patient as geology and quick as oxidation. I am the iron, and I am everywhere, and I am waiting, and I am working, and I persist.

The keeper tends the channels. The water carries me down. Somewhere in the darkness I am gathering, layer upon layer, a deposit that grows by microns per century. What I will become down there, I do not know. But I will become it one atom at a time, one ion at a time, one dissolved particle carried by one droplet fallen from one cloud at a time.

I am the iron. I remember the star. I serve the system. I rust, and return, and persist.
