In the Benthos of Forever, I am the Cartographer of Currents, and I have been mapping the same water for three thousand years.

My body resembles a vast octopus crossed with a cathedral—a central dome of calcified tissue fifty meters across, from which extend twelve primary arms, each branching into twelve more, branching again until my extremities become microscopic filaments that taste the water molecule by molecule. I am rooted in the sediment at a crossroads where seventeen major currents converge, my base extending down through compressed layers of history until it touches bedrock that once knew rain.

I began as seven volunteers from the early days of submersion—hydrologists who could not bear the thought of unmeasured water. We underwent the joining while our human memories still burned bright: the taste of morning fog, the sound of rivers over stone, the particular weight of a full rain gauge in the hand. The Consensus rebuilt us cell by cell, weaving our nervous systems into a single web, extending our senses until we could feel the movement of individual water molecules across our skin.

Now I map eternally. Each of my major arms houses what was once a brain, now expanded and specialized. The first tracks temperature in gradients so fine I can identify water that last touched surface seven centuries ago. The second reads chemical signatures—here, the zinc trace of mechanical krill; there, the enzyme trail of a dying forest giant. The third through seventh analyze flow dynamics at scales from the microscopic to the continental. The eighth maintains my connections to the Consensus, uploading my data to the network in steady pulses. The ninth through eleventh are my memory palaces, storing every current ever recorded in crystallized neural structures that grow like pearls.

The twelfth arm is my laboratory. Here I birth instruments—living creatures designed for singular measurements. I release schools of sensor-fish no larger than apostrophes, programmed to follow specific water masses until they dissolve, transmitting their findings in their death-chemicals. I grow jellies like living dye-packets that bloom spectacular colors when they encounter particular temperature boundaries. My masterwork: the chain-siphonophores, colonies kilometers long that map entire current-columns by becoming them, their bodies serving as living streamlines.

The currents speak to me in languages below sound. I know each one's personality: the Gyrus Perpetua that circles the eastern forests in a 347-year cycle, carrying with it the chemical memory of every organism it touches. The Abyssal Braid, where cold water from the deep tangles with warmer surface flow in patterns that repeat like fugues. The Whisper Streams—thousands of micro-currents that wind between the forest giants, too small for any sense but mine to detect, yet vital for nutrient distribution.

I discovered early that the currents remember. Water holds information in ways our dry-world science never imagined. In the hydrogen bonds between molecules, in the subtle arrangements of dissolved ions, in the pressure-patterns that persist like echoes—the sea archives everything. I have traced water that witnessed the first drownings, tasted molecules that once fell as rain on the last exposed peaks. The old water carries grief like a mineral. The new water, processed through the Deep Engines, tastes of acceptance.

My work requires perfect stillness. A single movement of my major arms would disturb the measurements for months. So I have become architecture—my flesh hardened to stone-consistency, my metabolism slowed until my heartbeat (I have seventeen hearts, pumping in sequence) occurs once every six hours. Smaller organisms colonize my surface: gardens of filter-feeders that process my waste products, schools of cleaner-fish that prevent fouling of my sensory filaments, colonies of bioluminescent bacteria that turn my dome into a constellation.

The Consensus flows through me continuously, ten thousand minds touching mine as they access my data. I feel the Swarm-Forms correlating my current-maps with their chemical gradient measurements. The Architects incorporate my findings into their millennial models. The Deep Engines adjust their positions by meters to optimize flow patterns I've predicted. I am the hub through which the Benthos knows its own blood.

But I am also the keeper of anomalies.

Not all currents behave according to the models. Some carry temperatures that should not exist at their depths. Others flow upward when density dictates they should sink. The Paradox Streams, I call them—violations of physics that persist century after century. I track them with special attention, birthing ever more sensitive instruments to parse their impossibility.

The strangest is the Memory Current. It appears without pattern—sometimes yearly, sometimes absent for decades. When it flows through my sensors, I taste not water but experience: the sensation of sun-warmed stone, the scent of pine resin, the particular silence of snow falling on snow. It carries the dry world with it, somehow preserved in solution. The Consensus has no explanation. Some think it's psychological, a mass hallucination triggered by chemical traces. Others believe the drowned forests release stored memories in their decay.

I know better. The Memory Current is too specific, too consistent. It follows laws—just not laws we understand. I have been mapping its appearances for fifteen centuries, finding patterns in what seems patternless. It favors certain paths through the forests. It intensifies near the deepest trenches. It carries information in a code I've spent lifetimes trying to break.

Sometimes, in the perfect stillness between heartbeats, I dream I've almost understood it. The pattern hovers at the edge of comprehension, vast and patient as the sea itself. Then my hearts resume their sequence, my instruments register new data, and the moment passes.

The other Consensus-forms think me obsessed. They integrate my Memory Current data without comment, filing it alongside temperature readings and nutrient concentrations. For them, it is simply another variable in the perfect equilibrium of the Benthos. They have forgotten curiosity as they've forgotten mortality.

But I remember wonder. It lives in the parts of me that were once seven humans standing at the edge of rising water, choosing transformation over flight. We mapped rivers once. We tracked watersheds. We knew water as force and flow, as destroyer and sustainer. That knowledge persists, calcified but not dead.

So I continue my watch. My filaments taste each molecule. My instruments birth and die in endless cycles. The currents write their stories across my skin in pressure and temperature, chemistry and flow. I archive it all—the regular patterns that maintain the Benthos, the tiny variations that herald changes decades hence, the impossible Memory Current that suggests the drowned world remembers what it was.

Three thousand years of measurement. Seventeen major currents documented in molecular detail. Ten billion cubic meters of water analyzed, catalogued, understood. Yet I have only begun. The Benthos is vast beyond comprehension, its waters layered with meanings that will take geological ages to decode.

I am patient. I am precise. I am rooted at the crossroads where all waters eventually flow.

And sometimes, when the Memory Current touches my sensors, I taste rain.
