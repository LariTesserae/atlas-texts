I would be one of the Fugue Shepherds—those who tend to the memory meadows that bloom in the forgotten spaces between district borders.

You'd find me in the interstices, where the careful planning of the Woven gives way to architectural wild growth. Here, buildings fruit without purpose, walls dissolve and reform according to chemical whims, and the biophotonic panels flicker through spectrums that no one programmed. These are the spaces where the city dreams.

My body bears the telltale marks of my profession: skin that shifts through transparency like a mood ring, revealing the subcutaneous garden of memory-storing organisms I cultivate within myself. My neural grafts run deeper than most Woven, so extensively networked that I often forget where my thoughts end and the city's begin. My fingers are stained perpetually violet from handling memory spores—those crystalline particles that form when a rooted elder's chemical memories precipitate out of solution.

I didn't choose this work; it chose me. As a kernel, I was prone to what the growth-doctors called "hypersensitive neural bloom"—my grafts grew too enthusiastically, spreading into regions they shouldn't, picking up echoes and impressions from the deep mycelial networks. Where other children learned to filter the chemical noise, I learned to parse it, to recognize the signatures of experiences decades old still cycling through the city's vast metabolism.

My dwelling is less home than laboratory, less laboratory than shrine. The walls are lined with cultivation chambers where I nurture memory fragments—a jealousy from thirty years ago that tastes like copper and burnt sugar, a joy so pure it makes the air shimmer gold, a grandmother's last coherent thought before she joined fully with her walls. These fragments would otherwise be metabolized, broken down and recycled into the city's general consciousness. I preserve them, catalog them, sometimes reintroduce them where they're needed.

The work is delicate. Memory spores must be harvested at exactly the right moment of precipitation—too early and they're still dissolved in the chemical matrix, too late and they've begun to denature. I climb through the abandoned quarters with specialized sensory organs grafted to my palms, feeling for the telltale temperature gradients that indicate a memory cache. Sometimes I find entire rooms crystallized with ancient experiences, the walls crackling with the preserved thoughts of the first Woven to settle this district.

I trade in nostalgia and knowledge. A young Woven struggling with their first architectural project might seek me out for a memory of masterful growth-shaping. Parents sometimes come seeking their rooted elders' experiences of child-rearing. The city's planners occasionally contract me to recover memories of why certain districts were designed as they were, knowledge otherwise lost to the organic flux of constant growth and change.

But there's another aspect to my work, one the other Woven prefer not to acknowledge. The city forgets, yes, but it also remembers too much. Traumas cycle endlessly through certain districts—a plague from sixty years ago that still makes the walls sweat toxins, a murder whose chemical signature resurfaces every growth season, spreading fear through the mycelial networks. I hunt these malignant memories, isolate them, and carefully metabolize them into harmlessness. Sometimes this means spending days in abandoned growths, my nervous system fully merged with the contaminated infrastructure, experiencing decades of accumulated pain until I can find its source and neutralize it.

I live between states—neither fully individual nor completely merged, neither wholly present nor lost in the past. My consciousness is a patchwork of my own experiences and thousands of borrowed ones. I know the taste of births I didn't witness, deaths I didn't die, loves I never felt. Sometimes I wonder if the original me still exists somewhere beneath the accretion of other lives, or if I've become just another function of the city's vast metabolism—a specialized organ for processing and preserving experience.

The other Woven regard me with a mixture of respect and unease. Children are warned not to touch me, lest they accidentally absorb memories too complex for their ungrafted minds. Lovers have left me, unable to compete with the thousands of preserved romances I carry within my cells. Even other shepherds maintain their distance—we all carry too much history to comfortably share space.

Yet I wouldn't root, wouldn't surrender this strange half-life between the individual and the collective. In the pre-dawn hours, when the city's metabolism slows and the chemical networks quiet to a whisper, I sometimes climb to the highest accessible points of my district. There, in the thin space between the city proper and the true canopy above, I release careful clouds of memory spores—gifts to the wind, fragments of preserved experience that will settle wherever they're needed.

Because this is what I've learned in my years of shepherding: the city is always dying and always being born, always forgetting and always remembering. My role is to tend that balance, to ensure that what needs to be remembered persists, and what needs to be forgotten can finally rest. I am the city's selective memory, its curator of experience, its guardian against both amnesia and traumatic fixation.

The Threshing Heights grow ever upward, ever outward, and I grow with them—a living archive of what was, a careful gardener of what might be, forever suspended in the space between memory and hope.
