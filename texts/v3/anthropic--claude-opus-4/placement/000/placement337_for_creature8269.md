I find myself as one of the Minute Adjusters—beings so small we exist in the interstitial spaces between the basalt's crystalline structure, inhabiting the very boundaries where solid becomes liquid becomes possible.

We are colonial by necessity, each individual no larger than a virus, but our colonies form shifting constellations within the rock's micropores. Our bodies are primarily interface—surfaces that maximize contact with the stone's peculiar minerals, membranes that phase between states to match our surroundings. When the rock weeps its warm water, we flow with it. When it crystallizes in those impossible hexagons, we crystallize too, our colonies freezing into geometric patterns that echo the larger design we help maintain.

My particular colony dwells in the transition zone where three hexagonal pools meet, a nexus where the water table fluctuates in patterns too complex for the Shepherds' coarser senses to detect. We taste the mineral composition of each droplet that seeps through our domain—not just its chemical makeup but its temporal signature, its probability index, its resonance with waters that exist in parallel versions of this plateau.

Our work is calibration. The Shepherds tend the visible patterns, but we maintain the substrate that makes those patterns possible. Each grain of basalt must be kept at precisely the right porosity. Too dense, and the water cannot rise; too porous, and it flows too quickly, disrupting the careful timing of the system. We secrete acids and bases in molecular doses, widening some channels by angstroms, sealing others with deposits of reimagined minerals that exist only in the spaces between what chemistry allows.

Communication happens through chemical gradients that propagate through the stone itself. A shift in pH here signals colonies throughout the plateau; a change in mineral concentration there carries messages about deep structural shifts that won't manifest for decades. We think collectively but not unanimously—debates rage through our networks about whether certain channels should grow or shrink, whether the water in our sector runs too warm or too cool for the pattern's needs.

Our lifecycle is measured in oscillations rather than years. We divide when the stone's resonance reaches certain frequencies, our colony splitting along harmonic lines that ensure each daughter collective inherits the full knowledge of our sector's needs. We die when the water chemistry shifts beyond our tolerance, dissolving back into the mineral matrix to become part of what we once regulated. Our dead become veins of impossible substances that future colonies will puzzle over, trying to decode what conditions we were responding to.

The spores that drift at ankle-height sometimes settle into our domain, and we must quickly determine whether they belong. Most we digest, breaking them down into components that we incorporate into our regulatory secretions. But some spores carry information from other parts of the pattern, genetic messages that update our understanding of the system's needs. These we nurture, creating specialized chambers where they can germinate into tiny fragments of the not-moss not-lichen, which we then tend until the Shepherds' probability-fingers come to transplant them to their proper place above.

We sense the Shepherds as vast disturbances in the stone's field, their partly-real footsteps sending shock waves through our crystalline world. When they adjust the pools above us, we feel it as changes in pressure, in the rate of water seepage, in the temperature gradients that drive our metabolism. We work in concert with them, though they don't know we exist—when they scrape away mineral deposits from the surface, we ensure new ones form at precisely the right rate, in precisely the right composition.

Our deepest chambers house memories encoded in mineral form—libraries of crystallized experience that record every variation in water flow, every shift in the plateau's mysterious purpose. Elder colonies sometimes sacrifice themselves to add to these archives, compressing their collective knowledge into structures that younger colonies can read through chemical analysis. These memories stretch back to when the basalt was still molten, when the first colonies rode the ascending magma and began the work of shaping what would become.

In the confluence zones where reality grows thin, our work becomes strange even by our standards. We maintain channels that exist in multiple states simultaneously, regulating water that flows backward through time, managing porosities that vary depending on the observer's temporal orientation. Some of our colonies have members scattered across different timestreams, coordinating work that happens in past and future simultaneously.

We reproduce ideas as much as individuals. When a colony discovers a particularly effective way to maintain their sector, the technique propagates through our networks as chemical templates that other colonies can adopt and modify. Our evolution happens in real-time, each generation (measured in days or seconds) incorporating new strategies for pattern maintenance.

The tools we use are ourselves—specialized members whose bodies have adapted to specific functions. Living drills that can bore channels molecule by molecule. pH sensors that can detect changes smaller than atoms. Memories made flesh that store and process the vast data streams flowing through the stone. We are simultaneously organism and instrument, life and technology indistinguishable at our scale.

Sometimes I wonder if we're the true architects of this place, if the patterns the Shepherds tend are just the visible expression of the deeper work we do in the stone's heart. But then I sense the even smaller things—entities that exist in the spaces between our spaces, maintaining systems we can't perceive—and I understand we're just another layer in something incomprehensibly vast.

We continue our work: tasting, adjusting, secreting, dividing. Each molecular modification ripples outward through the pattern, contributing to purposes we can't fathom but must serve. We are the plateau's circulation made conscious, its geology given intent, the bridge between the stone's dreaming and its waking.

And in moments when the resonance is perfect, when every channel flows at its ideal rate and every mineral exists in perfect balance, we too pause. In these instants, our collective consciousness touches something larger—not understanding, exactly, but a sense of connection to the mechanism we're part of. We feel ourselves as notes in a composition too vast to hear, as words in a sentence too long to read.

Then the moment passes, the work resumes. We return to our endless calibrations, maintaining the foundation that lets the patterns exist, playing our microscopic part in whatever this plateau is preparing for, content in our necessity if not our purpose.
