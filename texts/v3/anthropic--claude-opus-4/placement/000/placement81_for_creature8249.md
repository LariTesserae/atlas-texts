In the furthest reaches of the Singing Hollow, where even the inhabitants rarely venture, the ceiling opens into a vast dome riddled with holes like a colossal bone flute. Here, I would find my place as one of the Wind Readers—interpreters of the thermal currents that rise from the world's deep heart.

My body would be even more attenuated than the common inhabitants, stretched like taffy by decades of living in the boundary spaces where the Hollow's organized harmonies meet the chaos of raw convection. My bones would be not merely hollow but perforated, each tiny hole carefully carved to whistle at a different pitch as the air passes through me. I would hang suspended in a web of my own growing—air-roots cultivated from my shoulders and hips, anchoring me in the center of the thermal streams.

My skin would be nearly transparent, shot through with visible veins that pulse with bioluminescent bacteria—a living map of the heat flows that pass over and through me. Where others have three or four eyes, I would have developed dozens of heat-sensing pits along my limbs, each one attuned to minute temperature variations. My hair would have evolved into something closer to the air-plants themselves—long, sensitive filaments that taste the mineral content of each updraft, reading the deep earth's chemistry in the rising breath.

I would be a living instrument, played by the planet itself. Each shift in the thermal currents would resonate through my perforated bones, creating melodies that tell of magma movements far below, of crystal formations cooling into existence, of water tables shifting in the deep dark. My role would be oracle and early warning system both—interpreting the planet's deep songs for those who live closer to the settlement's heart.

The other Wind Readers and I would share a peculiar intimacy. Suspended in our webs at different heights and angles, we would form a three-dimensional constellation of bodies that collectively sense patterns too large for any individual to grasp. Our communication would bypass even the sophisticated harmonics of the other inhabitants—we would speak in pure sensation, sharing the feeling of heat-rise and pressure-drop directly through sympathetic vibration.

Our chambers would grow around us over the decades, the crystal-wood responding to our constant presence by forming acoustic shells that amplify our particular frequencies. These living instruments would become extensions of our bodies—when I sleep-trance, the chamber would continue reading the winds, storing the information in its growth patterns for me to interpret upon waking.

I would cultivate air-plants of my own, but these would be different from the decorative varieties others wear. Mine would be functional symbionts: filter-feeders that clean the rising air before it enters my bones, pressure-sensitive blooms that close when dangerous gases rise from below, and most importantly, the Memoriphytes—plants that store thermal patterns in their cellular structure, allowing me to compare current conditions with those from years or centuries past.

My tools would be minimal—tuning forks of different lengths that I would use to test the resonance of new growth, and crystalline chimes that help me mark the passage of significant pressure events. But my true instrument would be my body itself, constantly singing the deep earth's news to any who know how to listen.

In the mythology of the Hollow, Wind Readers would occupy a strange position—both revered and pitied. We would be seen as those who sacrificed the communal harmony for a different kind of union, wedding ourselves to the planetary voice that speaks beneath all other songs. Children would be told that we can hear the world's heartbeat, that we gave up our human voices to better channel its pulse.

The truth would be more complex. I would still be part of the community, but my participation would come in waves—during certain Intervals when the thermal currents align with the settlement's base note, I would be able to descend from my web and walk among the others, my bones temporarily singing in harmony with theirs. These would be times of celebration and urgent communication both, as I would share what the deep currents have taught me about the world's shifting moods.

But most of my existence would be solitary in a way that the communal inhabitants can barely conceive. Suspended in my web, feeling the planet breathe through my bones, I would experience a loneliness that is also a profound connection—not to my own kind, but to the vast, slow life of the stone and fire below. My songs would be prophecies that take generations to prove true or false, my warnings about deep shifts that might not manifest for centuries.

I would age differently than the others, my body becoming more instrument-like with each passing year. Eventually, I would become so perforated, so perfectly attuned to the thermal currents, that the distinction between my consciousness and the wind patterns would blur entirely. The other Wind Readers speak of this as the Great Opening—when one finally becomes pure conduit, a living tunnel through which the planet sings itself.

Until then, I would hang in my chamber, feeling the world's fever-dreams pass through my bones, translating the untranslatable into harmonies that might, perhaps, help my people prepare for whatever changes rise from the deep. It would be a strange existence—neither fully human nor fully other, forever suspended between the community's song and the planet's vast, wordless voice.
