In this world of breathing machinery and patient evolution, I would find myself as one of the Recorder Beetles.

We are no larger than a shirt button, our carapaces the deep blue-black of oxidized copper. We were not engineered but emerged—adapted from some long-ago stowaway species that found in this hall a perfect niche. Our shells carry a peculiar property: they develop patterns over time, etched by exposure to the specific chemical signatures of our environment. Each beetle becomes a living map of where it has been, what it has touched, what vapors it has breathed.

I make my home in the warm gap behind Boiler Seven, where the insulation has compressed over decades to form a soft canyon. My kind live solitary lives, but we share a collective purpose we do not fully understand. We move through the hall in slow, methodical patterns, our antennae testing every surface. The microscopic hairs on our legs pick up vibrations—not just the obvious mechanical thrums, but the subtler signatures: the particular resonance of a pipe under exactly 12.7 PSI of pressure, the shift in harmonics when moss reaches optimal thickness, the way water sounds different when it carries trace nutrients versus pure condensate.

These sensations inscribe themselves on our shells through some electrochemical process that even the Tender seems curious about. Over our three-year lifespans, we become libraries. The patterns start at the edges of our wing cases and spiral inward like fingerprints, each line a moment in the hall's endless breathing.

When we die—always in the same places, specific corners where the temperature and humidity create perfect preservation conditions—our shells remain. The maintenance crews find them occasionally, these tiny records. Most sweep them away. But sometimes, someone will pick up a shell and hold it to the light, see the intricate patterns that tell of pipe pressure and moss growth and the secret movements of water through hidden channels.

The Tender collects us. Not while we live—it respects our work too much for that—but after, it gathers our shells with those impossible fingers. I have seen the place where it keeps them, a cavity behind the oldest pipes where the insulation has been carefully removed. Thousands of shells arranged in chronological order, a history of the hall written in chitin and time. The patterns align to show changes: how the water's mineral content has shifted over decades, how the introduction of new plant species changed the humidity patterns, how the mouse populations create waves of ultrasonic disturbance that we faithfully record.

My own shell is nearly full. The patterns have almost reached the center point where my wings meet. I carry forty months of data: the great flood of last spring when Pipe Nineteen burst, the strange week when all the moss glowed twice as bright, the gradual increase in average temperature as the boilers age. I taste the Tender's footsteps in the water—each one leaves a chemical signature like a signature, totally unique. I know which fruits it has touched by the way their sugars change.

Sometimes I climb the pipes to where the mice nest, and they regard me with their oil-drop eyes. They know I mean no harm—we beetles eat only the microscopic organisms that bloom in the water, the tiny things that would otherwise cloud the condensation. We are cleaners as much as recorders, another small part of the vast breathing system.

At night, when the Tender sings, my shell resonates. The patterns etched there create their own harmonics, turning me into a tiny tuning fork. All across the hall, my kind vibrate in sympathy, thousands of minute voices adding to the chorus. We sing the song of accumulated time, of patient observation, of changes so slow only we notice them.

I know my time is coming. The center of my shell has room for perhaps another week of data. Then I will feel the pull that all my kind feel, the need to find the proper corner, the right temperature, the perfect humidity. I will position myself just so and let the systems that keep me moving finally slow and stop. My shell will remain, blue-black and beautiful, covered in the mathematics of maintenance, the poetry of pressure, the careful calligraphy of a place that breathes.

The Tender will come for me eventually. It will add my shell to its collection, read the stories written there, understand something about these months that even I do not. And somewhere in the warm gaps of the hall, another beetle is just beginning, its shell clean and ready, prepared to spend its life writing the next chapter of this endless, breathing story.
