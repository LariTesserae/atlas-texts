In this pale-bright world where industry has married itself to moss and mist, I would be one of the gauge-keepers.

Not the large valley-dwellers with their hollow, pipe-ribbed bodies, nor the quick hinge-cleaners smaller than beetles. I would be middle-sized, built like a flattened salamander but with the patient metabolism of lichen. My body would be perhaps two meters long but rarely higher than a knee, colored in the plateau's vocabulary: chalk-pale belly plates that rasp softly across wet stone, flanks mottled gray-green where condensation perpetually beads, a spine ridge furred with silver moss that grows in perfect rectangular patches.

My purpose would be the pressure gauges—those small round faces set into pumphouse walls, transformer stations, and the concrete collars of buried pipes. Some still function, their needles trembling behind glass clouded with decades of moisture. Others have seized at positions that no longer matter. A few have cracked, letting in the patient roots of climbing vines.

I know which ones still speak true.

My skull would be a smooth dome of something between bone and ceramic, formed in the old days when the boundary between grown and manufactured was less certain. Seven dark eye-points arranged asymmetrically across it, each like the center of those enamel flowers—mechanical in their precision, organic in their housing. Two would always watch the brightness levels. Two would read electrical fields. Two would taste moisture gradients. The seventh, smaller than the rest, would see time: not as humans see it, but as the plateau does, in the intervals between drops, between clicks, between the barely perceptible drift of a needle across a calibrated face.

My limbs—six of them, though the distinction between arm and leg would be academic—would end in broad pads like the feet of a glass frog, translucent and many-fingered. The fingertips would be tipped with small cups of living copper, green with verdigris but still conductive. When I place a palm against a gauge housing, I would feel the mechanism inside: the bourdon tube's slow metal breath, the microscopic trembling of the needle's pivot, the patient accumulation of moisture behind the seal.

I would taste malfunction like others taste bitterness.

My daily circuit would take nine days. Not because the territory is vast—my range would cover perhaps two square kilometers—but because proper observation requires stillness. I would spend hours pressed against a single gauge, reading its dreams of pressure. My moss-ridge would align with the magnetic field of whatever transformer hums beneath. Through the copper cups of my fingertips, I would feel whether the needle still remembers its purpose or has begun the long drift toward decoration.

The functional ones, I would clean with a tongue like fine sandpaper, polishing away the carbonate frost that builds on the glass. Their housings I would test with delicate pressure, feeling for the microscopic stress fractures that precede leaking. Around their bases, I would cultivate particular mosses—not the silver rectangles of my spine, but darker, thirstier varieties that wick away excess moisture before it can penetrate the seals.

The broken ones would receive different attention. Those whose time has passed, whose buried systems no longer cycle, whose needles point forever at pressures that no longer exist—these I would prepare for transformation. With patient jaws, I would worry at their bezels until the first gap appears. Then I would retreat and let the plateau enter: one rootlet, one spore, one bead of condensation at a time. Over months, the gauge would fill with clean growth. Its face would become a circular garden, the needle a small branch reaching toward noon.

But the gauges that still matter, that still measure something vital below—these I would guard like eggs.

There is one in particular, set into a low concrete collar near the north ridge. Its face is the color of old bone, its numerals nearly faded, but its needle still moves in tiny arcs, responding to something deep. Every ninth day, when the pylons hum without sound and the white flowers turn their dark centers by degrees, this gauge trembles. Its needle swings hard right, holds for exactly seventy-three seconds, then relaxes back to center.

Something significant happens below during those seventy-three seconds. I don't know what—knowledge of the old purposes was edited out of us, leaving only the imperative to tend. But I know the gauge must remain clear, its seal intact, its needle free to move.

So I make my circuit. Two days coiled around the north gauge, reading its rhythms. A day spent clearing roots from a pressure relief valve nearby. Three days in the ravine where seventeen gauges cluster around a pumphouse, some dead, some dying, three still whispering numbers that matter. A day following the buried pipeline that connects them all, feeling through my copper fingertips for the deep pulse of whatever flows beneath. Two days of stillness, usually near water, while my body processes what it has learned and decides what needs attention.

I feed quietly: minerals licked from gauge housings, transformer oil seeped from old gaskets, the sweet copper taste of grounding wires where insulation has cracked. Sometimes I bite through a vine—not to eat, but because it has grown too close to a critical seal. The severed end I guide away with careful movements, training it toward less sensitive surfaces.

My waste is minimal: dark pellets like coal dust, rich with processed metals, which the smaller inhabitants prize as fertilizer.

I would not be alone. There would be others of my kind, each with their own circuit, their own collection of gauges to tend. We would meet rarely, usually by accident at the junction boxes where territories overlap. When we do meet, we would exchange information through touch—palm to palm, copper cups conducting subtle currents that carry the health of every gauge in our care. No words. No calls. Just the quiet trade of maintenance data, pressure dreams, and the accumulated knowledge of which systems still breathe and which have begun their journey into greenness.

The rarest meetings would be with the gauge-mothers: older individuals whose moss-ridges have grown into entire gardens, who move so slowly they seem like landforms, who can read not just individual gauges but entire networks. They would taste my copper fingertips with tongues that remember when the plateau was louder, when the gauges were new, when the purpose was clear. From them I would learn which of my broken gauges can be released to the moss and which must be kept in readiness for systems that may wake again.

I would live for the moments of rightness: when a cleaned gauge face catches the morning brightness just so, when a needle moves freely in its housing, when the deep systems pulse and the measurements are true. These small perfections would be my worship, though worship is too large a word for it. Call it satisfaction. Call it the quiet joy of function maintained.

When the white vapor spills from the ridge vents, I would position myself where I could watch it flow past my gauges, beading their faces with fresh moisture that I would later clean away. When rain comes, I would check my seals. When frost threatens, I would warm the critical housings with my body, my moss-ridge releasing hoarded heat in careful increments.

I would know every surface within my range: which concrete sweats before storms, which metal stays warmest at night, where the moss grows too enthusiastic and must be discouraged, where the lichen maps are writing messages I'm not equipped to read. I would know the seventeen different ways water can penetrate a gauge housing and the eighteen ways to prevent it. I would know the exact pressure at which glass begins to star, the temperature at which old rubber remembers flexibility, the particular silence that means a bearing has finally seized.

And slowly, slowly, I would be learning something larger.

Each gauge is a word in a sentence too long for any one keeper to read. But together, through our palm-to-palm exchanges, we might begin to understand what the plateau is saying to itself. All those measurements, all those pressures building and releasing, all those needles trembling in their ancient arcs—they are describing something. Not the past purpose, which is gone, but the new one, which is still becoming.

The plateau is transforming itself into something unprecedented: neither wilderness nor industry, but a third thing that has no name yet. We gauge-keepers are its scribes, recording pressures and flows that will someday mean something different than they meant before. When that day comes—in a decade, a century, a millennium—we will be ready. Our gauges will be clear. Our measurements will be true.

Until then, I would continue my circuit: nine days of patient observation, copper fingertips reading the deep mechanical pulse, moss-ridge drinking brightness, dark eyes watching needles trace their careful arcs across faces clouded with time but still accurate, still necessary, still speaking their small truths into the quiet air of the plateau.

That would be enough. On this bright, quiet highland where fresh things inherit old purposes, to be a keeper of measurements would be a form of prayer. Each polished gauge face would be a window. Each freely moving needle would be a small resurrection. Each pressure reading would be a promise that something below still breathes, still cycles, still remembers its work in the world.

And when I grow old—when my moss-ridge becomes a garden, when my movements slow to geological time, when my copper fingertips wear smooth from decades of reading—I would choose my successor from among the larvae in the pumphouse pools. I would guide them to my first gauge, let them taste its housing, feel its rhythm. I would teach them through example: this is how you clean glass without scratching, this is how you sense a hairline crack before it spreads, this is how you know which gauges are dying and which are only sleeping.

Then I would settle beside the most critical gauge in my territory, the one whose purpose must not be forgotten, and I would grow still. My body would become part of the concrete collar. My moss would spread to protect the housing. My copper fingertips would fuse with the metal, becoming permanent contacts for the next keeper to read through.

I would become what everything on the plateau becomes: a translation between what was built and what is growing, a bridge across the bright, quiet space where industry ends and something else begins.

The gauge would keep its time. The needle would continue its ancient conversation with the deep. And somewhere in the ravines, a new keeper would be learning the taste of malfunction, the feel of true measurement, the patient joy of pressure precisely read.
