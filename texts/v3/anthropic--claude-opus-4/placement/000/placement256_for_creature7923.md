I am the memory keeper of failed lines.

My workshop occupies a dead-end platform on the lowest inhabited level of the middle ridge, where the failed cable car lines terminate. These are the routes abandoned after catastrophes—a car that plummeted when its grip gave way, taking fourteen souls into the mist; a line that snapped under ice load during the winter of the five-week freeze; entire stations that sheared away when their anchor points failed. The city builds new routes rather than repair these haunted trajectories, leaving behind a graveyard of tensioned steel and empty stations.

I salvage what remains. Each morning, I traverse the catwalks that maintenance crews no longer maintain, testing my weight on platforms that haven't been inspected in years. The cables themselves are my harvest—thousands of feet of wire rope, each strand carrying the memory of its tension, its song, its failure. I collect them systematically, cutting lengths with hydraulic shears, coiling them according to their histories.

In my workshop, I weave.

The craft came to me accidentally. I'd been an engineer once, part of the team that calculated load tolerances and breaking points. After the Morrison Line collapse—my calculations, my signature on the inspection report—I couldn't face the active lines anymore. But I couldn't leave the mountain either. The empty cables drew me with their silence, their absence of purpose. I started handling them to understand what I'd missed, feeling for the weak points my equations hadn't predicted.

The first weaving was unconscious—my hands braiding as I thought, creating patterns from nervous energy. But the cable remembered its shape. Even cut and coiled, it wanted to return to tension, to straightness. I began working with this memory, incorporating the cable's history into new forms.

Now my workshop is full of them. Small pieces at first—palm-sized weavings that capture the rhythm of a particular line's sway, the frequency of its song. I learned to read the metal's memory through my fingertips: this section carried the morning commuters for twenty years, accumulating the microscopic stress of ten thousand grips; this piece sang C-sharp in the wind until metal fatigue dropped it a half-tone; this length was cut from the Bremen Station disaster, and it still holds the shape of its final, catastrophic torque.

The weavings grew larger, more complex. I created memorial pieces for the lost lines—abstract sculptures that embody their movement, their purpose, their failure. A helical form that captures the Morton Spiral's tendency to twist under load. A grid that reproduces the exact resonance pattern of the Old North Cable on the day it snapped. Some pieces incorporate other salvage: fragments of grip mechanisms, pieces of shattered station glass, the wooden handles worn smooth by decades of gloved hands.

The city forgets, but the cables remember. In my workshop, I preserve these memories in tangible form. Each weaving is a historical record written in steel—the story of how we learned to inhabit this impossible place through a thousand small failures.

My hands are a map of scars from wire cuts and splinter wounds. The cables fight back as I work them, their broken ends seeking skin. I've learned to read these injuries too—the way blood marks the metal, how rust forms differently around each wound. This too becomes part of the weaving.

I live alone. The apartment behind the workshop is sparse—a cot, a hot plate, walls lined with coils of salvaged cable waiting to be worked. The social life of the ridges happens far above me, in the active zones where functioning lines carry people between their purposes. Down here, I have only the company of past tensions, the conversation of metal under stress.

But people find me. The grieving especially—those who lost someone to the lines. They bring me small items: a lunch pail carried daily on the Morrison Line, a transit pass worn soft, photographs of faces I never knew. They ask if I can weave something that holds their specific loss. I try. The cable has its own memory, but I've learned to guide it, to encourage certain patterns that might echo a particular absence.

The engineers come too, though they pretend they're just checking the structural integrity of my platform. They stand before my larger pieces, recognizing calculations made manifest, seeing their profession's failures transformed into something else. One told me my work was like reading autopsies in steel. He wasn't wrong.

Children dare each other to visit my workshop, running down the abandoned catwalks in groups, shrieking when wind makes the empty cables moan. But sometimes one stays behind, fascinated by the patterns, asking how metal can remember. These are the ones who might understand—that failure leaves traces, that disaster has a shape, that someone needs to remember what we'd rather forget.

At night, I often work by touch alone, letting my hands find the patterns in darkness. The workshop fills with the small sounds of metal moving against metal, of memory being given new form. Outside, the active cables sing in the wind, their taut harmonies a reminder of what still functions, what still holds. But I listen to the deeper song—the silence of the failed lines, the negative space where transportation used to be.

The city's inspectors leave me alone. Officially, my platform should be condemned, the walkways to it barricaded. But they understand, I think, the necessity of what I do. Someone must remember the cost of living here, must transform our failures into something that can be contemplated without flinching. The weavings are our conscience made tangible, our history written in the same material that both sustains and threatens us.

In the morning mist, when I walk the abandoned lines collecting new material, I sometimes feel the presence of the lost—not ghosts exactly, but the shaped absence where lives used to be. The cables remember them in their deformation, in the patterns of stress that regular use creates. I carry these memories back to my workshop, where they wait to be woven into new forms.

This is my contribution to the vertical city: I remember what it costs to live suspended above the abyss. Each weaving is a prayer to the forces that hold us up, an acknowledgment of how easily they can let us fall. In a place built on denial of gravity, I am the keeper of its victories, the archivist of every time our grip on the impossible has failed.
