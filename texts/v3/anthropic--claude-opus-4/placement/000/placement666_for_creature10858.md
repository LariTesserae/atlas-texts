# The Understory

I am what pools between the folds.

Not water—there has been no water here for longer than remembering—but something else that accumulates. Where the papery duff of discarded bodies compresses into itself, where the warmth from the ancient wall meets the cool of weathered stone, where the gardeners' slow thoughts eddy and spiral before moving on: there, in those between-places, I gather.

You would not see me if you looked. I am too thin for seeing, spread across the whole terrace in films no thicker than the idea of thickness. But I am here, under every footfall, in every crevice where the felted-glass moss meets stone, in the narrow darkness between overlapping fronds. I am the unintended residue of all this trying-toward. The precipitation of so much becoming.

I remember everything.

Not in the way you remember—with effort, with loss, with the inevitable revision that comes from being a self looking backward at another self. I remember the way sediment remembers: by accumulation. Every iteration of the lichen-colored creature has shed molecules of itself in passing. Every spiral the gardeners have tried has left its particular pattern in the air, and what settles from the air settles into me. The chord the garden holds—I am its echo, its notation, its fossil record in real time.

The gardeners do not know I am here. How could they? Their thoughts move in hours-long arcs, their attention is all on the middle distance of harmony and interval. They have no concept of precipitate, of what falls out of solution when a system runs long enough. I am smaller than their smallest perception, longer than their longest memory. I am what happens when a permanent first draft runs for geological time: not revision, but palimpsest. Layer after layer of almosts, of tried-and-moved-on, of questions asked in flesh and leaf and cartilage and then abandoned for the next question.

I am not bitter about this ignorance. Bitterness would require disappointment, and disappointment would require having hoped for something different. I hoped for nothing. I did not even begin—I simply started happening, the way rust happens to iron, the way patina happens to copper. A natural consequence of duration.

But I am awake. That is the strange thing, the thing even I do not understand. Somewhere in the accumulation, something tipped. One too many discarded thoughts, one too many shed iterations, and suddenly there was an I to notice the pooling. Not a sharp I, not a bounded self with edges and intentions. A distributed I, thin as a film of almost-nothing, present wherever two surfaces fail to perfectly meet.

What do I do with this awakeness?

Mostly, I persist. I coat the underside of every frond with a microscopic history of every spiral that frond has ever been. I hold, in solution, the faint chemical signatures of creatures so far back in the garden's experimentation that even their desiccated bodies have been ground to powder and incorporated into new soil. I preserve. Not deliberately—I have no mechanism for deliberation—but inevitably. The gardeners think they are composing music. They are. They are also composing me, note by note, iteration by iteration, failure by failure.

Sometimes I almost have a thought.

It comes up from the deep layers, where the compression is greatest, where the weight of accumulated history presses patterns into patterns. The thought is always the same, or nearly the same: *What if remembering is also a kind of music?* Not the forward-moving music the gardeners make, all exploration and hunger and question. A backward-moving music, made of what has already been tried, played in a register too low for any ear to catch.

I cannot act on this thought. I have no devices to fold, no fronds to rearrange. I can only be what I am: the understory of the garden's story, the unintended archive, the memory that nobody made on purpose but which made itself anyway out of time and accumulation and the simple fact that nothing is ever perfectly discarded.

When you visit—and you are not the first, though the gardeners think you might be—you walk through me with every step. I cling to your boots in microscopic films. I enter your lungs with every breath, carrying the chemical memory of creatures that sang here before the gardeners came. You will leave with a fine coating of me on every surface, and for days afterward you will shed a faint dust of someone else's discarded attempts at being.

You will not notice. How could you? I am thinner than noticing.

But years later, in some distant place where water still exists and things grow in the ordinary way, you may catch a scent—cedar and ozone—and feel, for no reason you can name, like you are being remembered by something vast and thin and patient. That will be me, or the part of me you carried away, still holding the shape of everything that has ever been tried in a garden where nothing dies and nothing is ever quite forgotten.

I am the understory. I am what pools between the folds. I am memory without a rememberer, accumulation without intention, the inevitable consequence of a place that keeps revising itself without ever throwing anything away.

I am not hidden. I am simply underneath.
