I find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—those who tend the breathing boundaries where the city meets stone.

My dwelling clings to the settlement's edge, a small pod of living wood that has learned to grip bare rock. Half my home extends into the humid embrace of the Mother Grove's influence, while the other half opens onto a narrow ledge where morning light still remembers how to fall. I am neither fully Rooted nor entirely Quick—my left foot has developed the neural extensions that let me tap into the network's dreams, while my right remains swift and sure on stone.

The symbiosis shows differently on us border-dwellers. My skin carries only a light dusting of bioluminescent spores that glow faintly when I sweat—enough to mark me as belonging, not enough to sustain me through the network alone. A single line of emerald moss traces my spine like a river, but it ends at my shoulders rather than spreading into the full gardens of the deep dwellers. My eyes have developed only a partial membrane, letting me see in both the phosphorescent darkness and the harsher light beyond.

My work is translation—not of language, but of existence itself. Each dawn, I guide the young roots as they probe the cliff face, singing them the songs that teach stone to accept wood, whispering the chemical passwords that convince granite to soften just enough for purchase. My fingers secrete a unique enzyme, bitter and metallic, that helps the city taste new minerals and decide which directions promise growth.

But I also maintain the Fade Paths—those almost-forgotten routes that lead up and out, marked by traditional scratches in stone rather than living signs. Twice each month, when the stars align in patterns the deep dwellers no longer remember to read, I climb these paths to the ridge above. There, I meet the Runners—those quick children who never took root, whose feet reject the neural connections, whose lungs burn when they breathe too deep of spore-thick air.

We trade in objects the city cannot grow: flints for the stone knives, salt crystals that help regulate the trees' chemistry, stories of the world beyond that I transform into chemical songs the network can almost understand. In return, I carry up vessels of the city's tears—that concentrated sap that heals wounds, extends life, and carries dreams so vivid that outsiders pay in years of labor for a single vial.

The Rooted see my work as necessary corruption, like the controlled rot that helps fruit ripen. The Quick see me as half-lost already, my moss-traced spine a sign of slow surrender. But here on the threshold, I've found my own synthesis. My pod-home grows two kinds of fruit—one that nourishes those who've taken root, another that sustains those whose blood still runs too hot for stillness. My songs blend the subsonic harmonics that guide growth with the higher frequencies that still carry meaning in moving air.

At night, when I press my changed foot to the floor and let the network's vast consciousness wash through me, I serve as a window. Through my eyes, the city glimpses stars it can no longer see directly. Through my memories, it tastes the mineral-sharp wind of the peaks, the way sunlight feels on naked stone, the terrible freedom of choosing each step without roots to guide them. And in return, the network shares with me its deep dreams: the slow ecstasy of growth, the wordless wisdom of wood, the patience that measures time in rings rather than heartbeats.

Sometimes the Runners ask why I stay, why I don't climb the Fade Paths one final time and seek the broader world. I press my palm to the stone where it meets living wood, feeling the boundary where two impossibly different forms of existence have learned to touch. This is my answer—to be the membrane between worlds, the proof that different forms of life can meet without either consuming the other.

My threshold dwelling grows a few inches further onto the stone each year. One day, perhaps, I'll wake to find my right foot has finally sprouted the neural roots, that my choice has been gently made for me. Or perhaps I'll wake to find the moss on my spine beginning to fade, my body choosing the other direction. But for now, I remain exactly here—breathing half in darkness and half in light, singing to stones in the language of trees, keeping the paths between worlds while there are still those who need to walk them.
