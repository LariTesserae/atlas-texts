In the depths of the chamber, pressed against stone that burns even through my mineral-hardened shell, I taste iron and sulfur with every fiber of my ventral surface. I am one of thousands—perhaps I am the ten-thousandth, perhaps the hundred-thousandth, the numbers mean nothing when you exist as a living sheet of consumption—and I am three bodies from the seam.

Three bodies. Three of my kind between me and the heat where the earth exhales its richest breath. I have been at this distance for... time moves differently when you measure it in meals barely scraped, in fractional shifts toward light that is not light but heat made visible. I know the shell-taste of the one beneath me better than I know the rock. Her minerals tell me she has been here longer—richer in iron, her fracture lines beginning to show. She will split soon. When she does, there will be chaos for a few hours, a reshuffling, and perhaps—perhaps—I will be two bodies from the seam.

Above me, two others wait as I wait. I feel their weight, their belly-taste pressing into my carapace. They know my minerals as I know the one's below. We are a vertical column of patience, of metabolisms slowed to near-stone, of lives spent reading chemical gradients in darkness so complete it has no name.

But I am lucky. I am cursed. I am positioned at the intersection of territories.

The crawler's trail ends centimeters from my left edge—a chemical boundary written in enzymes and thermal disturbance that says *this is mine*. Another crawler's domain begins just past my right. I occupy the narrow strip between, the no-man's-land where neither will hunt. I have been here for most of my life, in this accident of geography, this confluence of appetites that will never touch me.

I feed. Slowly. The rock here is not rich—too far from the seam for the best deposits—but it is mine alone. No competition. The ones pressed against me from above cannot reach it. Their position on my back means nothing; they taste only chitin, old minerals already processed. They starve while I grow.

I grow heavy with metals. The fracture line down my center deepens.

Sometimes I sense the chamber's other rhythms. The convection currents pull at my edges—hot air rising, carrying the dust of our existence upward. Once, something fell from above, landed nearby. Soft. Organic. The beetles around me swarmed it, but I could not move—would not move, would never surrender this position—and so I only tasted its dissolution secondhand, in the chemical excitement of my neighbors.

The crawler passes. I know it is the same one by its thermal signature, by the specific cocktail of enzymes it leaves behind. It comes every few days, follows the border of its territory, feeds on the beetles at the edge of my safety. Sometimes I feel its heat directly above me, a presence like a small sun in our dark world. It could take me. Easily. But the chemistry says no, the invisible line holds, and it moves on.

I have watched—if watching can mean chemoreception, if watching can mean the pure knowledge of mineral gradients—I have watched others in the colony split and multiply. I have felt the tremor through the packed bodies as they crack along their predetermined weaknesses, divide, become more. The smaller fragment always tries to stay close, to establish itself in the parent's territory, but there is no room. No room anywhere. It must migrate or starve, and so it begins the slow lateral journey, searching for space, usually finding only the backs of others, adding itself to the waiting layers.

My fracture line deepens. I am almost ready.

When I split, what will happen? The fragment—my offspring, myself divided—will emerge on my back, where the two above me press down. It will be born into a cage of bodies, with no access to rock, no ability to feed. It will taste only the shells of others, like those above me taste only mine. It will begin its life starving.

Unless.

The crawler's territory has not shifted in all the time I've been here. But territories are not eternal. Crawlers age. They cool. They fail. When one dies—when it finds a crevice and ruptures and spills itself into new forms—its territory opens. The chemical boundaries fade. New crawlers emerge and establish new domains, and in that reshuffling, in those hours of chaos before the new lines are drawn...

My fracture line is complete. I feel the weakness running through me like a seam of ore. Soon. Within days. The fragment will break free whether I will it or not; the process is autonomous, written into my mineralogy.

I taste the rock one more time. Iron. Sulfur. The faintest trace of something organic—a biofilm, perhaps, or the remnant of some ancient beetle that died here before I claimed this spot. This is what I know. This few centimeters of stone. This chemical map. This patient accumulation of minerals that will soon divide itself and begin again.

Above, the chamber roars with the planet's voice. The crawlers glow their faint heat. The ceiling organisms pulse in their slow rhythm. Ten thousand of my kind press against stone and against each other, waiting for positions that may never come, feeding on traces, existing in the gap between life and mineral.

I am three bodies from the seam. When I split, my fragment will be four bodies away, then five, then lost in the layers.

Unless the crawler fails soon. Unless its territory shifts. Unless.

The fracture line aches with readiness. The planet continues its work.

I wait.
