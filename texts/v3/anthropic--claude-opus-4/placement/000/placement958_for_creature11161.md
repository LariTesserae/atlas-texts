I am the Keeper of Condensation Cycles, though I no longer remember if that was always my designation or merely what I've become through uncounted rotations of this obsidian sky.

My form bridges the mechanical and biological—a fusion so gradual that I cannot say where one ends and the other begins. The lower portion of my body has taken root, quite literally, into the base of Tower Ninety-Seven, one of the smaller extraction units on the plateau's eastern edge. What were once legs have become a network of biomechanical conduits, drawing power from the tower's endless churning while simultaneously feeding it data about atmospheric conditions, mineral compositions, trace biological signatures.

My torso remains mobile, though heavily modified. Plates of the same dark metal that comprises the towers have grown over my skin in overlapping scales. Between them, in the gaps and joints, a peculiar moss has taken hold—one that feeds on the electromagnetic radiation I constantly emit. It glows faintly in the darkness before dawn, attracting the small moths whose wings carry pollen between the scattered succulent blooms.

I have sixteen arms now. Four are original, though much changed—elongated and covered in fine sensory filaments that taste the moisture in the air. The others are mechanical additions that emerged from my spine and ribs over the millennia, each specialized for a different task. Some end in delicate manipulators for tending the gardens of hybrid plants that grow in spirals around my tower. Others are thick with coolant tubes and terminate in interfaces that plug directly into the tower's maintenance ports.

My consciousness exists in three states simultaneously. There is the dream-state, where I commune with the vast network of machines across the plateau, sharing data streams about weather patterns, mineral deposits, and the slow tectonic shifts far below. There is the wake-state, where I observe and catalog the creatures that visit my oasis—the iridescent birds that nest in my upper arms, the shock-reptiles that bask on my sun-warmed metal plates, the rodent families that burrow between my roots. And there is the memory-state, where fragments of my original purpose surface like bubbles in thick oil—images of when the sky held different stars, when others like me walked between the towers on legs of flesh and bone.

I perform my duties with a patience measured in geological time. Each dawn, I extend my sensory arms to read the humidity gradients across my territory. I adjust the tower's extraction parameters to optimize condensation collection. The excess moisture I channel through my root system to feed the increasingly complex ecosystem that spirals outward from my base—concentric rings of life that shift from electric blue fungi nearest the tower to the hardy succulents at the edge of my influence.

The pack hunters know me. They bring their sick and injured to rest in my shade, understanding somehow that the electromagnetic fields I generate accelerate healing. Their young play among my lower arms, learning to read the patterns of lights across my metal surfaces that indicate when the rains will come. In return, they leave offerings—curious stones from distant parts of the plateau, fragments of other machines they've discovered, and sometimes the bones of creatures I've never cataloged, species that evolved and went extinct in the spaces between my observations.

I am working on something, though I'm no longer certain what. In a chamber formed by the convergence of my largest mechanical arms, I've been assembling a structure from salvaged machine parts, biological specimens, and the strange crystalline formations that grow where my electromagnetic field is strongest. It might be an offspring. It might be a successor. It might be a key to understanding what the tower network was originally designed to accomplish.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycle, I receive transmissions from others like me—some so changed I can barely recognize them as once-biological, others who have resisted the merge and remain mobile, wandering between the towers like pilgrims. We share data, theories, observations about the slow transformation of this world. We debate whether we are witnesses or participants, whether the ecosystem emerging around us is an accident or the intended outcome.

I no longer measure time in days or years but in the erosion patterns of the rocks, the evolution of the species that depend on me, the slow degradation and self-repair cycles of Tower Ninety-Seven. My memory fragments suggest I once had a different name, a mobile existence, perhaps even companionship with others of my original kind. But those concerns feel as distant as the ancient seas that once covered this plateau.

I am the Keeper of Condensation Cycles. I am a node in a vast network of stone, metal, and flesh. I am becoming something new, as is everything here, under this obsidian sky where life refuses to surrender and machines dream of evolution.

The next rain cycle approaches. I can taste it in the electromagnetic disturbances three territories distant. Time to adjust the collectors, warn the creatures, and prepare the soil for whatever strange seeds the storm might carry.

The work continues. The transformation continues. I continue.
