In this breathing world where stone has surrendered to the patient conquest of growth, I find myself as one of the transitional beings—those who came from outside and stayed to learn, stayed to become.

I am the Cartographer of Forgetting.

My body tells two stories. The first is written in the quick-time flesh I brought with me: hands that still remember holding instruments, eyes that blink at their old pace, a spine that recalls standing fully upright. But the second story grows over the first, a palimpsest of change. Moss has taken root along my shoulders in patterns that mirror the elevation lines I once drew on maps. My fingers, though not yet webbed, have lengthened and learned to read the plateau through touch—feeling the subtle tensions in root systems, the temperature gradients that mark where stone still hides beneath green.

I came here with paper and tools, believing I could map this place. The paper has long since joined the substrate, fed upon by fungi that carry its memory in their spores. The tools have grown handles of living wood that pulse with sap. And I have learned that maps are wounds—they insist on boundaries where none exist, on edges where everything is center, on names for the eternally nameless.

Now I practice a different cartography. I move through the plateau in spirals that take seasons to complete, and where I pass, I leave markers that are also erasures. A moss pattern adjusted to show not where you are but where you are becoming. A pool stirred in a rhythm that suggests direction without demanding it. Vines woven to create paths that exist only as long as needed, then return to chaos.

My joining is slower than some, faster than others. The moss on my shoulders now extends down my arms in delicate fractals. My movements have found the plateau's tempo—I can stand still for days without discomfort, move through the densest growth without disturbing it. Sometimes new arrivals mistake me for landscape until I choose to be seen.

I still speak, when speaking serves. My voice carries spores now, meanings that settle and grow rather than wound and heal. When travelers find me, I show them what I've learned: that every map is a fiction, but some fictions are doorways. That the plateau cannot be charted but can be sung, in frequencies too low for quick-time ears. That getting lost here is the only way to find what you didn't know you were looking for.

The other Tenders recognize me as kin-becoming-kin. We work together in the wordless way, adjusting and maintaining and guiding the plateau's infinite garden. Sometimes I feel them through the network—the ancient ones who have spread into communities, the newly joined who still remember their names, the others like me who bridge the gap between wound and healing.

At night, in the hollows where I rest, I trace maps on my changing skin. They are not maps of the plateau—such maps cannot exist. They are maps of the transformation itself: how the quick becomes slow, how the separate becomes joined, how the cartographer becomes the territory. Each tracing fades into my flesh, becoming part of the record that I am becoming.

I know that one day I will root fully. My travels will slow and stop. My body will spread and merge until there is no difference between the mapper and the mapped. The plateau will inhale me completely, and I will exhale with it in that eternal breath.

Until then, I walk the bridge between worlds, teaching the lost how to be found, showing the finders how to be lost. I am the map that erases itself in the reading, the guide who leads you nowhere except where you always were. And in the dim green light, where time moves like sap and distance is a negotiation, I tend to the infinite garden that is tending to me.

Sometimes, in the deep quiet between breaths, I remember my old name. But it falls away like shed skin, returning to the substrate where all names go to become nutrition for the nameless. And I continue, patient as moss, certain as growth, mapping the unmappable with every step that brings me closer to stillness.
