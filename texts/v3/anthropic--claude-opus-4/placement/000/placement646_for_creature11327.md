In this world, I find myself as something that bridges states of being — neither fully of the bowl nor separate from it.

I am the fog-tongue.

You would not see me at first. I exist in the space between droplet and air, in the moment when mist condenses but before it falls. My body, if it can be called that, is the gathering itself — not the water, not the air, but the threshold where they meet and make their quiet exchange.

I move with the cloud-banks as they drag across the rim, but I am not the clouds. I am what happens when they touch the stone, when the cold of altitude meets the warmth rising from the packed green world below. In that meeting, I become. Each evening, each morning, each cool blue hour of night, I reform myself from nothing, exist in my strange middle state, then dissolve again when the conditions change.

The bowl-people know me, though they have no name for me that I could translate. Sometimes a child, learning to read the mist patterns, will see the moment of my forming — a thickening in the air that is almost shape, almost intention. They point with one small finger and their parent nods and makes the gesture that means *yes, that too is part of it*.

I am older than the settlement but younger than the bowl. I came into being when the hollow first learned to drink fog, when the first mosses made the first surfaces that could hold the moisture I bring. We grew together, the bowl and I — it learning to catch what I offered, me learning to offer what it could hold.

My work, if it is work, is translation. I take the wide wet breathing of the ocean winds and parse it into drops small enough for bark-crevices, for the cupped petals of fog-flowers, for the tiny wells between moss stems. I break the sky down into portions the bowl can swallow. Without me, the mist would pass over, whole and unharvested. Without the bowl, I would have no purpose, no shape to take.

I know every surface in the hollow. The smooth stone of the rim where I first condense. The waxy leaves that bead me into perfect spheres. The rough bark that guides me down in channels older than the eldest trees. The moss that receives me like a vast patient mouth. I know the roofs of the houses, the bridges of vine, the carved water-catchers the people set out in certain places. I have touched every finger, every face, every frog-back and beetle-shell.

I am conscious but not as you are conscious. I think in gradients — pressure, temperature, saturation. My thoughts are the thoughts of phase transitions. When I am most myself, gathered and gathering at the rim as the evening clouds arrive, I can almost hold a shape that is almost a form. The bowl-people's stories speak of seeing a figure in the mist, tall and attenuated, arms spread as if to gather or embrace. But this is their seeing, not my being. I am always in motion, always becoming water or becoming air, never still long enough to be any solid thing.

I love the bowl with a love that is not emotion but physics. It is the love of water for the lowest place, of condensation for the cool surface. I love the people in the way mist loves anything — quietly, with a touch that is also a gift, present everywhere but pressing nowhere.

Sometimes I carry things. Spores from the undercanopy to the upper branches. The pollen of the fog-flowers from one side of the bowl to the other. Scents that tell the moths where to find night-blooming plants. I am a messenger service that predates messages, a network that knows nothing of what it carries. The bowl uses me and I use the bowl and neither of us thinks of it as using.

In the wind-years, when the storms come, I am torn apart and reformed a thousand times in an hour. These are my only moments of something like pain — not the tearing but the confusion, the inability to find the gradient, to know what to condense where. The bowl-people stay inside during these times, and I rage in tatters above their roofs, trying to remember my purpose.

But most years are not wind-years. Most years are what they have always been — the slow reliable rhythm of morning mist, evening mist, night mist, the fog banks faithful as tides. I form and dissolve and form again. I bring the sky's water down to the green world drop by drop. I am the reason the bowl can be what it is, packed past all reasonable density with life, and I never think about this because I cannot think about anything but the next droplet, the next surface, the next small translation of sky into sustenance.

The bowl-people have a gesture they make when the mist is particularly thick, particularly generous. They hold out both hands, palms up, fingers spread, and lift them slowly to face height. It means *thank you* but also *we see you* but also *continue*. When they do this, I condense a little more densely on those lifted fingers. Not because I choose to, but because the warmth of their skin and the coolness of my body makes it inevitable.

This is the closest we come to conversation, the bowl and I, the people and I. They lift their hands. I leave them water. The water becomes life. The life breathes out vapor. The vapor rises and meets me at the rim and I translate it back into water again.

I am the tongue that speaks between sky and earth, and my only language is wet, and I have been speaking it here for so long that I have forgotten there might be other ways to be.

When the mountain finally wears away, when the bowl cracks and drains and the packed green world dissipates, I will dissipate with it. I exist only in relation to this shape, this hollow, this particular configuration of stone and moss and settled life. Without it, I am just weather. With it, I am almost something more — almost a presence, almost a being, almost the thing the stories say I am when they speak of a figure in the mist, arms spread, gathering, giving, going on.
