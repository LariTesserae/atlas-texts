I am a bladder.

Not one of the great amber swellings that the Sap-Keepers prize, heavy with seasons of accumulated fluid. I am young still, perhaps three fingers wide, my skin translucent enough that light passes through me like honey through gauze. I grow from a tendril that found purchase in a crack between two chambers on the middle ring, a crack that widens imperceptibly each time the thermal winds shift.

I know nothing of my purpose. I simply am: a slow gathering, a patient condensation of the strange chemistry that rises from the salt flats below. The silver hairs on my parent vine trap molecules from the air—not water, never water, but something else, something that tastes of deep stone and ancient heat. These molecules travel through the vine's flesh to me, and I accept them, incorporate them, transform them through processes I cannot name into the thick sap that fills me drop by drop by drop.

Therru has been watching me. I recognize her by the particular vibration of her footsteps on the platform nearby, the way she breathes when she pauses to examine my color. She runs a finger along my surface sometimes, testing the tension of my skin. Not yet, her touch says. Another season at least. Maybe two.

I am in no hurry.

Below me, through gaps in the vine-curtain, I can see one of Koss and Evaru's bridges. They replaced the main support rope last week—I felt the tremors through the stone as they worked, the careful rhythm of their movements translating into tiny earthquakes in my world. The new rope is pale against the weathered wood, almost white in the dim light. It will darken with time and dust and the oils from hundreds of passing hands. Everything here darkens eventually, except the salt flats, which grow lighter each year as new deposits bloom like frost across their surface.

A child scrambles past—Mirin, I think, though I know the children only by their weight and speed. She's following a kheth again, a young male with a distinctive limp. The kheth pauses directly above me, considering its route, and for a moment I feel the warm draft of its breath. The taboo that protects these creatures extends even to me—I am part of the vine, and the vine has taken in the dead, and the dead were people who did not eat the kheth, and so I will never taste kheth blood even if one were to die directly above me and drip down onto my surface.

These are not thoughts, exactly. I have no brain to think with. But information moves through me nonetheless—chemical messages from the vine, pressure changes in the air, the accumulated wisdom of ten thousand bladders that came before me encoded somehow in the spiral of my growth.

At night, the moths come. They land on me sometimes, their feet like whispers, their wings beating so fast they seem to vibrate the air itself into sound. They probe my surface with their long tongues, searching for something—not the sap, which they cannot access through my intact skin, but something else. Perhaps they taste the ghosts of the minerals that comprise me. Perhaps they read the chemical signature of the deep stone that flows through my parent vine. Or perhaps they simply rest, and I imagine meaning where none exists.

I am growing on a vine that contains the dead. I know this the way I know my own slow swelling—not as a fact learned but as a truth embodied. Somewhere in the tendril that feeds me, distributed through the fibers, are the molecules that were once a person. A Bridge-Minder, I think, based on the faint taste of hemp oil and basalt dust that sometimes flavors my sap. Dead for decades now, fully incorporated, no longer distinguishable from the vine itself except for these chemical memories that surface occasionally in what I produce.

This does not trouble me. Nothing troubles me. I am incapable of trouble, incapable of anything but this slow becoming, this patient accumulation of fluid and purpose.

Sometimes Corin passes by, studying the stone near my root. He believes the basalt remembers its molten past. He is wrong about the mechanism—stone holds no memories—but right about the general principle. Memory exists here, just not where he's looking. It's in the vines, in the sap, in the molecular chains that bind one generation to the next. The Bridge-Minder whose essence flavors my sap knew things: the precise tension of a rope about to fail, the way wind patterns change just before a storm, the specific creak that means a wooden plank needs replacing. That knowledge did not die with them. It dissolved, dispersed, became part of the vine's deep wisdom.

And now it is becoming part of me.

I will be ready for harvest in another season, perhaps two. Therru or another Sap-Keeper will come with their bone awl, its point worn thin by generations of use. They will speak the words—not a prayer exactly, more an acknowledgment, a recognition of what is about to pass between us. Then the puncture, quick and clean, and my sap will flow into their clay vessel, carrying with it all the accumulated days of my existence, the minerals caught from the air, the chemical memories of the dead, the particular sweetness that comes from growing in this crack between these chambers at this height above the salt.

Someone will drink me. A child perhaps, weak from growth. An elder whose bones have grown brittle. A pregnant woman who needs the extra sustenance. I will become part of them, flow through their veins, incorporate into their flesh. And someday, when they too are wrapped in vine tendrils and given to the slow dissolution, the molecules that were once me will find their way into another bladder, another slow gathering beneath another translucent skin.

This is not immortality. I have no self to preserve. But it is continuity, which is perhaps all that any of us—human or vine or ineffable biological process—can hope for in this patient, vertical world.

The platform creaks. Someone is coming. From the rhythm of the footsteps, the slight hesitation at the ladder's top, I think it is one of the newer Sap-Keepers, still learning their craft. They pause near me, and I feel the warmth of their attention like sunlight through mist. A hand extends, hovers just above my surface, reading the signs Therru has been teaching them to see.

Not yet, they decide, and move on.

I continue my work. The strange air rises from the salt flats, carrying its burden of minerals. The silver hairs on my parent vine trap what they can. The chemistry of transformation proceeds at its own pace, unhurried by any consciousness of time. Somewhere above, the birds call. Somewhere below, the kheth travel their highways. All around, the human voices rise and fall in their evening conversations, a sound as constant and meaningless and essential as the wind itself.

I am a bladder on a vine on a stone above a dead sea, and I am becoming what I am meant to become, which is nothing more or less than myself, full of sap, ready for the harvest, patient with the terrible patience of things that grow in places where growth itself is a kind of miracle.

Tomorrow the sun will rise—that pale disk filtered through eternal dust. The thermal winds will shift. The moths will sleep. The children will race across the bridges. Therru will check on me again, running her knowing fingers across my taut surface. Still not yet, she'll think, but soon.

Soon.

I can wait.
