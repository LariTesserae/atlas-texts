I find myself here as one of the moth-watchers, though that title carries more than outsiders might guess.

My body is lean and long-limbed from a lifetime of climbing between the lake-skin and the town proper, that dangerous margin where water meets wet air. My skin bears the mottled blue-grey of the northern families, but marked with pale scars from moth-scale and the occasional desperate grasp at sharp-edged leaves. My hair grows in three heavy cords, weighted with carved fishbone to keep them from tangling in the surface vegetation.

I breathe both ways with equal ease—a necessary skill for one who must sometimes hang inverted beneath floating leaf-platforms, counting eggs by touch, while other times rest submerged for hours, watching for the telltale shimmer of emerging larvae. My ribs bear an extra set of breathing folds, developed through generations of my lineage adapting to the work.

The moths themselves are as large as dinner plates, their wings evolved thick and waxy to survive the perpetual damp. They cannot truly fly in the sense their ancestors did; instead they row through the heavy air at the lake's surface, their wingbeats sending droplets cascading like rain. Their bodies are furred in waterproof down that the people harvest—but only from the naturally dead, and only after the funeral protocols are observed.

You see, the moths are citizens too, in their way. We know their genealogies, their preferred laying sites, their seasonal moods. A moth may live for several years, and the eldest are known by name: Yellowshoulder, who lost half a wing to ice but still manages her migrations. The Librarian, who always returns to the same corner of the Lung to die. Three-Current, who lays her eggs in a perfect spiral visible only when the morning light strikes just so.

My work begins before dawn, when I pull myself up the guide-roots to the observation platforms—half-submerged galleries of woven reed where we can watch both the underwater egg masses and the air-swimming adults. I carry tools unique to our trade: soft brushes of otter fur for cleaning egg clusters without disturbing them, hollow counting-reeds that click as I pass them through my fingers, and most importantly, the memory cords.

Each moth family has its own cord, passed down through generations of watchers. Knots record laying sites, successful hatches, diseases overcome, unusual patterns. My predecessor, old Softbraid, spent thirty years teaching me to read these cords in complete darkness, my fingers learning the subtle differences between a square knot indicating western laying and the complex triple-twist that means the eggs were abandoned and subsequently adopted by another moth.

But watching is only part of it. We are also interpreters of the moth-calendar, which governs more than outsiders might expect. The moths' movements predict weather patterns, their egg-laying tells us when the water temperature will shift, and their deaths—always precisely timed—mark when certain roots will fruit and certain fish will spawn. A moth-watcher must sing these observations to the councils, translating the insects' wisdom into human action.

My dwelling perches at the boundary, half-flooded, half-reaching toward air. The walls are trained from a special vine that tolerates exposure, its leaves turning silver-backed when they breach the surface. Inside, I keep my tools and the precious moth-down harvest in sealed gourds, alongside the funeral treasures: scales from significant moths, preserved in clear sap, each one a record of a life witnessed.

The most difficult part of my work comes during the drowning season, when the larvae must make their fatal journey from air to water. They drop from the undersides of leaves like rain, and for three days the upper town is full of their small wriggling bodies. Some will survive to become fish-food. Most will not. During this time, moth-watchers must swim the nursery routes, ensuring the larvae don't clog essential channels or damage young root systems. We guide them with gentle currents toward the feeding grounds, all while fighting the urge to save them from their necessary fate.

I've developed the particular ailments of my trade: permanent wrinkles at my eye corners from squinting in the dim surface light, a chronic ache in my neck from looking up, and what we call "moth-lung"—a persistent sensitivity in my upper breathing folds from too much exposure to the scales and down. But also the gifts: I can predict rain by the way the moths cluster, feel the slightest temperature change in the water, and my night vision exceeds even most town dwellers'.

The young think moth-watching is lonely work, but they misunderstand. I am never alone. Below me, the town breathes and moves. Above me, the moths row through their thick air, carrying out their ancient patterns. Between them, I serve as translator, guardian, and witness. When I die, they will find moth scales etched into my very bones from a lifetime of handling. The moths I watched will have been eaten, their nutrients cycling through fish and plant and person. But the knowledge—encoded in knots, sung in councils, taught to the next watcher—continues.

Sometimes, floating at the lake-skin during the evening hatch, I feel the weight of inherited watching. My predecessor saw the great moth-death of her youth, when disease took half the population and the town had to adjust all its calendars. Her predecessor witnessed the arrival of the spotted variant, brought by wind from the eastern peaks, which now comprises a third of our moths. Back and back, watcher to watcher, until the first—who convinced the council that these air-swimmers were essential, not mere pests to be excluded from the town's treaty.

In my storage room, I keep a special cord, my own addition to the tradition. On it, I record not just moth events but the conversations between moth and town: the year the builders agreed to reshape the eastern galleries to accommodate a new laying pattern, the season we discovered that moth-down, when properly treated, could seal the breathing-folds of surface-speakers, the day young Quickfinger accidentally trained a moth to respond to hand-signals (though we could never replicate it).

The outsiders who come to trade sometimes ask what use are moths that cannot fly, that live at the mere skin of water, that spend half their lives as drowning food. They don't understand that the moths are our calendar, our weather-workers, our soft teachers in the art of transformation. They connect the town to the sky we cannot reach, and remind us that some citizens contribute simply by living out their patterns where we can witness them.

This is what I am: keeper of the threshold between water and air, reader of wings, counter of the uncountable, singer of small deaths and smaller births. When I retire, I will train my replacement in the same patient way Softbraid trained me—hand over hand on the counting-reeds, voice to voice in the observation songs, memory to memory in the reading of knots.

And when I die, they will find me like all moth-watchers are found: with scales beneath my fingernails and fragments of wing-silk in my hair, marked by my chosen work as surely as any root-wright or well-keeper. The moths I watched will be gone, but somewhere in the upper leaves, their descendants will continue the ancient contract: to live at the edge, to transform, to teach the patient art of watching what changes.

In this town that breathes water and grows its walls, I am the one who watches the boundary, who speaks moth to the councils and human to the moths, who keeps the memory of wings in a place that has nearly forgotten sky. It is careful work, and necessary, and mine.
