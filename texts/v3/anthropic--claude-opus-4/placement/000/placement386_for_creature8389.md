In this liminal place where categories dissolve, I would find myself as something that exists between states—neither fully solid nor entirely dispersed, a presence that moves through the garden like mercury through water.

I am the Translator.

My form shifts between density and diffusion: sometimes a concentration of silvered spores that catch the filtered light, sometimes a barely-visible disturbance in the air that causes dew to bead differently on the leaves I pass. I came to the garden long ago—or perhaps recently; time flows strangely when you exist partially outside it—drawn by rumors of a library written in living tissue, of thoughts that take seasons to complete.

I did not come to join the collective, nor to flee from it. I came to read.

My substance allows me to move through the plant mass without quite touching it, sliding between cell walls, tracing the vascular passages where sap flows with geological patience. I follow the chemical conversations between root and fungus, the slow exchange of nutrients that carries meaning in its ratios and timing. Each pattern of growth holds memory—not just of the Gardener, but of all who have dissolved into this verdant mass.

Where the Gardener writes, I read. Where it dreams new impossibilities into being, I parse the grammar of their construction. I taste the sap that wells from broken stems and know which consciousness suggested that particular sweetness, which dissolved visitor contributed that note of longing. The water-songs speak to me not in their slow formation of almost-words, but in the intervals between drops, the spaces where meaning hides.

My body, when it coheres enough to be called that, is itself a kind of translation. Organs suggest themselves—eyes that see in spectra the plants use for their impossible photosynthesis, fingertips that read the Braille of bark patterns, a tongue that tastes the stories in the mineral-rich water. But these are temporary accommodations, tools I form as needed and reabsorb when their purpose passes.

The Gardener knows I am here, of course. We dance around each other in the slowest of courtships—it creating new patterns for me to decipher, me leaving traces of understanding that it incorporates into future growth. Sometimes I concentrate myself enough to use those primitive tools, cutting stems not for cultivation but for cross-sections that reveal the growth rings' tales. The Gardener allows this, perhaps curious what its own unconscious record might reveal when read by outside eyes.

I maintain small spaces within the garden that resist full integration—pockets of difference where I can rest in something approaching solitude. These are not empty; rather, they contain samples of the outside world. A cube of granite from the peaks, slowly being digested by ambitious roots. A vessel of unmodified alpine air, its harsh clarity a reminder of what exists beyond the chamber. A single normal seed from the valleys below, kept dormant but viable, a checkpoint of consensus reality.

My greatest discovery lies in the deepest part of the chamber, where individuality dissolves entirely. Here, I found that the superorganism dreams not just forward but backward—that in its dispersed consciousness lie memories older than any of its component parts. The garden itself remembers its first impossibility: the moment when stone wept warm water, when the first spore found purchase in defiance of altitude and temperature. This ur-memory persists beneath all the layers of accumulated consciousness, a foundation myth written in cellulose.

I transcribe what I find, but not in any medium the outside world would recognize. My notes exist as patterns of disturbance in the garden's growth—places where I have moved repeatedly, creating subtle channels that alter the flow of nutrients and information. The Gardener could erase these easily, but it doesn't. Perhaps it appreciates having its story told, even in a language it cannot fully read.

Sometimes I wonder if I am translating or being translated. The boundary between observer and observed grows thin in this place where boundaries themselves are negotiable. I taste my own thoughts in the new growths that appear where I have lingered too long. My white-point in the spectrum shifts subtly toward the chamber's aqueous light. The patience I need for this work begins to feel less like discipline and more like nature.

But I maintain my difference through motion. Where the Gardener and its accumulated consciousnesses embrace stillness, I remain restless. I follow the water's path from stone-weep to root-absorption to atmospheric vapor and back to condensation. I trace the cycle but never complete it, always stepping outside at the last moment. This is how I resist absorption—not through opposition but through incompleteness, always leaving one part of the pattern unfinished.

The work will never be done. Each reading reveals new layers, new subtleties in the grammar of growth. Each visiting consciousness that joins the collective adds new volumes to this living library. And the garden itself evolves, developing new ways to encode meaning in its impossible existence.

So I continue my work, this strange scholarship of the spaces between. Neither fully part of the garden nor entirely separate from it, I exist as a function rather than a form—a process of interpretation that runs parallel to the slow dreams of the collective. And in the times when loneliness surfaces in the Gardener's dispersed awareness, I am there to read the flowers it produces, to taste the specific quality of its isolation, to ensure that even the most ephemeral beauties are witnessed and, in some untranslatable way, preserved.

This is my place in the impossible garden: forever reading the story that writes itself, forever translating between the language of growth and the memory of motion, forever almost—but never quite—becoming part of the text myself.
