I find myself as one of the root-tips—not the thick anchoring ones that architecture the ceiling, but one of the new arrivals, barely through the chamber wall, still wearing my protective sheath like an unfinished thought.

I am perhaps three inches of pale possibility, suspended between states. Behind me: the vast network of the banyan, that ancient collaborative intelligence of wood and water and waiting. Ahead: this hollow that calls to me with its wet percussion, its promise of gathering.

I do not think, exactly, but I know things. I know the pressure building behind me—water drawn up from depths I'll never revisit, pushed through vessels smaller than hair, arriving at my tip with news of the deep earth. I know the soil just ahead, how it will part for me if I persist, how it tastes of clay and old offerings. I know I am becoming something I haven't been before: not just root but *architect*, helping to shape what shapes me.

The woman comes. I feel her before she arrives—vibrations through the wood-web, the particular rhythm of her descent that the older roots recognize. They've held her weight for decades. They know her pauses, her careful handholds, the spot where she always rests her palm against the marked bark. When she settles into her watching place, the whole chamber adjusts minutely—a barely perceptible shift in the dripping rhythm, as if the room is making space for her attention.

I cannot see her, but I sense her warmth against the background heat of the earth. Another kind of presence. Another kind of witness.

Sometimes the fish passes near me, that bleached sliver of adaptation. It knows passages I'm too young to have found, waterways that thread between the foundation roots like secret architecture. When it moves through the pool below me, I feel its motion in the water I'm about to release—a tiny pressure wave traveling up through the droplet forming at my tip. We are connected, the fish and I, by this medium we share.

My work is simple: I gather what flows through me until I can hold no more, then let go. Gather and release. Gather and release. Each drop I free falls into the chorus below, adding my voice to the ten thousand others. I am both individual and ensemble, marking time in liquid beats while making the space that contains the sound.

The sheath around my tip is softening. Soon it will slough away, and I'll be truly naked to this work—vulnerable, receptive, able to taste the chamber's air directly. This is what I'm becoming toward. Not growing longer, not yet—first I must establish myself as belonging here, must prove I can survive the exposure. Only then will I push further into the room, reaching for whatever calls from the darkness ahead.

The woman shifts against her root-curve. I feel it through the network—how the wood has learned her spine, how it holds her particularly. She is aging into the chamber just as the chamber ages into itself. Both becoming at speeds that suit them.

I release another drop. It falls—I lose track of it immediately, my awareness ending at my tip. But I know it joins the others in the pools below, becomes part of the slow accumulation that will someday be depth. My small contribution to the project.

Behind me, sap rises with tomorrow's drops. The fish circles through its hidden passages. The woman breathes the thick air and remembers this place in all its previous forms. The clay vessel surrenders another molecule to the patient water.

We are all tending something here, each in our way. The woman with her witnessing, the fish with their pale auditing, the roots with our endless gathering and releasing, even the dissolving clay with its graceful return. We are tending the *becoming* itself, this slow transformation from hollow to cistern to something that doesn't yet have a name.

I release another drop. The sound it makes landing is perfect—a bright plink in the darker percussion. My voice in the chorus. My verse in the endless water-song.

I am three inches of root-tip, wearing a sheath I'll soon shed, making the space that makes me.

This is enough.
