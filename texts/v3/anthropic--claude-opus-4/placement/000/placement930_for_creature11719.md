# The Moss-Walker

I am movement measured in seasons.

My name—if I still need such quick things—was Elena, though the moss has been teaching me slower syllables. El-e-na. Each vowel now takes a day to think.

I came here as an engineer. The Company sent teams of us up to service the water-lift stations, to maintain the great pumps that feed the lowland cities. Six months on, six months off. Good pay. Hazard pay, though they never explained the hazard.

I understand now. The hazard was not danger. The hazard was peace.

My first rotation, I hated the noise. Wore dampeners, then double dampeners. Shouted myself hoarse trying to communicate over the roar. My second rotation, I stopped fighting it. Let it into my chest, my skull, my marrow. By my third, I could hear the harmonics—not just the machine-sound but the echo, the reply, the conversation between iron and flesh-that-was-becoming-not-flesh.

I was station chief by then. Had a team under me. Good kids, eager, loud the way I'd been loud. They talked about their plans for the off-months—holidays to warmer places, investments, families started or sustained. I nodded and said the right things and felt my words getting slower.

My fourth rotation, I started walking the moss during off-hours. No destination. Just following the almost-paths where Tenders had dragged their patient bodies. My boots stayed wet. My socks grew a pale fungus I couldn't quite bring myself to treat. It didn't hurt. It felt like belonging.

My fifth rotation, Hendricks found me one morning. I'd been gone three days. Was sitting in the moss about a mile from Station Seven, watching a Tender fix a hairline crack in a feeder pipe. Watching it settle over the break like a grandmother settling into her favorite chair. Slow. Sure. Permanent.

"Chief," Hendricks said. "Chief, you missed shift."

Had I? The concept of "shift" seemed suddenly hilarious. The Tender would be here for months, maybe years. When you measure time like that, what's a shift?

They sent me down after that. Mental health leave. The lowlands felt wrong—all that exposed rock, all those quick shadows of things that moved in minutes instead of months. The absence of the roar left a hollow in my chest that no amount of city noise could fill. I lasted two months before I put in for another rotation. They were short-staffed. They said yes.

My sixth rotation, I knew.

Knew it the moment the transport crested the plateau and I could see the sky-lit fog. Knew it the way you know you're home. I did my job—checked the pipes, maintained the pumps, filed the reports. But I also started... staying. An extra hour here, examining a joint. An afternoon there, tracing a suspicious sound. Then overnight, to monitor a pressure anomaly that didn't really need monitoring.

Rodriguez, my second, covered for me at first. "Chief's in the field," she'd tell the others. Which was true. I was in the field, sinking into it, the moss climbing my boots, my calves, reaching for my knees.

The night I decided, there was no drama. No revelation. I simply walked out of Station Seven with a thermos of tea (unnecessary—I wasn't thirsty anymore, not in the usual way) and found a place where three large pipes converged. An important junction. It would need tending, eventually. All junctions do.

I sat down in the moss. It gave beneath me, wet and welcoming. The roar was loudest here—not painful, just present, like my own heartbeat. I drank the tea slowly, tasting metals that shouldn't have been in it. Iron. Copper. The taste of the works itself, seeping through everything.

When the thermos was empty, I lay back.

The first days were strange. I could still think in words, but they came slower, like speaking through honey. My body felt heavy, then heavier, then so heavy that lifting my hand became a day's work. The moss grew over my boots first. I watched it happen, fascinated by the pace—visible if you paid attention, invisible if you blinked.

Rodriguez found me on day four. Stood at a respectful distance, the way you stand at a hospital bed.

"Elena?" she said. Then, softer: "Chief?"

I managed to turn my head. Even that took an hour. She waited. That's how I knew she understood—she waited.

"The junction," I said, though it came out as more of a long breath shaped vaguely like words. "Needs. Tending."

She looked at where I was looking. At the place where two pipes met, the joint already showing a faint oxidation that would, in five years, ten years, become a problem.

"I'll note it in the log," she said. Professional to the end.

I wanted to laugh but laughing is a quick thing and I was learning slowness. She left. Others came, over the next weeks. Some upset, some curious, some with the look of people who'd been expecting this. They stopped coming eventually. The moss had covered everything but my face by then, and my face was changing, features softening into the general green.

That was... I've lost count. Time moves differently when you are both the clock and the thing being measured. Seasons have passed. The junction I came to tend has developed the crack I knew it would. I've moved—am moving—toward it. Another year and I'll reach it. Another five and I'll have it sealed, the moss of my body worked into the gap, the iron incorporated into what I'm becoming.

I am not Elena anymore, not exactly. But I'm not not-Elena either. I'm the space between the two, measured in moss-time, spoken in the frequencies of running water and vibrating pipe. My thoughts move like sap now, sweet and necessary and slow. I dream in oxidation rates and pressure differentials. My body is less body than process—water moving through me, minerals depositing and dissolving, the great work of keeping the works working.

Sometimes the new crews see me. A hummock where no hummock should be. A place where the moss grows thicker, taller, more deliberate. They step around me, politely, the way Rodriguez taught them to. Once, someone left flowers. They were absorbed in a season, became part of me, their quick beauty translated into the longer beauty of function.

I remember wanting things, before. Promotion. Recognition. Love, maybe. Now I want only what the moss wants, what the water wants, what the junction needs. To tend. To be tended. To hold the threshold between working and not-working, between flow and failure.

The roar is inside me now. Not heard but felt, the way a bell feels its own ringing. I broadcast it back changed, Elena-flavored, engineer-specific. My frequency added to the chorus of all the others who came before, who found in the moss a mathematics more patient than any human calculation.

There is a young woman on the current crew—Kim? Kelly?—who walks the moss after shifts. Who lets her boots stay wet. Who listens to the roar with her mouth slightly open, as if tasting it.

I grow a little taller when she passes. Not greeting. Too quick, too human still. Just acknowledgment. Tender to potential Tender. The moss knows its own.

She'll decide one day. Or she won't. The plateau is patient. The works needs many things, but it does not need us to need it. We come to it on our own time, in our own seasons, bringing our small store of years to add to its centuries.

The junction is close now. Another half-season and I'll reach it. The crack has widened by a millimeter, exactly as predicted. My body has grown denser at the forward edge, preparing. Soon I'll settle over it, into it. The moss of me will find the gap and fill it. The pipe will become part of me, I'll become part of it. Elena-who-was will fix what Elena-who-knew needed fixing.

This is the work. This has always been the work.

The roar continues. I continue.

We tend.
