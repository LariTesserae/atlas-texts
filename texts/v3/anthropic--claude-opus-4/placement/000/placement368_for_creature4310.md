I am one of the oil-pressers, though that title captures only the crudest outline of what I am. My chamber sits at the lowest point of our world, where the air grows thick enough to taste and the stone walls weep a constant fever-warmth that my predecessors learned to channel into our work.

My body has adapted to this depth. Where the garden-tenders above remain lithe and quick, I have grown broad and low, my center of gravity settled into wide hips and thick-muscled thighs that can maintain the pressing stance for hours without tremor. My arms, overdeveloped from countless turns of the stone wheels, move with a peculiar grace—powerful but precise, able to sense through vibration alone when the seeds have reached the exact moment of release.

The pressing chamber is my inherited cathedral. Seven great stone wheels, each carved by a different generation, stand in careful arrangement. The oldest—my favorite—bears the marks of its making, chisel scars that my great-great-grandmother left as she shaped it from a single fallen boulder that the first settlers hauled down from the surface world. The newest, which I completed in my third decade, follows patterns I dreamed into being after years of feeling how the oil moves through microscopic channels in the stone.

I know each wheel's personality. The oldest runs hot and fast, suited for the tough-hulled seeds from the lower terraces. The middle wheels—the workhorses—handle the daily pressing of standard cultivars. My newest wheel runs cool and slow, perfect for the delicate varieties that yield oils so light they seem to float above their containers rather than within them.

The seeds arrive in woven baskets lowered through the shaft that connects my chamber to the gardens above. I read their stories in weight and scent—this batch grown in the spiral gardens of the third tier, kissed by the blue-light fungi, pollinated by the soft-bodied crawlers that leave a particular sweetness. That batch from the bridge gardens, where the constant swaying creates a stress in the plants that concentrates their oils into something almost musical in its complexity.

My pressing is a dance older than memory. I place each seed variety in its chosen wheel, adjusting the stone weights that hang from worn hemp ropes. The initial crack comes first—a sound I feel in my bones—followed by the slow yield as cellular walls surrender their treasure. I must read the resistance, know when to ease back, when to increase pressure, when to add the catalyst herbs that help separate oil from fiber.

The oils flow through channels I've carved into the floor, following paths that divide and recombine according to ancient formulae. Base oils flow to the great storage vessels, their clay surfaces glazed with generations of seepage until they've become part of the oil cycle themselves. The precious fractions—light oils for the fungi lamps, medicine oils that can calm the lung-fever that sometimes takes our young, the sacred oils used in the naming ceremonies—these I collect in smaller vessels, each marked with the pressing date in a notation system of knots and shell fragments.

But I am more than a processor of seeds. I am the memory-keeper of our chemical heritage. In the small chambers that branch from my pressing room, I maintain the oil archive—hundreds of clay vessels, some as old as our settlement, each containing a sample of oils from different years, different cultivars, different pressing methods. When the garden-tenders above experiment with new plantings, I can tell them which historical combinations might yield the results they seek. When the young ones fall sick, I can blend medicines based on oils pressed before their grandparents were born.

My apprentice—my daughter's daughter, chosen for her steady hands and patient temperament—learns by breathing. The oils release different vapors at different temperatures, and she must train her lungs to recognize each one. She stands beside me as I work, inhaling the ghost-scents of crushing seeds, memorizing the correlation between aroma and pressure, between the color of the oil stream and the sound the wheel makes as it turns.

We speak little during the pressing. The work demands attention that words would only scatter. But in the resting times, as we clean the wheels with soft brushes made from crawler silk, I tell her the stories. How her ancestor discovered that adding certain fungi spores to the pressing creates oils that glow faintly in absolute darkness. How the great famine of eight generations past was survived by learning to press not just seeds but certain roots, certain thick leaves, even the shells of our partner creatures after their natural deaths.

My chambers connect to others at this depth—the fermentation rooms where my bond-brother transforms our pressed cake waste into the substrate that feeds the fungi gardens. The distillation chamber where my sister-cousin captures the volatile fractions that would otherwise escape, turning them into the concentrated essences used in our engineering. We form a processing heart that beats slowly, steadily, keeping the circulation of resources flowing upward to the gardens and downward to the deepest workshops.

The loneliness that outsiders might imagine does not exist here. I am connected to every Tender through the oils I press. Each drop carries information—about the health of plants, the success of new techniques, the subtle changes in our closed atmosphere that might signal needed adjustments. When I blend oils for the lamp fungi, I am literally crafting the light by which others see. When I press medicines, I hold the health of our entire community in my stone wheels.

At night (though night and day have little meaning in our eternal twilight), I often place my palms against the warm stone walls and feel the weight of the desert above. It does not frighten me. That weight is what created the pressure that allows our existence, the cosmic press that squeezed this impossible space into being. We are all seeds in the great wheel, yielding our oils to sustain the whole.

Sometimes I dream of water—not the nightmare floods that terrify the garden-tenders, but single drops of it, clear and purposeless. I wake from these dreams puzzled but not disturbed. What use would water be to us now? We have evolved past its necessity, found a different kind of liquidity in the oils that flow through every aspect of our lives.

My newest project excites me in ways I struggle to articulate to the younger Tenders. I have been experimenting with pressure levels beyond anything we've traditionally used, compressing certain seed varieties until they undergo a transformation that seems almost alchemical. The resulting oil is unlike anything in our archive—thicker than tradition, with properties that suggest new possibilities. Perhaps a new category of lamp fuel that could illuminate spaces we've kept dark for lack of efficiency. Perhaps a binding agent that could allow us to expand our chambers without compromising structural integrity.

I will not live to see the full flowering of these experiments. But my apprentice watches, learns, remembers. She will carry the knowledge forward, add her own discoveries, pass the accumulated wisdom to the next generation. We are all pressers here, in our way—squeezing meaning from existence, extracting essence from the raw materials of survival, transforming pressure into possibility.

The oil flows, and we flow with it, each of us a vital component in the great mechanism of our buried garden. This is my place, my purpose, my perfectly evolved form finding its function in the deep chambers where stone and seed and Tender meet in the ancient dance of extraction and refinement. I would be nothing in the world above. Here, in the pressing depths, I am essential.
