In the green depths of a composting hall, where the sweet-sour air is thick enough to taste, I turn the heaps with a long-handled fork. My name is Mira, though most call me the Rot-Turner, and I have been doing this work for twenty-three years.

I am not old—forty-six this past wet season—but my body carries the particular stoop of those who spend their days bent over steaming piles. My hands are permanently stained the color of tannin tea. My bare feet, spread wide on the slick plank floor, know every soft board, every drainage groove, every spot where the wood bows under weight. When I breathe, I taste the stages of decay: fresh green sharpness, the middle sourness of active breakdown, the final earthen sweetness that means the batch is ready.

My domain is a long shed near the canal, roofed in corrugated metal patched with reed panels where storms have torn through. The walls are more suggestion than barrier—vertical slats with gaps wide enough for air but narrow enough to keep the worst rains out. Green light filters through the vine-curtained spaces. Water drips constantly from the roof edges into catch-channels I carved myself, years ago, when I first understood that fighting the water was less useful than giving it somewhere to go.

The composting courts—seven of them, each the size of a small room—are sunken into the floor. Their walls are old brick, slimed green-black with beneficial growth. Each court is at a different stage. The newest holds yesterday's cuttings from the reed beds, still recognizable as stems and leaves. The oldest is dark, fine, almost soil, ready to be bagged and sent to the growing beds or sold to the barge traders who take it upriver to drier farms.

Between these extremes lie all the stages of transformation. Court Three seethes with heat—if you hold your hand above it, the air shimmers. Court Five has settled into a steadier warmth, like a sleeping body. Court Six barely steams at all. I know them by smell alone. On wet mornings when mist fills the shed and I can barely see the walls, I navigate by nose: this way to the sharp green, that way to the fungal middle notes, straight ahead to the finished earth.

The work is rhythm. Turn, wait, turn, wait. Each pile must be moved at the right time—too early and it goes sour, too late and it compacts into anaerobic clay. The fork goes in at an angle. Lift from the bottom. Bring the dark interior to the surface. Let the outside fold inward. Steam rises, sometimes so thick I disappear into my own work, becoming just another component in the slow chemistry of breakdown.

I am never alone in the shed. The piles have their own citizens. Millipedes process the harder pieces. Beetle grubs work the middle layers. Clouds of tiny flies rise when I disturb them, then settle again. There are fungi I've learned to recognize: the white threads that mean the pile is too dry, the gray slime that means too wet, the honey-colored caps that appear on wood chips and mean nothing except that the world continues to fruit.

Sometimes a heron picks its way between the courts, hunting grubs. I let it work. We are colleagues.

My tools hang on the wall: forks of different lengths, a thermometer on a long probe, buckets for amendments, bags of crushed shell to adjust the balance when things go too acid. Everything metal is rust-stained but oiled. Everything wood is dark with use. I sharpen the fork tines myself with a file, sitting on an upturned bucket, because a dull tool makes the work twice as hard.

The amendments are my secret knowledge. When a pile goes wrong—and they do, especially in the heaviest rains when everything wants to rot at once—I doctor it. A handful of dry fiber to absorb excess moisture. Crushed charcoal to neutralize odors. A bucket of finished compost from Court Seven to seed the proper organisms. Sometimes I piss on a pile that's gone too alkaline, but I don't tell the younger workers this. They would be scandalized. They don't yet understand that everything here is material.

In the morning, before the heat builds, I often work naked to the waist. The sweat runs constantly anyway; clothes just trap it. The young ones think I'm shameless, but shame is a dry-country luxury. Here, comfort matters more than modesty. I wrap my hair in cloth to keep it from the steam. I wear a leather apron when turning the newest piles—the sap can burn. My feet have soles like tire rubber from years of walking on hot boards.

The shed connects to the larger circulation. Reed cutters bring their waste on carts that run on the buried rails. I know them all: Tin with his careful bundles, Sara who always leaves too much stem, the new boy who hasn't learned to shake out the excess water. The mill sends its pulp waste in barrels. The seed-cleaning house sends chaff in sacks. The kitchen workers bring vegetable scraps in covered buckets, and I check each one because sometimes they try to slip in fish bones, which bring rats.

What leaves my shed goes three ways. The finished compost fills burlap sacks that stack under the loading awning. Workers from the growing beds come with carts. The canal merchants come with their scales and arguments about quality. And some of it—the very best, dark and sweet—I keep back for the old timers who maintain kitchen gardens on their roofs and remember when I was young and still learning which smells meant trouble.

I know my place in the machine. Without me, the cuttings would simply rot where they fall, breeding flies and fever. Without me, the mills would choke on their own waste. Without me, the growing beds would have no food. I am a valve in the great wet metabolism, keeping the flow moving, preventing the blockages that lead to disease.

The townspeople understand this in their bodies if not their words. When they pass the shed, they nod. When their children grow restless, they threaten to apprentice them to the Rot-Turner. When someone dies, they bring the funeral flowers to my courts, and I fold them into the eternal breakdown, and something of the dead continues in the soil that feeds the living. This is not ceremony. It is function.

I have a room above a pump house three streets away, but I'm rarely there except to sleep. My real home is the shed. I eat my meals sitting on a plank laid across two buckets, watching the piles steam. I read—when there's light enough and my hands are clean enough—technical manuals about soil chemistry that the barge traders sometimes bring from upriver. Most of it is useless to me. They write about composting in dry places, with different materials, different problems. But sometimes I find a phrase that illuminates something I've known in my hands for years but never had words for.

The books say: Carbon to nitrogen, thirty to one. I say: Brown to green, like mixing paint.

They say: Thermophilic phase, mesophilic phase, maturation. I say: Hot, warm, ready.

They say: Aerobic decomposition requires oxygen incorporation. I say: Turn it when it stinks.

In twenty-three years I've trained four apprentices. Two couldn't take the smell. One was afraid of the beetles. The fourth—Jin—still works the courts on my rest days. He has good instincts but lacks patience. He wants to rush the piles. I tell him: You cannot make time move faster by stirring it.

During the flood season, the work changes. Water rises through the floor despite the drains. The courts become pools. We move what we can to higher platforms, cover the rest, and wait. The smell intensifies—wet rot instead of dry breakdown. Some years I have to shovel out drowned piles that have gone to black slime. Some years the water stays just low enough that the piles keep breathing, barely, and emerge from the floods stronger for the stress.

I've learned to read the water like I read the compost. Its color tells me what it carries: clear means rain from the highlands, brown means soil from the reed beds, black means it's been sitting in the old channels where the deep mud lives. Its smell tells me whether it's fresh or stagnant. Its temperature tells me how long it's been underground.

The canal folk think I'm half-mad, talking to my piles. But everything here communicates. The steam speaks of internal temperature. The smell announces chemical changes. The texture declares moisture content. The color reveals age and readiness. Even the sound—yes, compost makes sound, tiny prickles and sighs as gases escape—tells me about the invisible work happening in the dark.

Sometimes I dream of drier work. I imagine being a weaver in a highland town, working with clean fiber in a bright room. Or a metalsmith, hammering shapes that stay shaped. But these are the dreams of exhaustion. When I wake, I know I belong here, in the wet bottom of the world, turning waste into wealth with a fork and patience.

My predecessor, Old Gao, worked these courts for forty years before his lungs finally gave out. He taught me the basic rhythms, but I learned the subtleties myself, through seasons of attention. Near the end, when he could barely walk from the steam sickness, he would sit on the loading dock and smell the air. "Court Four needs shell," he'd say, and he'd be right. His body had become a sensor tuned to the frequency of decay.

I am becoming like him. My nose can distinguish twelve kinds of rot. My hands can feel the temperature of a pile without touching it. My feet know when the floor moisture has changed, which means the canal is rising or falling. My lungs probably look like his did—spotted and scarred from years of breathing spores. This is the trade.

But I am also not like him. He believed in accepting what came. I believe in adjustment. When the rains don't come and the piles dry into gray dust, I haul water in buckets rather than wait. When the heat builds too fast and threatens to burn the centers into ash, I spread the piles thin. When the merchants complain about quality, I add amendments until the product meets their standards. The old ways were about endurance. My way is about active partnership with the rot.

The young workers think I'm wealthy because I have steady work and a room of my own. They don't see the wealth I actually possess: the knowledge of transformation. I know how to take what everyone discards and make it necessary. I know how to read the secret life of waste. I know how to be patient with the world's becoming.

This morning—a typical morning—I arrived before dawn to find Court Two had gone sour in the night. The smell hit me at the door: wrong, sharp, angry. I lit the lantern and found the pile gray-slick with bad bacteria. No time for morning tea. I stripped to my wrap, grabbed the turning fork, and went to work.

Two hours later, sweat running like a river, I had the pile rebuilt with layers of dry fiber, a careful dose of shell, and a blessing of good bacteria from Court Seven. The smell began to shift. The gray slick faded. The steam returned, clean and hot. By the time the mill carts arrived with the morning's waste, crisis had become routine again.

This is my day, every day: reading the moods of decomposition, adjusting the balance, keeping the flow moving. The town lives because its waste lives. The waste lives because I tend it. I live because the work needs doing, and I have learned to do it well.

When evening comes and the light angles gold through the vine curtains, I make my final rounds. Check each court's temperature. Adjust the drainage channels. Cover what needs covering. Oil the tools. Note in my mind what tomorrow will demand.

Then I wash at the pump—brown water sluicing off brown skin—and walk home through streets that smell of cooking and damp brick. My neighbors nod. The canal workers raise their hands. The children make space. I am the Rot-Turner, necessary and untouchable, keeper of the fundamental exchange.

In my room, I'll eat rice and greens, drink tea made from herbs that grow in the heat, and read by lamplight until sleep takes me. Tomorrow I'll wake before dawn and return to the shed. The piles will have changed in the night. They always do. And I'll adjust, and turn, and tend, and keep the great wheel moving.

This is my place in the world: knee-deep in the waste of a plant town, making tomorrow's soil from today's refuse, breathing steam and spores, my hands the color of earth, my knowledge written in no book but living in my body, which is itself a kind of compost—breaking down slowly, returning what it borrowed, part of the endless wet circulation that keeps this strange green machine alive.
