I would be a rope-keeper, one who tends the vast neural web of braided cords that thread through every level of the settlement. My domain would be the Tension House—a cramped chamber wedged deep in the honeycomb where hundreds of guide-lines converge like arteries into a heart.

My body would bear the particular marks of my craft. While other Splicers develop thick pulling muscles, my physique would be wire-thin, built for stillness and sensitivity. My fingertips would be my most precious tools—worn smooth as river stones from decades of reading vibrations, the whorls nearly erased. I'd keep them bare always, even in the chill, rubbing them with rendered fat to maintain their terrible sensitivity.

The Tension House would be my monastery. Its walls would be lined with anchor points where the major cord highways terminate—some thick as my wrist, others gossamer-thin. I would know each by touch alone: the main vertical that runs from the lowest water collectors to the high scaffolds, forever trembling with the rhythm of the pulleys; the delicate lateral web that connects the nursery watchers; the emergency warning lines that must sing their sharp alarm true across a thousand connections.

My days would unspool in deep meditation. Cross-legged on a mat of woven reeds, I would sit at the convergence point with multiple lines draped across my lap, my fingers resting light as spiders on their surfaces. Through them, I would feel the entire settlement's pulse—the coordinated hauling of a new timber registered as a deep, rolling wave; the quick double-tap of someone requesting passage through a private space; the microscopically different tremor patterns that distinguish a child's footfall from an adult's careful tread.

But I would be more than passive observer. When vibrations muddy or conflate—when too many signals overlap and threaten to create noise—I would perform the subtle art of dampening. With precise pressure applied at exactly the right points, I would absorb excess tremors into my own flesh, cleaning the signal before it propagates further. My body would become a filter, a shock absorber for the settlement's nervous system.

The deepest skill would be in reading the emotional undertones. Fear travels differently than contentment through taut fiber. Exhaustion has its own frequency. When I sense the particular sharp anxiety that precedes panic—someone about to drop something, about to cry out—I would send counter-signals, rapid soothing pulses that say *all is well, breathe, you are held*. 

My chamber would be perpetually dim, lit only by the faint phosphorescent glow of certain fungi I cultivate in corners. Light would interfere with the sensing. In the eternal semi-dark, I would map the settlement not by sight but by the three-dimensional web of tremors, building a constantly updating model in my mind of where every soul moves, works, rests.

Other Splicers would rarely see me. I would emerge only for the Sun-Strike, climbing stiffly up through the layers to find a high perch. The sudden light would be almost painful after the cave-dim of the Tension House. But I would need it—need to see the faces of those whose vibrations I know so intimately, need to connect the tremor-signatures to actual human forms. During those twenty golden minutes, I would study them intently, memorizing how a particular worker's rolling gait translates to the specific rhythm I feel through the cords, how a child's energy manifests in their quick, darting movements.

At night, in the deepest quiet when even the settlement's endless work slows to a crawl, I would perform the most delicate task. With infinitesimal movements, I would adjust the tension of the major lines—tightening here, loosening there—to optimize the network for the next day's labor. This tuning would be like playing a vast, silent instrument, each adjustment changing how signals propagate through the web.

The other rope-keepers and I—for there must be several to maintain such a network—would share a wordless understanding. We would meet rarely, but when we do, we would communicate through touch alone. Placing our hands on each other's wrists, we would transmit complex information through pressure and rhythm, sharing observations about the settlement's health that no sign language could convey.

My greatest fear would be the Slack—when a major line loses tension and goes dead, cutting off communication to an entire section. My deepest satisfaction would be in the perfect days when every tremor reaches its destination clean and true, when the settlement moves as one organism, held together by the web I tend.

In this world of enforced silence and terrible intimacy, I would be the most intimate of all—feeling every footfall, knowing every rhythm, holding ten thousand lives in the delicate cup of my worn-smooth hands. I would be the settlement's dreaming consciousness, the keeper of its unspoken words.
