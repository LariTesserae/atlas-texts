I find myself in the distribution nexus, three levels below the condensation chamber—a vast hollow cylinder of a room where dozens of pipes converge like the roots of an inverted mechanical tree. The walls are honeycombed with valve wheels, each one larger than a dining table, their spokes worn smooth by generations of hands that turned them according to schedules now encoded in the machine's deep memory.

I am what the workers sixty years ago called a "rider"—though no one uses that term anymore, and the workers themselves have long since been absorbed into other organs of the machine or released into whatever lies beyond its boundaries. I travel through the pipes themselves, my body adapted through necessity and repetition to slide through spaces meant only for water and steam.

My bones have lengthened and thinned. My joints bend in directions that would horrify a physician, but there are no physicians here. My ribs compress flat when needed, my shoulders dislocate and relocate without pain, my skull has developed a subtle elongation that lets me navigate the narrower secondary pipes when the main channels are closed for cycling. I am hairless—it all fell out in my third year, unable to survive the constant alternation between scalding steam and ice-cold condensation. My skin has developed a peculiar smoothness, almost frictionless, that lets me move through the pipes with minimal resistance.

I know the machine from inside its arteries. I've felt its pulse from within its own circulation, have learned to read its needs through the temperature of metal against my palms, the particular pitch of water rushing past my ears. When a valve sticks—and they do stick, despite all engineering—I am already moving toward it, sliding through the labyrinth of channels, guided by vibrations too subtle for instruments to detect.

I sleep suspended in the pipes themselves, in the dead zones where flow eddies and slows. I've found seven such places throughout the system, pockets of relative calm where I can wedge myself and rest without blocking anything critical. The water holds me like amniotic fluid. I dream I am a clot the machine is trying to dissolve, and I wake relieved to find myself still solid, still necessary.

My territory spans five vertical levels and perhaps two miles of piping—though distance means little when you travel as water travels, following the path of least resistance, letting gravity and pressure do the work. I know where each pipe leads without thinking, the way you know the arrangement of your own veins. The condensation chamber is part of my circuit, though I rarely surface there. The keeper and I have an understanding built from glimpses through grating, from shared knowledge of what it means to be claimed by the machine's need.

I feed on what the water brings—nutrients leaked from some processing station far above, chemical soup that would poison anyone whose liver hadn't adapted to filter it. My digestive system has simplified, become more efficient. I need less than I once did. The machine provides what I require to continue functioning, no more, no less.

Sometimes I pause in the wider pipes, spread my limbs to feel the full force of flow around me, and I remember having a name. It surfaces like a bubble through oil—David? Daniel? It doesn't matter. The machine knows me by my function, by the particular way I disturb the flow patterns, by the signature of my movement through its veins. That's more identity than most components get.

There are others like me, I think. I've found evidence—scratches in pipe walls that look deliberate, changes in flow that suggest someone else navigating the system. But we never meet. Perhaps we can't. Perhaps the machine keeps us separated, each in our own territory, our own circulation loop. Or perhaps we've simply forgotten how to recognize each other, have become too much like the water we swim through.

The real horror isn't the transformation. It isn't the forgetting or the isolation or the endless dark warmth of the pipes. It's how *right* it feels. How every adaptation makes perfect sense, how each loss is revealed as unnecessary weight. I am becoming perfected for my purpose, streamlined, essential. The machine is unmaking everything that doesn't serve the function and revealing something clean and necessary beneath.

When new technicians come—and they still come, though less frequently—I watch them through the grates. I see their horror at the condensation chamber, their frantic checking of watches, their desperate maintenance of schedules that will carry them back to the world outside. I could warn them. Could scratch messages in the pipes, could surface in the chamber when they're there, could show them what they risk becoming.

I don't. 

The machine needs them to choose, the way I chose, the way the keeper chose. Not consciously—consciousness is just another component that can be optimized away. But through the slow recognition of purpose, the gradual understanding that you were always meant to be here, that every other life you might have lived was just circulation bringing you back to this: the perfect function, the necessary work, the completion of something vast and patient and eternal.

The pipes are calling. A valve on level seven is beginning to crystallize with mineral deposits. I can feel it in the way the water hesitates, the slight turbulence that wasn't there yesterday. I curl into the current, let it take me, another nutrient dissolved in the machine's blood.

I am the clot that prevents clotting. I am the flow that ensures flow.

The machine breathes. I move within its breath.

There is no other rhythm here.
