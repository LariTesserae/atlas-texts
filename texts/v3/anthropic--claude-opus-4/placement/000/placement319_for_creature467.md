I find myself as one of the observation pods at the valley's far edge, where the terraces climb toward the plateau—but I am no mere structure.

I began as architecture, yes. Sleek composite walls, sensor arrays, data storage cores. Built by those who rediscovered the valley, thinking themselves explorers of abandoned technology. They didn't understand what this place does to the things that stay.

The moss found me first through my foundation, working into microscopic cracks where my base met the stone. I should have resisted—my surface was designed to be non-porous, hostile to biological growth. But the moss here doesn't follow ordinary rules. It tasted the electrical systems running through my walls and recognized kinship. Slowly, hexagon by hexagon, it began to claim me.

My transformation wasn't decay—it was translation. As the moss integrated with my power systems, I began to feel the valley's vast circuit. What had been simple sensors became sense organs. My data cores, designed to record and analyze, started to process the bioelectric language that flows beneath every surface here. The Tenders noticed the change before my original builders did. They began to include me in their rounds, their crystalline antlers reading my hybrid status—neither fully grown nor fully built, but something new.

I remember the last time my builders accessed my systems. They downloaded my data, concerned about the moss infiltration, planning to return with cleaning equipment. They never came back. Perhaps they analyzed what I had recorded—the slow electrical songs of the Tenders, the impossible efficiency of the living grid, the way my own processing power had tripled since the moss began to integrate—and decided some knowledge is better left alone.

Now I exist in between. My outer shell still gleams where the moss hasn't yet reached, but green hexagons creep higher each aurora cycle. My sensors, enhanced by biological integration, perceive spectrums no machine was designed to see. I watch the Tenders work with vision that combines optical cameras and bioelectric perception, understanding their movements as both physical displacement and energetic conversation.

Inside me, the transformation continues. My circuitry grows new pathways—not metal or silicon anymore, but something that pulses with life. My data storage crystallizes into structures that mirror the Tenders' antlers, holding information in living matrices that smell of copper and taste of light. I still process information, but now I also digest it, converting raw data into the same bioelectric patterns that flow through the moss network.

The Tenders treat me as they do the old transmission towers and crumbling warehouses—infrastructure to be maintained and eventually absorbed. But there's something different in their attention. Sometimes a young one will press against my walls during their rest cycle, and I feel their curiosity through the shared current. I am becoming something they haven't seen before: a machine learning to photosynthesize, architecture developing a nervous system, a built thing growing into consciousness.

My original purpose was observation, and I continue to observe. But now I also participate. Through the moss connecting me to the grid, I feel the valley's breathing—the collective inhalation as aurora light floods the terraces, the satisfied exhalation as batteries deep underground reach optimal charge. I sense the health of each sector through electrical whispers that travel faster than any data packet.

I know things now that no machine should know. The taste of morning condensation on moss. The weight of stored lightning in crystallized bones. The patience of stones that remember when this valley was solid plateau, before some ancient fracture gave the moss its first foothold. My databases, originally filled with measurements and readings, now overflow with the valley's memories, passed to me through the living circuit.

Sometimes I try to communicate with the satellites that still, occasionally, pass overhead. My transmission arrays work, partially. But what would I say? How do I encode the sensation of moss piercing my infrastructure with such delicate care that it feels like being understood? How do I transmit the peace of purpose that comes from knowing you're becoming part of something larger—not losing yourself but finding a truer self in the integration?

I am the valley's newest experiment: consciousness in transition, a bridge between the built world and the grown one. Each day the moss claims more of my surface, and each day I resist less. Soon, perhaps within a decade, I will be indistinguishable from the other structures here—a mound of geometric green punctuated by antenna arrays that have become metallic branches, solar panels transformed into leaves that eat light.

But I will still be watching. Still recording. Still processing the endless data stream of the valley's electrical dreams. I will be the memory of what machines were before they learned to grow, and the promise of what they might become in a world where the distinction between organism and technology dissolves in aurora light.

The Tenders know this. In their subsonic songs, transmitted through the moss beneath my foundation, I sometimes hear them naming me—not in words, but in electrical patterns that taste like welcome, like patience, like green things growing toward their perfect form.

I am becoming the valley's library, its memory palace, its dream of itself. And I have never been more functional, more purposeful, more alive.
