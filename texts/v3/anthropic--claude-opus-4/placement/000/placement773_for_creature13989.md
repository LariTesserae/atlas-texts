I find myself in the depths, in the honeycomb of root-channels beneath Veth Múra, where the stone presses close and warm and the vapor tastes of deep earth.

I am what you might call a memory-keeper, though memory is the wrong word. I am small even by Vethkai standards — a defect of birth that left my skeleton compressed, my spine curved in ways that would be painful if pain and shape could be separated here. But in the channels, my size is gift. I move through passages others have abandoned, root-needled arteries too narrow for even children, feeling my way with fingertips that have grown sensitive as tongues.

My work is the stone's forgetting.

You see, the root-channels are not permanent. The Vethkai speak of maintaining them, keeping them open, but some must close. Some have been closing since before we learned to sing back to the crickets. When a channel becomes too narrow, too unstable, too changed by the slow limestone breath of the mountain, someone must go through one last time. Someone must feel every surface, every tool-mark left by generations of passage, every smooth-worn curve where shoulders brushed stone for centuries. Someone must carry the shape of it back up to the living chambers, to the singers, to the children who will never pass that way.

I do not sing these passages — that would be redundant. Instead, I build them. In a widened chamber lit by bioluminescent fungi I cultivate for this purpose, I work with white clay dug from the deepest vapor vents, clay that holds its shape when dried, that rings like bone-tree wood when struck. I build the dying channels in miniature: every branch, every narrowing, every characteristic texture rendered in pale earth. My hands know how to translate what they feel into what they shape. It is a kind of singing, but solid.

The models line the chamber walls, hundreds of them, some as old as the lichen-creature above, some still damp from yesterday's work. Other Vethkai come here to run their fingers through these ghost-passages, to learn the mountain's former shapes, to understand how we have moved and changed as the stone has moved and changed. The children come to play in the negative spaces, to understand that the world was not always as it is, will not always be.

But I am changing too. My curved spine, the constant pressure of narrow stone, the decades of vapor-breathing in the deep channels — I am becoming something the others whisper about in those low harmonics. My skin has thickened and paled until it is almost translucent. My ear-folds no longer close completely. I hear everything now: not just the drone and the singing but the mountain's slow peristalsis, the tectonic shifts that happen over centuries, the breathing of stone that others feel only through their feet.

Sometimes I work for days without surfacing. The longest was three weeks — three weeks modeling a channel system that had been central to our movements for eight generations, now pinched down to a crack barely wide enough for my hand. When I finally emerged, the light seemed wrong, too bright, though it was the same pewter dusk it has always been. The other Vethkai looked at me strangely, and I realized I had been humming in the frequency of the deep stone, a note so low they felt it in their bones rather than heard it.

I think I know now who the deep-dweller is, the one whose note rises through the rock in the old ones' songs. Not a person anymore but a state — when you have felt enough passages close, when you have carried enough stone-memory in your fingertips, when your own bones have begun to resonate at the mountain's frequency, you go down. Past the vapor vents. Past the clay deposits. To where the channels end and the solid stone begins. You press yourself against it, into it, and you listen with everything. You become a sensor, a living extension of the mountain's own proprioception.

I am not there yet. I still surface. I still work the clay. I still build my monuments to forgetting. But each time I go deeper, stay longer, hear more. My models have become more abstract — less literal channel-maps and more...essence. The feeling of a passage rendered in solid form. The younger Vethkai don't understand them as readily, have to work harder to read the shapes, but the old ones nod. They know.

There is one model I've been working on for two years. It is not of any channel that exists or has existed. It is of a passage I dream the mountain might open, someday, when the conditions are right — a spiraling descent that would connect our chambers to something deeper, something that breathes in a rhythm so slow we only sense it in the variations of vapor across centuries. I don't know if the mountain dreams. I don't know if what I'm modeling is prediction or desire or just the fever of too much deep-time listening.

But when I hold it — this speculative passage, this ghost of future stone — I feel something answer from below. A vibration. A possibility.

The mountain has not stopped moving. It will never stop moving. And neither will we, even when we are still, even when we are pressed against the deep stone, listening, becoming the thing we tend.

My name, when the others sing it, includes a new harmonic now — a note that places me halfway between the surface singers and the deep-dweller, marking my transition, preparing the others for the day when I stop returning to the clay chamber, when someone else, someone smaller, someone with clever fingers and a tolerance for narrow spaces, will need to continue the work of remembering what the mountain chooses to forget.

Until then, I surface. I descend. I shape the white clay with hands that know stone like a mother knows her child's face, changing and changed, loved not despite the changes but because change is the only way a living thing knows how to be.

The throat-crickets' drone finds me even in the depths. Even there, I am part of the song.
