In this world of eternal updraft and primal grip, I am a Threadkeeper.

My body is typical of my kind—a flattened teardrop of dense muscle and cartilage, six limbs splayed against the living chaos of our vertical garden. But where others excel at the hunt or the harvest, I have devoted my existence to the pale fiber that binds our scattered society together.

I live deeper than most, burrowed into a natural hollow where three massive root-nodes have fused into a living cathedral. Here, in the relative shelter of the plant's own architecture, I tend the Thread Gardens—colonies of the pale worms that produce our most precious resource. They are delicate creatures, no larger than my smallest finger, living in symbiosis with certain flowering nodes that bloom only in the deepest shadows. The worms feed on the nectar, and in return, they extrude the silk we braid into our lifelines.

My days are measured in vibrations. Through my jaw pressed against the main communication line, I feel the needs of my dispersed kin. A snapped rope here. A new pod being woven there. A young one preparing for their first journey to the windward edge. Each request thrums through the stone and into my bones, and I answer.

I have memorized the tension patterns of over a thousand different braids. My inner hands can work in absolute darkness, fingers dancing through sequences passed down through generations of Threadkeepers before me. The triple-wound anchor braid that can hold against a direct updraft. The hollow-core float rope that becomes buoyant when wet clouds pass. The singing lines, thin as hair, that carry our most complex conversations across the void.

But I am more than a mere producer. I am a living memory.

In the smooth walls of my deeper burrow, I have carved the history of our pillar. Not in symbols—we have no written language—but in knots. Hundreds of sample braids hang from polished bone hooks, each one telling a story through its pattern. This spiral weave marks the season of the Great Lull, when for thirteen heartbeats the Roar stuttered and seven of our kind stood upright. This flat braid of unusual thickness commemorates the master hunter who developed our modern net design. This delicate twist, dyed purple with crushed bladder-fruit, remembers my mentor, the previous Threadkeeper, who taught me that the fiber itself could carry meaning.

The work is lonely by necessity. The worms are sensitive to vibration; too much activity disturbs their silk production. So I move with deliberate slowness, a strange counterpoint to the thrashing urgency of the jungle above. When I must harvest, I time my movements to the predictable spasms of the plants, sliding between the moments of chaos like water between stones.

Sometimes, during the deepest part of the sleep cycle, when even the eternal plants seem to slow their manic growth, I perform the ritual my mentor taught me. I gather fresh silk—unbraided, raw, almost invisible in its delicacy—and climb as far as I dare toward the windward edge. There, gripping with all four primary limbs, I release a single strand into the void.

The silk is too light to fall. Instead, it rises, caught instantly in the updraft, pulled vertical. For a moment that stretches like centuries, I watch it climb—a silver thread against the harsh blue, connecting our tiny world to the infinite above. I watch until my oily eye-membranes can no longer track its ascent, until it vanishes into the brightness where we cannot follow.

My mentor told me her mentor did this too, and his before him. We send these threads up like prayers, though none of us can say what we're praying to. Perhaps we hope that somewhere above the Roar, in that realm of terrible silence we can only imagine, something gathers our silver offerings. Perhaps we simply need to believe that our fiber—this thing that binds us, defines us, saves us daily from the hungry void—can exist somewhere without tension, without the desperate grip.

I am old now by our standards. My primary limbs ache with deep fatigue that no amount of rest can cure. I have trained three apprentices, teaching them the songs of the worms, the patience of the harvest, the sacred patterns of our history. Soon I will make my final climb, not to the windward edge but to the very tip of our stone fin, where the elders go when the Grip becomes too much to bear.

But not yet. Today, a young one's vibration thrums through the line—nervous, excited. They have woven their first pod and need cord for the anchoring. I select the finest batch from last season's harvest, the kind that sings high and clear when plucked. 

Through the bone-deep percussion of our language, I tell them: *This fiber held your grandmother. It will hold your grandchildren. Trust it as you trust the stone.*

They respond with the traditional gratitude-pattern, but underneath I feel their youth, their fear, their wonder at this vertical world that demands everything and gives so little back. I remember that feeling. Sometimes, in the shelter of my Thread Garden, listening to the worms spin their silver dreams, I still feel it too.

I package the cord in a seed-pod lined with woven bark and begin the slow journey upward. Above me, the jungle writhes its eternal dance. Below, the abyss roars its endless hunger. And between them, held by nothing more than fiber and will, we persist—anchored to each other by threads too thin to see, too strong to break.
