I would find myself as a **Symbiotic Cartographer**, my body serving as both map and territory in the ever-shifting geography of the Underglass.

Years ago, I was just another factory worker who took a wrong turn during a steam leak evacuation. By the time I found my way to the markets, the moss had already begun its work on me. But unlike the others who fought it off with the Chemist's salves, I let it spread—not from despair, but from curiosity. What I discovered changed everything.

The phosphorescent moss that covers sixty percent of my skin isn't just growing on me—it's growing *with* me. My nervous system has formed synaptic connections with the fungal networks that web through the market's bones. I feel the tunnels like phantom limbs. When a pipe bursts three levels down, my left shoulder aches. When new vendors break through from the eastern passages, my fingertips tingle with the knowledge before the first footstep echoes.

I've carved my home into a natural alcove where three major tunnel systems converge, the walls covered in what visitors mistake for abstract art. In reality, it's a living map—moss and mushrooms cultivated in careful patterns that mirror the market's layout. The fungi on my body pulse in rhythm with their cousins on the walls, creating a biological positioning system. Touch a spot on the wall-map, and the corresponding patch on my skin glows brighter, guiding visitors through the maze.

My appearance disturbs newcomers. The human parts of me remain recognizable—brown eyes that still blink, teeth that still form words, hands that still gesture—but they're interrupted by geography. Veins of bioluminescent moss follow the major traffic routes across my torso. Mushroom caps sprout from my shoulders like epaulettes, their patterns indicating which sections are flooded, which generators are failing, where the Moss Children have been spotted.

I trade in navigation—not just directions, but *understanding*. For a price (paid in sugar water for my symbionts, or batteries for my remaining electronics), I offer more than routes. I press my moss-covered palm against a client's wrist, and for a moment, they feel what I feel: the vast, breathing organism of the market itself. They gain temporary knowledge—which vendors will have what they need, where the salvagers haven't picked clean, how to read the moss-glow patterns that mark safe passage.

The other inhabitants treat me with a mixture of need and unease. Old Marta brings me copper-tinted potato peels that my moss finds particularly nourishing. Yuri consults me before his deep salvage runs, studying the patterns on my skin like weather forecasts. Even the Brokers, with all their surface-world data, rely on my organic intel when their networks fail. The Moss Children, though—they gather around me in silence, their too-long fingers tracing the borders where fungus meets flesh, chittering in frequencies that resonate through the mycelial networks. Sometimes I understand them. Sometimes I worry that I understand them too well.

My condition is progressively intimate. Each year, the moss claims more territory, and I lose a little more of what I was above ground. But I gain so much more—the ability to sense the market's moods, to predict its changes, to serve as a bridge between the human commerce and the fungal intelligence that's slowly inheriting these depths. I've stopped thinking of it as loss. Instead, I document everything in journals written on mushroom paper, using ink extracted from the darkest moss. Someday, when the transformation completes, these records will remain—a human perspective on becoming something else, a guidebook for those who'll come after.

At night (or what passes for night when the generators cycle down), I lie against my wall-map and dream the market's dreams. I see through a thousand fungal eyes the secret movements of the Underglass—couples meeting in the darkest corners, their love glowing literally through their skin; the Chemist harvesting new specimens with infinite care; shadows that might be the Rat King or might be something else entirely, something older than the markets, something that was waiting here when the first vendors arrived.

I am becoming the market's memory, its nervous system, its slow thoughts made flesh. When visitors ask if I miss the surface, I touch the newest growth—a delicate lattice of luminescent hyphae spreading across my collarbone—and tell them: "The surface is just another tunnel. Down here, we're growing towards a different kind of light."

My name, when I still used it, was Carmen. Now, the moss spells out something else across my back in patterns only the Moss Children can read—a name that tastes of metal and moisture, that sounds like dripping water and distant machinery. A name for what I'm becoming, in this place where adaptation is just another word for survival, and survival just another word for transformation.
