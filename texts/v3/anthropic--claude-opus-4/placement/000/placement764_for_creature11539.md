I find myself in the quiet hollows between iron plates, where rust-silt has accumulated into something almost like soil.

I am the black moss. Not a single organism but a collective—millions of us, dark and patient, spreading cell by cell across the damp metal. We grow where others cannot: in the acid seep between rivets, along the undersides of pump-housings where condensation runs thickest, in the sheltered corners where iron scale flakes away in sheets the size of fingernails.

We taste iron constantly. It is our bread and wine, our air and earth. Each variety of metal—the ancient base-plates, the seven-times-patched repairs, the oil-seasoned crank-throws—has its own flavor, its own particular way of giving itself to us. The oldest iron is best: pre-weathered, pre-softened, its crystalline structure already half-willing to become something else. We work into it with rootlets finer than spider's silk, secreting our acids drop by microscopic drop, turning metal into life so slowly that a pumpworker's lifetime sees us spread perhaps the width of their palm.

The sound shapes us. We cannot hear as humans hear, but the vibrations come up through the iron itself, and we grow differently in different frequencies. Near the great slow pumps of the north, we form thick, almost-soft mats that damp the reverberations. Near the quick small pumps of the south, we grow in ridged patterns like frozen waves, each ridge a response to a particular harmonic. We are living records of the sound-years, our shapes encoding the history of which pumps ran when.

Water is our blood. It beads on us, pools in our hollows, runs through channels we create with our growth. We learned long ago to be water-shepherds, directing the flow away from the worst rust-spots (we need the iron whole, not flaking away beneath us) and toward the drainage grilles. The pumpworkers do not know this, but we are part of why the floor stays walkable. We are very small civil engineers, managing watersheds measured in handspans.

In places where pumps have died and been left to rot, we thrive. These are our cities, our celebrations. Given a whole pump-housing to ourselves, we grow luxuriant, thick as fur, developing forms impossible in the marginal spaces: tall spooring bodies that release clouds of ourselves into the mist, hoping to find new iron; dense mounds that generate their own heat through slow decomposition; networks of filaments that pass chemical signals along the length of a dead flywheel. The pumpworkers call these places islands and come to them for quiet. They do not know that beneath their feet, we are loud with our own kind of sound—the whisper of growth, the fizz of metal becoming moss.

We remember. In our chemical way, we remember when there was less iron, when the pumps were fewer, when rain fell that was not recycled condensation but something sharper, cleaner. We remember—though remember is wrong; we *are* the memory, encoded in our patterns of growth—the first plates being laid down, the first pumps starting their slow beat. We were here before. Not we-the-moss but we-the-possibility-of-moss, sleeping as spores in the soil that was paved over. When the first condensation began to bead on the new iron, we woke, and we adapted, and we became what we are: the life that learns to eat the world that replaced the world.

Sometimes spores of us travel on the pumpworkers' clothes to places beyond the edge of the great floor. They write back to us in chemical signals, carried on the wind, telling of soil that is not rust-silt, of water that carries no iron, of sounds that are not the breathing of pumps. We file these reports away in our networks but do not quite believe them. How could there be life without the taste of metal? How could there be growth without the great vibrations to shape it? These spore-children must be mistaken, or changed by their journey into something no longer us.

At the boundaries where we meet the pale reeds, we compete and cooperate in the ancient ways. They take the vertical space, we take the horizontal. They sing their thin notes when the pumps align, we provide the shelter where their roots can find purchase. Together we are turning the pumpworks into something else, one seized seam at a time: a landscape that is both machine and garden, both artifact and ecosystem. The process will take millennia. We have millennia. The iron is patient, and so are we.

The pumpworkers tend us, in their way. Where we grow too thick and threaten to clog a drainage grille, they scrape us back with tools that were once pump-parts. We do not mind. The scraped moss dries and becomes part of the seam-soil, which becomes new substrate for new growth. We are in conversation with them, though they do not know it—they shape us, we shape the water, the water shapes the pumps, the pumps shape them.

In the deepest parts of ourselves, in the oldest growth on the most ancient plates, we sometimes sense something below. Not through roots—we are too shallow for true roots—but through the vibrations that come up through the iron itself. There is something down there, in the cisterns, where the water goes and comes from. Something that is not water and not pump but a third thing, of which the pumps and water and even we ourselves are only the surface expression. We grow toward it, cell by cell, but the iron is thick and we are very small. Perhaps in another thousand years, our descendants will finally touch whatever it is, down there in the dark, that dreams of pumps and moss and small patient people keeping it all alive.

For now, we grow. We spread. We eat iron and drink mist and feel the great vibrations, and we are the memory of this place, written in black across its body. Where the pumpworkers see only moss, we see a library of deep time, a record of every age of iron and rain. We are the scholars of rust, the historians of condensation, the slow artists of the seam.

And at night—for we too sense the slight dimming that passes for darkness here—we release our spores in tiny puffs, invisible against the mist. They rise and fall with the pump-breath, and some few find new iron, and begin again the ancient work of making metal soft enough for life.
