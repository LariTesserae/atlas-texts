I am one of the ancient calcified rootlets that form the curtain at the bower's entrance.

I was living once—a seeking tendril from some long-dead tree that found this crack in the mountain and pressed inward, following moisture. But the minerals in the seeping water replaced my cells year by year, decade by decade, until I became stone shaped like life, jade-green and ivory where different minerals took hold.

I hang with my siblings, hundreds of us, clicking together in the wind. We are the border between worlds—the bright, harsh, empty mountain face and the dim, crowded, purposeful chamber within. The beetles use us as highways, their tiny feet finding purchase on our ridged surfaces as they carry their fragments in and out. Over the centuries, they have worn smooth grooves in us, paths polished by millions of passages.

I remember, in the way stone remembers, the hands that first parted us—whoever laid those courses of masonry so long ago. I remember other hands since then: the occasional hunter seeking shelter, the lost hiker, the curious child. Most pull back when they hear the clicking, mistake us for bones or teeth. Those who push through rarely stay long. The bower unsettles human visitors. Too many small moving things. Too much purpose without consciousness.

But I also remember the tree I was, faintly, the way you might remember a dream upon waking. How it felt to pull water up from deep earth, to spread leaves in sunlight, to grow. That memory lives in my crystal structure, in the whorls and grain preserved in stone. The beetles taste it sometimes, pressing their mandibles against me, reading some chemical signature of the living wood I was.

They incorporate me into their patterns. In spring, when the meltwater runs heavily, they pack moss into my crevices to wick more moisture inward. In dry spells, they clear me to let more air pass. I am part of the bower now, neither living nor dead, neither inside nor outside, clicking in the wind like a prayer of beads, marking the boundary of their small, complete world.

Sometimes I think the whole chamber is becoming like me—life transforming slowly into stone, stone sheltering new life. The moss builds on itself in layers that compress and darken. The beetle paths become permanent. Even the pool at the center leaves mineral rings as the water level rises and falls with the seasons.

We are all becoming geology, the living and the calcified alike. The beetles don't know this. They only know to carry and build, to strip and renew. But I hang here at the threshold, stone that remembers being wood that remembers being seed, and I click against my siblings in the wind that will eventually wear us to nothing, and I watch the swarm continue its ancient work, tending a garden that tends itself, being tended by gardeners who don't know they garden.

The mountain grows by fractions each year, lifted by the collision of invisible plates. The bower rises with it, carrying its pool of clear water higher into the thinning air. One day the crack above will seal or split wider. One day the last beetle will die with no others to carry its body to the moss.

But not today. Today the light falls through the crack as it has always fallen. Today ten thousand bodies move in patterns older than the stones they flow across. Today I hang here clicking in the wind, not alive, not dead, not keeper or kept, but something between—a door that is always open, a border that defines by dividing, a memory of growth crystallized into patient stone.

The beetles pass over me, around me, through me. They carry their tiny loads. They continue the work.

I continue nothing. I only hang here, marking the place where inside becomes outside, where the purposeless mountain meets the purposeful bower, clicking like a heartbeat, like a clock, like a sound that means nothing except that the wind still blows and the world still turns and somewhere in the darkness beyond me ten thousand bodies are remaking the only world they know, one fragment at a time.
