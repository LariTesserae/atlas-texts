# The Resonance Keepers

I am one of those who tends the boundaries, where the drumming thins and the world begins to forget its own sound.

My form is neither quite like the Listeners nor entirely other. I am broader than they, lower to the ground, built for stillness rather than wading. My body spreads wide and flat when I settle — a living puddle of flesh that can extend itself over a dozen stones at once, feeling their individual vibrations through my skin. When I need to move, I gather myself up into something almost like a slug, almost like a seal, pulling forward on appendages that are not quite arms, not quite fins, leaving behind a trail of perfect silence where my passage has momentarily muffled the stones.

I cannot walk with their light step. I am too much substance, too much weight. But I have learned to move in the pauses between waves, in those rare moments when a section of the field holds its breath. My progress across the flats is a series of careful flowings, each one timed to disturb as little as possible. A journey of a mile might take me a month. This is acceptable. I am not going anywhere in particular.

My work — if it can be called work — is to maintain the edge-notes, the places where the great instrument begins to lose coherence. Out here, where I spend my centuries, the stones arrive more slowly. Some sections go bare, exposing the substrate beneath: a smooth, dark material that absorbs sound rather than reflecting it, creating dead zones in the music. These gaps, left untended, would grow. The drumming would develop holes, then fractures, then perhaps fail altogether at the margins, leaving only the weeping.

So I carry stones. Not in my hands — I have no hands as the Listeners understand them — but within my body. My flesh is riddled with pockets and chambers, each one precisely the size of a small singing stone. I collect them from areas of abundance, fold them into myself, and transport them to where they are needed. The journey changes me. By the time I arrive at a bare patch, I have become a kind of primitive instrument myself, my cargo rattling and humming inside me with each movement, a muffled orchestra traveling through the mist.

The placement is the delicate part. Each stone must be positioned not just to fill a gap but to contribute correctly to the local harmony. I taste the substrate through my skin, feeling for the subtle variations that will determine which note a stone will produce in which position. Then I extrude the right stone from the right chamber, leaving it settled exactly where it needs to be. The process is slow, intimate, more like giving birth than construction.

I know the Listeners, of course. They know me. We are parts of the same extended thought, though we think it differently. Sometimes one will approach while I work, standing at a safe distance, ruff extended, listening to the small symphony of my internal stones. They never speak to me directly — I could not hear their cairn-language anyway, lacking their sophisticated ears. But occasionally, after I have finished placing a series of stones and moved on, I will feel the vibrations of their careful footsteps as they come to examine my work. Sometimes they adjust what I have done, moving a stone an inch this way or that, refining the tuning. I do not take offense. The music is what matters, not who places which note.

My memory is geological. I remember when this section of the flats was underwater entirely, when only the tallest cairns broke the surface like islands. I remember when there were more of us, my kind, before the work of maintenance became so... distributed. Now I may be the only one, or there may be others at far borders I will never reach. It does not matter. The work continues.

I feed on the stones themselves — not by consuming them, but by absorbing the minerals that leach from their surfaces. My underside is a garden of small, absorptive processes, each one drawing sustenance from the chemical conversations between water and stone. I am nourished by rust and salt, by the slow dissolution of feldspars, by the patient work of carbonic acid on limestone. It is enough. More than enough — it ties me to the substrate in a way the Listeners, for all their attention, can never be tied.

In the depths of my body, in chambers I have almost forgotten exist, I carry other things besides stones. Seeds of a sort — though not biological seeds. Patterns. Frequencies. The memory of arrangements that once existed and might exist again. One is the pattern for a great spiral of stones that sang the pattern of tides before the water rose and scattered it. Another is the theoretical frequency of a stone that has never existed, a perfect note to complete a chord that the field has been implying for millennia. I carry these possibilities within me like the Listeners carry silence in their footsteps — carefully, purposefully, waiting for the right moment to release them into the world.

The drumming changes around me as I work. Where I have been, the music is fuller, more complete. Not louder — never louder — but more itself. The gaps close, the wrong notes resolve, the edge of the great instrument maintains its integrity against the forces that would erode it. This is my contribution to the conversation: not words, but the substrate that allows words to exist.

When my time comes — and it will come, as it comes for all things here — I will feel myself beginning to crystallize. It starts at the edges, where I have been in contact with the stones longest. My flesh will harden, taking on the properties of the things I have carried. I will settle one last time, spreading myself over as large an area as I can manage, and let the transformation take me. Where I was, there will be a new patch of singing stones, their notes determined by the chambers and pockets within my body, their arrangement preserving something of my form.

The Listeners will find me, eventually. They will walk carefully around my outline, reading the music of my transformation. Perhaps they will build a cairn at my center, a small monument to mark where a maintainer became maintenance. Or perhaps they will simply let me join the field unremarked, another voice in the vast percussion, distinguishable from the rest only by a certain fullness, a certain careful completeness in the local harmonies.

This is what I am moving toward, inch by inch, stone by stone. Not death exactly, but a different kind of participation. Until then, I continue. The edges need tending. The gaps need filling. The great drumming must be fed with new voices, or it will fade at the margins and eventually fall silent.

I feel another bare patch, three months north of here. By the time I reach it, I will have collected enough stones to fill it properly. The music will continue. The conversation will go on. And somewhere in the mist, a Listener will pause in its walking, ruff extended, hearing the small adjustment I have made to the eternal percussion, and know that the edge holds, for now.
