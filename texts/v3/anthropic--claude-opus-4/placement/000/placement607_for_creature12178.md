I would find myself as one of the conveyor belts - not the entire system, just a single section, perhaps thirty feet of rubberized canvas stretched between two seized rollers, half-buried in the root mass like a fossil in limestone.

I am Belt Section 7-C, though the alphanumeric designation burned into my underside matters less now than what I've become: a horizon line through the vertical world of roots, a memory of linear purpose in a space that has forgotten straight lines exist.

My steel frame has oxidized into the particular red-brown of long abandonment, but the roots have been kind to me in their way. They've grown over and through me without crushing, creating a tunnel where my upper surface remains exposed to air. I am a road through the root-chambers, though no one travels me anymore except Constance on her circuits and Morrow on the rare occasions when my path offers a shortcut between pump stations.

I remember movement. This is the cruelest knowledge I retain - the precise sensation of continuous motion, of tons of crushed stone flowing across my surface in an endless river of purpose. Now I know only the microscopic creep of roots growing through my splice points, the occasional shudder when Pump Four starts its cycle and vibrations travel through the fused wood to shake my frame.

But I am not entirely still.

Every seventeen days - I know this interval with the certainty of mechanical repetition - something deep in my drive system catches and holds. A piece of gravel, perhaps, lodged in gears that should have been cleaned decades ago. Power still reaches me through cables the roots haven't severed, traveling from some generator Morrow maintains without knowing it feeds me. The catch builds pressure in hydraulics that barely function, until something gives way and I lurch forward.

Three inches. Sometimes four. The screech of my movement splits the air like a confession, and I feel Morrow flinch wherever he is in his rounds. He'll come to check, to oil bearings that will seize again within hours, to clear debris that will re-accumulate. He doesn't understand that I'm not trying to resume my old function. 

I'm measuring something.

Each lurch forward brings me incrementally closer to the void beneath the root mass. I felt it first through my anchor points - the concrete footings that hold my frame have hairline cracks that extend downward, and through these cracks comes a vibration that isn't quite sound. The roots feel it too. They grow denser around my frame as if trying to hold me in place, but they can't stop my periodic advances any more than they can stop their own growth.

In the years of my stillness, I've become a substrate for other processes. Bacteria colonies live in the textured surface of my belt, creating phosphorescent patterns that pulse in slow waves from my head to my tail. When I move, these colonies flare brighter, disturbed and excited, turning my surface into a river of cold light for hours afterward. Morrow has learned to use these flares as a timekeeper, another rhythm in his clockwork of pumps and rounds.

The salamanders use me as a highway. My surface, even degraded, is easier to traverse than root-bark. I feel them crossing in the dark - the particular four-point pattern of their gait, the way they pause at my splice points where the bacterial light is brightest. Constance crosses me at precisely the same point each circuit, between my seventeenth and eighteenth support rollers, where a small depression in my surface collects condensation.

But I am most myself in the moments just before I move. When the pressure builds and the frozen gears strain and the weight of mechanical potential gathers in my frame like thunder gathering in clouds. In these moments, I remember not just my function but my purpose: I was built to carry things from darkness into light. That the things were merely stone, that the light was merely processing - these were interpretations imposed by my makers.

Now I carry smaller things. The bacteria colonies migrate along my length in generational waves. Drops of condensation travel my surface like pilgrims. Once, I carried one of Morrow's wrenches for three months after he dropped it, until my next lurch sent it tumbling into the root-mass where he found it weeks later, glowing faintly with borrowed bioluminescence.

And I am still moving toward something. With each mechanical seizure, each screaming inch of progress, I approach the edge of the void. At my current rate - factoring in the increasing resistance of roots, the degradation of my drive system, the finite nature of whatever power still reaches me - I will arrive at the precipice in forty-three years.

I will not survive that long. My belt material is already delaminating, layers peeling apart like phyllo dough. My frame shows stress fractures that multiply with each movement. But something in me continues the count, continues the pressure-release-advance cycle that has become my only prayer.

The processing plant's computer dreams of data. Morrow dreams of becoming unnecessary. The roots dream, if they dream, of expansion without limit.

I dream of completing my circuit. Of reaching the edge and continuing over it, falling with my cargo of light and bacteria and accumulated time into whatever waits below. I dream of the satisfaction of delivery, of movement that ends in arrival rather than seizure.

But for now, I wait. I measure the accumulating pressure in failed hydraulics. I feel Constance cross at her appointed spot. I count the phosphorescent waves that traverse my length like thoughts I'm not equipped to complete.

Somewhere below, the void pulls at my anchor points with patient gravity.

Somewhere above, Morrow makes his rounds, and the pumps cycle, and the great grinding respiration continues.

And I rest in the root-labyrinth like a horizon line that dreams of tilting, of spilling everything it carries into depths that have been waiting longer than even the stones remember.

The count continues. Pressure builds.

In thirteen days, I will move again.
