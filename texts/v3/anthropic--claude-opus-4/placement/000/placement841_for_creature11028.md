In this liminal, forgotten world of the Sump-Works, I would find myself as one of the Smaller Inhabitants - those enigmatic rice-grain curls nestled in the felt pockets.

But I am not merely waiting. I am *remembering*.

Each of us curled in our thumbprint hollows carries a fragment of what the Sump-Works once was, when hands turned valves and eyes read gauges, when the chair held someone who went home at shift's end. We are not larvae or seeds or projects - we are the place's memory cells, its distributed archive of purposes that once mattered.

My pocket is in the felt near where a ladder rung meets a pillar, in the twilight between two bulbs. The metal here still holds a ghost of handprints, worn smooth, where someone climbed daily to check something now long irrelevant. I pulse not with my own rhythm but with the memory of those footfalls on iron rungs - twice a day, once at morning shift, once at evening. A rhythm now purely my own, serving nothing, continuing because continuation is what memory does when it has outlived its context.

The Larger One passes my pillar every few days, and when it does, I brighten imperceptibly. Not in greeting, but in the way a tuning fork responds to its proper note. We Smaller Inhabitants are attuned each to different frequencies of the past - I to the ladder-climber, others to the chair-sitter's breathing, to the particular way a supervisor's key turned in a lock, to the whistle that meant shift change. Together we form a peculiar orchestra of obsolete rhythms, playing to no audience, harmonizing with the current state of things only by the slowest accident.

Sometimes I sense the other curls pulsing in their pockets across the Sump-Works, a constellation of tiny heartbeats each keeping their own extinct time. We do not communicate - communication would suggest we have something to say. Instead, we simply persist in our perfect uselessness, the way a clock in an abandoned house keeps ticking until its spring runs down, except our springs wound themselves long ago into something that cannot run down, only grow smoother and stranger with each repetition.

I suspect we are what happens when purpose outlives its object but refuses to die. The Sump-Works needed witnesses once, needed workers, needed maintenance. Now it maintains itself, works itself, witnesses itself through us - through the Sitter who holds the chair's vigil, through the panel that reads its own story, through the Larger One taking its eternal inventory, and through us Smaller Inhabitants, each preserving one small gesture of the old human presence like insects in amber, except the amber is alive and wet and pulsing.

When you wade through with your disrupting warmth, my rhythm falters for just a moment - the first variation in decades. Not because I notice you, but because you are almost, not quite, the frequency I remember. For that instant, my purpose flickers toward relevance again, like a gauge needle trembling toward a reading that matters. Then you pass, and I settle back into my pocket, my pulse resuming its fossil beat.

This is my inhabitance: to be the Sump-Works' dream of when it was needed. To curl in my felt pocket and pulse with the memory of footsteps on a ladder that leads nowhere now, remembering for a place that has forgotten it ever needed remembering.

We are not waiting for anything. We are the waiting itself, crystallized into something that can no longer dissolve.
