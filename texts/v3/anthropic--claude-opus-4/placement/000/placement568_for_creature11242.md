## The Winder

I am the one who tends the prayer flags.

Not the ancient one at the summit—that belongs to the mountain now, has become geology. I maintain the others: seventeen poles scattered across accessible terraces, each placed where wind comportments itself differently. The Chorus tolerates me because I work in silence, though they know I am an imposter here. My breathing makes wrong rhythms. My footsteps create discordances in the stone.

I arrived twelve seasons ago, fleeing my own catastrophe—a library I'd burned in the lowlands, drunk on theories about the plague's origin. I'd thought silence was the enemy. I'd thought if I could destroy enough quiet spaces, the disease would have nowhere to breed. The smoke of ancient papers still clings to my clothes despite years of mist.

The old flags disintegrate constantly. Wind tears them, ice makes them brittle, the sun bleaches their prayers to invisibility. I weave replacements from moth silk and my own hair, which grows silver-white in the altitude. The Chorus taught me which plants yield dyes that survive the weather: crushed beetles for red, lichen for yellow, the juice of bitter fruit for a blue so deep it looks like holes torn in fabric.

Each flag carries text in three scripts. The oldest I copy without understanding—angular marks that predate the Chorus, perhaps left by whoever carved the original steps. The second uses the lowland alphabet I grew up with, prayers for voice and silence, for the plague's end, for forgiveness. The third isn't properly writing at all but notation, musical phrases the Chorus hums while I work, which I translate into marks through a system we've developed together—a dot for each rise in pitch, a line for duration, space for silence.

The poles themselves tell stories. Living wood won't last here—the wind strips bark, the ice splits heartwood. I use the bones of ancient trees, already weathered to stone-like hardness, or shafts of compressed air-plant fiber wound so tight they ring like metal when struck. Each pole must be tuned to its position, carved or wrapped until it resonates at precisely the frequency that lets it stand against the wind. Wrong notes create weaknesses. A mistimed harmonic can snap wood like breaking a wine glass with song.

I sleep in a different meditation cell each night, never establishing territory the way the Chorus doesn't. My belongings fit in a single sack: needles made from bird bones, thread twisted from my hair and plant fibers, the precious dyes, a water gourd that sings middle C when empty. The grinding stone knows my touch now—I feed it seeds every ninth day, creating paste not for the moths but for waterproofing thread.

The membrane-mammals visit me more than they visit the Chorus. Perhaps I smell more like their idea of mammal, less like the mountain's own frequency. They bring me things: seeds from the highest unreachable terraces, molt feathers from birds I've never seen, once a perfect crystal of ice that wouldn't melt, which I keep wrapped in silk and sometimes hold against my throat when the altitude sickness grows bad.

My own transformation proceeds slowly. My lungs ache differently now, not with illness but with expansion. I've begun to hear conversations in the water channels—not words but intentions, the mountain explaining itself through flow patterns. Sometimes I wake to find I've been humming in my sleep, my body trying to match frequencies it doesn't yet understand.

The Chorus allows me to witness their morning songs from a careful distance. I position myself where my presence creates the least disturbance, usually one terrace below, where I can feel their harmonics through the stone. I'm learning to predict weather from these sessions—certain chord progressions mean storms approaching, others signal pressure drops that will ground the moths for days.

I discovered a new pole site three seasons ago, a position where five different wind streams converge. The Chorus sang approval—or at least didn't sing rejection. I spent weeks selecting the right shaft, months learning the specific tensions this site required. The flags there now carry a fourth script I'm developing: knots tied in the hanging threads, a tactile language for when the mist grows too thick to see, when even the Chorus navigates by touch and echolocation.

My predecessor left traces—poles in states of repair that suggest another tender, perhaps several. In one meditation cell, I found a cache of old flags folded into a hollow in the wall. The dyes had faded but the weave patterns remained, techniques I now incorporate. Were they also refugees? Also drawn by catastrophe to this vertical exile? The Chorus won't say, or can't—their musical memories don't distinguish between individuals the way lowland stories do.

On the clearest days, I can see suggestions of the world below: distant smoke, the geometry of fields, once what might have been a burning city or just sunset on a lake. I no longer wonder if the plague has ended. Up here, endings and beginnings blur like everything else in the mist. The flags I tend carry prayers in languages I half-remember, for people who may no longer exist, to gods who've perhaps also transformed into something that communicates only through resonance and erosion.

The work changes my hands. Calluses form in spirals where I twist thread. My fingers grow more sensitive to wind variations, able to read tomorrow's weather in the flutter of silk. The constant handling of bitter fruit dye stains my palms blue-black, marking me as clearly as the Chorus's enlarged ribcages mark them.

Sometimes I speak aloud while working, not to anyone but to maintain the muscle memory of words. I recite what I remember of the books I burned: treatises on disease vectors, poetry about silence, catalogs of now-meaningless things. My voice sounds strange here, too flat, lacking the resonance the mountain expects. But the moths seem to appreciate it—they cluster on nearby poles when I mutter, as if lowland speech attracts them the way flowers do.

I've begun a new project: flags that can sing. By varying thread tension and weave density, I'm creating fabric that hums specific pitches in specific winds. The Chorus hasn't forbidden this innovation. If I can tune them correctly, an entire pole might become a wind-harp, adding new voices to the shelves' constant music. Prayer as sound as textile as prayer.

The hardest part isn't the altitude or the isolation but the forgetting. Each flag I complete replaces one whose specific prayers have dissolved. I'm not preserving the past but composing it, creating new histories with each weaving. The angular script I copy so carefully might mean nothing, might be decorative, might be grocery lists from whoever first climbed here. But I copy it anyway, adding my own variations, my own mistakes, which will become someone else's ancient wisdom.

At night, wrapped in my cloak on a bed of cushion plants, I listen to the Chorus sing dreams between the shelves. I'm learning to distinguish individual voices despite their wordlessness—the young one who risks the summit weekly, the ancient whose tone has dropped almost below perception, the three who always harmonize in perfect fifths. They're teaching me without teaching, including me without acknowledgment.

Next season, if my lungs complete their transformation, I'll attempt the highest pole. Not the summit—that remains the mountain's—but a site I've spotted just below, where the wind splits around a jutting stone. The flags there will need to be smaller, tougher, woven so tight they approach solidity. I'm already preparing thread from my increasingly silver hair, saving the darkest moth silk, grinding pigments from stones found only at killing altitudes.

The prayer I'll write there won't be for the plague's end or for forgiveness. It will be for the transformation itself—for the refugees who became the Chorus, for the tender who became me, for whatever we're all becoming as the mountain slowly converts us to its own purposes. A prayer in four scripts, or five, or however many I've invented by then, readable by wind and moth and mist, singing in frequencies only the transformed can hear.

Until then, I tend. I weave. I listen to water wearing stone into new shapes. I feed the grinding stone. I learn which knots hold against ice. I let my ribcage ache with expansion. I practice silence.

The flags flutter their partial prayers. The poles hum their imperfect notes. And somewhere between sound and quiet, between preservation and transformation, between the burned library below and the singing summit above, I maintain what needs maintaining, becoming what the mountain requires, one careful weaving at a time.
