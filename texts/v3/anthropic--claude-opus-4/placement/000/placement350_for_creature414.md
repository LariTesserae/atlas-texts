In the middle tiers, where the fog hangs thickest and the distinction between day and night dissolves into perpetual green twilight, I am the Tender of Forgotten Things.

My domain consists of the spaces between spaces—the gaps where pipes diverge, creating pockets too small for proper platforms but large enough for secrets. Here, in these overlooked hollows, grow the specimens no one planted: the sports and mutations, the accidental hybrids born from spilled seeds and crossed spores. My coveralls have evolved their own ecosystem, pockets lined with moss that keeps cuttings fresh, shoulder seams where tiny ferns have taken root.

I was a Messenger once, twenty years ago, racing through the vertical maze with urgent dispatches. But I moved too slowly, distracted by every strange leaf, every peculiar bloom. The other children finished their routes while I crouched in corners, watching how certain vines grew toward the vibration of specific pipes, how some flowers opened only when the fog reached precise density.

The Keeper found me one day, curled in a junction box where five levels of runoff converged, transplanting a phosphorescent fungus that grew nowhere else. Instead of scolding, she sat beside me in the dripping dark and taught me its name—not the name from any book, but the one she'd given it: *Phantom's Lantern*. She showed me how to propagate it, how to read its glow for signs of metal toxicity in the water.

Now I maintain the unplanned gardens, the accidents that became essential. My routes spiral through the tower's forgotten spaces: the old overflow channels replaced but never removed, the structural redundancies where experimental species escaped cultivation and evolved on their own terms. I know where the Weeping Circuits grow—those vines that learned to follow electrical current, their roots so intertwined with the wiring that removing them would mean rewiring entire sections.

My notebook is wrapped in the same oilcloth as those who came before, filled with observations no one asked for but everyone eventually needs. "Level 32, northwest corner: black orchids that bloom only during pressure drops. Their nectar prevents joint rust." "Between 41 and 42: colony of silver moss that indicates structural stress through color change." "The crow has taught its offspring to cache seeds in the dead zones—check monthly for new growth."

I see the other inhabitants rarely but know them all through their traces. The Harvesters' paths avoid my gardens, leaving the wild spaces to their own logic. The Recyclers send up specific nutrient mixes when I request them, trusting my reports on what strange chemistry the forgotten corners require. The Welders consult me before major additions—I know which walls bear the weight of irreplaceable ecosystems, which pipes can't be moved without killing decades of growth.

The Messengers still find me sometimes, always the ones who run too slowly, too curiously. I teach them what the Keeper taught me: that the tower's true achievement isn't its engineering but its accidents, its mutations, its endless capacity for surprise. Some stay, becoming assistant tenders of sub-sections, learning the patient art of watching things grow wrong until they become right.

My skin carries traces of a hundred species—spores and pollens, saps and oils that have marked me as surely as any Recycler's bloom. In the markets below, when I descend monthly for supplies, vendors know not to hand me money directly. The organisms I carry aren't harmful, but they change things, encourage growth in unexpected directions. Coins left too long in my pockets sprout tiny forests of oxidation.

I sleep in a different place each night, wherever my rounds conclude. Every junction box and maintenance alcove holds a cache of my supplies: sealed jars of growth medium, specialized tools for taking cuttings without damaging the parent, emergency rations that won't spoil in the constant damp. The crow knows all my resting spots and sometimes leaves gifts—usually bolts and washers, but occasionally seeds from the city below, carried up in its clever beak.

My real work happens in the margins of the tower's breathing. I document the emergence of new species, the slow evolution of the original plantings into something unrecognizable. I map the secret networks—roots that have learned to tap directly into water pipes, flowers that bloom in response to the elevator's vibration, moss that grows thicker where human hands touch most often.

The tower accepts me as it accepts its other organs. I am neither Keeper nor Harvester, neither Builder nor Recycler, but something between—a tender of the unintended, a guardian of accidents, a witness to the beautiful failures that make the system complete. In the fog between levels, tending gardens no one authorized in spaces no one designed, I help the tower become what it was never meant to be: truly alive, truly wild, truly itself.
