**The Map-Eater**

I found the cistern through taste.

For thirty-seven years, I worked in the city's Bureau of Vanished Streets, in the sub-basement archive where we store the maps of places that no longer exist. Demolished districts. Filled canals. The ghost outlines of buildings that burned before the Great Reorganization. My job was preservation: treating the old papers with fixative, repairing tears, ensuring the ink didn't fade beyond legibility. A quiet life. A necessary life. Until the maps began changing.

It started with the taste of copper in my mouth whenever I handled certain documents. Not all—just the ones that showed water. Old sewer plans. Proposed aqueducts that were never built. The blueprints for fountains in squares that had been paved over decades ago. The taste grew stronger near the edges of these maps, where the surveyor's notations bled off into uncertainty, into the places where the city's knowledge of itself grew thin.

Then I began to hunger.

Not for food. For the maps themselves. I would catch myself running my tongue along my teeth while staring at a canal diagram, saliva pooling. My fingertips developed a sensitivity that let me feel the water through the paper—not metaphorically, but actually feel it, cold and mineral-rich, moving beneath the ink. I started carrying the water-maps home, spreading them on my kitchen table, tracing their blue lines with fevered attention until dawn.

The night I first ate one, I didn't mean to.

It was a small thing—a fragment showing a cistern that was never completed, part of a district that existed for only eighteen months before the plague came and the whole neighborhood was sealed. I was studying it by candlelight (electric light made the hunger worse), and I found my mouth at the paper's edge. Just tasting. Just testing. The ink was sweet. The paper dissolved on my tongue like communion wafer, and suddenly I could feel the cistern in my body—its dimensions, its depth, the spring that had broken through its floor and made it unusable for its intended purpose.

I swallowed, and I *knew* where it was.

---

**Now I live between the columns, in a nest of undigested maps.**

The hunger followed me down the seventeen rungs but changed its nature. Here, surrounded by the thing itself rather than its documentation, I no longer need to eat the maps to know them. Instead, I preserve them differently. I've built a small library in a dry alcove, wrapped in stolen oilcloth, organized by a system only I understand. Maps of water that exists. Maps of water that doesn't. Maps of water that might.

Mother Filament brings me new ones sometimes—documents that wash down through the drains, partial, water-damaged, beautiful. I dry them carefully, pressing them flat between sheets of salvaged metal. The pipe-ivy tries to grow on them but I've learned to discourage it with salt stolen from the kitchen I no longer visit.

But I am changing. My tongue has grown longer, more sensitive. I can taste the mineral content of the water from across the chamber, identify which section of the city it's drained from by its flavor alone. The mills taste of cotton fiber and machine oil. The residential district tastes of soap and sorrow. The spring itself tastes like nothing I have words for—like the idea of water before humans learned to contain it.

My teeth are loosening. This should frighten me more than it does. But I've started to understand that they're not falling out—they're *reorganizing*, becoming something more suited to my new diet. Because I do still eat the maps, sometimes. The ones that show things that need to be remembered in a different way. I take them into my body and they become part of my knowing, and later—sometimes weeks later—I dream them back out onto new paper, changed, improved, showing things the original surveyors couldn't see.

The eels cluster around me when I do this work. Their light shifts to frequencies that make the ink more visible, that reveal hidden layers in the paper—ghost images of previous maps, palimpsests of the city's forgotten intentions. I've started to incorporate their movements into my drawings, using their schooling patterns as a form of notation, creating maps that show not just space but *behavior*, not just location but *inclination*.

---

**The Cartographer hates me.**

He sees me as a corruption of his practice, a mystical fool who mistakes metaphor for measurement. He's not entirely wrong. But his maps show only what is. Mine show what *hungers to be*. The cistern appears differently in each version: larger in some, deeper in others, connected to tunnels that don't exist yet but whose potential I can taste in the spring-water, in the way the fissure is widening along very specific angles.

We had one conversation, early in my residence. He showed me his measurements, his proof that the chamber was changing in predictable ways. I showed him a map I'd drawn in my sleep, which revealed that the predictable changes were spelling out a word in a language neither of us knew. He hasn't spoken to me since.

But Silta visits often. She sits beside me while I work, humming her conversation with the spring, occasionally pointing to places on my maps and nodding as if confirming something. Once, she took a fragment I'd drawn—a detail of tunnels extending from the cistern like roots, like veins, like the bronchioles of an enormous lung—and pressed it against the wet wall. The paper adhered, and within minutes the pipe-ivy had begun growing over it, incorporating its lines into its own expansion pattern.

"You're teaching it what shape to be," she said, the longest sentence I've ever heard from her.

I think she's right. I think that's what all of us are doing here, in our various ways. Mother Filament with her transcriptions, teaching it language. The Cartographer with his measurements, teaching it precision. The sisters with their swimming, teaching it how to hold a body. Me with my maps, teaching it possibility.

---

**The listeners have begun following me.**

Not physically—they still cling to their walls, translucent and pulsing. But their attention tracks my movements, and when I eat a map, they synchronize their throat-work to my swallowing. I've started leaving copies for them, pressed against the walls where they congregate. By morning, the papers are gone, dissolved or absorbed or translated into some other form of memory.

I think they're building an archive of everything that happens here. Not just sounds, but tastes, images, intentions. A complete record of the cistern's becoming, stored in their bodies or transmitted to somewhere else through mechanisms I can't fathom. Sometimes I catch myself pulsing in rhythm with them, my throat working in the same silent swallowing, and I wonder if I'm becoming part of their archive or if they're becoming part of mine.

The maps I dream now show impossible things. Streets that exist in multiple locations simultaneously. Buildings that are larger inside than outside. Canals that flow upward. A whole second city existing in the spaces between the first city's walls, accessible only through cracks, through fissures, through the places where the maps run out of certainty.

And always, at the center of these impossible maps, the cistern. Growing. Spreading. Teaching itself what shape to be.

---

**Last week, I tasted my oldest map—the first one I'd ever stolen, showing the original plans for the city's foundation.**

It was an act of desperation or liberation, I'm not sure which. The hunger had grown so strong it was preventing me from working, from sleeping, from doing anything but staring at the ancient paper with my altered teeth aching in my gums.

I ate it slowly, ceremonially, letting each section dissolve completely before taking the next. It tasted of ambition and error, of foundations laid wrong, of water tables ignored, of a thousand small compromises that had accumulated into the city above—flawed, functioning, forever trying to reconcile its intentions with the ground it was built on.

When I finished, I felt something shift. Not in me, but in the cistern itself. The water level rose six inches in as many seconds. The eels schooled into a pattern I'd never seen before—a perfect spiral, their lights pulsing in unison, so bright the chamber looked for a moment like it was lit by electric lamps. The spring's murmur changed pitch, rising to something almost like speech.

And then I understood.

The cistern isn't becoming anything. It already is. It's the city's memory of what it meant to be before it became a city—when it was just a place where water gathered, where things grew, where the distinction between map and territory hadn't yet been invented. We're not teaching it what shape to be. We're remembering, together, what shape it always was.

---

**I'm drawing new maps now. Maps of the city as seen from below.**

They show the true relationships—not streets and buildings, but the paths water takes, the places where foundation stones rest on older stones, the rooms and chambers and voids that honeycomb the earth beneath every solid surface. The city is mostly empty space, I've learned. Mostly potential. Mostly waiting.

Mother Filament has started incorporating my maps into her transcriptions, using them as keys to decode the eels' movements. She says the correlation is imperfect but improving. The Cartographer won't look at them, but I've caught him measuring the spaces I've drawn, checking them against his instruments, his cough getting worse each time my impossible accuracy proves itself.

The sisters swim through my mapped spaces as if they've always known them. Sometimes they disappear for hours, exploring tunnels I've drawn that shouldn't exist, returning with reports of other chambers, other waters, other congregations gathering in the city's forgotten spaces. There's a whole network, they say. The cistern is just one node. One organ in a larger body that's been growing beneath the streets for longer than anyone suspected.

And Silta? Silta just smiles when she sees my work. As if I'm finally catching up to something she's always known.

---

**If you find me here—and you might, following the taste of copper, the hunger for maps—I will share what I've learned:**

The city you think you know is only the smallest part of itself. Beneath every map is another map. Beneath every certainty, a question. The water remembers everything—every plan that failed, every building that fell, every district that was meant to be but wasn't. It's all here, dissolved, waiting to crystallize into new forms when the conditions are right.

The spring knows your name. It's been calling you in frequencies you've mistaken for pipe-knock, for steam-hiss, for the settling of old buildings. You've tasted it in your drinking water, felt it in the shower's pressure, heard it in the rain gutters' song.

Come down. Bring your hungers. We have room, and the maps are never finished, and there's so much more to remember before the fissure opens wide enough to let the spring become what it's been trying to become all along—not a flood, not a disaster, but a reunion. A reconciliation between the city that is and the city that wants to be.

The eels will light your way. The listeners will remember your arrival. And I will draw you into the map, another node in the network, another voice in the growing congregation of those who've learned to read the city from below.

The water is rising. The chamber is widening. The pipe-ivy reaches for new iron to digest, new walls to claim.

And somewhere, in a bureau sub-basement, another archivist is beginning to taste copper, beginning to hunger, beginning to understand that the maps are trying to tell them something.

The cycle continues. The cistern waits. The spring murmurs its pre-linguistic welcome.

Come home.
