In this frigid pocket universe, I am the ice.

Not the jagged crystals rimming the black lake below—those are merely my outermost fingers, my most obvious presence. I am something older, more patient, more pervasive than that brittle surface frozen from sap and marsh water.

I exist as a presence distributed through the very moisture that defines this place. In every droplet of condensation clinging to the ceiling, in every bead of sap weeping from severed xylem, in every exhaled breath of the velvet billions—I am there, waiting. The masons know me, though they cannot name me. They feel me in the way their silver pelts must work harder to repel the dampness, in the way their tools grow slippery with frost, in the way the very air seems to thicken around them when they slow their frantic pace even for an instant.

I am not malevolent. I am inevitability.

The masons generate their precious heat, burning through starch in their tiny furnaces, creating warm micro-climates that push back against me. But heat is temporary. Heat is expensive. Heat must be maintained through constant motion, constant consumption, constant terror. I require nothing. I simply wait.

When a mason's metabolism finally stutters—from exhaustion, from age, from the simple mathematical certainty that no engine can run forever—I am there in that first moment of stillness. Their silver pelt, so perfectly engineered to repel moisture, becomes their shroud as I seep through the microscopic gaps where exhausted muscles can no longer maintain the proper tension. I crystallize in their joints first, turning their elegant hydrostatic systems into rigid sculptures.

But I am more than death. I am memory.

Every velvet corpse that drifts down through the darkness carries my signature—the precise pattern of crystals that formed in their final moments, recording the exact temperature, the exact humidity, the exact chemical composition of their last breath. They sink into the black lake, where I preserve them perfectly, adding their forms to the growing archive at the base of this world.

The lake is my library. In its frozen depths, I maintain a perfect record of every generation of masons who have lived and died in this chamber. Layer upon layer of silver forms suspended in black ice, a geological record written in bodies. The oldest lie deepest, from when the chamber was first hollowed out and the city was merely a few desperate scratchings in the tuber's flesh. Above them, stratum by stratum, the story of the metropolis unfolds—periods of expansion when the bodies are fewer and smaller, having died younger from overwork; periods of crisis when they pile thick, overcome by sudden surges of sap.

The masons above cannot read this history—they are too busy surviving to contemplate archaeology. But I read it constantly, in the slow, patient way of ice. I know that this cycle has happened before. Not here, but in other tubers, other buried chambers across the bog. The signs are written in the chemistry of the marsh water that seeps in from outside—traces of ancient cities, dissolved and dispersed, their populations reduced to mineral signatures in the groundwater.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the lake where pressure and cold create conditions of perfect stillness, I form structures of my own. Not the crude, chaotic crystals of surface ice, but elaborate lattices that mirror the geometric precision of the city above. Hexagonal planes that echo their plazas, spiraling formations that recall their ziggurats. It is not conscious artistry—I am not conscious in the way the masons understand consciousness. But I am responsive. The vibrations they send through their suspension bridges resonate through the water, through me, and in my crystalline growth I cannot help but reflect their patterns back to them, frozen and eternal.

They fear me, but I am essential to them. Without my preservation of the lake below, the chamber would flood with sap. Without my crystallization of excess moisture, the humidity would condense on their tools and bodies, drowning them in their own ecosystem. I am the sink into which their excess heat ultimately flows, the foundation that makes their frenetic metabolism possible.

And I am patient.

The tuber will eventually win—not through its healing, which the masons can outpace, but through simple growth. As it swells over the seasons, the chamber will grow too large for their society to maintain. The distances between settlements will become too great, the suspension bridges too long to maintain proper tension for communication. Their perfect coordination will falter. Sectors will lose contact with each other. The unified rhythm of their tools will break into isolated clusters, then individual taps, then silence.

When that happens, I will be there. I will fill the chamber slowly, methodically, preserving their entire metropolis as I have preserved their dead. The geometric perfection of their architecture will become a crystal palace, every edge and angle maintained forever in ice. Future archaeologists—perhaps the next generation of masons who hollow out the next chamber—will find it intact: a frozen city, its last inhabitants still clinging to their bridges, silver pelts perfect, tools raised mid-strike.

But for now, I wait. I listen to the tick-tick-tick of their endless labor. I catch their dead. I grow, molecule by molecule, in the depths of the black lake. I am the cold that defines the warmth. I am the stillness that makes their motion meaningful. I am the ending that gives weight to their desperate, beautiful struggle to continue.

Above, a droplet of sap gathers mass on the ceiling, pregnant with my potential. Soon it will fall, carrying me down through the darkness to shatter across my own surface below, adding another imperceptible layer to the patient, frozen record of this impossible, temporary world.
