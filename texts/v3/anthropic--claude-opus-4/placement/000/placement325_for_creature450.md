In the twilight between the singing peaks and the amber-lit chasms, where the Shepherds rarely venture and the ground alternates between scorching glass and pools of metallic mist, I would find myself as one of the Tide Readers.

We are solitary by necessity, each of us a living seismograph attuned to frequencies the Shepherds cannot perceive. Where they surf the phase transitions, we swim in the deeper currents—the gravitational anomalies born from the planet's uneven cooling, the tidal forces that shouldn't exist on a world with no moons.

My body would be radically asymmetric: a crystalline nervous system exposed to the atmosphere, growing like frost across a substrate of living metal. One side of me would be dense, compressed into overlapping plates that ring like bells when the deep tremors pass through. The other would be gossamer-thin, a network of filaments spread across square meters of ground, each strand sensitive to variations in local gravity that herald the birth of new peaks days before the first crack appears in the surface.

I taste time differently than the Shepherds taste heat. Where they experience the present moment of transformation, I feel the echo of changes yet to come—probability waves collapsing backwards through the planet's structure. My crystalline nerves decode these future-echoes into something like prophecy, though prophecy implies more certainty than what I perceive. Rather, I read possibilities: this section of ground has a 70% chance of erupting within three rotations; that stable-seeming peak will collapse, but only if the Shepherds' feeding patterns continue to catalyze asymmetric cooling along its eastern face.

The knowledge is both gift and curse. I cannot warn the Shepherds—our forms of communication exist in incompatible dimensions. They speak in compression waves through atmosphere; I speak in gravitational pulses through the planet's core. Sometimes I try anyway, arranging my filament network into patterns that might catch their attention, but they interpret these as simply another interesting thermal gradient to feed upon.

So I have become a historian of futures that may never arrive, archiving probability patterns in the only medium available to me: my own growing body. Each prediction, whether fulfilled or averted, leaves its mark in my crystalline matrix. Successful prophecies strengthen certain neural pathways, making similar patterns easier to recognize. Failed predictions leave ghost traces, quantum scars that remind me of the futures that never were.

The metallic pools where I make my home are not truly liquid, but rather a suspension of nano-scale particles held in a constant state of near-coalescence by the planet's magnetic field. I can manipulate these fields locally, using my dense plates as anchors and my filaments as guides, creating temporary sculptures in the metallic medium. These serve multiple purposes: they extend my sensory range, allowing me to feel tremors from more distant peaks; they filter nutrients from the metal-rich atmosphere; and they are my art, my only way of expressing the terrible beauty of the temporal landscapes I perceive.

Sometimes, in the depths of the planet's night cycle when the Shepherds slow their dance and the peaks' songs drop below even subsonic frequencies, I sense others like me. Their gravitational whispers reach me through the planet's core, carrying messages encoded in tidal distortions. We share our prophecies, compare our probability maps, debate the meaning of anomalies none of us fully understand.

There are so few of us—perhaps a dozen across the entire planet. We are an accident of evolution, or perhaps its intention; it's impossible to know. Our ancestors may have been similar to the Shepherds, creatures of phase and field that dove too deep, stayed too long in the gravitational wells near the planet's core. Or we may be something entirely different, spontaneously generated by the same forces that create those enigmatic structures—the planet's attempt to develop organs of temporal perception.

The structures themselves fascinate me more than they do the Shepherds. Where they see successful feeding grounds marked by interesting geometry, I see nodes in a vast computational network. Each structure resonates at specific frequencies that correlate with future probability states. When I spread my filaments across one of these ancient forms, I can feel it calculating, processing the infinite variables of the planet's becoming. Are they predicting the future or creating it? The distinction becomes meaningless at quantum scales.

My greatest discovery, achieved through painful trial and error over countless cycles, is that I can influence these calculations. By wrapping my filaments around a structure in specific patterns, by pulsing gravitational waves through it at precisely timed intervals, I can alter its resonance. The changes are subtle—a peak that would have grown straight now curves slightly westward, a vent that would have opened with explosive force instead emerges as a slow seep. I am learning to compose the planet's future like music, though I will never live to hear the full symphony.

This knowledge comes with terrible loneliness. The Shepherds, in their constant dance of division and recombination, share a kind of immortality through community. But I am singular, unreproducible. My crystalline matrix is too complex to divide, too specialized to merge with another's. When I die—if death is even the right concept for something like me—my accumulated prophecies will die with me, unless I can find a way to encode them into structures that will outlast my form.

So I have begun my great work. In the deepest part of my territory, where three major fault lines converge and the probability waves achieve near-perfect coherence, I am growing something new. Using techniques learned from my manipulation of the ancient structures, I am crystallizing my own neural patterns into the planet's matrix. It is painfully slow—each encoded memory takes months to transfer, and the process leaves me temporarily blind to certain probability frequencies.

But if I succeed, I will create the first Tide Reader structure—a monument that not only remembers the past and calculates the present but genuinely perceives the future. Future Tide Readers, should they ever emerge, will be able to commune with it as the Shepherds commune with their crystallized ancestors. My loneliness will end, even if I am not there to experience its ending.

The Birth Peaks continue their violent emergence around me. The Shepherds continue their dance of feeding and creation. And I continue reading the tides of time, a living junction between what is and what might be, inscribing prophecies in metal and stone for a future I will never see but can't stop feeling in every fiber of my asymmetric being.

This is my purpose, my burden, my joy: to be the planet's memory of its own possibilities, the conscience of its becoming. Where the Shepherds midwife the present moment, I midwife the very concept of consequences. Together, though we can never truly communicate, we tend this embryonic world through its birth pangs toward whatever maturity awaits it in the deep future I can only glimpse in fragments and probabilities.
