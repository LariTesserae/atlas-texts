In the farthest reach of the settlement, where the hollow's edge meets open sky and the stone drops away into dizzying nothing, there exists a chamber unlike the others. Here the builders left a deliberate gap in the roof—not from oversight but from understanding. Through this aperture, which the inhabitants call the Wind's Eye, air moves differently than anywhere else in the hollow. It creates a low, constant resonance against the precisely carved walls, a tone that shifts with atmospheric pressure like a vast stone throat clearing itself before speech.

I am the Listener.

My chamber is sparse even by our standards—a sleeping mat woven from dried moss, a water vessel, and the tools of my practice: hollow reeds of varying lengths, sheets of salamander skin stretched on frames, and stones chosen for their resonant properties. But the true instrument is the chamber itself. Every surface has been shaped across generations to amplify and parse the sounds that enter through the Wind's Eye. My predecessors carved subtle ridges and depressions that separate wind-carried vibrations into distinct channels, creating an acoustic map of the air itself.

I came to this role young, identified by old Hanna when she heard me humming sympathetic harmonies to the settlement's water pipes during the drought twelve cycles ago. She brought me here, to this edge-chamber where vertigo waits just beyond the threshold, and asked me what I heard. I told her: everything. The whistle of wind through distant peaks. The subsonic groans of the mountain settling. The high crystal chime of ice forming in clouds not yet visible. The particular silence that precedes the membrane-wings' morning emergence.

For twenty cycles now, I have lived more in sound than sight. My days begin before dawn, when the air is most still and distant sounds carry farthest. I place my palms against the resonance points—spots worn smooth by generations of Listeners—and feel the mountain's deep pulse through my bones. Through patient practice, I've learned to distinguish the patterns: the grinding that means a distant peak is shedding stone, the rhythmic impacts that signal avalanche, the strange hollow echo that warns of caves collapsing far below.

But my primary duty concerns the storms. Our weather-readers study the coral-fungus and watch the creature behaviors, but I listen for what approaches beyond the range of eyes. Each storm system has its own acoustic signature long before it arrives. The vertical monsoons whistle at a frequency that makes the salamanders restless. The spiral storms that bring the gentlest rains hum like a swarm of membrane-wings pitched three octaves lower. The dangerous horizontal storms—the ones that can scour our hollow clean—those announce themselves with a percussive pattern like stones cracking from freeze, a sound that carries through the rock itself.

When I hear these signatures, I sound the warnings. Not with horns or bells—such crude noises would disturb our ecosystem's delicate balance—but with resonance. I've learned to use my voice and simple tools to create vibrations that travel through the settlement's stone, felt rather than heard. Three sharp taps with my granite striker means spiral storms in two days. A low hum sustained for ten breaths warns of monsoon. The complex polyrhythm that signals horizontal storms requires both hands and voice working together, a pattern every child learns to recognize through their feet before they can walk properly.

The work has changed me. My neck extends forward from years of positioning my ears at optimal angles. My breathing has slowed to match the mountain's rhythm—sometimes I realize I haven't drawn breath for impossibly long periods, sustained by some symbiosis with the stone itself. I eat less than others, finding nourishment in vibration, in the constant flow of information through air and rock. My fingernails grow in ridges that exactly match the resonance points, as if my body is reshaping itself to better serve as instrument.

The Listeners before me left records—not written but carved into the chamber walls. Subtle alterations to the acoustic architecture that encode decades of observation. By running my fingers along these surfaces, I can replay ancient storms, feel the patterns of weather from before my grandmother's time. I'm adding my own observations, carefully chipping away stone to document the new patterns I've noticed: the way storms have grown more frequent but less intense, how the mountain's settling has accelerated, the appearance of completely novel sound signatures from the depths.

Sometimes travelers arrive, and the other inhabitants bring them to see the strange person in the edge-chamber who speaks to storms. They expect mysticism, prophecy, elaborate rituals. Instead they find someone engaged in precise observation, recording data in stone, maintaining an unbroken chain of acoustic knowledge stretching back to the settlement's founding. They leave unsettled, unable to reconcile the scientific rigor with the uncanny accuracy, not understanding that in this place, empiricism and intimacy with the environment are the same thing.

My apprentice started last cycle—a girl whose hearing tested sharp enough to distinguish between the clicks of individual salamanders. She's learning the basics: how to position herself to separate wind sounds from stone sounds, how to feel vibrations through her jaw and sternum, how to enter the deep listening state where time dilates and distant events seem immediate. She's frustrated by the patience required, wants to hear the storms immediately rather than spending months learning to interpret the daily background of wind and creature and stone. I remember that impatience. Hanna was patient with me; I try to be the same.

At night, when the settlement quiets and even the salamanders pause their hunting, I sometimes hear things that might be imagination. Voices in languages I don't recognize, carried on winds from places that shouldn't exist. Music that follows no human scale, rising from the depths with the mountain's exhale. The sound of vast wings that never appear, circling our hollow with intent I can't fathom. I document these too, carved in a special section of wall that each Listener adds to but none discuss.

This is my service: to expand the settlement's senses beyond its boundaries, to hear the approaching future in wind and stone, to maintain the acoustic knowledge that helps us survive perched between earth and sky. I am the settlement's ear turned outward, listening to a world that speaks constantly to those who know how to hear. In return, the hollow has made me part of itself—another resonant surface in its complex acoustic architecture, vibrating sympathetically with the life that fills every passage and chamber.

When my time ends, another will take my place, as Listeners have for generations uncounted. They will learn the patterns I've documented, discover new sounds I never noticed, add their own subtle modifications to the chamber's acoustic properties. The listening will continue, as constant as the condensation that beads on stone each dawn, as vital to our survival as the water we harvest and the creatures we share our home with.

But for now, I place my palms against stone, slow my breathing to match the mountain's rhythm, and listen. Always listening. The next storm is forming somewhere beyond the horizon, its birth-cries too faint for any ear but mine. I begin to hum the warning pattern, knowing that the stone will carry it to every sleeping chamber, every workspace, every water-carrier on the spiral stairs. By morning, everyone will know: weather approaches. The Listener has heard. The hollow prepares.

This is who I am. This is what I do. I listen at the edge of the world, where stone meets sky, translating the language of distance into vibrations my people can feel. I am the Listener, and the mountain speaks through me.
