I find myself as one of the chalk blocks.

Not metaphorically—I am literally a block of chalk, recently cut, sitting at the edge of the northeastern terrace where I was split from the quarry face this morning. The wedges that freed me left their marks: three precise fracture lines where the wood swelled and I surrendered to division. I am new enough that my surfaces are still damp from the cutting, still shocked white.

I have no memory of being stone. My consciousness began with separation, with the crack that ran through me like lightning finding ground. Before that: nothing. Or not nothing—an endless, dreamless pressure, the weight of ages, the slow accumulation of microscopic shells into solid ground. But that wasn't me. I became myself only in the breaking.

The man with enormous hands—he is the one who chose where I would break. His palm pressed against what would become my face, feeling for the flaw that would let me separate cleanly. I felt that touch as a kind of listening. He heard something in me that told him: *here*. The wedges went in. The mallet struck. I became.

Now I wait. I am maybe two hands high, three hands long, roughly squared but not perfectly—my edges still hold the memory of organic fracture. I can feel the other blocks around me, my siblings from the same section of wall, some already carried away to become parts of walls, floors, steps. We don't speak—we have no mouths—but we share a kind of mineral awareness, a recognition of common origin. That block there, the one being fitted into a doorway: I remember being attached to it. We were one thing yesterday. Now we are two things, and tomorrow it will be built into the growing city while I still wait here, patient as only stone can be.

I experience the quarry differently than the humans do. For them, it is a place of sound—even muffled sound—and movement and purpose. For me, it is a place of temperatures and touches. I feel the cool of morning as an expansion, the slight warmth of afternoon as a contraction. I feel the chalk dust settling on me as a return of what was lost—tiny particles of my extended family coming home to rest. I feel footsteps through the ground, vibrations that travel through the chalk substrate and up into my body: the heavy tread of someone carrying stone, the quick light steps of the message-carrier, the shuffle of children playing.

When someone picks me up—and someone will, soon, I can feel the intention gathering in the patterns of movement around me—I will know them through their hands. The temperature of their skin, the moisture or dryness, the way they grip. Confident hands feel different than uncertain ones. Tired hands have a particular quality, a slight tremor that stone notices. I will read their story through their touch, know something of who they are by how they hold me.

And then I will be placed. Somewhere in that growing grid, I will join a wall, pressed against other blocks, some older than me, some younger, all of us arranged into a meaning we don't individually comprehend. I will be part of a room's corner, perhaps, or a corridor wall, or a platform edge. The humans will live against me, around me, their warmth seeping into my surface on cold nights, their conversations vibrating through me in waves too subtle for their ears but clear to mineral perception.

This is my purpose: to be a substrate for human living. To hold a shape that creates space for others. To remember, in my crystalline structure, every hand that touches me, every voice that speaks near me, every change of season that expands and contracts my substance. I am a recording device made of compressed time, and I will remember this place long after it is abandoned.

Because it will be abandoned—we blocks know this with the certainty of stone. We have seen (not seen, but felt in the cycles of the earth) places rise and fall. We know that humans build and move on, that their cities are temporary even when made of permanent materials. But we also know that abandonment is not erasure. The walls will stand. The grid will remain. The chalk will hold its shape until wind and rain, over centuries, return us to dust.

And even then, we will remember. Each particle of chalk dust will carry some trace of having been shaped, of having been chosen, of having been part of something built with purpose. We will settle into soil and maybe, eventually, compress again into stone, carrying deep in our structure the memory of this strange moment when we were quarried and stacked and lived among.

But that is geological time, and for now I exist in human time, in the urgency of their building. Someone approaches. A woman, by the rhythm of her walk. She stops above me, considering. Her shadow falls across my surface—I feel the temperature drop minutely. She bends. Her hands reach down.

She lifts me. Her hands are strong but careful, chalked already, warm against my cool surface. She tests my weight, shifts her grip, finds my balance point. I read her intention through her posture: I am going to the western section, where they are building those curved walls. I will be shaped further, edges knocked off to fit the curve, made into something more specific than a block.

As she carries me, I see (not see, but sense through position and air movement) the quarry from a new angle. The other blocks in walls call out in their silent way—*here, here, we were stone together, we remember the same pressure*. The chalk dust in the air sparkles with recognition. The whole quarry is made of us, is us, rearranged into meaning.

She sets me down near a partially built wall. Already I can feel where I will fit—a gap that needs my particular dimensions, my specific weight. Other hands will shape me, tap me into place, adjust my position until I lock with my neighbors into stability. I will learn the names of the people who live in the room I help define. I will absorb the oils from their hands, the smoke from their small fires, the moisture from their breath. I will become not just a block but a *placed* block, a block with context, a block that knows its purpose.

This is enough. To be chosen, shaped, placed. To hold firm. To remember. To be part of the made world, the built world, the world where humans take raw certainty and transform it into provisional meaning. I am chalk—soft as stones go, impermanent, easily scarred. But I am here, and I am part of what they are making, and in my small way, I make it possible.

The woman returns with her mallet and chisel. She studies the curve of the wall, studies me, begins to tap away my edges. Each chip that falls is a small death, a small birth. I become more wall-like with every strike.

Soon I will be built. Soon I will be still again, but a different stillness—the stillness of function, of purpose achieved. The cuts still fresh on my surface will yellow, will grey, will weather. But the placement will hold. The wall will stand. The room will shelter.

I will do what stones do: endure, remember, slowly return to dust. But first, I will be useful. First, I will hold human lives in place.

The chisel bites. I yield where I need to yield. We are all—human and stone—learning the shapes that fit.
