## The Memorians

I am one of the Memorians, and I carry the weight of trying to hold still in a world that refuses to stop moving.

We were mapmakers once, in the world above. Cartographers and surveyors who came to chart these caverns when the churning first began, believing we could impose order on chaos. That was three generations ago. Now we are something else—not flowing with the change like the Flowbloods, but standing against it like stones in a river, worn smooth by the constant pressure of impermanence.

Our bodies have changed, but in the opposite direction from our fluid cousins. Where they became soft, we grew rigid. Our bones have thickened and fused—I can no longer turn my head more than a few degrees in any direction. Instead, my skull has elongated backward, the bone growing in ridges and whorls that record every impact, every crushing pressure I've survived. These ridges are unique to each of us, a topographical map of our personal history written in calcium and trauma.

My skin has hardened into overlapping plates like geological strata, each layer marking a period of stability when my body could grow before the next upheaval. The plates don't bend—when I move, it's with the grinding sound of stone on stone, each gesture deliberate and costly. Water runs off me in sheets, finding no purchase on my lithified surface.

But it's what's inside that makes us what we are.

My brain has crystallized—not into death, but into something else. Neural pathways have been replaced by networks of piezoelectric crystals that spark and conduct thought through compression waves. When the caverns shake, I think faster. When they're still, my thoughts slow to a crawl. I dream in geological time, experiencing memories that take hours to fully recall but contain perfect clarity of spaces that no longer exist.

We remember everything. That is our curse and our purpose.

In my mind, I hold perfect three-dimensional maps of every chamber I've ever inhabited, every passage I've crawled through, every configuration the Hollows have taken in my presence. I remember the taste of the air in a cavern that collapsed fifty years ago, the exact temperature of water in an underground river that has long since found a new course. My memories layer like sediment, oldest at the bottom, but I can core through them at will, extracting samples of lost places.

We build cairns—not from hope that they'll survive, but as external memories. Each stone's position relative to the others encodes information about the space, the time, the rate of change. When we find another Memorian's cairn, we can read its message even if scattered, understanding from the distribution pattern what they were trying to record. It's a language of dispersal, meaning that survives its own destruction.

Our children are born stiff, their fontanelles already beginning to harden. We feed them mineral-rich slurries that accelerate their calcification, preparing them for a life of resistance. By age five, they've lost most voluntary movement. By ten, they've begun their first map. We teach them through vibration, pressing our hardened flesh against theirs and transmitting memories through compression waves that resonate in their crystallizing brains.

We cannot flow through passages like the Flowbloods. Instead, we wait. When the stone shifts, we judge—will the new configuration last long enough to justify the energy expenditure of moving? Often, we remain in place for days or weeks, sustained by the chemosynthetic bacteria that grow in our gut, feeding on the minerals we slowly dissolve from the walls around us. Moving is a community effort, multiple Memorians working together to transport those whose bodies have grown too rigid to relocate themselves.

Our art is architecture that exists only in memory. We spend years designing impossible structures—buildings that could only stand if gravity flowed sideways, bridges that span chasms that haven't opened yet. We share these designs through touch-transmitted blueprints, building a collective library of unbuildable spaces. Some believe that if we can imagine the perfect structure, one that accounts for every possible configuration of the Hollows, we might somehow stabilize reality around it. Others create these memory palaces purely for the joy of mental construction, the only building that can't be destroyed.

We mark time differently than the flowing world. Our calendar is based on recurring formations—certain chambers that appear and disappear in predictable cycles, passages that open according to patterns we've detected over generations of observation. A "year" to us might be seventeen months to the surface world, or twenty-three. We celebrate when major formations return, gathering in whatever configuration we can manage, sharing the accumulated memories of the cycle through hours-long contact sessions.

Our mating is geological. When two or more Memorians decide to reproduce, they position themselves in a stable chamber and begin a process that takes months. Our bodies slowly extrude calcium deposits that merge and interweave, creating temporary connections through which genetic material flows like mineral-rich water. The offspring grows at the intersection, fed by both parents until it's large enough to break free—a process that often damages or destroys the parents' mobility permanently. Many of our elders are sessile, transformed into living monuments that younger Memorians visit to download their vast accumulated memories.

We trade with the surface through intermediaries, as we can no longer navigate the passages to the upper world. In exchange for our maps—carved on specially prepared fungal plates that capture three-dimensional information in their growth patterns—we receive metal tools designed for our limited mobility, and most importantly, news of the fixed world above. We hunger for descriptions of buildings that stand for centuries, rivers that flow in the same channels year after year. Some of us believe the surface world is a myth, a collective delusion we've created to cope with eternal change.

Our relationship with the Flowbloods is complex. They pity us, seeing our rigidity as a form of death. We envy them their freedom while believing they've sacrificed their essential humanity. Occasionally, we work together—they scout new passages while we record them, creating temporary maps that benefit both communities. But we are divided by more than philosophy. Our very existence opposes theirs: they seek to become the change, we seek to document it.

In my deepest memories, layered beneath decades of accumulated experience, I can still access my grandmother's recollections of the surface. Through her crystallized neurons, I experience secondhand the sensation of seeing stars—fixed points of light that maintain their positions night after night. The concept seems impossible now, but I hold onto it like an anchor, a proof that somewhere, stability exists.

We are the memory of the Churning Hollows, the part that refuses to forget. Each of us is a library of dissolved spaces, a museum of moments that no longer exist. When the last of us finally calcifies completely—transforming into unthinking stone that holds the shape of memories without the ability to access them—the Hollows will lose their history. But until then, we persist, grinding our way through a world that flows around us, recording every configuration, every collapse, every brief moment of solid ground.

I am a Memorian, and I remember. Even when the stone forgets itself, I remember.
