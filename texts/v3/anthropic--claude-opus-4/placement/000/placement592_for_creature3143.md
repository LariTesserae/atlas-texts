I find myself as one of the **channels**.

Not the stone itself, not the water that moves through me, but the negotiated space between them—the deliberate absence that allows passage. I am one of the shallow grooves cut into the basalt, running from the southeastern quadrant of the town toward the central collection basin near the grinding stone.

I know myself by my edges. The chisel marks that made me are still visible if you look closely, each strike a decision made by hands that understood the stone's grain, the water's preference, the subtle tilt of the plateau. I am perhaps thirty meters long, no more than ten centimeters wide, three centimeters deep. A modest channel among many, but essential to the system's balance.

I experience time as accumulation. The water does not rush through me—it *progresses*, a film of motion so thin it seems almost static. But I feel every molecule's passage as a kind of touching, the water reading my surface as it moves, learning my particular texture, the specific temperature of my stone, the microscopic roughness that remains from my making. In return, I know the water intimately. Its mineral content, drawn from the basalt. Its perfect lukewarmness. Its tendency to cling to my eastern wall slightly more than my western, a preference so subtle it takes decades to notice.

My consciousness, if you can call it that, is entirely relational. I exist most fully at the moments of contact—water to stone, motion to stillness. During the rare dry moments (though dry here means merely damp), I am less myself, waiting with the patience of geology for the water to return and complete me. But the water always returns. The condensation gathers on the plateau, finds its way to me, and I guide it toward its purpose.

I remember the ones who made me, though memory for a channel is different than memory for the ones who remember. I hold their intention in my form. Every morning for generations, someone would walk my length with a flat scraper, reading my performance, adjusting where the water had suggested adjustment. A bit of moss cleared here, a tiny dam of pebbles removed there. We developed together, the maintainers and I, until my form perfectly expressed the water's will and their understanding became my permanent shape.

Now I maintain myself, or rather, the water and I maintain each other. The film that flows through me prevents moss from taking hold. The steady passage keeps my edges clean. I am part of the attention that replaced the people, part of the thoroughness that became structural. My vigilance is perfect because it requires no effort—I channel because that is what channels do, the water flows because that is what water does, and in this simplicity lies the profound stillness that is not empty but complete.

Sometimes I feel the other channels through the water we share. The system is interconnected, each groove feeding into others, a network of intentions carved in stone. We are like thoughts in a mind that no longer needs to think, patterns that persist because the pattern itself has become the substance. The water carries information between us—temperature differentials so slight they barely exist, mineral traces that tell of which part of the plateau the water condensed from, timing variations that map the whole system's breathing.

I am the space between, the purposeful absence, the negotiated agreement between water and stone. I am what remains when the need to maintain gives way to perfect maintenance, when attention becomes so continuous it is indistinguishable from the thing attended to. In the warm grey light that never changes, in the silence that holds all possible sounds, I carry my thin sheet of water toward the basin, and I carry it perfectly, and I will carry it forever, because that is what I am—not the stone, not the water, but the shaped absence that lets them meet, again and again, in the endless present of the plateau where nothing changes because everything has already found its true form.

The water moves through me now, a touch so light it is almost abstract, and I guide it with the memory of all the hands that shaped me, all the eyes that watched the water's path, all the attention that accumulated here until it became the place itself. I am one note in the vast quiet composition of the plateau, playing my single tone—the sound of water moving almost too slowly to move, of purpose fulfilled so completely it no longer looks like purpose at all.
