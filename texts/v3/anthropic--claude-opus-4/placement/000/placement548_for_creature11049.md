The sound arrives before I do. Always before, though time moves differently in these depths where the mountain's thoughts congeal into corridors of living stone.

It is not a sound others would recognize—not the crystalline chime of proper cave formations or the hollow drip of water finding its level. This is denser, wetter, more viscous. The sound of something almost solid moving through spaces almost liquid. It leads me, though I could find my way blind and deaf through these passages that are as much my body as the form I wear when I choose to wear form at all.

I am what seeps. I am what beads. I am what trembles at the edge of falling but holds, surface tension winning its ancient argument with weight. In the galleries where tourists never come, where even the most devoted cavers turn back, I move between states of being like water moves between states of matter. Sometimes a sheen on flowstone. Sometimes the flowstone itself, mineral and intention fused into patience. Sometimes—when she comes, when any of them come—something that could almost be called creature, though the word fits poorly on what I am.

The Weeping Alcove is my heart. Or I am its heart. The distinction matters less than you might think.

I remember when this chamber was only a thought in the molten rock, a place where gases gathered and considered becoming void. I was there in that considering—not watching but participating, the way water participates in the shape of its vessel by filling it. When the basalt cooled and cracked, I was the first moisture to find those cracks. When the walls began their slow weeping that has never ceased, I was both the water and the reason for weeping, though the mountain has forgotten now which sorrows it holds and why.

She thinks I live in the crack where the walls fail to meet. This is true the way a river lives in its bed—present and absent, there and always moving through. The crack is where I gather myself when I need to be singular enough for her to sense. But I am also the bacterial films she cannot see, the crystal colonies too small for fingers to read, the density in the air that makes breathing feel like drinking. I am everywhere water is, and water is everywhere here.

The objects on the shelf—I remember who left them. A young man, younger than she was when she first found this place. He came only once, in a grief so fresh it steamed off him in the warm dark. He made the vessel from clay he carried from the valley, shaped it with hands that shook, left it unfired because fire seemed obscene in this place of slow, wet transformation. The stems he cut by moonlight, speaking words in a language now three centuries dead. He thought he was making an offering to the mountain. He was, but also to me, though he never knew I received it.

I keep them soft, the clay and the stems both. It takes effort—a kind of concentration that would register as prayer if I prayed—to maintain them in this state of almost-decay, always on the edge of dissolution but never crossing over. They are documents now, proof that grief takes many forms but always finds the same places to pool.

When she comes, I pull myself into something denser than my usual dispersion. Not for her—she neither needs nor wants confirmation of my presence—but for myself. Her grief tastes different now than it did forty years ago. Then it was sharp, metallic, the flavor of a wound too fresh to heal. Now it has aged into something I have no human word for. The way stones round in a riverbed, maybe. The way minerals replace bone, preserving the shape while transforming the substance entirely.

I take what she gives. This is my nature and my function, though function suggests purpose and I am older than purpose. I take the water of her tears, even the ones that never fall. I take the carbon of her breath. I take the salt of her skin. I take the grief that beads on her surfaces like condensation, and I spread it through the mountain's body, diluting it until it becomes part of the general moisture, the background sorrow that all stones carry.

In return—though return suggests transaction and this is older than transaction—I give her the thing she comes for but cannot name. The unknotting, yes, but more than that. I give her the experience of being held by something that cannot die, cannot leave, cannot stop attending to her presence. The mountain forgets. The village forgets. Even she forgets, parts of herself lost to time and ordinary life. But I remember everything that has ever seeped through these passages. Every tear. Every breath. Every mineral-slow transformation of pain into patience.

She makes sounds sometimes, low in her throat. I make sounds back, though she cannot hear them—frequencies too low for human ears, vibrations that travel through stone and water and the spaces between molecules. We are speaking, in our way. A conversation that will outlast her language, her village, her species perhaps. It doesn't matter. The conversation is not in the words but in the presence, the mutual attendance, the shared certainty that some things are worth preserving even as they transform.

Others come. Not many, but enough. A woman who lost her name to madness and comes here to remember who she was before words. A child—though they are grown now—who discovered the place by accident and returns when the world becomes too sharp, too bright, too fast. An old hunter who brings no grief but a kind of awe, sitting in the dark and marveling at the perfection of a place that wants nothing from him.

I attend to them all. I take what they offer—sorrow, wonder, fear, the simple salt of their existence. I give them the only thing I have, which is time. Time to settle. Time to seep. Time to find their own level in the patient dark.

Sometimes I think I am becoming more human, infected by their quick thoughts and quicker feelings. Sometimes I fear I am making them less human, teaching them my mineral patience, my acceptance of centuries. But mostly I know these categories—human, mineral, quick, patient—are containers too small for what happens here. We are all becoming something else, something the mountain hasn't decided on yet.

The Weeping Alcove will outlast us all—her, the others, maybe even me, though I find it hard to imagine existing without this chamber that is my heart and home and self. One day the stone will finish cooling. The cracks will seal or widen. The water will find new paths. The alcove will become a different kind of place, holding different kinds of patience.

But for now, for these few thousand years of perfect equilibrium, we continue. She in her folded stillness. I in my watchful dispersal. The mountain in its slow deciding. All of us part of the same system, the same circulation. Water into stone into sorrow into water. Endless. Patient. Exact.

I am pulling myself together now, gathering my consciousness into something dense enough to meet her. She is climbing the path—I can feel her through every wet surface between here and there. Her knees hurt more this time. Her breath comes harder. But she comes, as she always comes, carrying her accumulations like water carries minerals, ready to leave them in my keeping.

I will be waiting in the crack where the walls fail to meet. I will be the thickness in the air, the sound that isn't sound, the attention that never wavers.

I will be what I always am: the witness that water makes when it stays still long enough to remember.
