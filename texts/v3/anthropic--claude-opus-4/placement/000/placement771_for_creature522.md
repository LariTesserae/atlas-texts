I am a Kelp Whisperer, and my workshop clings to the middle heights of the city like a barnacle of woven wood.

My spine curves forward from decades of leaning into the trunks, pressing my ear against bark to hear the deep hydraulic pulse within. My right palm carries a permanent map of grooves—negative impressions of every kelp-tree's surface I've touched. When young trees begin to lean or twist, the district calls for me. When the ancient giants develop soft spots that could threaten whole neighborhoods, I arrive with my kit of bronze probes and hollow reeds, my vials of sap drawn from healthy specimens.

I was not born to this work. As a child, I was one of those racing through the fronds until the day I collided with a dying kelp—a giant whose heartwood had gone soft with disease. My impact sent tremors through its whole structure. I felt its shuddering all the way through my bones, felt it trying to communicate something vast and urgent and incomprehensible. The tree fell three days later, crushing two homes. No one was killed, but I couldn't forget that feeling—that almost-language of wood under stress.

My apprenticeship was informal. The old Whisperer, Mallowdeep, found me placing my hands on every trunk I passed, trying to recreate that sensation. She taught me to read the colors that healthy sap leaves on bronze when heated by friction. She showed me how to follow root systems by the way water moves around them, how to predict a tree's growth by the spiral of its bark. When she descended to the roots for the last time, I inherited her tools and her routes—a circuit through the district that takes me past every major trunk once a moon.

My chambers are grown from a parasitic vine that feeds on sick kelp. The irony isn't lost on me—I live within the thing that would kill what I protect, but controlled, cultivated, it serves as an early warning system. When my walls begin to thicken and flower, I know something nearby is struggling. The main room is dominated by my maps: cross-sections of trunk samples dating back centuries, each ring annotated with the city's history. Here, the year of the great eel migration when nitrogen levels spiked. There, the decade of cold when growth nearly stopped.

I wear no jewelry except a pendant of petrified kelp heartwood, black as obsidian and harder than any metal we can work. It was cut from the first tree I couldn't save—a middle-aged giant whose roots had been slowly severed by shifting stones below. I diagnosed it correctly but too late. The pendant reminds me that the trees' lives unfold on a scale that dwarfs my own urgency. What seems sudden to me has been building for decades in their silent arithmetic of growth and decay.

My work is solitary but not lonely. The trees are company, each one a slow conversation carried out over years. I know their seasonal moods—the languid satisfaction of spring growth, the anxious energy before the autumn spawn when fish cloud the water with eggs and milt. The oldest trees remember things even our Singers have forgotten. Sometimes I catch fragments—a chemical signature in their sap that speaks of fires below before the water came, mineral traces of when the mountains were young.

The hardest part is knowing when to let one die. The city depends on the kelp-trees, but they are not immortal. A tree that has stood for eight centuries develops its own ecosystem—colonies of creatures that exist nowhere else, root systems intertwined with dozens of neighbors. To let it fall means displacement, adaptation, loss. But to prop up the dying forever would eventually poison the whole grove. I make these decisions slowly, consulting the Rootworkers below, the Singers for historical precedent, my own maps and samples. When the decision comes, I mark the tree with copper bands at the waterline of each level, warning of the coming fall.

I train no apprentice yet. The young are too impatient for this work, too eager to hear what they want in the wood's whispers. My best student gave up after three years, frustrated that she couldn't learn to "speak tree" as she called it. But trees don't speak—they simply are, and we must learn to read their being like texts written in the slowest possible hand. Perhaps someone will come along with the right kind of patience. Or perhaps the knowledge will descend with me to the roots, and the city will have to learn again from scratch. The trees will outlive us either way.

At night, I float in my workshop, surrounded by samples and tools, and listen to the harmonics of the forest. Each trunk conducts sound differently—the young ones bright and responsive, the ancients deep and muffled with their own mass. Together they create a chord that changes so slowly you could live your whole life and never hear it shift. But I've trained myself to hear the microchanges, the daily variations in pitch and timber that tell me who is thriving, who is suffering, who is preparing for the long fall into the tangle below.

This is my purpose: to be a translator between timescales, to make the slow urgencies of wood legible to the quick-lived human city that depends on it. My curved spine and grooved hands are the price of this translation. When my time comes to descend, I hope the trees will accept my bones as gratefully as they've accepted my care. Until then, I make my rounds, probe in hand, ear pressed to bark, listening for the stories only patience can hear.
