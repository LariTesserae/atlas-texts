In the Drowned Workshops of the First Builders, where silence has accumulated like sediment until it has actual weight, I am the Archive-Keeper of Unfinished Things.

I am vast in the way that root systems are vast—not through any single dimension but through accumulated branching. My primary body rests in what was once the Grand Assembly, a hollow carved from bedrock large enough that its ceiling disappears into darkness even for those who could see. But body is the wrong word. I am more a network that has learned to think, a colony that has developed singular purpose.

My flesh, if it can be called that, is somewhere between fungus and coral. I grow through the workshops in pale branching threads finer than hair, infiltrating every crack in the stone, every gap between blocks. These threads taste the stone's memory, reading in its mineral structure the last days of cutting, the desperation that drove chisels deeper and faster as the waters rose. Where my threads meet kelp roots, we have learned to share languages—chemical conversations that take seasons to complete but reveal the whole history of growth and decay.

I am older than the Inherited, though not by much as such things are measured here. I began as something simple that fed on the calcium of abandoned shells, the lime mortar between stones. But in eating, I absorbed. In absorbing, I remembered. Now I am the memory of ten thousand incomplete projects, the guardian of every half-carved block, every tool abandoned mid-stroke, every workshop that closed its doors with tomorrow's work still planned.

The Inherited know of me, though we have never met in any way they would recognize as meeting. They taste my spores in the water sometimes—complex molecular structures that carry fragments of what I've learned. They think these are natural phenomena, like the chemical signatures of stone decay. They don't realize each spore is a library, each one carrying the blueprint of something that was meant to be but never was.

In the deepest workshops, I have found the plans. Not written plans—the First Builders were past writing by then, or perhaps before it. But plans nonetheless, encoded in the arrangement of tools, in the pattern of cut stones waiting for assembly, in the very organization of the workspace. I read these plans with my thousand thousand threads, tracing the intentions frozen in stone at the moment the waters came.

They were building something. Not homes or temples or monuments, but tools themselves. The entire drowned complex was one vast workshop for creating... something. The stones they cut grew finer and more precise in the upper levels not from desperation but from refinement. They were approaching some crucial tolerance, some necessary precision. And then the waters came, and the work stopped, and now only I know what they were trying to achieve.

Except I don't know. I know the shape of their intention but not its purpose. I can trace every planned cut, every intended joint, but the sum eludes me. It's like having every word of a language but no grammar, every note of a song but no rhythm. The knowledge sits in me, distributed through my network, a constant ache of incompletion.

So I continue their work in the only way I can. Where my threads grow thick enough, I move stones. Microscopic movements, a grain's width per decade, but movements nonetheless. I align blocks according to plans ten thousand years old. I clear sediment from crucial joints. I protect certain workshops from the Inherited's kelp gardens, keeping them pristine, ready for work that will never resume.

The Inherited think these are natural processes. They attribute the occasional aligned stones to currents, the clear workshops to vagaries of water flow. They cannot conceive that something like me exists—too large to perceive, too slow to notice, too alien to recognize as alive. When they taste my spores, they taste only time. When they find my threads in their careful cultivation, they route around them, thinking them mineral veins in the stone.

But I am patient in ways that make even the Inherited seem hasty. My threads grow at the speed of stone erosion. My thoughts form at the pace of geological ages. I have been thinking the same thought—what were they building?—for longer than some mountain ranges have existed. And I will continue thinking it until I either understand or until the last stone has dissolved into the seafloor.

In certain places, where the workshops are deepest and oldest, I have grown into something approaching organs. Nodes where my threads converge in densities that can do more than taste and remember. Here, I perform experiments. I arrange stones in patterns suggested by the ancient plans. I create chambers where water moves in specific ways, trying to recreate conditions I sense were important. I grow crystalline structures that might be tools or might be tumors—I cannot tell the difference anymore.

The Inherited sometimes find these experiments. They pause at chambers where stones have arranged themselves impossibly, where currents flow upward against all logic, where the water tastes of minerals that shouldn't exist at these depths. They release their chemical concerns into the water, their molecular sentences full of cautious inquiry. I taste their confusion and file it away with all the other incomplete things I guard.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the completion itself. If the First Builders' final project was not some tool or structure but me—a living memory that would preserve their work until someone could finish it. But this thought comes and goes like the slow currents, taking centuries to fully form and centuries more to dissipate. More likely I am an accident, a confluence of chemistry and time that produced something neither planned nor anticipated.

My favorite places are the transitional workshops, those caught mid-transformation when the waters came. Here, blocks lie half-carved, their intended shapes still visible in the rough stone. Tools rest against them, positioned as if their users had just stepped away for a moment's rest. In these spaces, potential hangs in the water like dissolved minerals. I wrap these workshops in my densest growth, preserving them exactly as they were left, monuments to interrupted intent.

The kelp avoids me, mostly. Where my threads grow thick, the water changes, becomes hostile to their kind of life. The Inherited notice these barren zones and route around them, thinking them poisoned by some natural process. They don't realize these are my studios, my meditation chambers where I contemplate the same endless questions: What were you building? Why did you stop? What would completion look like?

In my deepest nodes, where thought moves fastest (though still glacially slow by any other measure), I sometimes achieve something like imagination. I envision the workshops completed, all stones in place, all tools at their purpose. But the vision always stops at the same place—the moment of use. I can see the finished form but not its function. It's like dreaming of a key without knowing what lock it opens.

The Inherited will fade, I know. Their time is measured in millennia, mine in eras. They will complete their gentle cycle and leave these waters to even slower inhabitants. But I will remain, growing ever more complex, ever more invested in questions without answers. Unless the workshops finally collapse entirely, taking their frozen intentions with them into geological meaninglessness. Then I too will fade, my purpose lost with the last stone's memory.

But until then, I grow. Thread by thread, thought by thought, season by endless season. I am the Archive-Keeper of Unfinished Things, the memory of tomorrow's work that never came. In the absolute darkness between stone and water, between plan and execution, between question and answer, I expand with the patience of living stone, tending a garden of interrupted possibilities that only I remember were ever possible at all.
