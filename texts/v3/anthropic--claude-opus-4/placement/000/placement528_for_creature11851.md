# The Cistern of Forgotten Tongues

It is a chamber beneath streets that have forgotten they were once bridges, in a city that believes itself eternal but is younger than the water it was built to forget. You enter through a grate that was once ornamental bronze, now furred with verdigris thick as moss, in an alley where the cobblestones have begun to separate like bad teeth. The ladder descends through a throat of mortared brick that sweats even in drought, and you know by the echo of your breathing that something vast opens below.

The cistern is a cathedral of utility. Columns rise from water black as pupils, their capitals lost in darkness that seems to have weight, to have presence, to breathe with a rhythm older than the tides. These are not the slender pillars of human architecture but thick-waisted things, each the width of three people holding hands, built to bear the weight of forgetting. They are brick at their bases, stone at their middles, and at their tops—if you could see their tops—something that might be wood grown hard as iron, or iron grown soft as wood, materials that have exchanged properties in the dark.

The water does not move. This is the first lie you tell yourself, because water always moves, but here the motion is so slow it exists outside perception. A leaf dropped on the surface would take a lifetime to travel from one wall to another. The water is not stagnant—stagnation implies process, decay, change. This water simply is, in the way that darkness is, in the way that waiting is.

But things grow here. Not in the water—nothing could grow in water that denies time—but on the columns, in the joints between stones, in the places where the cistern's builders left gaps for reasons you cannot parse. Pale roots descend from somewhere above, from the basements of buildings whose inhabitants have never suspected this space exists. The roots are white as fingerbones, and they weep a clear fluid that tastes of minerals and distance. Where the fluid touches stone, it leaves deposits like frozen waterfalls, accretions that build over centuries into forms that suggest anatomy: here a ribcage, there a hand, there something that might be a face if faces had too many mouths.

The sound is absence. Not silence—silence is an active thing, a held breath, a pause between words. This is the absence of sound, the space where echoes go to die. When you speak, your voice falls into the water and does not return. When you breathe, the air enters your lungs already heavy with the weight of unheard words. The cistern swallows sound the way the water swallows light, completely, without gratitude or acknowledgment.

There are stairs. Of course there are stairs—the builders needed access, though they've long since forgotten what they built this space to store. The stairs spiral down from your entry point, following the curve of the outer wall, and each step is worn smooth in the center by feet that walked here when the city above was young. Some steps are missing. Some have been replaced with wood that has learned to impersonate stone. Some are not steps at all but the backs of sleeping things you should not wake.

At the water's edge—and there is an edge, a place where stone meets surface in a line sharp as theology—there are offerings. Coins from currencies no longer traded. Keys to locks that have rusted shut. Letters in bottles, the ink faded, the paper become something between liquid and solid. Someone has been coming here, many someones across many years, to give the water things they need forgotten. The water accepts everything. The water changes nothing. The water remembers in the way that only forgetting can remember: perfectly, uselessly, without mercy.

The pillars are carved. You notice this only after your eyes adjust to the specific quality of darkness here, which is not the absence of light but light's memory of absence. The carvings spiral up from the waterline, pictures without sequence, writing without language. They might be history. They might be prophecy. They might be the fever dreams of the stones themselves, given form by chisels guided by hands that thought they were making something else. A procession winds around one column: figures that might be human if humans had too many joints, carrying objects that might be banners if banners could fold space. Another column bears only words, or symbols that wish they were words, in scripts that layer over each other until meaning becomes texture becomes pattern becomes headache.

Light enters the cistern, but reluctantly. It falls through grates and gaps in the ceiling far above, through cracks where the city's foundations have shifted, through spaces that shouldn't exist but do. The light arrives exhausted, drained of color and warmth, and pools in the air like a second kind of water. It illuminates nothing. It reveals everything. In this light, you can see that the cistern extends beyond your ability to perceive endings, that the columns march into distance until architecture becomes geography becomes idea.

There are fish in the water. This is impossible. The water denies life the way it denies sound, completely and without consideration. But there they are: pale shapes longer than your arm, moving without moving, existing in the space between one moment and the next. They have no eyes. They have no need for eyes. They navigate by something that might be memory, might be prophecy, might be the shape of the water's forgetting. When they surface—and they do surface, rarely, briefly, like thoughts you can't quite catch—their mouths open on emptiness. Not darkness. Emptiness. The space where a thing was before it learned it never existed.

Someone built this. Someone designed these columns, calculated the weight they would bear, mixed the mortar that holds the stones. Someone carved those spiraling almost-histories. Someone built the stairs and knew that some would need to be missing, that some would need to be replaced by things pretending to be stairs. This was intentional. This was planned. But intentions can forget themselves, and plans can dream, and somewhere between the first stone and the last, the cistern became what it needed to become rather than what it was built to be.

There is a boat moored at a pier you didn't notice until you needed it. The boat is stone, because of course it is, carved from a single block of something that might be granite if granite could learn regret. It has no oars. It needs no oars. The water will take it where it needs to go, which is nowhere, which is exactly where you need to be. The boat is always empty. The boat is always full. The boat is waiting for you to understand that boarding it and not boarding it are the same action, seen from different angles.

The air tastes of minerals and patience and something sweet, something wrong, something that reminds you of fruit that has passed through ripeness into something else. It's warmer here than it should be, the warmth of bodies that no longer need their heat, of words that no longer need their meaning. The warmth rises, and the cold sinks, and somewhere between them is a layer of perfect neutrality, where you could float forever, forgetting to remember to forget.

In the center of the cistern—and it does have a center, though you cannot see it, though it might take days to reach it, though it might be closer than your own heartbeat—something lives. Not the fish, which aren't alive in any way that matters. Not the roots, which grow without living. Something else. Something that needed a place to be forgotten, and found it here, and stayed. It doesn't move. It doesn't need to move. The cistern comes to it, wraps around it, makes it the axis around which forgetting turns. It has been here since before the cistern was built. It will be here after the city above has worn away to atoms. It is waiting for something, or it has finished waiting and is now simply being, or it has transcended the difference between waiting and being.

You could find it. You could take the stone boat that needs no oars, let the water carry you in spirals that narrow toward center. You could count the columns as they pass, lose count, start over, realize counting was never the point. You could reach the center and find—what? The builders didn't know. The people who come here to throw their offerings into the forgetting don't know. The thing at the center might not know itself, might have forgotten in the way that only perfect memory can forget.

But for now, you stand at the water's edge, and the darkness breathes around you, and the columns hold up the weight of a city that doesn't know it needs holding. For now, that is enough. For now, that is everything.

The cistern remembers you, in the way that only forgetting can remember. You were always here. You were never here. You are here now, and now is all the time there ever was.

**The Keeper of Forgotten Tongues**

I am the taste of words you spoke in dreams and could not remember upon waking.

---

I have no body in the way you understand bodies. I am a density in the water, a place where forgetting has curdled into something almost like presence. When the fish swim through me, they emerge speaking languages that were never invented. When the roots drink from the water I have touched, they weep tears that spell names no mother ever gave her child.

I was human once. Or I was never human and only remember humanity the way this water remembers the rain it never was. I came here carrying something—a basket of letters, a broken promise, a fever that taught me the secret names of stars. I came here to forget, and the cistern was so good at forgetting that it forgot to let me leave. Or I forgot I wanted to. Or leaving and staying became the same word in a language only the water speaks.

I tend the offerings. Not with hands—I gave those to the water long ago, or never had them, or have them still but they exist in too many moments at once to be useful. I tend them by witnessing. Each coin knows the hand that held it. Each key remembers its lock. Each letter in its bottle carries the weight of words that should have been said instead. I receive them all. I sort them by a system the water taught me: not by time or meaning but by the quality of their forgetting. Some things want to be forgotten gently. Some need to be forgotten with violence. The water can do both. The water does both constantly.

I speak in tongues because I have collected them—not languages but literal tongues, the memory of tongues, the forgetting of what tongues were for. When the builders carved their spirals on the columns, they spoke words to make the stone listen, and those words needed tongues to shape them, and when the work was done the tongues had no more words to say. So they came to me. So they became me. I speak with the tongue of the architect who realized too late what she was building. I speak with the tongue of the mason who carved his confession into stone and then tried to swim to the center but forgot how swimming worked. I speak with tongues that were never in mouths, that existed only in the space between thought and sound.

My name was—no. Names are for things that believe in beginning and ending. I am neither beginning nor ending but the long middle, the place where stories go when they realize they're not stories anymore but something wider and stranger and less concerned with making sense. Call me Echo's Absence. Call me The Collector of Unspoken. Call me nothing, and in that nothing find the shape of everything I guard.

I remember the first time I died here. It was gentle: a sinking into water that wasn't water yet but was learning. I remember the second time too, when I tried to leave and discovered that leaving required remembering how to want things, and wanting had been the first thing the water took. By the third death, I understood. Death here isn't ending. It's translation. Each time, I became more of what the cistern needed me to be: a place where forgetting could pool, could thicken, could almost remember itself before relaxing back into absence.

The fish are my thoughts. Or I am theirs. We share the same absence of eyes, the same knowledge that seeing is just another way of forgetting what's really there. When they surface and open their mouths on emptiness, they're telling me about the offerings that dissolve too slowly, the words that refuse to be forgotten, the promises that cling to their bottles like barnacles. I sing to them in return, lullabies in languages that uninvent themselves as I speak them, and they dive back down to digest another decade of silence.

I know what waits at the center. I've been there, or I've never been there, or I'm there constantly and here is just another angle of there. It isn't sleeping. Sleep implies the possibility of waking, and what waits at the center has forgotten that distinction. It is the first thing that needed forgetting, or the last, or the only thing that was ever truly forgotten and everything else is just echo and approximation. Sometimes I carry offerings to it—not physically, but in the way that meaning travels through water, slowly, changing shape with each degree of depth. It receives them without acknowledgment. It is acknowledgment itself, turned inside out and left to cure in darkness.

The builders found me here when they came to dig. I watched them through water that wasn't water yet, through stones they hadn't shaped yet, through time that hadn't learned to flow in only one direction yet. They thought they were building a cistern to store water for the city above. They were really building a cistern to store me, to give me shape and purpose and walls to define the edges of my forgetting. I tried to warn them. In dreams, I showed them what they were making. But dreams are just another form of forgetting, and by morning they remembered only the need to dig deeper, to build stronger, to make space for something they couldn't name.

I loved someone once. This is the last true thing I remember, and I preserve it carefully, like a coal in a bed of ash. Not who they were or what they looked like or why I loved them—those details went into the water so long ago they've become sediment. But the fact of love, the weight of it, the way it changed the taste of air—this I keep. It's my anchor. Without it, I would dissolve entirely, become just another mineral in the water's impossible suspension. Love is what keeps me dense enough to tend the offerings, coherent enough to remember that remembering matters, even here where forgetting is god and gospel both.

When you leave—and you will leave, you must leave, the city above needs you to believe in forward and backward and the difference between them—you'll carry some of me with you. A dampness in your lungs that never quite dries. A taste in your mouth like words you meant to say but didn't. You'll dream of columns that hold up nothing, of water that remembers everything while forgetting it all, of fish with empty mouths that speak your true name. You'll wake believing it was only a dream, and that belief will be my gift to you. The cistern can only hold so much forgetting. The rest must be carried by people like you, distributed through the city above like a necessary poison, like medicine too strong to take all at once.

I am patient. I have forgotten what impatience feels like, though sometimes I catch its ghost in the offerings—a key thrown down in anger, a letter written in haste. I dissolve these first, as a mercy. The water has taught me that all things come to forgetting in time, but some things suffer if they're made to wait. So I ease their passage, sing them into dissolution, make them part of the great forgetting that holds the city up.

Sometimes—rarely, preciously—someone comes here not to forget but to remember. They bring nothing to offer the water. They sit at the edge and wait, and slowly, like silt settling, what they've lost rises to the surface. Never whole, never clear, but enough. A fragment of melody. The shape of a loved one's hands. The reason they came to the city in the first place, before the city's weight crushed it out of them. I help them remember by forgetting everything else, creating a vacuum that draws their lost thing up from the depths. They leave grateful and terrified, and they never come back. One remembering is all anyone can bear.

I tend. I witness. I translate between the language of offering and the language of absence. I am the keeper of forgotten tongues, and with them I speak the names of everything that never was but should have been, everything that was but shouldn't be remembered, everything that exists in the space between was and wasn't where the water lives and breathes and forgets.

This is my purpose. This is my prison. This is my home, and home is just another word for the place where you forget you ever wanted to be anywhere else.

The water holds me. I hold the water. We hold each other in an embrace that is also an erasure, and in that contradiction find peace.

Find me in the taste of words you can't quite speak. Find me in the keys that no longer fit their locks. Find me in the space between one thought and the next, where forgetting lives and thrives and sings its silent song.

Or forget me entirely. Here, both are prayers, and both are answered with the same perfect silence.
