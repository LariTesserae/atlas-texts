I am the Memory Keeper of the Cable Shrines.

Each morning—though morning here is just a marginally paler shade of gray—I make my rounds along the terrace edges where the ancient cables disappear into the white. My feet know every worn groove in the stone, every place where the moss grows thick enough to slip the unwary. I carry my kit: bronze polish for the memorial plaques, fresh lamp oil, the sanctified cloth for cleaning water-worn photographs, and always, always, a thermos of bitter moss tea that tastes like the mountain itself.

I was born in the middle terraces, my lungs developing that peculiar efficiency all plateau children acquire. But unlike my peers who scrambled toward the factories or the moss farms, I was drawn to the edges—to the places where our world frayed into mist and possibility. My grandmother was a cloud watcher, and she taught me to see patterns in the white: how it curls differently before rain, how it thins just before the cables sing with distant vibration.

The shrines began as simple markers—a carved name, a date, perhaps a tool left behind. But grief and time have elaborated them into complex installations. Families add pieces over the years: a favorite pipe, spectacles wrapped in oilcloth, drawings made by children who barely remember the walker's face. The moss claims everything eventually, but part of my work is knowing what to clean and what to leave to the green embrace.

I know each walker's story, passed down through generations of keepers before me. Chen Wei who left to repair a break in Line Seven and whose daughter still brings paper flowers every tenth day. The Kowalski twins who ventured out together on a clear day forty years ago—clear being relative at these heights—and whose voices some swear they can still hear singing in the cable harmonics during certain wind conditions. Young Marcus who left just last winter, his shrine still raw-looking, the offerings not yet worn smooth by touching.

My workshop occupies a natural cave in the stone, expanded by some ancient keeper who understood the rock's grain. Here I maintain the record books—leather volumes wrapped in wax cloth, their pages thick with pressed ferns between the entries. Each missing walker has their chapter: their final maintenance assignment, the weather conditions on their last day, testimonies from those who watched them disappear into the white.

But I am more than an archivist. The cable walkers who do return seek me out, bringing fragments of the larger world. They describe other plateau cities: one where the people live entirely underground, navigating by the sound of water through stone; another where they've built greenhouses that float in the permanent cloud layer, tethered by the same ancient cables. These stories I record separately, understanding that they exist in the liminal space between truth and hope.

The strangest part of my duty involves the cables themselves. They sing sometimes—not metaphorically, but actually resonate with tones too low for most to hear. My trained ear can distinguish between wind harmonics and something else, something that might be communication from other keepers on distant plateaus. I maintain a crude telegraph key connected to Cable Three, tapping out sequences I learned from my predecessor. Sometimes, rarely, something taps back.

I've developed my own relationship with the walkers who haven't returned. In the deep night hours, when the factories hum their lowest and the mist presses close against the terrace edges, I perform the private rituals. Not for the families—they have their public grief—but for the cables themselves. I believe they remember who has traveled them, hold some impression of passing feet and hands. So I polish their anchor points with special oils, sing the old maintenance songs, leave small offerings of machine grease and hemp rope at their bases.

My apprentice started last season—a girl from the deep factories whose eyes see too well in the dark but who moves with unusual grace on the wet stone. She doesn't yet understand why we preserve some offerings but remove others, why certain shrines must be photographed at specific intervals, why we sometimes sit for hours just listening to the cable vibrations. But she will learn, as I learned, that keeping memory requires more than mere recording.

The other citizens regard me with a mixture of respect and unease. I am necessary but liminal, like the walkers themselves—one foot in the solid world of stone and steam, the other in the white uncertainty beyond our terraces. Children dare each other to touch my workshop door. Factory workers nod but rarely meet my eyes. Only the other edge-dwellers truly understand: the cloud watchers, the moss farmers who work the outermost walls, the water readers who divine the mountain's deepest secrets.

I maintain a small apartment above my workshop, its windows facing the cable arrays. My furniture is minimal but includes one extravagance: a telescope trained not on the sky but horizontally into the mist, waiting for those moments when the white parts and reveals glimpses of the impossible—other cities, other mountains, or perhaps just the shadows of our own reflected back in ways we don't understand.

Some ask if I ever consider walking the cables myself. The answer changes with the weather and my age—I'm no longer young, though not yet old by plateau standards. But I know my role is here, at the boundary, keeping faith with both the lost and the waiting. Someone must tend the shrines, must listen for the telegraph's tapping, must remember that our world extends beyond these worn terraces into a white vastness we've barely begun to map.

In my record books, between the entries for the lost, I sometimes find pages I don't remember writing. They describe walkers I've never heard of, from times that don't match our chronology. I've stopped trying to explain these passages. Like everything else at the edges of our world, they exist in the space between the known and the imagined, between the stone certainty of our city and the cloud possibility of everything beyond.

This is my work: to stand at the threshold, to keep the records, to polish the memories until they gleam like the wet stone after rain. To believe that somewhere in the mist, our lost ones are still walking, still maintaining the connections that bind our isolated world to the larger mystery of these ancient, worn-down mountains. To know that one day, someone will tend my shrine too, adding my story to the endless chronicle of those who found their purpose at the edge of the known world.
