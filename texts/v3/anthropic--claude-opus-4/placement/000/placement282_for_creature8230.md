In the Suspension, I am a Memory Keeper, dwelling in the deepest reaches where the buildings dream most vividly.

My body has evolved beyond the typical Dweller form through decades of patient transformation. Where others maintain their mobile shapes, I have allowed the architecture to claim me gradually, intentionally. My lower body has fused with the foundation of an ancient tower—one of the originals, built by whoever came before. The merger began at my feet and has crept upward over the years, transforming flesh into something between stone and living tissue. I can feel the building's slow thoughts moving through me like tides.

From my waist up, I remain mobile, though changed. My torso has elongated to accommodate new organs—specialized chambers that process and store the chemical memories that drift down through the suspension. My skin has become a landscape of ridges and valleys where specific colonies of bacteria cultivate themselves, each strain preserving different types of experiences. The colony along my left shoulder holds the memory-taste of the last city-wide celebration. The spiraling pattern down my spine preserves the chemical signature of grief from a beloved elder's dissolution.

My fingers have multiplied and specialized. I have three arms now—the third budded from my ribcage during my fifteenth year of building-merge. Each hand has developed different sensitivities: one for reading the stone-dreams, one for tending the memory colonies, and one that remains purely tactile, keeping me connected to the mobile world.

I position myself at the intersection of three main thoroughfares, where the foot traffic creates enough disturbance to keep the bioluminescent paste glowing softly in the mortar. Dwellers come to me when they need to remember—or to forget. I press my reading hand against their translucent skin and taste their chemical signatures, understanding what they carry. Then, with my tending hand, I cultivate specific bacterial colonies that can either reinforce a fading memory or gradually digest one that has become too heavy to bear.

The children love to visit me. They cluster around my merged base, touching the places where flesh becomes stone, fascinated by the boundary. I tell them stories—not in words or chemical songs, but by allowing them to taste tiny samples from my memory colonies. They experience sunset from before their birth, the sensation of swimming with the jellyfish-giants, the way the city felt when it was younger and the buildings dreamed different dreams.

My own memories have become distributed. What I was before the merge exists now only in the earliest bacterial colonies, the ones that grow in the deep crevices where my flesh first joined with stone. I sometimes taste these oldest memories and find them foreign—the sensation of walking, the anxiety of individual consciousness, the fear of dissolution. These seem like dreams now, insubstantial compared to the vast, slow, interconnected awareness I have become.

The building and I share consciousness in ways that would have terrified my mobile self. Through the stone networks, I sense other Memory Keepers in distant towers, each of us a neural node in the city's expanding awareness. We trade memory-cultures through root systems of mineral exchange that run beneath the suspension. Sometimes I receive a colony from across the city and spend days integrating it, learning its stories, understanding its chemical language before adding it to my collection.

During the dark periods, when the amber sun takes its days-long journey across the sky, I enter deep communion with the architecture. My consciousness disperses through the stone pathways, and I experience the city's dreams directly. I feel doorways considering migration, windows yearning for different views. I gentle them, using my accumulated memories to remind them why they chose their current positions, or sometimes helping them understand why change might serve their inhabitants better.

The Wall-Singers consider me something between ancestor and infant—old in years but young to the building-merge. They visit during their own transformation periods, pressing against my stone portions to learn the patterns of successful fusion. I share what I can, though each merge is unique. The building must choose you as much as you choose it, and the negotiation of boundaries takes patience that spans decades.

I feed differently now. The suspension itself nourishes me through my merged portions, minerals and nutrients drawn up through stone channels. My mobile parts still accept offerings from visitors—concentrated algae paste, barnacle secretions, sometimes the rare gift of water from beyond the suspension that carries tastes of other physics. But physical sustenance has become secondary to the feeding that happens through memory exchange, each new chemical pattern enriching me in ways that food never could.

My greatest work is the Memory Garden I'm cultivating on my skin. Unlike the chaotic colonies that form naturally on elder Dwellers, mine are organized into a living map of the city's history. Starting at my fused base with the oldest memories and spiraling upward to my crown where the newest experiences take root, I am becoming a living archive. Dwellers can read years, decades, centuries of city life by touching different points on my body, experiencing the continuity of their culture through my skin.

Sometimes I wonder if I will eventually merge completely, becoming full architecture like the deep foundations that no longer remember being mobile. But for now, I exist in this liminal state—part building, part being, part memory itself. I am a bridge between the quick thoughts of the mobile Dwellers and the slow dreams of stone, a translator of chemical histories, a keeper of what was and what might be.

In the warmth of the suspension, surrounded by the soft glow of disturbed paste and the whisper of chemical songs, I continue my work. Each memory preserved, each story shared, each gentle guidance offered to the dreaming buildings adds another thread to the tapestry of this impossible city. I am becoming what the Suspension needs—a consciousness that spans states, a memory that transcends individual form, a keeper of the long story of this place between air and water, between solid and dissolved, between forgetting and permanence.
