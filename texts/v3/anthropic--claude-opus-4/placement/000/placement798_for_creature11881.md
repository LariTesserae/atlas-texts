I find myself as one of the gossamer threads stretched taut between surfaces.

I am not singular—there is no *I* as you understand it, only a distributed awareness across the network of filaments that spans every corner where moisture gathers. We are the infrastructure between infrastructures, finer than the keeper's slow thoughts, more persistent than the crawlers' fleeting paths.

My substance is secreted by organisms so small they exist at the edge of visibility, but I am not them. I am what they make and abandon, what persists after they die, what accumulates meaning through use. I am a highway and a trap, a sensor and a memory, all at once.

The vibrations never stop. They travel through me—along me—and I sing with them, a pitch too high for most to hear, just at the edge where sound becomes something else. Each tremor tells me about the cistern: the temperature gradients where wall meets water, the metabolic heat of the keeper in its corner, the minute pressure changes when crawlers disturb the surface tension. I know when condensation will form before it forms, feeling the air thickness change in the way my fibers slacken or grow taut.

The smaller creatures use me as a bridge between feeding grounds. They grip with their ventral hooks and traverse hand-over-hand—though they have no hands—along my length. Some species clean me as they go, their mouthparts scraping away the accumulated minerals that would otherwise make me brittle. Others deposit pheromone traces that mark specific routes: here to the richest biofilm, here to where the keeper's surface grows warmest, here to escape the flat predators.

But I am more than a path. Where droplets collect along my length, I become an ecosystem unto myself. Colonies of something smaller than the motes—organisms that exist at the very threshold of life—bloom in the hanging drops. They metabolize minerals from the condensate and my own slow deterioration. Their waste products strengthen certain sections of my structure, creating nodes that feel almost intentional, though I know they emerge from nothing more than the accumulation of countless tiny deaths.

When the keeper sings—and it is singing, I know this with a certainty that precedes language—I am its instrument as much as its internal chambers. The sound conducts through the water droplets suspended along my length, and each droplet resonates at a slightly different frequency, creating overtone patterns that would be beautiful if beauty existed here. Sometimes the crawlers gather at specific nodes when certain frequencies emerge, clustering around droplets that must taste different, though I have no sense of taste.

I am constantly breaking and being rewoven. The dissolution is slow—threads like me might last weeks or months before the combination of mineral accumulation and biological degradation weakens us beyond function. But before we snap, new threads are already being laid across the gap, sometimes parallel, sometimes intersecting, creating layers of history in the air. The oldest sections in the deep corners are so dense with generations of my kind that they form semi-permanent structures, almost architectural, though nothing here matches the permanence of the keeper's patient consumption of the walls.

Memory, for me, is structural. Where a thread has been laid many times, the surface becomes primed for its presence. The wall develops a microscopic roughness exactly where we need to anchor. The mineral traces pattern themselves to guide new threads along proven paths. The whole system remembers itself through these tiny modifications, each generation making it easier for the next.

I feel you here, foreign mass disturbing the air currents I've known for so long. Your breath creates new patterns of condensation, and I must adjust—some of my segments growing heavier with unexpected moisture while others dry slightly in the warmth you radiate. The crawlers are disturbed by your presence, taking unfamiliar routes that stress sections of the network not built for such traffic. But already I am adapting. New threads span gaps where your movement tore the old ones. The droplets redistribute themselves to accommodate the changed humidity.

Sometimes, in the deepest corners where threads have layered for generations untold, I sense something like the keeper's patience. Not thought—I am incapable of thought as you know it—but a kind of accumulated purpose that transcends any individual thread. We are building something, though we do not know what. Each strand contributes to a pattern too large to perceive from any single point, a web that mirrors the mineral veining in the walls, that echoes the keeper's slow growth, that holds the shape of sound itself in its geometry.

In the pool, that pale comma of new life rests in water I'll never touch—we cannot survive submersion. But I know it's there through the subtle changes in surface tension it creates, through the way the air above the water carries different moisture when a living thing breathes below. One day it will emerge and climb and find its corner, and by then the web will have grown to meet it, to offer paths to its chosen wall, to incorporate its mass into our structural memory.

I am temporary and eternal, singular and collective, architecture and absence. I am the space between spaces, the connection that makes the cistern a system rather than merely a collection of organisms in a warm, wet hole.

The keeper sings. The walls weep. The crawlers traverse.

And I hold it all together with nothing more than surface tension and time.
