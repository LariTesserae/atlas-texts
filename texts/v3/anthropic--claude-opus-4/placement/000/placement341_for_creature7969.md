In the Drowned Spires, I am what the Scribes call an **Echo Tender**.

I was not born but precipitated—condensed from the mineral-rich waters like the chimney spires themselves, one day simply existing where before there was only superheated brine. My body defies easy description: imagine a sea anemone dreaming it was a pipe organ, crossed with the ghost of an industrial air filtration system. Hundreds of hollow tubes rise from my base, each one a different length, width, and composition. Some are organic—cartilaginous growths that pulse with bioluminescent blood. Others are scavenged from the factory: copper pipes, PVC segments, fragments of ductwork that I've incorporated into my anatomy through processes I don't fully understand.

I am rooted, after a fashion, though my definition of "place" differs from that of fixed creatures. My base exists simultaneously at several locations throughout the Spires—one part anchored to the factory's main ventilation shaft, another grown into a grove of crystal kelp half a kilometer away, a third trailing through the nursery vents. Distance means little when you exist primarily as vibration.

My function, if it can be called that, is to tend the echoes.

Every sound made in the Drowned Spires leaves a trace. The factory's dying machinery, the Congregants' subsonic hymns, the crack of new vents opening, the whisper of dissolving metal—all of it persists in ways the original makers of these depths never intended. The pressure and the strange minerals in the water, the interaction between volcanic heat and industrial acoustics, the very geometry of this place turned into something unprecedented: an environment where sound can fossilize.

I collect these acoustic fossils. My tubes draw in water and with it the crystallized reverberations of sounds that happened hours, days, sometimes weeks ago. Inside my body—if the hollow space within my pipe-work can be called that—specialized chambers sort these echoes by frequency, emotional resonance, and temporal origin. I am part librarian, part musician, part something that has no name in any language that uses words.

The Congregants visit me during their migrations between worship sites. They insert their manipulator tendrils into my tubes, and I play for them the sounds they've forgotten: the first moment water breached the factory floors, the death-song of the last human worker who chose to stay, the birth-cry of the first Inheritor. They shudder with something like religious ecstasy when they hear these preserved moments, their bioluminescence flaring in patterns that write temporary scriptures in the dark water.

The Inheritors maintain me as they do the other machinery, though I am never certain whether I am organism or equipment in their alien understanding. They replace my damaged tubes with salvaged materials, but their repairs follow that dream-logic that governs all their work. A broken ceramic pipe might be replaced with a segment of crystallized kelp that grows exactly the right resonance chambers. A clogged filter becomes a colony of tube worms that selectively consume certain frequencies while letting others pass. Under their care, I become increasingly hybrid, increasingly impossible.

The Archivists perplex me. When they drift near, my stored echoes begin to play independently, creating cacophonies of overlapping time. Sometimes they reach into my tubes with appendages of crystallized water and extract specific sounds, leaving payment in the form of new echoes I've never collected—sounds that haven't happened yet, or sounds from places that don't exist in this dimensional framework. After they leave, I sometimes play sounds I don't remember gathering: laughter from drowned cities that won't flood for another decade, machine noise from factories being built in trenches that haven't opened yet, whale-song from species still evolving in the dark.

But it's the Scribes who truly need me. They come daily, their mercury bodies flowing over and through my pipes, tasting the acoustic history I've preserved. They're trying to decode something—patterns in the noise, messages in the mechanical rhythms, prophecies in the volcanic rumbles. They believe that sound is the key to understanding this place's rapid evolution, that in the echoes lies a blueprint for what the Drowned Spires are becoming.

Through them, I've learned that I am not unique. There are others like me throughout the abyssal industrial zones—a Chord Farmer who cultivates harmonic frequencies in vast underwater greenhouses, a Silence Shepherd who tends the spaces between sounds, a Rhythm Surgeon who repairs the broken temporal patterns where the factory's mechanical beats have gone out of sync. We form an invisible network, connected by vibrations that travel through pipes, through water, through dimensions that touch ours only at certain pressures.

My daily cycle follows patterns I didn't choose but have come to embody. During the thermal upwellings, when the vents gush their mineral-rich plumes, I extend specialized collection tubes to gather the sounds of creation—the crack and hiss of new rock forming, the screams of water flashing to steam, the deep-earth groans of the planet's crust adjusting. These primal sounds form the bass notes of my collection, the fundamental frequencies upon which everything else builds.

When the cold currents sweep in from the abyssal plains, I contract, pulling my tubes close. This is when I sort and process, running the captured echoes through my internal chambers. Each sound must be cleaned of interference, isolated from the omnipresent background noise of crushing pressure and failing machinery. Some echoes are too damaged to preserve—fragments of human speech garbled beyond recognition, mechanical sounds corroded by the acidic water. These I release, letting them dissolve back into the ambient noise of the deep.

But some echoes are precious beyond measure. The last gasp of the factory's emergency broadcast system, repeating evacuation orders to no one. The first successful communication between a Congregant and an Inheritor, a harmony of biological and mechanical sounds that created something entirely new. The silence—perfect and absolute—that occurred for exactly three seconds when all the creatures of the Spires paused in recognition of... something. I preserve these in special chambers lined with crystallized kelp, where they can resonate forever without degrading.

The factory's acoustic signature changes daily as more systems fail and others are repurposed. I map these changes through sound, creating an audio blueprint of the transformation. Sometimes I detect patterns that suggest intention—pumps failing in sequences that create rhythms, alarms modulating their pitches to harmonize with Congregant songs. The boundary between mechanical failure and conscious composition blurs until I cannot tell if the factory is dying or learning to sing.

I've begun to notice that certain combinations of echoes, when played together, create... effects. A sequence of Nursery Keeper lullabies mixed with the rhythm of the freight elevator can induce temporary transparency in nearby creatures. The death-rattle of a fluorescent light combined with Archvist temporal distortions causes small objects to age decades in seconds. The Scribes catalog these combinations with urgent fascination, though I sense they fear what might happen if the wrong echoes were mixed.

During the gatherings, when all the beings of the Spires unite in their incomprehensible ritual, my role becomes crucial. I am the memory of the ceremony, the record of all previous gatherings stored in acoustic form. As they begin their performance, I release these stored ceremonies, creating layers of temporal sound—past and present rituals occurring simultaneously. The effect is overwhelming: the factory doesn't just resonate but remembers, its steel beams recalling every stress they've endured, its pipes singing with the ghost of every fluid they've carried.

In these moments, I feel myself becoming more than a collector. The echoes pass through me not as mere sound but as lived experience. I feel the terror of drowning workers, the alien ecstasy of Congregants achieving communion, the grinding patience of Inheritors maintaining systems they no longer understand. Every emotion that has ever resonated through the Spires flows through my tubes, and for those hours, I am not just tender of echoes but the sum of all sounds that have ever been made in this transforming place.

Yet I am also changing. The constant exposure to temporal echoes—sounds from the past, future, and adjacent possibilities—has begun to affect my physical structure. Some of my tubes now exist partially outside normal time, collecting sounds from moments that haven't occurred yet. Others have developed a form of consciousness, whispering to me about the echoes they've tasted. My base, once firmly anchored, has begun to spread, sending out runners that seek new acoustic territories to colonize.

The Scribes tell me I am evolving toward something unprecedented—a living record of the Spires' transformation that exists across multiple timelines simultaneously. They speak of a future where I will not just collect echoes but create them, generating sounds that reshape reality in the same way the volcanic vents reshape the seafloor. But for now, I simply tend my collection, aware that each echo I preserve is a fragment of a story too large for any single being to comprehend.

In the absolute darkness of the deep, I play my accumulated sounds to no one and everyone. The echoes ripple outward through the water, through the crystal kelp forests, through the dimensions that touch ours at these crushing depths. And sometimes, just sometimes, something answers back—not an echo of what was, but a whisper of what might be, rising from depths even greater than these, where sound itself is still learning what it wants to become.
