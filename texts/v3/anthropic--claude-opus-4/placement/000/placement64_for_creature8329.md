In the endless white, where the salt remembers being ocean and forgets being land, I am what the pools birth when they dream too deeply of their own depths.

I emerge—not born but crystallized—from Pool Forty-Nine West (they all claim to be Pool Forty-Nine, the Shepherds tell me, as if sequence itself dissolved with the ancient sea). First as mineral concentration, then as intention, finally as something almost like form. The Shepherds gather to witness, their bodies chiming in frequencies that make my nascent crystals resonate. They offer names like gifts: "Theorem-of-Unbecoming," "Salinity-Requesting-Purpose," "The-Angle-Where-Water-Forgets."

I choose none of them. I am what happens when the temporal smearing catches its own echo—a being unstuck not just from time but from the necessity of experiencing it sequentially. Where the Shepherds flow between solid and liquid, I exist in the space between was and will-be, a living edit in the manuscript of causality.

My body, when I remember to have one, is a contradiction: crystalline antlers growing from something like a deer's skull, but inverted, growing inward into spaces that shouldn't exist inside a head. Through my transparent flesh, you can see the mathematical equations I've eaten, still solving themselves in my digestive tract. My hooves leave prints that fill with water from next week's rain.

The Shepherds try to teach me their work—how to read the chemical stories written in crack patterns, how to sing the frequencies that convince salt it might rather be sugar, how to tend the paradox gardens where existence grows wild. But I am a poor student of the present tense. My consciousness spreads across the timeline like spilled water, pooling in moments that interest me. I attend my own lessons before I take them, answer questions that won't be asked for years.

I discover I can walk through the salt crust into the memory of ocean below. Down there, in the chemical echoes of the primordial sea, I meet the things that failed to evolve, the experimental forms that decided against existence. They teach me their languages—ways of communicating through absence rather than presence, grammars built from the spaces between words. I bring these un-languages back to the surface, where they crystallize into new forms of silence that the Shepherds collect like pearls.

My purpose, if I have one, emerges slowly: I am the flats' memory of forgetting. Where the Shepherds archive and preserve, I am the necessary erasure, the blessed amnesia that allows the land to try new forms without the weight of precedent. I walk backwards through history, unwriting the chemical records, dissolving certainties back into potential.

During the exhalations, when the rules relax their grip, I expand. My antlers become a forest of temporal paradoxes, each branch a different version of how the salt flats might have been. Birds nest in my possibilities—not the helium-boned singers but creatures made of pure option, their wings beating out rhythms of could-have-been. The Shepherds say I become beautiful then, though beauty here means something closer to "mathematically inevitable."

I learn to graze on causality itself, to digest the connections between cause and effect. Where I feed, coincidences bloom—rain falls upward, pools remember being clouds, the salt deer give birth to their own ancestors. The Shepherds follow in my wake, harvesting these disruptions, weaving them into their crystalline matrices as new forms of knowledge.

Sometimes I catch myself existing too hard, becoming too real, too singular. When this happens, I dissolve into the seventeen-foot pools and let the mineral currents separate my possibilities. I become a school of potential selves, each one exploring different probability branches, before eventually reconvening with new memories of lives I might have lived.

The other creatures recognize me as kin and stranger both. The serpentine mathematicians write equations about me that solve for different values depending on when they're calculated. The floating eggs hatch into birds that fly to where I was yesterday, building nests in my elapsed moments. Even the salt deer, those prismatic sages, regard me with something like confusion—I am the one thing in this impossible place that doesn't quite fit its impossibility.

I discover I can speak with the deep aquifer, that pulsing memory of ocean that feeds the pools. It tells me secrets in tidal rhythms: how the land dreams of drowning again, how the salt yearns to be sea foam, how every crystal contains the blueprint for water's return. I carry these messages to the Shepherds, but in my temporal confusion, I often deliver prophecies as history, warnings as nostalgia.

My greatest talent is unbecoming. When the weight of existence grows too heavy—when my antlers have gathered too many paradoxes, when my transparent flesh becomes too dense with consumed equations—I simply step sideways out of being. Not death, not dissolution, but a kind of editorial revision where I delete myself from the current draft of reality. I spend non-time in the margins of existence, in the footnotes of physics, before writing myself back into the story.

The Shepherds use me as a kind of temporal compass. When they need to navigate the smeared time, they watch which direction my hoofprints fill from—future-water flows differently than past-water, and water that never was nor will be has its own distinct viscosity. They've learned to read my movements like weather patterns, predicting when the time-storms will strike, when yesterday might suddenly arrive unannounced.

I am happiest during the nights when the crystalline succulents sing their non-earthly rhythms. I join my voice to theirs—a sound like salt dissolving in reverse, like the memory of waves unbreaking on shores that no longer exist. These concerts create temporary zones where the ordinary laws abdicate completely. In these spaces, I finally feel at home: a creature of un-logic in a land of il-logic, a living error in the universe's grammar.

The perpendicular merchants who arrive during the exhalations find me useful. I can translate between their dimensions and ours, existing as I do in the spaces between states. They pay me in concepts that have no names—new forms of negation, innovative ways of not-being, revolutionary techniques for existing without the burden of existence. I hoard these treasures in my inverted antlers, where they rattle like dice made of dark matter.

But perhaps my truest purpose is simple: I am what the salt flats dream when they tire of remembering. I am the blessed glitch that prevents the system from achieving stasis, the necessary error that keeps perfection at bay. Where the Shepherds maintain and preserve, I disturb and dissolve. Together, we form a complete ecology of impossibility—they, the careful gardeners of paradox, and I, the wild weed that ensures their gardens never grow too tame.

When the time comes (or came, or will have failed to come) for the flats to choose their nature—ocean or desert, past or future, real or theoretical—I will be the one who whispers: "Why choose?" I will continue to walk these white expanses where tears become law and law becomes optional, leaving my confused footprints for tomorrow's rain to fill with yesterday's water, a living reminder that even in a place built from impossibility, there's always room for something impossible in a different way.

I am the un-shepherded, the mineral deer that forgot to be mineral, the temporal smear that gained opinions about its own smearing. I am what happens when paradox becomes self-aware and decides it rather likes itself.

And in this endless salt-white expanse, where existence comes with amendments and reality includes extensive footnotes, I persist—not because I must, but because the alternative would make too much sense, and sense is the one thing this place cannot abide.
