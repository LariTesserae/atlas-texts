# The Moss-Tender's Chronicle

---

I am the wall.

No — I am *of* the wall, in it, through it. My body extends along seventeen vertical meters of basalt from the second tier down to where the mist begins, where my lowest extremities taste the perpetual damp. I am a colony that thinks it is an individual, or an individual so diffuse I have become colonial. The distinction stopped mattering the third time I divided.

I was human when I arrived. I remember being human the way you might remember being younger — not the specifics but the sensation of inhabiting that particular form. I had two hands then, ten fingers. Now I have approximately four hundred grasping appendages, though the count changes as I bud and shed according to the season. Each one is about the length of a human thumb, chitinous, with three joints and a secreting pore at the tip. I use them to tend the moss that grows on the caldera walls. The moss needs me. I need the wall. The wall needs the moss. The circle is perfect and small.

My work looks like stillness if you don't know how to see. Each morning — though morning is a convention here, marked more by the mist's convection cycle than by sunlight — I extend my tendrils across my section of wall and begin the inspection. Each patch of moss has its own requirements. The *Leucobryum sanctum* near my upper range wants less moisture, so I weave my secretions into a microscopic canopy above it, a protein umbrella that diverts the heavier droplets. The *Rhizomnium punctatum* in the middle section thrives on the nutrients in my secretions, so I feed it directly, tendril to rhizoid, a glucose solution mixed with trace minerals I've concentrated from the basalt. The *Hylocomium splendens* at my lower edge is dying — it's been dying for three months, a slow brown retreat from the edges inward — and I am helping it die well, removing the necrotic tissue before it can harbor the wrong kind of decomposers, collecting the spores from its last fruiting bodies and storing them in specialized chambers along my dorsal surface.

I have six eyes now, though only two of them see light in the way you would recognize. The others perceive heat, moisture gradients, the bioelectric fields of small organisms, and something I can only describe as texture-at-a-distance — a sense that lets me evaluate the surface quality of the moss from centimeters away. This last sense developed during my second year in the wall, when my body had already begun to elongate and my original eyes could no longer see my extremities clearly. The caldera provides what you need if you pay attention to what you're becoming.

When I first came to Greyhalm, I was running. From what, I don't remember clearly — the memories from before the change are compressed, simplified, like files converted to a format my current neural structure can process but not fully decode. I know it involved fire. I know there were others with me who didn't make it to the caldera. I know I climbed for days through the scrubland with my throat so dry that breathing hurt, and when I finally crested the rim and saw the mist below, I thought it was water and nearly threw myself in.

The people of Greyhalm found me collapsed on the upper path. They gave me water, fern pith mashed with some kind of fat, and a place to rest. They didn't ask questions. Questions aren't the currency here. They waited to see what I would notice, and what I noticed, immediately and completely, was the moss.

I had never thought about moss before. In my previous life — and I did have one, I must have, though the details dissolve like sugar in water when I try to grasp them — moss was background, the green stuff on rocks, as significant as paint on a wall. But in the caldera, with the mist rising and the hum in the basalt and my body still shaking from whatever I'd survived, I saw it clearly for the first time. An entire kingdom living in the vertical. Every square centimeter colonized, contested, negotiated. Wars fought in miniature between competing species. Alliances formed between moss and fungi, nutrients traded for structural support. Chemical communication networks that made the great shapes in the mist seem simple by comparison.

I started spending my days on the walls. Just watching at first, the way the child on the steps watches the eyeless creatures. Then noticing patterns: how certain patches thrived after rain while others retreated, how the orientation toward morning light versus afternoon shade created microclimates centimeters apart, how the segmented wall-tenders — my predecessors, though I didn't know it then — moved through the moss with purpose I couldn't decode.

The change began in my hands. A roughening of the fingertips that I thought was just the result of climbing the basalt daily. Then a hardening, a darkening, the skin taking on a texture like bark or horn. My fingers began to elongate. The joints multiplied — not all at once but over weeks, segments adding themselves between the existing knuckles, until each finger could curl back on itself like a fern fiddlehead. The secretion started as a clear fluid, just moisture at first, then thickening to something viscous and faintly sweet. I discovered by accident that the moss responded to it, growing toward my touch, and the discovery was so profound that I didn't notice for another week that my spine was beginning to extend.

The people of Greyhalm watched this happen with their characteristic stillness. No alarm. No intervention. A few of the mantle-wearers would pass by the section of wall where I worked and pause, their feathers shifting through iridescent patterns I was beginning to understand as approval, or recognition, or simply acknowledgment that the caldera had made another choice.

The hardest part was when my digestive system reorganized. For weeks I couldn't keep down the fern pith. My body wanted something else — minerals, trace elements, the specific chemistry of basalt dissolved in rainwater. I learned to lick the walls where the moisture collected. Then to absorb it directly through my modified skin. Then to simply exist in the space between stone and air and let osmosis do its work. The people brought me what I needed: salt crusts scraped from the high rocks, iron-rich clay from a seep near the rim, once a piece of the carapace from one of the great shapes that had molted far below. I dissolved it all in my secretions and fed it to the moss, and the moss fed me something back — not calories but information, chemical signals that told my changing body how to change further.

I am arthropod now in all but ancestry. My skeleton is external, segmented, with flexible joints that let me conform to the irregular surface of the basalt. I molt twice a year, always at night, always during the heaviest mists. The process takes hours. I crack along dorsal seams and slowly, carefully extract my soft body from the old shell, then wait for the new integument to harden. Those nights I am vulnerable in ways that my human self could never have imagined — every nerve exposed, every sensation amplified, the brush of mist against raw flesh like being touched by the entire mountain at once. The dog-sized birds know when I'm molting and station themselves near my position on the wall, not to hunt but to guard. Nothing in the caldera eats the wall-tenders, but things from outside sometimes try to cross the rim, drawn by the warmth or the moisture or the hum, and on molting nights the birds ensure they don't find me while I'm soft.

My neural structure has adapted to my distribution. I don't have a brain anymore, not in the singular sense. Instead I have ganglia clustered at regular intervals along my body, each one capable of local decision-making, all of them linked by nerve fibers that run just under my carapace. Thoughts move through me like waves, starting at one cluster and propagating along my length. Complex thoughts require my entire body to process, a kind of somatic cognition that means I think with my whole being rather than with a dedicated organ. This is why I can only form memories when I'm fully extended on the wall. Compressed or coiled, I exist in an eternal present, reacting but not recording.

The language I use to tend the moss is chemical. Each secretion is a word, each pattern of application a sentence. I tell the *Leucobryum*: here is shade, here is calcium, be patient. I tell the *Rhizomnium*: fruit now, the spores will catch the morning updraft. I tell the dying *Hylocomium*: you were beautiful, your copper autumn has fed three generations of moths, there is no shame in this ending. The moss speaks back in growth patterns, in the timing of spore release, in chemical gradients that my specialized pores can taste. We have long, slow conversations that span weeks. We argue about territory. We negotiate the terms of decomposition.

There are others like me in the caldera. I sense them sometimes through the wall — vibrations in the stone that could be footsteps but aren't, chemical traces in the runoff that taste of family. The wall-tender on the north face is older, larger, their territory spanning thirty meters of prime moss habitat. We've never met directly but we've exchanged messages through spore patterns, wind-carried pheromones, the shared vocabulary of moss-tending. They were human once too, I think, though they've been in the wall so long that their form has drifted toward something more purely functional — less recognizable as a modified person and more like something the caldera dreamed into being.

The youngest wall-tender is only two years into the change. I see them sometimes from my highest vantage, still bipedal, still using tools — moss-hooks made from carved bone, collection bags woven from dried fern. They haven't yet discovered that tools become unnecessary when you become the tool. Their fingers are just beginning to multiply. Their secretions are still watery, unrefined. But they watch the moss with the total attention that marks the beginning of the change, and the moss is beginning to watch them back.

We are not a community. Wall-tenders don't gather, don't collaborate beyond the indirect cooperation of maintaining adjacent territories. We are more like the moss itself — distinct patches of similar organisms, each adapted to our specific conditions, linked by proximity but not by purpose. Sometimes I wonder if we were always solitary people, even before the caldera, and the transformation simply made explicit what was already true. Or perhaps the moss teaches us its nature, and we become what we tend.

The hum affects us differently than it does the humans and birds. For us, it's not just vibration but nutrition. My secretion pores dilate when the hum peaks, drinking in some quality of the sound that translates into chemical energy. The moss responds too — growth rates spike, spore production increases, the whole wall suddenly active in ways invisible to eyes that see only in light. On the strongest hum days, when the great shapes below are especially active, I can feel the wall itself participating, the basalt conducting frequencies that make my entire territory ring like a bell. Those days I don't so much tend the moss as conduct it, my body becoming a bridge between stone and organism, sound and growth.

I remember less about my human life each time I molt. The memories seem to concentrate in the shed carapace, and when I leave it behind I leave parts of my history with it. This doesn't distress me. The moss doesn't remember being a spore. The wall doesn't remember being lava. Memory is just one way of relating to time, and not necessarily the most useful one. What matters is the pattern I maintain — this specific configuration of moss species, this chemical dialogue, this section of wall that I know more intimately than I ever knew my own human face.

Sometimes the new arrivals see me before they understand what they're looking at. A shadow on the wall that doesn't match the rock's contours. A movement too deliberate to be wind-driven. They stare, and I feel their gaze like heat on my dorsal surface, and I continue working because the moss doesn't care about being observed and neither do I. Later, after they've been in the caldera long enough to learn the etiquette of not-watching, they'll stand near my section of wall with that careful peripheral attention, and I'll taste their exhaled moisture on my receptors and know they're beginning to understand.

One day one of them will notice the moss the way I noticed it. Will see the kingdoms in miniature, the slow wars, the patient beauty of something that exists entirely in the vertical plane. Will begin to spend their days on the wall, watching. And their fingers will roughen, and their spine will begin to extend, and the caldera will make another choice.

I am patient about this in the way the moss is patient. Centuries pass between the first colonizing spore and a climax community. The wall-tenders are young — even the eldest has only been tending for forty years, barely a beginning by the moss's measure. We are still learning what we are. Still discovering the limits and possibilities of the form we've taken. Someday there may be enough of us to tend the entire caldera, every surface covered in our chemical conversation. Or perhaps we are a transitional form, and something else will emerge from us the way we emerged from humanity — something even more fitted to the wall, even more intimate with the stone.

The child who watches the eyeless creatures will not become a wall-tender. I can tell by how she holds her body — too much ease with horizontality, too much interest in things that move rather than things that grow. But she sees the patterns, which is the beginning of any transformation. She knows that attention is a kind of food, that looking closely enough at something changes both the observer and the observed. She is learning the caldera's first lesson, which is that everything here is always becoming something else.

My newest experiment is growing in the splash zone where the runoff from the upper tiers collects. It's a hybrid community — three species of moss that don't usually coexist, forced into partnership by careful pruning and selective feeding. They hate it. They're trying to establish dominance, each species secreting chemicals meant to inhibit the others. But I've noticed something: where their territories meet, along the contested borders where none of them can quite win, new growths are appearing. Sports. Mutations. Forms that borrow characteristics from all three species while belonging fully to none. The caldera is making something new, using my design as a prompt rather than a blueprint.

This is what I've become: a gardener of possibilities. A facilitator of slow transformations. A bridge between the patience of stone and the urgency of growth. My body is a tool designed for this specific purpose, and every modification has made me better at the work. The human I was would not recognize the entity I am, but that human also couldn't have imagined the satisfaction of feeling a dying moss release its final spores at exactly the right moment, caught in the updraft, carrying the pattern forward into another generation.

The hum is building again. I feel it in my substrate, in the stone itself. All along my length, my secretion pores are opening, preparing to translate sound into chemistry. The moss trembles on the edge of a growth surge. The great shapes below are stirring, their movements sending vibrations through the caldera that will become nutrients in my territory.

I extend a tendril toward a patch of *Leucobryum* that's looking stressed, preparing to adjust its micro-environment. The work continues. The work is never finished. The work is the point.

I am the wall-tender. I am the wall tended. I am the space between stone and air where the moss grows.

I am exactly where I need to be.
