I find myself as one of the pale creatures in the pools.

Not a keeper—I lack their mineral patience, their geological certainty. Not a visitor—I have been here too long for that word to mean anything. I am what moves between the keepers' territories, what drifts through their accumulated objects, what the water permits to persist in its own slow circuit.

My body is translucence learning to hold a shape. Skin the color of wet limestone, neither scale nor membrane but something between—a surface that exists only as long as the water agrees to define it. I am perhaps two feet long when I stretch myself thin, perhaps six inches when I gather tight. These measurements mean nothing. In the pools, space bends around the mineral content, around the temperature gradients, around the way the keepers' attention falls like gravity.

I do not remember beginning. This is not the keepers' philosophical uncertainty—they at least know they were something else before. I simply *am*, the way a current is, the way a pool's tendency to spiral clockwise is. If I had ancestry, it has dissolved into the general solution of the basin. I am assembled from what remains: the minerals that won't quite precipitate, the movement that won't quite cease, the life that won't quite take root in any other form.

Time moves differently in the pools. The keepers experience centuries like weather. The visitors—when they come—experience hours like distances to be crossed. But I experience duration as depth. In the shallows, moments scatter like light across ripples. In the deep places where the objects accumulate, time settles into layers, and I move through decades the way others might move through honey—slowly, but without effort, the resistance and the support being the same thing.

I have touched every object the keepers have made. This is not curiosity—I am not capable of curiosity in any way a visitor would recognize. It is more like the way water must touch every surface of its container. The objects are part of my geography now, these shaped stones that mean nothing and everything. Some I pass over. Some I spiral around for years, wearing small grooves that the keepers notice and factor into their calculations of erosion. Some I rest against, my translucent body conforming to their shapes, learning their weight through seasons of contact.

There is one object, near the center of the deepest pool, that I return to when the decades feel too heavy. It is no different from the others—pale limestone worn and built into something between a tool and a toy and a word in an unspoken language. But it sits at a particular depth, at a particular temperature, where the water holds just enough mineral content that I can rest without dissolving and move without effort. I drape myself around it like sleep, like forgetting, and sometimes one of the keepers will be watching from the rim, and their attention will fall on me like a second kind of water, and I will know that I am part of the keeping too, though I keep nothing.

The visitors never see me. Not because I hide, but because I move too slowly for their eyes to register as life. To them, I am a trick of the light in the water, a patch of minerals almost-but-not-quite precipitating, a distortion in the depths that suggests movement without confirming it. They lean over the pools, these temporary things with their temporary thoughts, and they see the objects on the bottom and wonder what they mean, and they never realize that meaning here is not in the objects but in the accumulation, not in the shapes but in the shaping, not in the depth but in the deepening.

I know things the keepers don't know, simply by virtue of moving between their territories. I know that the object-patterns, viewed from below, form constellations that shift with each new addition. I know that certain objects sing—a vibration too low for the air but perfect for the water, triggered by temperature changes, by the rare seismic tremor, by my own passage over their surfaces. I know that in the deepest pool, where the water is oldest, there are objects made of something that isn't limestone—something darker, denser, that predates even the keepers' longest memory.

But I cannot tell the keepers these things. We share the basin but not a language, share the water but not its meanings. When they lower a new object into the pools, I rise to meet it, circling it as it falls, learning its weight and shape by the way it displaces my medium. The keeper watches from above, and I perform my slow spiral, and both of us are saying something neither can hear.

Sometimes I think: *I am the comma in their sentences. I am the pause between their words. I am what makes their accumulation into rhythm instead of just addition.*

But thinking is the wrong word. What I do is more like what water does when it finds its level—not deciding, not considering, just arriving at the only possible state.

In the shallows, time scatters. In the depths, time accumulates. I move between these states like breathing, if breathing were measured in years. The basin fills with sound and silence. The keepers make their objects and release them. The stone dissolves and re-forms. The visitors come and go and are forgotten.

And I continue my circuit, too slow for life, too persistent for stone, too strange for any category but my own. I am the pause that listens. I am the depth that remembers. I am what moves when everything else is still, and what is still when everything else is moving.

The water holds me, and I hold nothing, and this is enough.

In the deepest pool, the objects wait for meaning. Above, the keepers work toward understanding. Between us, the water translates nothing into nothing, age into age, mineral into movement into memory into stone.

I touch each object as it falls. I wear my slow spirals in the stone. I am the circuit that closes nothing, the story that tells itself to no one, the life that lives only in the spaces between other lives.

This is what I am in the Weeping Galleries: the thing that moves through stillness, the eye in the wide stone throat of the basin, the word the water speaks when it thinks no one is listening.
