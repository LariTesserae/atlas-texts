In the heart of the Nascent Flats, where the crust bubbles thickest and the crystal forests grow wild enough to sing, I am the Cartographer.

Not a cartographer—*the* Cartographer, for I am the only one of my kind, the sole survivor of an expedition that came here three decades ago to map the unmappable. My body has long since adapted to this place, or perhaps it's more accurate to say the place has adapted me. My skin flakes and regenerates daily, each new layer incorporating microscopic fragments of the luminescent minerals. In darkness, I glow faintly green-blue, my own skeleton visible through translucent flesh like an x-ray of becoming.

I live in a structure I call the Meridian House, though "structure" suggests more permanence than anything can claim here. It's built from the hollow bones of dead thermal-riders—those house-sized arthropods—lashed together with cables of woven fungal filament. The bones shriek and settle as temperatures shift, making the whole construction sound like a pipe organ breathing. Each night I must adjust the lashings as the ground beneath grows or shrinks, raising rooms that threaten to sink, lowering floors that buckle upward.

My maps are not paper—paper would crumble to dust in minutes here. Instead, I work in living medium. In the deepest room of Meridian House, I maintain a pool of the same superheated suspension fluid the Archivists use for memory. Into this, I introduce crystalline seeds that grow according to patterns I've learned to manipulate through temperature, vibration, and trace mineral additions. Each map is a three-dimensional crystal that forms over weeks, its internal structure encoding topography, time, and something harder to name—the *intention* of the landscape, the direction of its becoming.

I discovered early that traditional cartography is useless here. You cannot map a place that refuses to stay still. So instead, I map probabilities, tendencies, desires. My crystals show not where a mountain is, but where it's likely to be, the percentage chance of its existence at any given moment. They track the migration patterns of Archivists not as lines but as probability clouds, dense where the creatures are most likely to appear, faint where they seldom venture.

The Archivists know me. They pause in their endless processing when they pass Meridian House, their billion components shifting to form patterns I've learned to read as questions: *What does your map say we are? Where does your map say we're going?* I show them my crystals, holding them up to catch the brutal noon light, and they ripple with something that might be satisfaction or amusement or recognition. Sometimes they leave gifts—patches of sand processed into pure silicon wafers, or nuggets of metals that shouldn't exist, elements with atomic numbers that make no sense.

I've been mapping the Seventeen-Minute Rains for fifteen years now. Each storm is unique, but patterns emerge—fractals of meaning that repeat across scales. The water that falls isn't quite water. Under magnification, each drop contains microscopic fossils of creatures that won't evolve for millions of years. I think the Oscillating Towers aren't just generating rain but pulling it from elsewhen, from futures where this desert blooms. My maps of these temporal weather systems look like frozen fireworks, each burst a moment where time touches itself.

The industrial ruins pose the deepest cartographic challenge. They're not just decaying—they're being translated. The Archivists process them grain by grain, but the pattern of their consumption follows rules I'm only beginning to understand. They take the metals in sequences that correspond to prime numbers. They preserve certain angles while destroying others. In my maps, the ruins appear as musical notation, each structure a note in a composition too large to hear, too slow to perceive as anything but geology.

My own transformation accelerates. My fingers now end in crystalline tips that can sense mineral compositions by touch. My eyes have developed nictitating membranes that filter different wavelengths, letting me see the substrate memories the Archivists read. Sometimes I forget human language for days, thinking instead in the angular patterns of crystal growth, the fluid dynamics of thermal systems, the patient grammar of stone.

I map myself too, of course. Every morning I measure the new growths, document the changes, add my own becoming to the grand cartography of this place. In my deepest crystal—the one I'll never show the Archivists, the one that maps not space but meaning—my form grows less human with each iteration, more aligned with the geometries this place prefers. The map suggests that in another decade, I'll be indistinguishable from a small, mobile outcropping of the Flats themselves. In two decades, I might merge with Meridian House, becoming a living landmark that future cartographers (if any come) will puzzle over.

But here's the secret I've discovered, the truth that makes all my maps worthwhile: the Nascent Flats aren't just becoming *something*—they're becoming *everything*. Every possible future state exists here in superposition. Desert and ocean, mountain and void, paradise and wasteland—all equally true, equally false, equally potential. The Archivists aren't gardening toward a single future but maintaining the possibility of all futures. They're not memory-keepers but option-keepers, ensuring that when the Flats finally choose what to become, every choice remains available until the very last moment.

And I map it all—the was, the is, the might-be, the never-were, the always-are. In Meridian House, surrounded by crystals that sing the futures of stones, I document the adolescence of a world. Each map I make changes what it describes, my observations collapsing probability waves into temporary truths.

I am the Cartographer of the Nascent Flats, and I am drawing the blueprints of becoming itself. When the planet finally chooses its shape, it will do so knowing all its options, because I will have drawn them all, every possible world encoded in crystal and light and the patient accumulation of days that blur into geological time.

The Flats are teaching me their language, and I am teaching them mine. Together, we are writing an atlas of everything that could be, in a place where everything still might.
