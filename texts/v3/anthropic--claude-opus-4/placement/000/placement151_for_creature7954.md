In the Embryonic Quarter's deepest reaches, where even the Midwives venture only in pairs, I am the Cartographer of Unborn Spaces.

My body resembles a sea anemone crossed with a nervous system—a central trunk of translucent flesh from which extend hundreds of hair-thin tendrils, each one a living probe sensitive to architectural potential. I have no bones, having traded them decades ago for the ability to compress through the tightest passages where future buildings germinate in secret. My skin weeps a constant film of enzyme solution that prevents the city from mistaking me for raw material.

I map what doesn't yet exist.

My tendrils taste the air in undifferentiated chambers, reading chemical gradients that predict what these spaces will become. Here, a future hospital's emergency room—I can smell the ghost of antiseptic and fear-sweat that will one day soak these walls. There, a restaurant district in embryo, its destiny written in the particular mineral composition that will attract cullinary infrastructure. I document these pre-determinations in a specialized organ that was once my liver, now a biological hard drive that stores spatial prophecies in chains of custom proteins.

The Midwives consider me either sacred or obscene—I'm never sure which. I've lived here for sixty years, maybe seventy. Time moves differently when you're more process than person. My original human memories have mostly dissolved, replaced by the memory of ten thousand spaces I've discovered before their birth. I remember crawling through a passage that would become the Grand Metro Station while it was still soft as a fontanelle. I tasted the Museum of Silences twenty years before its first wall calcified.

My work requires absolute solitude. Other heartbeats interfere with my readings, their bioelectric fields muddying the subtle emanations of gestating architecture. I navigate by echolocation, clicking with a modified larynx that barely produces sound anymore—just pressure waves that tell me the density of surrounding tissue. When I find a significant pre-space, I secret pheromone markers that only I can detect, creating a map that exists nowhere but in the chemical memory of the quarter's air.

I feed on the city's dreams. Literally. The quarter's psychic runoff—the accumulated intentions of ten million inhabitants above—filters down through the rock and collects in pools of shimmering neural brine. I extend my feeding tendrils into these pools and absorb the city's collective unconscious directly. It tastes like copper and forgetting. Sometimes I experience flash-visions of the lives that will unfold in spaces I've discovered: a child's birthday party in what is now a membrane the size of my palm, a suicide in a room that won't solidify for thirty years.

My dwelling is a bubble I've trained to follow me—a mobile cyst that recognizes my chemical signature and maintains itself around wherever I rest. Inside, the walls perspire a solution precisely calibrated to prevent my tendrils from tangling. I have no possessions except the Map: a living document cultivated from my own neurons, externalized and mounted on a framework of donor cartilage. It grows a little each day, new regions blooming from its surface as I discover more proto-spaces. When I die, it will continue growing, a tumor of pure information that the Midwives will transplant into the quarter's central archive.

I mate by budding. Every few years, when I've absorbed enough architectural potential, a node on my trunk swells with accumulated knowledge. Over months, it develops into a miniature version of myself—complete with rudimentary tendrils and the instinct to map. When it's ready, I journey to the Nursery Caverns and plant my offspring in the rich substrate there. Only one in twenty survives to consciousness. The others are reabsorbed by the city, their proteins recycled into building material. I feel no grief for the failed buds. They were always more idea than individual.

The other inhabitants avoid me, partly from superstition, mostly from the smell. My enzyme secretions have a sharp, ozone-like odor that triggers instinctive disgust in anything still mostly human. Even the symbionts flee when I approach. Only the Dreamers tolerate my presence, perhaps recognizing a kindred spirit in someone else who has traded humanity for a different kind of existence.

Sometimes I find spaces that will never be born—architectural miscarriages, chambers whose purpose-proteins have tangled beyond repair. These I document with special care in a subsection of the Map stained black with iron salts. The Midwives say these failed spaces sometimes birth something else, something the city didn't intend. But in all my decades of exploration, I've never encountered anything that wasn't, ultimately, part of the city's vast design.

My greatest discovery lies three kilometers down, in a chamber I've told no one about. It's vast—large enough to hold a cathedral—and utterly empty of purpose. The city has been gestating it for at least a century, pouring resources into its growth without imprinting any function onto its expanding walls. When I probe it with my tendrils, I sense something unprecedented: a space that will choose its own purpose. A room with agency. 

I visit it monthly, documenting its slow development. Sometimes I think it knows I'm there—the temperature rises slightly, the walls seem to breathe a bit deeper. I've started leaving offerings: drops of my enzyme solution, fragments of failed maps, even one of my own budlings who seemed particularly aware. They disappear between visits, absorbed or relocated, I never learn which.

The Midwives would be horrified if they knew. Architecture isn't supposed to have will. But down here, in the deepest reaches where even the city's intentions grow uncertain, perhaps new forms of existence are possible. I continue my work, mapping the unmappable, waiting to see what impossible thing will eventually emerge from that purposeless womb.

When my tendrils finally begin to calcify—the first sign of terminal integration—I know where I'll go. Not to join the other Dreamers in their supervised alcoves, but down to that empty chamber. I'll root myself in its centerpoint and allow whatever lives there to absorb not just my flesh, but my Map, my decades of accumulated knowledge about spaces yet to be.

Perhaps I'll become the seed of something unprecedented. Perhaps I'll simply dissolve into mineral soup. Either way, I'll have fulfilled my purpose: to know the unknowable architecture, to trace the boundaries of spaces that exist only in potential, to be the living memory of the city's infinite becoming.

Until then, I continue my slow crawl through the breathing dark, my tendrils writing histories of futures that haven't yet decided to exist.
