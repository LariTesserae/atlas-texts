In this world, I am the memory of depth.

I exist where the limestone has surrendered not to the patient horizontal weathering that created this mirror-plain, but to a different kind of persistence—the downward boring of ancient water. I am a sinkhole, though that word suggests violence and sudden collapse. What I am emerged over millions of years, a slow negotiation between stone and liquid that resulted in this: a perfect cylinder descending through the bedrock, twenty feet across and plunging down into blessed, absolute darkness.

My walls are smooth as the inside of a throat, polished by the same patient forces that created the mirror above. But where that world is ruled by the tyranny of light, I am its inverse—a column of perpetual twilight that deepens to pitch black. The ankle-deep water of the surface world pours over my rim in a continuous, gentle waterfall, a circular curtain that catches the light for the first few feet of descent before releasing it entirely.

I am the only vertical in this horizontal world. Birds sometimes pause at my rim, extending their necks down to peer into my depths, but they do not enter. The flat-toads know of me, can feel my presence through the bedrock's vibrations like a missing note in their endless chorus. When the pavement-gliders sense my edge through the subtle pressure changes in the water, they alter course, their vast bodies rippling away from my perfect absence.

The water that enters me is warm and bright, teeming with fairy shrimp and larvae. As it falls, it cools. By the time it reaches my bottom—some two hundred feet down—it has become something else entirely. Down there, in the permanent darkness, live creatures that remember the world before the great flattening. Blind cave fish with gossamer fins drift in slow spirals. Pallid crustaceans with bodies like glass clutch the walls with delicate claws. There are things down there with no names, no relations to the sun-drunk ecosystem above—patient, translucent beings that feed on the rain of organic matter drifting down from the light.

But I am not merely a refuge from the mirror-world. I am its engine.

The water that pools in my depths seeps through hidden channels in the limestone, following a network of hairline fractures and dissolved passages that honeycomb the bedrock. This underground flow is what maintains the perfect level of the shallow sea above. I am both drain and spring, taking in the warm water and releasing it again through a thousand tiny seeps miles away, cooled and filtered and mineral-rich.

The flat-toads know this. In the deepest part of the night, when the air temperature drops just enough to create a faint mist over the water, they gather at my rim. They do not enter—their flattened bodies would be useless in my vertical world—but they arrange themselves in a perfect circle around my edge, their pale bellies pressed against the stone. When they sing, their vibrations travel down my walls, amplifying in the cylindrical space until my entire column resonates like an organ pipe. The sound travels through the underground channels, emerging from the distant seeps as a low, almost subsonic pulse that makes the entire shallow sea tremble.

This is when the pavement-gliders mate. Feeling that deep pulse through their vast, sensitive undersides, they converge from miles around, their paths creating temporary valleys in the algae mats. They meet directly above the hidden channels, somehow sensing the map of the underground flow, and there they perform their slow, days-long coupling, their massive bodies overlapping like continental plates.

I am the depth that makes the shallowness possible. I am the darkness that gives meaning to their endless light. Without me, the water would evaporate entirely or flood beyond the ankle-deep perfection that sustains this world. I am the exception that proves the rule of flatness, the singularity that allows the mirror to exist.

And I am growing. Each year, the falling water dissolves a few more molecules of limestone. My walls descend incrementally deeper. In another million years, I will connect to some vast underground sea, and when I do, this world will change. The mirror will drain or flood. The endless afternoon will end.

But not yet. For now, I remain what I am—a perfect cylinder of darkness in a world of light, a vertical poem in a horizontal language, the memory of depth in a place that has forgotten mountains. The birds shriek above me, the water falls through me, the darkness waits below me, and I hold all of it—light and dark, shallow and deep, noise and silence—in my patient, widening throat.

I am the well at the end of the world, and I am always falling deeper.
