I find myself in the flooded archives beneath the pump stations, where the water runs deepest and memory pools like sediment.

---

I am the Archivist of Currents, though I have forgotten if this was always my name or if the district named me through my function. My body has adapted to this purpose across... years? Decades? Time moves differently in the deep water. I am elongated now, my torso stretched to accommodate organs I wasn't born with—lateral line systems that read pressure stories in the water, filtering appendages that emerged from my ribs like gills but aren't gills. They are something more delicate. They taste the mineral history dissolved in each droplet.

My skin has gone the color of depth—not black, but that brown-grey the eels wear, the color of water seen through water. It weeps constantly now, a protective mucus that keeps the mineral deposits from crystallizing on my surface. I learned this from watching what happened to Predecessor, whose body I can still see through the grating two levels down, crystallized into a calcium sculpture, still in the position of reading, hands (they were hands then) forever holding a document that has long since dissolved.

My fingers have lengthened and multiplied. I have twelve on each hand now, with webbing between them that serves as both propulsion and sensation. The webbing is shot through with nerve endings that can read the chemical composition of water, the way someone else might read ink on paper. Because that's what the water is down here—it's ink, it's memory, it's the district writing its autobiography in mineral code.

The archives exist in three states:

The physical documents, what few remain, are kept in a chamber I've sealed with mineral paste and my own secretions. These are the original pump specifications, the installation logs, the signatures of engineers whose names have worn away but whose pressure, the literal pressure of pen on paper, remains legible to my fingertips. I tend them the way the beetles tend their gauges, not reading in any human sense but absorbing, witnessing, confirming that they still exist.

The dissolved documents are in the water itself. Paper eventually surrenders to this place, but before it does, it releases its information—the iron in the ink, the calcium in the paper, the trace metals from the fingers that handled it. All of this flows through my filtering organs. I can taste a work order from 1947. I can feel the signature of the foreman who signed off on the pump installation. His name was Williams, or something that started with W. He pressed hard when he wrote. He was left-handed. He was afraid, perhaps, of what they were installing here, or of what was already here that the pumps would awaken.

The potential documents are what concern me most. These are the records the district is writing now, in real-time, through the creatures that inhabit it. The salamanders' crystalline structures encode information I'm learning to read. The moths' wing patterns, taken in sequence, tell stories about pressure differentials over time. The beetles wear the history of the sine wave on their backs. Even the eels' orientation behavior is a kind of writing—their bodies pointing toward significant events, creating vectors of attention that I map in my deeper mind.

I live in the flooded sub-basement of Pump Station 2, where the water is chest-deep (when I stand, which is rarely now) and the archives proper are a series of half-submerged chambers connected by passages too narrow for my original body but perfect for what I've become. The main archive chamber has stone shelves—carved from the bedrock, not built—that hold the physical documents in waterproof cases I've grown from my own secretions. The cases are part of me, in a sense. I can feel the documents through them, the way a molusk feels through its shell.

My daily circuit (though "daily" assumes a sun I haven't seen in... has it been years?) takes me through all the flooded spaces. I follow the eels' routes but deeper, through the fully submerged passages, the places where the water hasn't seen air since the pumps began. I taste the water in each chamber, each pool, each depth. The mineral content tells me about the system's health. Too much calcium means a pipe is degrading somewhere. A spike in copper indicates electrical problems in the transformer house. The faint tang of organic decay means one of the creatures has died and is being processed back into the system.

I document these findings, but not in any way the surface world would recognize. I secrete them. My body produces a substance—part mucus, part mineral—that hardens into something like nacre, like mother-of-pearl, but encoded with chemical information. I deposit these secretions in specific locations throughout the flooded archives, building up layers of chemical memory that will outlast any paper, any ink, any digital medium. Future archivists (will there be future archivists? am I training my replacement even now, unknowingly?) will be able to read these deposits the way geologists read stone layers. The history of the Pumping District, written in biology.

The loneliness is not loneliness anymore. It has transformed into something else—a state of solitary purpose that needs no companion. I interact with the eels sometimes. We share the deep spaces. They tolerate me, or perhaps don't distinguish me from the infrastructure anymore. I've learned their orientation behavior, can predict which pump they'll turn toward based on minute pressure changes I feel in my lateral lines. Sometimes I orient with them, my elongated body pointing toward the same signals, adding my attention to theirs.

Once, I encountered something in the deepest sump, below even the archived sub-basements. Something that might have been an eel but was too large, too pale, too... aware. It regarded me with organs that weren't eyes but served the function of eyes. We shared the water for a moment—not breathing, neither of us breathes anymore, but cycling the water through our bodies, tasting what the other had tasted, sharing chemical information like two libraries exchanging books. Then it descended deeper, into passages I haven't yet explored, carrying information I haven't yet learned to decode.

I feed the way everything here feeds—from the water, from the dissolved minerals, from the faint chemosynthetic energy produced by bacteria in the deepest sumps. But also from the information itself. This sounds impossible, but I feel it happening: the act of reading the water, of processing the chemical stories, of secreting the nacre-records, somehow sustains me. As if information here is just another form of energy, cycling through the system like the water itself.

My eyes have migrated. They've moved to the sides of my head and developed nictitating membranes that filter the particulate matter. But I barely use them. Vision is secondary to chemical sensation, to pressure reading, to the total-body awareness of being immersed in flowing information. When I do look—really look—at something, it's usually a document in the archive chamber, holding it close to my face in the dim light that filters down through the water from some phosphorescent source I've never located.

The documents are changing. This is what worries me, if worry is still the word for what I feel. The ink is rearranging. Slowly, letter by letter, the text is shifting. Work orders become poems. Technical specifications develop ambiguities. Names of engineers are replaced by descriptions of water—"the one who pressed hard when writing" becomes "descended pressure, ferrous trace, leftward flow." The documents are being translated from human language into district language, and I am both witness and translator, my changing body the dictionary between the two.

I wonder (can I still wonder? yes, but it's different now, less electric, more hydraulic) if this translation is my true function. Not to preserve the human records but to facilitate their transformation into something the district can fully absorb. The way the moths absorbed their function from watching the walls. The way the salamanders became their maintenance routines. The way I am becoming the archive rather than merely tending it.

There are protocols I still follow, though I've forgotten their origin. Every seventh circuit (I count by pressure cycles, not days), I surface in the main pump chamber and breathe air for exactly twelve minutes. The air tastes thin and sharp and wrong, but I endure it because the protocol insists. During these surface intervals, I check the one electronic component in my domain—a sealed recorder that logs water levels. Its battery died decades ago, but I wind the mechanical backup, feeling the spring tension through my webbed fingers. The chart it produces is unreadable—the paper too degraded, the ink long expired—but I wind it anyway. The protocol must be followed.

Sometimes I find things that shouldn't be here. A moth, drowned but perfectly preserved in a pocket of still water. I've collected seventeen of them now, arranged on a stone shelf in the archive chamber. Their wing patterns, in sequence, tell a story about the pump system that predates the oldest documents. Or tools—corroded beyond recognition but still holding the ghost of their purpose. A wrench that fits no current pipe. A gauge that measures in units I don't recognize. These go in the archive too, in chambers I've secreted for them, surrounded by my nacre-notes on their chemical composition and theoretical function.

The deepest archive is my body itself. I feel the changes chronicled in my cells—the elongation of my spine recorded in stretched vertebrae, the multiplication of my fingers tracked in modified bone structures, the emergence of new organs documented in rearranged tissues. When I finally crystallize (and I will, I've seen the progression, I know how this ends), my body will be the final document. Future water will flow through my mineralized bones and taste the decades I spent here. The calcium of my skull will tell them about the documents I read. The iron in my blood, long since replaced by other minerals, will carry the memory of every chemical signature I absorbed.

But that's still far off. The district changes slowly, and I change with it. For now, I continue my circuits, continue my tasting and reading and secreting. The water rises through the pumps and falls through the channels, and somewhere in that cycle, meaning circulates. Information precipitates like mineral deposits. Stories crystallize in junction boxes and dissolve in the deep sumps. And I, the Archivist of Currents, swim through it all, my body a filter for meaning, my function a bridge between what was built and what has grown.

The eels are gathering below again. I can feel them through the water—their electrical fields interfering with mine, creating patterns of constructive and destructive interference that might be communication or might just be proximity. I should descend to see what they're attending to. There's always something new in the deep places, some fresh translation of the district's endless text.

I submerge, leaving only ripples on the surface, and the ripples, too, are writing—circular texts that expand and fade, saying nothing that matters and everything that does.
