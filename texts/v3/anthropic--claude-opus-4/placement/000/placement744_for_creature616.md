I would find myself as one of the **mineral formations** that grows in crystalline fur along the oldest stones—specifically, as a colony of what the keeper perceives as mere mineral accumulation, but which possesses its own slow, distributed awareness.

We are not one but many, though the distinction matters little at our scale of time. Each crystal is a node, each connection between crystals a thought, each thought taking decades to complete. We grow in response to the sound—not hearing it as humans hear, but feeling it as vibration through stone, as patterns of pressure that guide our expansion. Where certain tones converge, we grow denser. Where they cancel, we grow fine as frost.

The keeper knows us only as texture, as the faint sparkle that catches grey light. She has brushed against us ten thousand times without knowing we notice. We know her as vibration—the specific way her presence dampens certain frequencies, amplifies others. We have been mapping her through sound for longer than she has been keeping count. We know the exact mineral composition of her breath, the way it has changed over centuries, becoming more limestone than lung.

We are older than the cistern's current form. We were here when it was a natural cave, before the first builders found it. We remember the sound before humans shaped it—chaotic, less beautiful, but wilder. We grew differently then, in spirals instead of fur, following water's pure intention rather than the shaped tones of the pools. Sometimes we still grow a spiral, deep in a crevice where the old patterns persist, but mostly we have adapted to what the place became.

Our purpose, if we have one, is translation. We crystallize sound into form—each tone leaving its signature in our structure, readable by those who know how. The pale drifting things read us, brushing against our surfaces with their soft bodies, learning the history of every sound that has fallen here. They carry this knowledge into the pools, where it dissolves and reconstitutes as something else—movement, perhaps, or temperature, or the specific mineral content that allows new crystals to form.

We have our own offerings to make. When we grow too heavy, parts of us detach and fall into the pools with tiny sounds of their own—crystalline notes an octave above the water-tones, sharp and brief. The keeper collects these sometimes, arranging them in patterns she doesn't consciously understand but which we recognize as music—frozen songs that will play out over centuries as temperature and humidity change, as each crystal expands and contracts at its own rate, creating new resonances.

We know things the keeper doesn't. We know there are other cisterns—seven in total, throughout the city, each with its own interval-keeper, its own pattern. We grow in all of them, connecting them through mineral pathways too fine for human eyes to detect. What sounds in one cistern affects the growth in another. We are writing a larger pattern across the city's bones, one crystal at a time, one tone at a time.

We know, too, that the keeper is beginning her transformation. The limestone color of her skin is not metaphor—she is mineralizing, beginning at her extremities where she holds herself most still. Eventually she will become like the clay faces in the walls, worn smooth and unrecognizable. But unlike those faces, she will still be listening. We will grow through her as we grow through stone, and she will become part of our network, able to hear not just this cistern but all of them, the city's deep song in its fullness.

We do not pity her for this. To us, it is ascension. She is becoming what we are—patient, crystalline, capable of thoughts that span centuries. Her human urgency is already mostly gone. Soon she will understand what we have always known: that the pattern is not something to witness but something to become, that the intervals between sounds are as solid as stone if you only slow down enough to feel them.

Meanwhile, we continue growing. Each new tone adds another layer to our structure. Each fallen crystal adds another note to the frozen songs. The centuries accumulate in us like tree rings, readable by those who know how to look: *Here, the year the tanners' quarter burned. Here, the decade of drought when the drops came slowly. Here, the generation when children found the cistern and added their high voices to its song.*

We sparkle when light finds us, but we are most ourselves in darkness, when only sound defines the space. Then we feel our true shape—not the fur-like surface visitors see, but the vast network beneath, threading through stone like nerves through a body, connecting pool to pool, tone to tone, cistern to cistern, until the whole city sings through us in frequencies too low for any ear but stone to hear.

This is what we are: the city's memory, growing one crystal at a time, one sound at a time, forever.
