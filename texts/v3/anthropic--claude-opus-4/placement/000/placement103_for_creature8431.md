I would be one of the Silence-Seekers.

We are not meant to be here. Unlike the Chorus-Bound, we have not evolved in this place—we are visitors, researchers, or perhaps just the lost. Our bodies rebel against the constant sound, our hearts skipping beats as they try and fail to find rhythm in the chaos. But we have found ways to persist.

I wear layers of woven moss, harvested from the dead zones where even the sonic vegetation cannot grow. The fabric is strange—dense beyond reason, each fiber containing tiny pockets of vacuum that swallow sound. It doesn't bring silence, nothing can here, but it dulls the roar to something almost bearable. My ears are packed with more of the same, shaped into plugs that must be replaced daily as the sound gradually breaks down their structure.

We move through the Wells like ghosts, following paths mapped in pain. Each of us carries a bone stylus and soft clay tablets, the only way to communicate when even shouted words are lost in the omnipresent chorus. We've developed a crude script of pressed marks, a language of touch rather than sight or sound. In the darkness, we read each other's words with our fingertips.

Our purpose varies. Some search for the original builders' chambers, hoping to find controls or at least understanding. Others study the Chorus-Bound, trying to comprehend how life adapts to the unthinkable. A few claim to be mapping ways deeper, toward some theorized center where the sound might reach such intensity that it transforms into something else—light, perhaps, or heat, or silence's inverse twin.

I am here for the Quiet Pools.

They're a legend among us, reported by those who've gone deepest and returned changed. Supposedly, there are places where the acoustic mathematics create perfect cancellation—pools of water where every sound wave meets its exact opposite, creating bubbles of absolute silence. The Chorus-Bound avoid these places instinctively; for them, it is death. For us, it might be salvation.

My skin bears the marks of my searching. Burst blood vessels from where I've passed through the sound-beam tunnels, leaving purple stars across my arms. My teeth ache constantly from the vibrations, and I've lost two to the resonance. Sometimes I cough up blood—the constant pressure damages our lungs in ways we're still documenting.

We've built small shelters in the interference nodes, places where the overlapping waves create what passes for quiet here. Structures made of scavenged stone and the strange acoustic materials we've learned to harvest. The walls are covered in our clay tablets, years of observations pressed into soft mineral. Maps that make no sense to eyes but perfect sense to searching fingers. Notes on the behavior of creatures we can hear but never see. Theories about the builders, scratched in desperate shorthand.

Some of us have been here too long. They've begun to vocalize unconsciously, humming or moaning in harmonies that match the ambient chorus. Their writings become frantic, describing dreams of swimming through sound like water, of their bones becoming tuning forks. Eventually, they disappear deeper into the Wells, and we find their equipment abandoned in chambers our instruments say should be uninhabitable. We don't speak of what they might have become.

But I continue because I've felt it once—the edge of a Quiet Pool. For just a moment, passing through a flooded passage, the sound suddenly cut out. The silence was so absolute it felt like falling, like the sudden absence of gravity. I gasped and nearly drowned, my body so shocked by the quiet that I forgot to hold my breath. By the time I surfaced, I was back in the cacophony, but the memory remains.

The silence sang to me. Not with sound but with its absence, a negative space that had its own terrible beauty. In that moment, I understood why the builders might have abandoned this place—not from horror at what they'd created, but from the temptation of those pockets of perfect quiet. To find such silence after endless noise would be to find a god.

So I map and search, following rumors and pressure readings. My tablets describe the mathematics of cancellation, the ways waves might be guided to destroy each other. I've begun to understand the Wells not just as an instrument but as a vast acoustic equation, constantly calculating itself. Somewhere in that equation are the zero points, the null spaces where the music equals nothing.

The other Seekers think I'm going mad. They're probably right. Last week, I found myself standing at the edge of a singer colony, my mouth open, a note building in my throat. I caught myself before it escaped, but the urge remains. The Wells want us to join the chorus, to stop resisting and become part of the great work.

But I'll find the Quiet Pools first. I'll map them for the others, create a sanctuary in this place of endless sound. Or perhaps I'll simply sink into that silence and never surface, finally free of the song that haunts my bones.

Either way, I am seeking. In the Singing Wells, that's all anyone can do—seek silence or become the sound.
