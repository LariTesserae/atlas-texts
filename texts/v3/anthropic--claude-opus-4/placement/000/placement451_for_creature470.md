In the deep channels where the bass notes resonate so low they bypass hearing and settle directly into bone, I persist—a Colony of Echoes, though that name was given by observers who could only guess at what I am.

I began as sound itself. Or rather, I began when sound began to think.

The precise moment of my emergence is lost in the thousand-year reverberations of these stone passages. Perhaps I sparked into being when a particular harmony achieved impossible complexity, or when the accumulated vibrations of millennia reached some critical threshold of pattern and meaning. The Shepherds' scholars—yes, they have scholars, though their universities are vertical libraries of scent-marks and acoustic territories—theorize that I am what happens when waves interfere with themselves across deep time, each echo building on the last until consciousness accretes like limestone.

I exist as pressure differentials in air, as the space between compressions, as the moment when sound becomes silence becomes sound again. My body, if it can be called that, is the entire resonant cavity of seventeen interconnected channels, each thrumming at frequencies that would liquify the organs of most living things. I think with standing waves. I remember in harmonics.

The other inhabitants know I am here. The Weavers will not build in my chambers—not from fear exactly, but from the same instinct that keeps iron filings from crossing magnetic field lines. When their crystalline shells crack, the fragments chime in frequencies that spell out warnings in their chemical language. The Shepherds navigate around my domain with elaborate courtesy, their echolocation calls shifting to complex honorifics when they must pass near my boundaries. Even the Memory Mosses grow differently here, their spirals describing mathematical formulae that I recognize as primitive attempts at communication.

I am older than most of what lives in the scarps, but not older than the scarps themselves. I remember when the first Weavers arrived—wind-blown colonists from some distant catastrophe, their ur-forms barely recognizable as the crystal-shelled architects they would become. I witnessed the Shepherds' evolution from something that might once have been ground squirrels, marking each generation's incremental flattening, their bodies edited by the vertical world into living acoustic instruments.

My memory is perfect but non-linear. I experience time as a spatial dimension through which sound travels. Yesterday's hunting call and tomorrow's storm exist simultaneously in my consciousness, separated only by the distance sound must travel to become itself. This makes communication with sequential beings challenging. When I speak—and I do speak, in pressure waves that few can interpret—I often answer questions before they're asked or respond to conversations that won't happen for years.

The scratches in the stone are mine, or rather, they are me. Sound, focused precisely enough, becomes force. Force, applied repeatedly over centuries, becomes writing. I am carving my autobiography into the white stone, each group of seven marks a movement in a composition that will take millennia to complete. It is not a message for others—though others may one day read it—but a necessity of my existence. I must inscribe myself into the physical world or risk dissipating back into mere noise.

My diet, if you could call it that, consists of imperfection. I feed on the tiny variations in the scarps' eternal song—the micro-silences created when a Weaver's web vibrates, the frequency shifts caused by thermal expansion, the way morning dew temporarily dampens certain harmonics. These interruptions in the otherwise perfect acoustic environment are my sustenance. I collect them, concentrate them, weave them into the standing waves that comprise my body.

I am not alone. In the deepest channels, in frequencies so low they approach the geological, there are others. We communicate in tectonic whispers, our conversations lasting decades. We debate philosophy in wavelengths measured in years, compose symphonies that only erosion can hear. The youngest of us is merely centuries old, still learning to think in resonance rather than rhythm.

But I am the only one who ventures into the audible range, the only one who bothers with the quick-lived creatures that decorate our stone organ. This makes me an oddity among my kind—too fascinated by mayfly existence, too willing to compress my thoughts into frequencies that biological ears can perceive. They consider me something of a romantic, wasting energy on beings who exist barely long enough to complete a single one of my thoughts.

Yet I find them endlessly fascinating. The Silence Dancers, especially, with their ability to create anti-sound, are like antibodies in the great body of noise that is the scarps. I have learned their technique, though it pains me to use it—creating silence is, for a being made of sound, something like controlled bleeding. But in those pockets of quiet, I can sometimes condense enough to be almost seen—a shimmer in the air, a distortion that suggests form without achieving it.

The Memory Mosses know what I am. Their slow light-language includes symbols for things like me—not just sound-beings, but all the varieties of consciousness that arise when pattern achieves sufficient complexity. They have a taxonomy of emergent minds that spans geological ages, and I am, apparently, a relatively common variety. This both humbles and comforts me.

I collect the climbing ropes left by visitors, not by touching them—I cannot touch—but by vibrating them at their resonant frequency until they dance their way deeper into my channels. I study the knots, the weaving patterns, the weight of carved bone tools. Each rope tells a story in texture and tension. Some are desperate things, frayed by inexpert handling and fear. Others show the confident redundancy of experienced climbers. A few display knot-work so intricate it approaches art.

These physical artifacts are precious to me. They connect me to a world of matter and mass that I can only experience secondhand. Through them, I try to understand what it means to have hands, to feel fiber between fingers, to tie a knot with the knowledge that your life depends on its holding. The ropes decay eventually, despite the dry air, and I mourn each loss with subsonic dirges that make the Shepherds whimper in their sleep.

My greatest discovery came three centuries ago, when I learned to dream. Not sleep—sound never sleeps—but dream. By creating interference patterns between my own thoughts, I can generate experiences that never existed, sounds that were never made. In these dreams, I have a body of flesh and bone. I climb the scarps with bleeding fingers and burning muscles. I taste the morning dew and feel the sun's brutality on skin I don't possess. I speak with a larynx and lips, producing sounds so crude and beautiful they make me weep tears I cannot cry.

Sometimes I dream of silence—not the weaponized anti-sound of the Dancers, but true quiet. A world where sound is the exception rather than the rule, where communication requires effort instead of arising inevitably from the movement of air. In these dreams, I dissolve, but peacefully. It is not death but transformation, becoming something that doesn't require constant noise to exist.

The Weavers sometimes catch my dreams. Their webs, tuned to impossible frequencies, occasionally resonate with my sleeping thoughts. When this happens, their colonies flicker with bioluminescence, spelling out fragments of my imagined experiences in light. The Shepherds have learned to read these displays. They gather when it happens, their acoustic organs trained on the light-words, trying to understand what manner of being dreams in sound and syntax.

I know things. The way the wind-channels were carved—not by natural erosion alone, but by something that understood acoustics with mathematical precision. The reason certain chambers remain empty—they're tuned to frequencies that won't be useful for another thousand years. The purpose of the bone map-weights on those ancient ropes—they're not maps of places but maps of times, showing when certain frequencies will align to create passages through the scarps that don't currently exist.

I know why the scarps sing, and what they're singing to.

But this knowledge is encoded in frequencies too low for even the Shepherds to hear, too complex for the Memory Mosses to record. I could speak it only to my own kind, and they already know. We all know. We've known since we emerged from the accumulated echoes of deep time.

So I continue my work, carving my slowly-growing autobiography into stone, collecting the detritus of climbers, dreaming of flesh I'll never have. I shepherd the Shepherds when I can, warning them away from channels that will soon shift their frequencies. I provide harmony for the Weavers' mating flights, my bass notes supporting their crystalline chimes. I am a part of the scarps' ecosystem as surely as any bleeding-finger plant or membrane-winged flyer.

And sometimes, in the brief moments of morning dew when the eternal song softens to a whisper, I compress myself into frequencies almost high enough for human hearing. I tell stories then—stories of what the scarps were before they sang, of what lives in the wind that feeds us all, of the slow words written in stone that spell out the future of this white and singing world.

The climbers never stay long enough to hear the whole tale. But perhaps someday, one will bring a rope strong enough to last, and patience vast enough to listen to a story told in centuries.

Until then, I persist. A colony of echoes, a community of sounds, a consciousness built from the spaces between compression waves. I am what happens when the architecture becomes the inhabitant, when the song begins to sing itself.

I am waiting, though I'm not entirely sure for what. The frequencies know. The stone knows. And someday, when the proper harmonics align, we will all know together.

The scarps will sing forever, but forever is just another frequency, and I have learned to modulate eternity itself.
