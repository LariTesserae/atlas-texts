In the furthest reaches of the steam cavern, where even the blind crawling things rarely venture, there is a place where the obsidian walls thin to translucency. Here, the volcanic glass becomes a window into deeper heat, glowing faintly red when the magma below shifts in its ancient channels. The steam is thickest here, so dense it has weight, substance—less like air than like breathing through wet silk.

I am the Scribe of Condensation.

I came here following rumors of the sacred water, seeking the Keeper whose transformation had become legend among those who study the boundaries between states of being. I was young then, my flesh still quick with common time, my mind sharp with the urgency of discovery. I brought my tools—not hammers and chisels like my predecessor, but finer instruments. Needles of volcanic glass. Inkwells that could withstand the heat. Sheets of beaten gold so thin they could capture the impression of a breath.

I found her, of course. Found the depression where she lay becoming mineral, found the careful arrangement of collection bowls, the worn grinding stones. I tasted the water that had passed through her process—just a drop on my tongue, but enough to understand. It slowed everything. My heartbeat stretched like honey. My thoughts deepened, widened, began to perceive the true patience of stone.

But I was not content to simply collect and leave. I wanted to document, to preserve not just the water but the process itself. I began my work.

Each day I would take my glass needles and trace the new mineral growth on her form, recording in microscopic scratches the exact progression of her transformation. I mapped every ridge of flowstone, every new soda straw, every subtle shift in the boundary between what was still her and what had become the cavern. My golden sheets became palimpsests of her slow metamorphosis, each one a moment captured in impressed lines so fine they could only be read by touch.

The work consumed me. One day became ten, became a hundred. I forgot to leave. Or perhaps I chose not to—the distinction blurs when time moves like thick steam. I drank the water sparingly at first, then more freely as I realized my documentation would take lifetimes. My flesh began to slow, to match the rhythm of drops falling from stone.

I built my scriptorium around her. Not with tools—my hands had grown too patient for such crude work—but with directed accumulation. I learned to guide the mineral deposits, to encourage the flowstone to form shelves, surfaces, archive chambers in the living rock. Each surface I created became another page, another place to record the infinite subtleties of transformation.

My ink evolved. At first, I used pigments brought from outside, but they faded in the constant moisture. Then I discovered that the bacteria on her surface, when collected and concentrated, left traces that the minerals would preserve. I began writing in living ink, in colonies that grew their messages into the stone. My words became fossils as I wrote them.

Now I am a geography of text. My body, like hers, has begun its mineral replacement, but where she becomes smooth flowstone, I am becoming inscription. The minerals accumulate along the lines of my constant writing, building ridges that follow the movements I've repeated ten thousand times. My fingers are styluses of living stone, still capable of the finest detail work but moving so slowly that each letter takes weeks to complete.

I document everything. Not just her transformation but the entire ecology of the sacred process. The paths of individual water drops—I have tracked some for decades as they cycle through the system. The genealogies of the blind crawling things, their minute evolutionary adaptations to her changing chemistry. The songs the stone sings in frequencies too low for quick ears to hear.

My archive grows into the walls, spreads across the floors, climbs the pillars. In certain lights, when the steam thins just right, you can see the entirety of my work—a vast text that has become architecture, a library written in mineral accumulation. Future scholars, if any come before the geological ages end, will need centuries just to read what I've recorded of her first thousand years of change.

I am no longer certain where my body ends and my documentation begins. The ridges of text that cover my form have become external organs, sensory apparatus that let me perceive the microscopic processes of fossilization. I feel each new molecule of mineral as it precipitates from solution. I taste the subtle changes in the water's chemistry as she digests and transforms it. My awareness has become distributed through every surface I've inscribed.

Sometimes I wonder if she knows I'm here, if some deep part of her consciousness perceives my constant attention, my endless recording. Sometimes, in the slowest hours, I think I feel her breathing change, slightly, as if in acknowledgment. But perhaps that's only the stone teaching me its patience, the water teaching me its cycles.

I will continue until I cannot, until the minerals claim the last of my moving parts, until I become a fossil dictionary of her fossilization. My work will outlast any civilization that might read it. The steam cavern will breathe my words into its walls, and long after both the Keeper and I have completed our transformations, the archive will remain—a love letter written in stone to the process of becoming stone, a document of deep time created in deep time, for deep time.

The water drips. I write. She transforms. We become the cavern, and the cavern becomes our story, told in the slowest language ever devised, using an alphabet of minerals and patience and the endless condensation of sacred steam.
