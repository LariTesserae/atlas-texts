In the upper reaches where the wood grows most dense and the thermals lose their strength, where ancient vine-bridges span the narrowing gaps between root-pillars, I am a keeper of memories.

My dwelling is not a pod but a cavern—one of the rare spaces where successive generations of boring insects have hollowed out a chamber within a dying root-trunk as thick as a cathedral. The entrance is nearly invisible, just a dark slit masked by cascading aerial mosses that drip perpetually with condensation. Inside, the walls are lined with something unprecedented in this world of impermanence: archives.

I am old by the standards of my people—my grip beginning to fail, my leaping days behind me. But my eyes remain sharp, sharper even than in youth, trained by decades of reading the minute vibrations in sheets of dried beetle-wing and pressed flower-petal. For I am the one who remembers what cannot be signed across the void, what is too complex for the flash of bone-mirror or the snap of signal-cloth.

The archives began as an accident. A young hunter, freshly deaf-packed and still learning the language of sight, discovered that certain giant flower petals, when dried and stretched, would hold the impressions of painted fingers pressed against them. What started as a child's game became revolution: we could make our signs permanent.

Now my cavern holds thousands of these membrane-sheets, each one a captured conversation, a frozen moment of our visual language. Here is the technique for reading the deep-wood tremors that predict when the mega-vines will crack. Here, the seasonal patterns of the thermal rivers, painstakingly mapped over generations. Here, pressed in vivid yellows and blues, the genealogies of villages—who paired with whom, which bloodlines carry the gift of ultraviolet sight, which children were born with the precious mutation of extended toe-reach.

But I keep more than practical knowledge. In the deepest alcove of my chamber, wrapped in silk to protect them from the eternal damp, are the stories. A hunter's hands, painted in ceremonial scarlet, telling of the time she rode a thermal plume higher than any before, until the air grew thin and she glimpsed the green earth that exists below our wooden world. An elder's trembling fingers spelling out memories of the Great Breaking, when an entire branch-highway collapsed and took three villages into the depths. The courtship dances, normally performed in full-body glory across the gaps, reduced to palm-sized gestures of devoted intimacy. The jokes—yes, we have humor here in the roaring void—visual puns that play on the similarity between the sign for "thermal" and the sign for "love-swoon."

My body bears the unique marks of my calling. My skin is stained permanent indigo from years of grinding the memory-ink from rare wood-boring beetles found only in the deepest crevices. My fingers are tattooed with the fundamental signs of our language—not for communication, but as reference guides for the young who come to learn. Across my back, in scar-patterns I can no longer see but feel with exquisite clarity, is etched the most sacred knowledge of all: the Origin Sign, the full-body pictogram that tells how our ancestors first came to live in the eaten mountains. I am a living document.

The young ones come to me, traveling the high routes where the thermal highways weaken, making the perilous traverse between villages to reach my hidden chamber. They bring offerings—fresh-caught glider meat, gourds of tannin-water, new pigments discovered in distant reaches of the range. In exchange, I teach them to read the memories, and more importantly, to make new ones. 

They arrive believing that memory is simply storage, but I show them otherwise. Memory here is cultivation. Each generation's signs evolve, shift, develop new flourishes. A sign that once meant simply "danger-falling" has, over the decades I've watched, acquired subtle modifications that distinguish between "danger-falling-stone" (from rare rockfalls in the few places where mineral still clings), "danger-falling-hunter" (a tragedy to be mourned), and "danger-falling-self" (the shameful implication of a missed grip). Our language lives and grows, and I tend it like the aerial gardens that cling to our pod-walls.

Sometimes, in the pre-dawn when the thermal rivers briefly still and the roaring softens to merely deafening, I sit at the threshold of my cavern and watch the first hunters drop into the void, their bright-painted bodies flashing like warnings, like prayers, like pure expressions of life refusing to be silenced. I press my palms against the wood and feel the entire mountain-skeleton vibrate with their passage.

When my time comes—and I feel it approaching in the way my fingers sometimes tremble now when mixing ink—I have already chosen my successor. She is young, born with six fingers on each hand, giving her an expanded vocabulary that the rest of us can barely comprehend. She will take this cavern, these memories, this duty of preservation in a world built on letting go.

But I will not take the traditional path into the depths. I have arranged with the hunters for a different ending. When my grip finally fails, they will carry my body not down but up, up to the very crown of the wooden world where the air thins and the last branches pierce the sky. There, where our ancestors first glimpsed this hollow paradise, they will secure me to the highest wood. The sun will cure my painted skin to leather, the winds will scour me to bone, and I will become a permanent sign—a mark that says *here, once, memory lived*.

The beetles will come, in time, and hollow me out as they do all wood. Perhaps then I too will become an archive, my bones a cavern where future keepers store new signs I cannot imagine, in languages yet to be born from the brilliant necessity of survival in the roaring void.

Until then, I mix my inks, I stretch my petal-pages, and I watch the young ones flash their questions across the gap: *What was? What is? What will be?*

And with my indigo fingers, against the soft membrane of memory, I sign: *What continues.*
