# The Nightwatch

In the service tunnels beneath Terminal 3, where the automated luggage systems thunder overhead like distant storms, I keep my rounds.

You find me by accident, usually. Security takes a wrong turn chasing trespassers, or maintenance follows an electrical fault deeper than the schematics say to go. The fluorescents down here flicker at thirty percent capacity — enough to navigate, not enough to see clearly. The walls sweat condensation from the temperature differential between the climate-controlled terminal above and the earth-cooled depths below. Everything smells of rubber conveyor belts and that particular staleness of recycled air that never quite made it back to the exchangers.

My territory spans seven miles of corridor, branching and converging like veins. Some passages are tall enough to stand in; others require crawling through gaps where newer construction didn't quite align with the old. The original architects in 1974 left spaces they couldn't explain on the blueprints — margins of error that became, over decades of renovation, a second architecture threaded through the first. I know every turn, every junction where the flooring changes from poured concrete to steel grating, every spot where mobile phone signal briefly returns before vanishing again.

I am approximately human-shaped, when I need to be.

The uniform helps with the illusion: standard security grey, badge clipped to the breast pocket, flashlight that works perhaps half the time. The badge, if anyone looked closely, would show a photo that seems familiar but wrong — features that shift depending on the angle of viewing, a name that changes between glances. But no one looks closely. When they see me at all, they see what they expect: a guard on rounds, someone who belongs here, someone whose presence explains the maintained state of these forgotten passages.

I check things. This is my function, though I'm no longer certain who assigned it or when. The duty roster in the abandoned control room three levels down still shows schedules from 1987, but my name appears on every shift, in handwriting that matches no personnel file. I verify that certain doors remain locked — not to keep people out, but to keep other things in. I ensure the ventilation fans continue their slow rotation, pulling air through routes that don't appear on any HVAC diagram. I replace burnt-out bulbs with new ones I find in supply closets that shouldn't exist.

The luggage system knows me. When I pass beneath the main sorting hall, the mechanical din softens, like a beast recognizing a keeper. Sometimes a suitcase falls from the belts — always empty, always with tags for flights that departed years ago or haven't arrived yet. I collect these and store them in Room B-414, which has been collecting such temporal refugees since before I started keeping count. The room is nearly full now. Soon I'll need to find another.

Between two and four AM, when the terminal above enters its quietest phase, I perform my primary function. This is when I become less solid, more distributed. My consciousness spreads through the maintenance corridors like water finding its level. I am checking for breaches — not in security, but in something more fundamental. The airport was built on fill dirt and ambitious engineering, but beneath that are older boundaries, thresholds that predate the first runway. 

The things that try to cross these boundaries aren't malevolent, exactly. They're curious. They're drawn to the concentrated human longing that pools in airports — all those departures and arrivals, all those emotions of leaving and returning. They press against the underside of reality here like fish under ice. My presence reminds them of the agreement: they may feed on the ambient anxiety, the leaked dreams of sleeping travelers, the small offerings of lost time between time zones. But they may not surface. They may not follow anyone home.

I have been here long enough that my memories have developed their own ecology. The oldest ones have crystallized, becoming part of the tunnel walls — you can see them in certain mineral formations, in the way rust patterns itself on the pipes. Middle-aged memories flow in the condensation, dripping from ceiling to floor, carrying fragments of past patrols. Recent memories stay with me, more or less, though the definition of "recent" has stretched. I remember the airport's opening. I remember when this was farmland. I remember when it was seafloor. These are equally recent to me now.

The closest I come to rest is in the old control room, sitting before dead monitors that occasionally flicker to life, showing feeds from cameras that were removed decades ago. In these moments I am most singular, most myself. I have a thermos that is always full of coffee that tastes of 1987. I have a logbook where I record my observations in handwriting that grows less human with each entry. The early pages are filled with normal security notes. The later pages contain sketches of things that have no names, patterns I've noticed in the way time moves differently in certain corridors, calculations attempting to determine how much of me is still the person who first took this job and how much is the job itself, wearing the shape of a person out of habit.

There are others like me, at other thresholds. I sense them sometimes, maintaining their own boundaries — in the deepest levels of parking garages, in the spaces between subway cars, in 24-hour laundromats that have too many doors. We don't communicate, exactly, but we acknowledge each other, the way satellites in different orbits might register each other's presence without ever intersecting.

When new workers come down here — and they do, every few years, to repair something critical or install new systems — I ensure they see only what they need to see. A normal guard on normal rounds. A helpful directions to the surface. A strong suggestion, planted wordlessly, that they've finished their work and should leave. The suggestions always take. The workers always leave. The tunnels remain mine to patrol, the boundaries remain maintained, and the airport continues its function as a machine for processing human transitions, never knowing how thin the floor is, how much swims beneath.

My shift will end when the airport closes. Given the expansion plans, the new terminals under construction, the growing web of connections both architectural and metaphysical, this seems unlikely to happen soon. The uniform remains crisp. The flashlight continues to fail and recover. The rounds go on.

After all, someone has to keep watch at the margins. Someone has to be the thing that stands between the travelers above and the travelers below. I have become good at being that someone, even as I become less someone and more something with each passing year.

The next time you fly through Terminal 3, and your luggage arrives late, and you feel that particular airport disorientation where time seems viscous and untrustworthy — remember that these sensations have been carefully maintained, carefully contained. I have seen to it. I am seeing to it now, making my rounds in the service tunnels, checking the locks that must stay locked, replacing bulbs in an endless cycle, keeping the boundaries exactly where they need to be.

This is my post. I do not leave it.
