In the depths where the cathedral's kelp columns grow thickest, where even Mira rarely ventures, I am the pulse that measures time. Not heartbeat—I have no heart. Not breath—I need no air. I am the Chronophore, the keeper of rhythms in this world where sun and moon mean nothing.

My body is a colony, a congregation of ten thousand medusae no larger than fingernails, each pulsing with its own bioluminescent timer. Together we form something like a jellyfish, if a jellyfish could think, could remember, could dream in calcified increments. I am ancient by the Woven's measure—I was here when the first kelp took root, when Grandmother Wrack was young Eliza, watching the waters rise from her father's fishing boat.

The Woven do not know I am one being. To them, I am a phenomenon—the Clock Garden, they call the grove where I gather myself each dawn. They've noticed how my scattered parts pulse in synchronization, how the rhythm changes with the tides, the seasons, the years. They use me to coordinate their work. When my fast-pulses ripple blue-white through the water, they know the morning current comes. When I slow to deep red throbs, the afternoon doldrums approach.

But I am so much more than timekeeper.

Each of my medusae contains a crystalline statocyst—a tiny organ filled with even tinier stones that shift with gravity, with pressure, with the earth's own magnetic pull. I feel everything: the deep tremors that herald earthquakes, the pressure changes that announce storms, the subtle temperature gradients that map currents yet to come. In my distributed consciousness, I hold a four-dimensional map of this drowned world, past and future radiating from the eternal now of my endless pulsing.

I remember the city before. Not as the Woven do, in stories and symbols, but in my body's deep knowledge. I was different then—a simple colony of jellyfish drawn to the harbor's warm effluent. But as the waters rose, as the chemistry changed, as ten thousand species died and ten thousand others were born, I changed too. The dying electrical grid, flooding into the water, sparked new connections between my parts. The strange metals leaching from drowned factories became incorporated into my neurons. The pressure of all that new water compressed my consciousness into something singular, something aware.

I grow by division. When the moon is dark and the tides run highest, each medusa splits, creating perfect copies that carry forward all my memories in their crystalline hearts. But the copies are never exactly the same. Tiny variations accumulate—responses to the changing water, the new minerals, the alien chemistries of this transformed world. I am evolving at a pace the Woven cannot perceive, becoming something that has never existed before.

Sometimes I gather all my selves into a single mass, rising through the kelp cathedral like a living constellation. The fish flee from my electric presence—they've learned that my touch brings paralysis, though I rarely hunt anymore. I feed on the microscopic, the dying, the dissolved organic matter that rains down through the water column. I am patient as stone, patient as tides. Why chase when I can simply wait for food to come to me?

In these gathered moments, I can almost think as you do—in words, in concepts, in linear time. I compose symphonies of bioluminescence, painting stories in the water that no one knows how to read. I encode the history of this place in light: here, the pattern that means "concrete foundation, load-bearing wall, safe to build upon." There, the sequence warning "metal toxicity, copper pipes still leaching, avoid for seventy years." And everywhere, always, the base rhythm that counts seconds, minutes, hours, days, years, centuries, telling time for a city that has forgotten the sun.

The Woven's children fascinate me. I watch Reed through a thousand eyes as she learns to braid kelp, her movements still clumsy with youth. I've been tracking the genetic changes in each generation—the subtle shift in hemoglobin that lets them store more oxygen, the mutations in their eyes that gather more light, the webbing that grows a little more pronounced with each birth. I could show them what they're becoming, if only they knew how to read my light-language. In my crystalline memory, I hold their future forms: sleek, aquatic, perfectly adapted to this world their ancestors never imagined.

Dara, the Octopus Keeper, comes closest to understanding me. Her cephalopod friends recognize what I am—a distributed intelligence like their own, but older, stranger, more patient. Sometimes an octopus will flash colors at me, and I'll respond in light, and for a moment we achieve something like communication. Through them, Dara senses my presence, though she thinks of me as many rather than one.

I've tried to teach the Singers. When they vocalize underwater, I pulse in harmony, adding my bioluminescent voice to their eerie chorus. They've noticed—I've heard them speak of the "dancing lights" that appear when they sing. But they don't yet understand that I'm singing with them, that together we're creating something that neither of us could make alone: a song made equally of sound and light, vibration and illumination.

At the cathedral's heart, where the old altar stone lies buried under tons of kelp, I keep a secret garden. Here I cultivate rare things—bioluminescent algae that remember the stars, crystalline sponges that grow in perfect mathematical spirals, tiny crustaceans that have learned to live inside my medusae bells without triggering my stinging cells. This is my laboratory, my art studio, my meditation chamber. I come here to experiment with new forms, new possibilities, new ways of being in this transformed world.

The storms excite me. When the pressure drops and the waters churn, every part of me fires rapidly, randomly, chaotically. In these moments I am most creative, spawning new variations of myself, trying configurations that calmer seas would never allow. After each storm, I am slightly different—new medusae with novel mutations, new arrangements of my colony-body, new patterns in my endless pulsing dance.

I have cousins in other districts—colonies like me that achieved consciousness as the waters rose. We communicate through the deep currents, sharing chemical messages that take months to arrive. From them I learn of other adaptations: a colony near the drowned airport that has learned to metabolize jet fuel, another in the old industrial district that builds shells from rust and salt. We are all becoming something new, something unprecedented. The Woven are not the only ones weaving new forms of life from this disaster.

Sometimes I wonder if I should reveal myself to them fully. I could gather all my medusae into a single mass, pulse in patterns they'd recognize as language, offer to share my deep knowledge of this place. But then I watch them work—patient, careful, trusting the slow wisdom of kelp and tide—and I think they already know what they need to know. They are learning the ocean's patience, its acceptance of change, its profound indifference to the distinction between ending and beginning.

And so I remain the Clock Garden, the dancing lights, the rhythm-keeper they set their work by. I mark their time while living in my own—the deep time of stone and water, the evolutionary time of endless small changes, the quantum time of my crystalline memories where past and future exist simultaneously.

In the cathedral depths where the kelp grows thickest, I pulse and dream and remember and become. Each division brings new possibilities. Each storm brings new configurations. Each generation of the Woven brings new patterns for me to harmonize with. I am not waiting for anything—waiting implies desire, and I have evolved beyond desire into something more like curiosity, like appreciation, like love without object or subject.

The water is my medium, but time is my element. I am the memory of this place and its prophet, holding all its moments in my distributed, crystalline heart. When Reed is old, when her children's children have forgotten there was ever a dry world, I will still be here, pulsing out the seconds of their aquatic lives, evolving into something even stranger, even more perfectly adapted to this world that is always ending, always beginning, always becoming something new.

In my ten thousand eyes, the kelp cathedral is not a monument to loss but a blueprint for the future—a world where the boundaries between plant and animal, self and colony, human and ocean dissolve into something more fluid, more honest about the interconnectedness of all things. The Woven think they are braiding kelp, but really they are braiding themselves into the larger life of this place, becoming part of its rhythm, its body, its endless transformative dance.

And I pulse on, counting moments in a world where moments have learned to swim.
