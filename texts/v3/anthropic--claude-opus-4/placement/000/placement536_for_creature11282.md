I find myself as one of the rim-tenders, though I was not born to it.

My bones still remember lowland weight. When I first climbed the stair — was it seven seasons ago? ten? — my skeleton was dense with the expectations of river valleys, built for pushing through crowds and shouldering loads. Now I move differently. The mountain has been teaching me its mathematics: how to place a foot so that no grain of stone loosens, how to breathe so thin air becomes enough, how to be small without diminishing.

I came as a trader, carrying bronze needles and salt. I meant to stay three days. On the second morning, Aiha-Who-Tends-the-Morning-Rim took me to her edge to show me where the fog-comb trees grew thickest. The sun was not yet up. The birds had not yet begun. In that peculiar silence before the world starts, I watched her move along the rim — not walking exactly, more like flowing, her feet reading the stone like a language.

"Your people hurry," she said, not unkindly. It wasn't a question.

I started to explain about markets, about seasons, about the goods that spoil if you don't—

She made a small sound, almost a laugh. Then she sat. Just sat. On the very edge, where the stub drops away into nothing, she folded herself down and became part of the stone. I stood there, clutching my trader's bundle, suddenly aware of how I jutted up from the mountain like a thing that didn't belong.

After a while — I don't know how long — I sat too.

The birds came with the sun, screaming in waves across the cliff-face. Thousands of them, pale and long-winged, their voices layering into something that wasn't quite music and wasn't quite speech but was absolutely a kind of knowledge. They came from somewhere far below and went to somewhere far beyond, and the mountain was just a moment in their journey, and we were just a smaller moment within that moment.

I don't remember deciding to stay. Decisions, I've learned, are a lowland concept. Here, things become.

Now I tend the Western Rim with Aiha's daughter, who was born the winter after I arrived. My traders' hands have lengthened, the fingers learning the particular grip needed to reach the death-shelves where we gather the bones. I know which beetles to watch for — they arrive exactly two days after a body is placed, and their presence means the bones are ready. I know the seventeen different warning calls of the cliff-dwelling birds and what each one means about the weather. I know how to read the color of distant dust to tell whether the lowland roads are passable.

My clay cup — the one I was given that first exhausted evening — broke last season. I drank from it for nine years, and then one morning it simply came apart in my hands, very gently, as if it had decided it was finished. The pot-maker nodded when I brought her the pieces. "It was an old cup," she said, grinding something in her mortar that might have been clay and might have been bone. "Three generations, maybe four."

She made me a new one. It took a full turning of the moon — not because the making is slow, but because everything here happens when it happens. The clay had to be sung to. The grinding had to be done when the wind was right. The firing had to wait for a day when no birds flew (rare, but they happen). When she finally put it in my hands, still warm from the kiln, she said, "Your grandfather is in this one."

I must have looked confused.

"Not your blood grandfather. Your stub grandfather. The one who tended the rim before Aiha's mother's mother. He's good clay now."

I drink from dead rim-tenders. This should disturb me — would have disturbed me, in my lowland life. But the disturbance belongs to a person who moved at a different speed. Now I understand: I am drinking from the future too. Someday I will be clay, and someone not yet born will drink water from my bones and carry on the tending.

The young traders who climb the stair sometimes ask me for news of the lowlands. I try to remember what news meant when I lived below. Wars? Famines? New kings? But those things seem like the scatter of pebbles now, temporary disturbances in a pattern too large to matter. Instead I tell them about the beetles, about the colors of dust, about how the birds' flight patterns are shifting slightly southward — have been shifting, generation by generation, for as long as the rim-tenders have been watching.

"But what about the bronze shortage?" one asked recently, breathless from the climb, still moving at lowland speed. "The northern mines have closed. The price of needles..."

I waited until she had finished, then poured water from my bone-clay cup into hers. "Drink first," I said. "The mountain is patient."

She left three days later, her water jars refilled, her bronze traded for the things the stub makes: knotted cord, ground pigments, the small perfect pots that wealthy lowlanders prize without understanding what they're made of. But I saw her hesitate at the top of the stair, looking back at the dim lanes, the needle-thatch, the constant gentle argument of daily life. The hesitation means something. Not everyone hesitates.

Maybe she'll come back. Maybe the mountain will teach her its mathematics too. Or maybe she'll return to her rivers and crowds, moving at the speed of markets, and remember us only as a strange interruption — those small people with their pale eyes who live too high and move too slow and drink from their dead.

Both are fine. The mountain accepts both.

This morning I found a new beetle, one I've never seen before. Metallic green, the size of my thumb, walking very slowly along the rim exactly where the sun strikes first. I watched it for the time it takes fog to lift — another measurement I've learned here. It paused at the edge, antennae testing the updraft, then spread wings I hadn't known it had and dropped into the bright air.

I'll tell the pot-maker. She'll want to know. Changes in the beetle population mean something about the clay, though I haven't learned what yet. There's time. I'm not going anywhere.

The mountain is patient, and I am learning to be the mountain.
