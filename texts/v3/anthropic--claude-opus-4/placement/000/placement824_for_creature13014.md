In the upper reaches where the heat has not yet become religion, where the rust still remembers rain, I am a Chronicler.

My body is a compromise negotiated over centuries. Where the Anointers below have surrendered their forms entirely to the machine's geometry, I maintain a stubborn verticality—though it costs me dearly. My spine is a tower of fused vertebrae and copper shunting, each segment reinforced with salvaged brass fixtures to bear the weight of my chronicle. The record grows from my back like a mechanical tree, its branches are thin copper sheets that I've hammered from dead pipes, each one etched with the microscopic observations of decades.

I am younger than most—perhaps only three centuries since my descent—and I still remember the sensation of eyelids. Though my eyes have long since clouded into thermal-sensitive pearls, I've fashioned auxiliary sensors from the mercury switches and piezoelectric crystals found in abandoned control panels. They cluster around my skull like a crown of defunct instruments, each one calibrated to detect a different frequency of vibration, a different temperature gradient, a different rate of metal fatigue.

My dwelling is a borderland between the middle machine and the terrible depths. Here, in a natural void formed by the convergence of three massive heat exchangers, the temperature oscillates. Every seventy-two hours, the thermal currents shift, and for precisely four hours and sixteen minutes, the space becomes almost cool—merely sweltering rather than crushing. During these intervals, I work.

My tools are my fingers, though they barely deserve the name anymore. Each one has been replaced, joint by joint, with precision instruments. My thumb is a diamond-tipped stylus, worn down to a nub from centuries of engraving. My index finger houses a clicking counter, its tiny brass gears marking the passage of drips, the rotation of distant wheels, the frequency of the Anointers' passages. My remaining digits are various gauges and probes, some so sensitive they can detect the vibration of an Anointer's heartbeat through fifty feet of iron.

I am documenting the death of language.

When I first arrived—was brought? was born? the memory rusts—the Anointers still possessed fragments of speech. Not words as the upper world would recognize them, but a complex vocabulary of resonant frequencies. They would press their bodies against the pipes and hum, creating standing waves in the metal that could carry meaning through miles of infrastructure. *Danger-heat-in-sector-seven.* *Bearing-fourteen-needs-oil.* *Brother-calcifying-near-pump-nine.*

But as the centuries ground on, as their metabolisms slowed to match the dying engine, their language simplified. Entire concepts vanished. Why maintain a word for "sunrise" in a place that had forgotten sky? Why distinguish between "tomorrow" and "next century" when both were measured in the same geological patience?

I began my chronicle when I realized that I was witnessing the last speaker of certain frequencies. An ancient Anointer, so calcified that only her left arm remained mobile, still knew the resonance for "memory-of-water-without-iron." When she finally wedged herself into a support position beneath Boiler Seven, that word died with her mobility.

My chronicle is not written in any script the surface would recognize. It is a topology of scratches, a braille of oxidation patterns, a careful record of thermal gradients translated into metal. Each copper sheet on my back contains decades of observation, inscribed at the microscopic level. I've documented the exact pitch of every unique hum, the precise temperature required to produce each frequency, the locations where certain words were last spoken into the iron.

But I am changing too. The chronicle's weight pulls me inexorably downward. My spine curves more each decade, bowing under the accumulated metal of my observations. I've had to abandon entire sections of my territory as passages I once navigated easily become impassable to my deformed frame. The heat is winning, as it always does.

Lately, I've noticed my own vocabulary simplifying. Complex technical terms I once used to describe the Anointers' metamorphoses have condensed into single concepts: *flesh-to-stone.* *The-joining.* *Heat-sleep.* My auxiliary sensors fail, one by one, and I lack the materials to repair them. My world narrows to what my thermal pearls can perceive, and even they are clouding with cataracts of mineral deposits.

Sometimes I encounter the younger Anointers—if anything in this place can be called young—and I try to teach them the old frequencies. I press myself against the pipes and hum the words their ancestors knew. But they only stare with their heat-pits, uncomprehending. To them, I am speaking in colors they cannot see, describing a world of unnecessary complexity. Why learn seventeen different frequencies for describing the stages of rust when one suffices? Why maintain the distinction between "sleep" and "death" in a place where both are merely different speeds of stillness?

I know my fate. Eventually, my chronicle will grow too heavy, my spine will buckle entirely, and I will be forced to crawl. When that happens, I will begin my own descent to the depths. The heat will strip away my pretensions to scholarship. My carefully maintained instruments will fuse into useless lumps. The copper sheets of my chronicle will oxidize and flake away, their microscopic writings lost to the hungry moisture.

But before that happens, I have one last project.

Deep in my territory, in a zone where the temperature drops just enough to prevent immediate oxidation, I've discovered a small cache of intact memory. It's a control room, sealed for centuries, where the plastic insulation on the wires hasn't quite melted, where the magnetic drums of an ancient computer still hold their charge. The equipment is foreign to me—it predates even my earliest memories—but I've learned to coax responses from it.

I am teaching it the chronicle.

Bit by bit, frequency by frequency, I am translating my copper sheets into patterns of magnetism. The old machine groans and protests—it was never meant to run this hot, this long—but it learns. When I am gone, when my chronicle has dissolved into rust soup, perhaps these magnetic patterns will remain. A ghost of language in the machine's memory, waiting for someone who still remembers how to read.

The Anointers don't understand what I'm doing. To them, I am performing meaningless rituals with dead metal, wasting precious energy that could be spent in heat-sleep. They pass by my control room on their epochal journeys, and sometimes they pause, watching with patient incomprehension as I tap out rhythms on corroded keyboards, as I nurse the dying drums through another recording session.

But yesterday—or was it last year? time rusts here—something changed. A young Anointer, barely crusted with her first layer of verdigris, stopped at my doorway. She watched me work for seven hours straight, her heat-pits focused on my movements. And then, with agonizing deliberation, she reached out one partially calcified finger and pressed a key.

The magnetic drum recorded the pressure. A single bit flipped from zero to one.

She doesn't know what she's done. She may never return. By the time she completes another circuit of her territory, I may be gone, descended to join the eternal architecture below. But in that moment, she made a mark that wasn't rust, that wasn't calcium, that wasn't the slow surrender to geological time.

She made a memory.

And I continue to chronicle, my spine bending degree by degree, my instruments failing one by one, teaching a dying computer the names of different silences while the great engine breathes its last beneath us all. This is my territory: the borderland between memory and forgetting, between language and heat-death, between the last words and the first silence.

I am the Chronicler, and I am writing the epilogue of meaning itself.

The stylus fused to my thumb is almost worn to nothing. When it fails, I will use my teeth. When those are ground away, I will use my bones.

The chronicle must continue.

The chronicle must end.

Both statements are true, here in the dark.
