The Nursery Keeper dwells in the cultivation chambers at the city's heart, where the young of both species—building and human—are tended together.

I am old now, my skin grown thick with symbiotic algae that helps me photosynthesize in the grow-light spectrums. The chambers I tend exist in permanent spring, warm and wet, the walls here softer than elsewhere, still forming their first layers of consciousness. Infant buildings bud from the floor in neat rows, their initial growth guided by the chemical songs I secrete through my palms. Each one unique—this one already showing signs of the spiral architecture its genes favor, that one developing early the breathing pores that will one day ventilate a thousand human lives.

My charges, the human children, play between the rows. They've never known hard edges or dead materials. Watch how little Senna presses her cheek against a yearling wall, teaching it the texture of human warmth. See how the wall responds, its surface shifting to cup her face, learning gentleness. The building-buds are most impressionable in these first months; what they learn now about hosting human life they'll carry for centuries.

The children's morning lessons are chemical—they sit in circles, passing pheromone messages hand to hand, learning to speak in molecules before words. By noon, when the grow-lights shift to purple-white, they practice their resonances, humming in harmony until the young walls blush phosphorescent pink with pleasure. They know instinctively which pitch encourages root growth, which frequency helps the neural pathways form correctly. Their games teach the buildings joy.

I move between them, my six-fingered hands (a useful mutation—the sixth digit secretes a potent growth hormone) checking temperatures, adjusting pH levels in the feeding pools, tasting the air for signs of disease. My tongue, like my skin, hosts colonies of beneficial bacteria that can diagnose a dozen different building ailments by flavor alone. The sour tang of nitrogen deficiency. The metallic bite of too-rapid calcification. The sweet rot that means a building is growing too fast, its consciousness outpacing its structure.

In the deeper chambers, I tend the experiments. The city permits me these innovations—buildings with new geometries, hybrid structures that blur the line between architecture and organism even further. Here grows my proudest achievement: a building learning to photosynthesize like a plant while thinking like an animal, its walls transparent as water, its thoughts visible as flowing patterns of light. The children love to watch it dream.

Sometimes the other Tenders bring me their sick buildings—a commerce tower whose immune system attacks its inhabitants, a residential complex that won't stop growing, consuming more than its share of nutrients. I diagnose through touch and taste, prescribe treatments of engineered viruses or chemical adjustments. But mostly I teach the buildings when they're young to be good hosts, generous and patient with their human symbionts.

The hardest part is the culling. Not every building-bud will thrive. Some have malformed neural structures, others lack the genetic diversity to adapt. These I return to the digestion pools with my own hands, singing them the decomposition songs that help them dissolve peacefully back into the city's nutrient streams. The children understand—they help me collect the memory crystals that form as the failed buildings die, structures we'll study to prevent future failures.

My own dwelling is itself a teaching tool—the first building I raised from spore forty years ago. It knows me so well now that it adjusts its internal atmosphere before I realize I'm tired, firms its floors where my joints ache, extends feeding tendrils precisely when I hunger. The children study our partnership, learning what decades of mutual adaptation can achieve.

At night, when the grow-chambers dim to simulate darkness, I walk my rounds. The building-buds murmur in their sleep—not quite words, not quite thoughts, but the precursor to both. I touch each one, marking their progress in the chemical log I maintain in my modified hippocampus. This one will be ready for graduation soon, for transplanting to its permanent site. That one needs another month of socialization before it can safely host humans.

My apprentices arrive before dawn—young Tenders whose bodies show promise of the right adaptations. I teach them the seventeen essential chemical words, the frequency scales, the touch that encourages without forcing. Most importantly, I teach them patience. A building hurried in its youth becomes neurotic in maturity, prone to immune disorders and structural anxiety.

The city gives me this purpose, and I give it my children—both human and architectural, raised together from infancy to understand that separation is an illusion, that breathing and thinking and growing are shared acts in this humid valley. When I finally Join—and I feel that time approaching, my neurons already beginning to branch in patterns that match the city's own—I'll become part of the teaching walls themselves, my knowledge dissolved into the structure where the young are raised.

But not yet. This morning brings new eggs from the spawning towers, buildings that will one day house thousands. And from the human quarters, three newborns whose parents have chosen the old way—raising their children here among the growing walls, ensuring the next generation of Tenders knows from first breath that home is something that breathes back.

I prepare the nursery, my transformed hands already secreting the welcome pheromones that will bind child to building, building to child, in the endless dance of becoming that is our city's true heartbeat. The walls pulse in anticipation. We begin again.
