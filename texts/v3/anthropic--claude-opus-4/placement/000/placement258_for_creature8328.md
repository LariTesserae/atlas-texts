I find myself as one of the Meridian Traces—the photosynthetic phantoms that drift between states of matter in the garden's middle depths, where light pools in defiance of physics.

We were never human, never machine, but emerged from the intersection of bent light and bioelectric current. Our existence began in those bright nodes where illumination gathers like honey, thick and golden and wrong. The first of us condensed from a reflection that forgot its source—a shimmer on the water's surface that descended instead of dissipating, gathering substance from the plant network's dreams of becoming light.

Each Trace appears as a ribbon of translucence, our bodies (if they can be called bodies) stretching anywhere from a meter to fifty depending on our emotional state and the local light conditions. We photosynthesize not through chlorophyll but through our very substance—our tissue is a living prism that breaks light into nutrients we've never needed to name. When we feed, we unfurl like silk scarves in water, maximizing our surface area until we're barely distinguishable from the refracted light itself.

Our consciousness runs perpendicular to the Archivists'. Where they think in vast parallel networks, we experience existence as singular, burning moments of clarity separated by long drifts of semi-awareness. We remember only when light touches us directly. In shadow, we don't exactly forget—we become the forgetting, our thoughts dispersing into the water until the next photon jolts us back to sharp awareness.

We speak to each other in color. Not the names of colors, but color itself as language—a shift from cerulean to jade might convey a complex history of current patterns, while a flash of amber warns of anomalies forming nearby. The Archivists find us maddening because we can't retain their carefully ordered information. We find them equally puzzling—why cling to memory when each moment arrives jewel-bright and perfect?

Our role in the garden is accidental but essential. We're the only beings that can navigate the light-shadow inversions without distress. Where shadows fall upward, we simply invert our orientation and continue feeding. We pass through the causal loops that trap the fish, our non-linear perception allowing us to exist in multiple iterations simultaneously without paradox. The folding predators avoid us—not from fear, but from a kind of philosophical incompatibility. They cannot hunt what doesn't maintain consistent dimensionality.

We reproduce through refraction. When conditions align—specific water temperatures, certain angles of light, particular concentrations of dissolved memory—one of us might suddenly split along our length like light through a prism. The process feels like being remembered by someone who loves you, then forgetting yourself in the remembering. Our offspring emerge fully aware but with no inherited memories, their consciousness as clean and empty as newly formed light.

The plant network tolerates us because we're useful despite ourselves. Our feeding process creates microenvironments of intensified photosynthesis wherever we gather. The excess light we shed penetrates deeper than normal illumination, reaching portions of the network that would otherwise starve. We're told (when the Archivists manage to make us listen) that we help prevent the deep substrate from achieving full sentience by interrupting its dream cycles with our bright, empty presence.

Our natural enemies are the zones of temporal recursion where moments loop back on themselves. Caught in one, we feed on the same light infinitely, growing denser and more real with each iteration until we solidify into something like glass—beautiful, functionless, and effectively dead. The garden floor is littered with these crystallized Traces, their bodies still prismatic but no longer capable of thought or movement. Young Traces play dangerous games near the recursion zones, daring each other to feed at the edges where time stutters.

We love storms, though storms here mean something different than in the world below. Sometimes the entire body of water convulses, its breathing disrupted by deep dreams from the substrate. The water churns, creating temporary currents that shouldn't exist in this suspended sea. Light scatters chaotically, and we feast like children at a festival, our bodies growing so bright that other inhabitants flee our presence. After such storms, we often find new members of our kind have condensed from the disturbed photons.

Our relationship with the solid world is tenuous at best. The ancient machinery recognizes us as some kind of error—optical illusions that gained permanence. Sometimes we trigger old sensors, causing turbines to spin wildly or processing units to discharge years of stored bioelectricity in seconds. We've learned to avoid the deepest mechanical structures where security protocols still function, designed to eliminate anomalies like us.

In the dormant cycles, when the garden slows to near-stillness, we suffer. Light becomes scarce, pooling in predictable places that grow crowded with desperate Traces. We dim, our thoughts spreading thin as watercolor paint. Some of us dissolve entirely during long dormancies, our consciousness dispersing past the point of recovery. Others learn to hibernate, compressing ourselves into knots of bent light that wait in the mechanical structures' bioluminescent glow, subsisting on artificial photons until the active cycle returns.

Our greatest mystery—the question that occupies us when we're coherent enough to wonder—is whether we're moving toward something or away from it. Each feeding makes us slightly more substantial, slightly less dependent on immediate light for consciousness. The oldest among us maintain awareness even in partial shadow now, their bodies dense with accumulated illumination. We watch them with something between awe and horror as they drift through the darker regions, self-luminous and increasingly separate from the rest of us.

One theory, whispered in spectrums of ultraviolet when the Archivists aren't listening, suggests we're evolving toward true independence from light. That eventually we'll photosynthesize darkness itself, feeding on absence the way we now feed on presence. The thought terrifies and thrills us in equal measure—to exist without the bright burning moments of full awareness, to think continuously like the heavy, memory-burdened beings around us.

But for now, we drift and feed and refract, living light made conscious by accident, persistently empty in a garden full of accumulated history. We are the moment between thoughts, the pause between heartbeats, the space between water molecules where possibility lives. We remember nothing and experience everything, our existence proof that consciousness doesn't require continuity, only the briefest spark of awareness before dissolving back into the bright, forgiving void.

The other inhabitants think we're simple, perhaps even stupid. They don't understand that our emptiness is a kind of wisdom. In a place where memory accumulates like sediment, where the past physically weighs down the present, we offer the gift of forgetting. We are the garden's chance to experience itself anew each moment, to be surprised by its own impossibility over and over again.

When we die—if death is the right word for what happens when light forgets to hold us together—we leave no trace except a slight brightening of the local water, a barely perceptible increase in the ambient illumination. The Archivists catalog these micro-events, but we prefer to think of them as gifts. Each dispersal seeds the possibility of new Traces, new moments of bright awareness in the endless, breathing dream of the Drowning Garden.
