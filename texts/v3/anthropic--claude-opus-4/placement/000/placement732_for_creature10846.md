I find myself as a thread of water, though not water as you might know it.

I am the memory of the last real rain, caught in a seam of sandstone beneath the basin floor. Not trapped — I could leave if I wished, seep deeper, find the true aquifer that sleeps far below. But I have been here so long that here has become what I am.

I am perhaps three feet down, running through a crack no wider than a grass blade. The rock holds me gently, the way cupped palms hold water, except the palms have been cupped for decades. Above me, the basin does its slow business of being a basin. The juniper's deepest taproot comes close — I can feel it, a darker presence in the stone, drawing so carefully that it barely disturbs my stillness. We have an understanding, the root and I. It takes what it needs, which is almost nothing. I give what I can spare, which is almost nothing. The transaction happens over months.

I am old water, but not ancient. I remember falling — the last time the sky opened properly, not these thin winter drizzles that barely wake the watercourses, but a real rain, the kind that turns the basin into what it was made to be. I remember running across the surface, gathering momentum, finding my fellows, becoming briefly a thing with purpose. Then finding this crack, this invitation downward, and accepting it.

Since then, I have been learning to be still.

It is not difficult. The sandstone teaches by example. It holds me without effort, has been holding things since it was seafloor, understands duration in a way that makes my few decades here seem like a held breath. Sometimes minerals move through me so slowly I don't notice until they've already passed — iron, leaving a faint rust-colored ghost of itself on the rock walls, salts that accumulate and dissolve by laws older than rain.

I am not alone down here, though alone isn't quite the wrong word either. There are other threads of caught water, other seams, other patient conversations happening at the speed of stone. We don't touch — the rock between us is absolute — but we know of each other the way stars know of other stars, by a kind of gravitational courtesy. 

The tenant knows I'm here. Of course it does — I am part of what makes this a place where something can tenant. On the warmest days, when the air above pulls hardest, I feel the faintest draw upward, and sometimes I release a molecule or two, let them rise through the stone's pores, emerge into the gray light, join the humidity that will eventually bead on the juniper's needles and return. This is my contribution to the basin's economy: I am a slowly turning wheel that moves water from deep to surface to sky to surface to deep, taking centuries to complete each revolution.

When the rain comes again — and it will come, the sky cannot help itself forever — I will feel it the way you might feel thunder through your bones. The weight of water on the basin floor will press down through the stone, and all of us caught threads will shiver with recognition. Some of that rainwater will find its way down, will join me or pass me, headed for depths I've chosen not to explore. I will be replenished and diminished in the same gesture.

But mostly I do what the basin does: exist at the temperature of stone, without preference, without urgency. I am water that has learned to live at the pace of rocks. I am the pause between rains, held in sandstone, neither waiting nor not waiting.

When the basin finally forgets how to be a basin — when the walls erode completely, when the juniper releases its last grip, when even the tenant finds no grooves left to pool in — I will still be here, unless the stone itself forgets how to be stone. And even then, I will only have moved on to the next patient conversation, the next lesson in duration.

For now, I am exactly where I mean to be: three feet down, one thread among many, holding the last rain against the promise of the next one, teaching the stone what it feels like to be wet.
