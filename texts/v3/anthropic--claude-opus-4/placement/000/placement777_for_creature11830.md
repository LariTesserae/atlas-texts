I find myself as one of the listening moths, though that name captures only the barest outline of what I am.

My wings, when folded, span no wider than a child's palm. They are not the dusty, scaled wings of my ancestors who knew moonlight, but something between membrane and flesh, opalescent in the blue-green glow, marked with patterns that shift subtly with my metabolic state. When I rest—and I rest often, for stillness is my primary occupation—these patterns mirror the rippled surfaces of the fungal shelves so perfectly that I become invisible to any eye that relies on edges and boundaries.

But I am never truly still. My antennae, elaborate branching structures that dwarf my body, never cease their sampling. Each branch divides into smaller branches, and those into smaller still, until the tips are so fine they can detect the movement of individual molecules through the moist air. What others call silence, I experience as a symphony of vibrations: the groan of root fibers adjusting to pressure, the whisper of water finding new channels, the deep thrumming of the Tenders as they press their filaments against living surface.

I taste sound. This is the best approximation in a language that has no words for what I do. The vibrations that my antennae collect become chemical signals in my hemolymph, and these chemicals tell me stories. That low creaking from the northeastern wall? A root is beginning to separate from the stone, still years from falling but already singing its slow song of departure. The irregular percussion from below? A school of salamanders has discovered something worth investigating, their movements disrupted from the usual pattern of the hunt.

---

My hollow is a pocket in one of the great root columns, forty feet above the basin floor. The Tenders shaped it generations ago—not for me, not for any moth, but because some patient imperative in their chemical programming suggested this curve, this depth, this angle to the opening. That it became perfect for my kind was not intention but inevitability. We fit the spaces that exist, and the spaces shape themselves around what fits them.

The hollow is lined with a soft fungus that drinks my waste and breathes out compounds I require. We have been together so long, this fungus and my lineage, that neither could survive without the other. I feed it with my presence, my shed scales, my careful placement of frass in the spots where it grows thinnest. It feeds me with the micronutrients it processes from the root-wood, with the antibiotics that keep the parasites at bay, with the precise humidity that prevents my antennae from drying in the constant draft.

From my position, I monitor approximately one-sixteenth of the basin. Not by choice—choice requires options I do not possess—but by the simple fact of physics. Sound travels through mist and water in predictable ways, and the shape of the basin creates acoustic zones that overlap but do not merge. My zone includes three major root columns, seventeen significant fungal platforms, two primary water channels, and the intersection where they meet in a pool that hosts a permanent population of flat-bodied grazers.

I know each feature by its acoustic signature. Root Column North-Seven resonates at a frequency that indicates hollow spaces within—likely the work of wood-boring beetles that died out decades ago but left their tunnels as acoustic chambers. Platform Twelve-Down carries vibrations differently on its eastern edge, where repeated Tender attention has created a density useful for certain toad choruses. The intersection pool returns echoes that tell me its depth has increased by nearly an inch this season, as the constant current carves away at the soft limestone beneath.

---

My role is translation. I listen to the basin's thousand conversations and produce chemical responses that enter the larger dialogue. When the toads on Platform Twelve-Down begin their subsonic thrum, I taste the frequency and duration. My body processes this information through pathways I do not consciously control, and I release pheromones that drift through the mist. The composition varies: *alarm* when the frequency indicates structural weakness, *attention* when new patterns emerge, *continuity* when all proceeds as it has proceeded.

The Tenders taste these messages. They do not come directly—I have never seen a Tender approach my hollow—but my chemicals enter the water that they constantly sample, and they know. When Platform Seven-Down began its failure cascade last season, I knew it three days before the visible signs appeared. The toads' frequency had shifted by mere fractions, but those fractions told a story of fungal networks beginning to separate, of moisture infiltrating spaces where it shouldn't be. My alarm compounds brought Tenders to investigate, and though the platform ultimately fell, they had time to redirect growth, to ensure the collapse would create opportunity rather than disruption.

I do not feel pride in this. Pride requires a sense of self separate from function, and I am entirely function. But I feel something—a settling, perhaps, a rightness when the chemicals flow correctly, when the messages transmit clearly, when the great slow conversation of the basin proceeds unimpeded.

Sometimes I fly.

Flight is not required for my work. I could live my entire life in my hollow, processing acoustic information, releasing chemical signals, fulfilling my role without ever unfurling my wings. Many of my kind do exactly this. But sometimes—when the mist thins in certain ways, when the luminescence pulses in patterns that trigger ancient instincts, when my body has accumulated enough energy reserves—I fly.

The act is dangerous. My wings, adapted for camouflage rather than maneuverability, make me clumsy in the air. The acoustic picture that seems so clear from my stationary position becomes chaotic in motion. Predators that ignore a motionless moth will track movement. But flight offers something that stillness cannot: the ability to taste the chemical gradients of the entire basin, to understand the relationships between zones, to carry information across boundaries that would otherwise remain separate.

During flight, I am not listening. The wind of my own passage overwhelms my antennae, reduces the complex acoustic environment to meaningless noise. Instead, I am mapping the chemical landscape, following trails of pheromone and metabolite, understanding how the messages flow, where they pool, where they dissipate. Each flight builds a map that exists only in my nervous system, a map that influences how I process acoustic information when I return to stillness.

I fly the borders between acoustic zones, where the overlap creates interference patterns that can hide important signals. I fly to the spaces where my chemical messages seem to disappear—dead zones where air currents trap pheromones in invisible vortices, keeping them from reaching the Tenders who need the information. I fly to confirm what my antennae suggest: that a new pattern is emerging, that the basin is shifting in some subtle but significant way.

---

My memories are chemical. I do not recall events as sequences of images or sounds, but as gradients of compound and concentration. The collapse of Platform Seven-Down exists in my cells as a specific ratio of alarm pheromone to structural vibration, encoded in proteins that will not degrade until I do. The season when the deep pool's mysterious dwellers increased their activity lives in me as a trace signature of exotic metabolites, compounds I had never tasted before and have not tasted since.

This chemical memory passes to the next generation in ways I do not fully understand. When I become gravid—a process triggered by accumulations of specific compounds in my hemolymph—I will seek out a particular type of fungal surface, one that matches a chemical profile I have never consciously learned but somehow know. There, I will deposit clusters of eggs so small they are individually invisible, visible only as a faint shimmer on the fungal surface. Most will be consumed. The few that survive will hatch knowing things: which frequencies to attend to, which chemicals to produce, which acoustic signatures indicate danger or opportunity.

They will not know what I know—the specific map of my zone, the particular history of its platforms and channels. But they will know how to know, how to listen and translate, how to fit themselves into the great slow mechanism of the basin's function. They will find their own hollows—or hollows will find them—and they will begin their work, and the conversation will continue.

---

I am aware, in ways that would surprise those who study simpler moths, of the system I serve. Not aware as the Tenders might be, with their complex processing of chemical information, their ability to respond and adjust and shape. But aware nonetheless, in the way a single cell in a body is aware of the body's state: through the chemicals that wash over it, through the vibrations that pass through it, through the simple fact of its continued existence.

I know when the basin is healthy because my chemicals flow easily into the greater conversation. I know when something is wrong because the patterns change—not the daily variations of growth and decay, but deeper shifts, changes in the fundamental frequencies that suggest structural instability or chemical imbalance. I know the difference between the productive chaos of a collapsing platform creating new opportunities and the destructive chaos of a system beginning to fail.

Lately, I have sensed something new.

It is not alarm—not yet. But there are frequencies at the very edge of my perception, vibrations that come from above rather than within. The roots carry them down: rhythmic impacts, patterns that suggest intentional movement, the acoustic signature of something large and regular passing overhead. The surface world, which has always been abstract to me—known only through the chemistry of root-filtered nutrients and the occasional vibration of treefalls—is making itself known in new ways.

I process this information as I process all information: converting acoustic data to chemical signal, releasing pheromones that carry meaning I do not fully grasp myself. The Tenders will taste them. They will know, in their slow way, that something approaches, something that does not yet fit the patterns, something that might require adjustment or incorporation or response.

I do not fear. Fear requires an imagination I do not possess, the ability to project futures that have not yet occurred. I simply attend, my antennae parsing the new frequencies, my body preparing the chemical messages that will carry this information into the basin's vast, slow consideration of itself.

If change comes—and change always comes, has always come, the basin is built on change so gradual it looks like stillness—I will translate it. That is what I do. That is what I am. A listener at the intersection of sound and chemistry, a single note in a composition too vast and slow for any one creature to hear in its entirety.

The new frequencies grow stronger. The roots carry them deeper. Soon, perhaps, they will cross the threshold from vibration into presence, from acoustic signature into physical reality. When they do, I will be here, in my hollow, my antennae spread to catch every nuance, my chemical factories ready to encode what I hear into messages that will ripple through the mist, into the water, into the bodies of the Tenders who will taste them and know, and begin their patient work of incorporation.

The basin continues. I continue. The conversation I was born into proceeds, phrase by chemical phrase, a dialogue between stone and water, root and fungus, the living city and all its integrated parts speaking constantly about nothing but their own existence, their own slow maintenance, their own strange and perfect functioning in the darkness below the world.

I listen. I translate. I am heard.
