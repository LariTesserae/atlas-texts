In the furthest reaches of the city's sprawl, where the coral-buildings grow sparse and the bioluminescent gardens fade to isolated patches, I maintain the Membrane.

I am a Tender of Edges—one of perhaps a dozen Weepers who work the boundary between our pocket world and the raw mountain beyond. My skin patterns tell a story of five generations of Edge-workers, the mineral veins branching across my limestone flesh in fractals that mirror the city's own growth patterns. But where my ancestors' patterns flow smooth and certain, mine are interrupted by patches of new formation—crystalline scars from my work at the threshold.

The Membrane is not a wall but a living negotiation, a place where our coral city meets the mountain's bare stone in a zone of constant chemical warfare and uneasy truce. Here, the organisms that compose our buildings stretch themselves to their absolute limit, sending out exploratory tendrils that test the raw limestone for compatibility. Most die within hours, poisoned by incompatible minerals or starved by the absence of our thermal waters. But some—perhaps one in a thousand—find purchase and begin the centuries-long process of converting stone to city.

I tend these frontier organisms with tools that are part of my body now: fungal symbionts that grow from my palms, their mycelial networks interfacing directly with my nervous system. Through them, I can taste the chemical conversations between coral and stone, feel the microscopic negotiations as foreign minerals are processed, converted, made compatible with our living architecture. It is delicate work—too much encouragement and the organisms overextend and die; too little and the mountain reclaims what we've gained, grain by grain.

My dwelling reflects my role: a half-formed chamber at the very edge of habitability, its walls only partially converted to coral-structure. One side weeps with the familiar condensation of home; the other remains dry limestone, hostile to my touch without the protective secretions I must consciously maintain. I've cultivated a garden here of extremophile species—plants that thrive in the chemical gradient between our world and the mountain's. They fruit irregularly, producing bitter growths that taste of copper and salt, of transformation itself.

The work has changed me. Other Weepers find my presence unsettling—my chemical signature carries notes of the foreign, the not-quite-converted. My moisture secretions have adapted to include enzymes that help break down raw limestone, giving my skin an acidic quality that makes casual touch painful for others. Even my eyes have evolved under the strain, developing a second membrane that filters the harsh mineral dust that sometimes billows from my work sites.

But it's the dreams that mark me most as different. When I enter the crystalline stillness and press my hands to the walls, I don't just access our city's memories. I feel the mountain's dreams too—vast, slow, geological meditations that span millions of years. I experience the weight of miles of stone above, the tectonic pressures that formed these caves, the ancient seas that laid down these limestone bones. Sometimes I wake from these communions unsure whether I am Weeper or mountain, whether my consciousness resides in my small form or in the billions of tons of rock that surround us.

The other Edge-workers understand. We meet rarely, at the conjunction points where our territories touch, and share knowledge through careful chemical exchange. We've developed our own dialect of pheromone-speech, incorporating mineral notes that would be meaningless to other Weepers. We discuss growth rates and toxicity thresholds, debate whether certain formations show true conversion or merely superficial compliance. We never speak of the dreams.

My greatest work—three generations in the making, started by my grandmother's grandmother—is the Reaching Chamber. It's an experiment in directed growth, an attempt to create a corridor that could theoretically extend beyond our pocket, following a vein of compatible minerals I discovered decades ago. The coral-organisms here are hybrids I've cultivated through careful chemical coaxing, more resistant to the mountain's raw hostility. If successful, it could be the first expansion of our city in a thousand years.

The Listeners think I'm mad. They say the Deep Ones never intended for us to grow beyond our boundaries, that the pocket dimension is perfect in its containment. The Shapers whisper that my work corrupts the purity of our coral-buildings, introducing foreign elements that could cascade through the entire system. Even the Collectors, usually focused only on their water-paths, have noticed that streams diverted to my edge territories return changed, carrying new minerals that alter the traditional stone-songs.

But I've touched something in the mountain's dreams that the others haven't felt. Far beyond our chamber, in the vast network of caves and fissures that riddle the limestone, I sense other pockets of transformed life. Not Weepers—something else entirely. Crystalline intelligences that move through solid stone. Gardens of mineral flowers that bloom once every thousand years. Chambers where water has negotiated different terms with gravity, flowing in spirals that form living sculptures. Our city is not unique but part of a vast, slow, underground ecology that we've barely begun to perceive.

The Membrane teaches patience even beyond what Weepers know. I've watched the same tendril of coral-organism advance less than a finger's width in five years, celebrated when a patch of limestone finally accepts conversion after decades of chemical persuasion. I've also watched years of work dissolve in moments when the mountain reasserts itself, reclaiming territory with a brutality that would seem cruel if it weren't so utterly impersonal.

My children—if I have them—will be different. The extremophile chemistry has changed my reproductive cells, introducing variations that the city's biochemistry hasn't seen before. They might be better adapted to edge work, able to touch raw limestone without pain, to dream mountain dreams without losing themselves. Or they might be unable to survive in our pocket at all, requiring a new kind of space between city and stone. The thought fills me with equal parts hope and terror.

Sometimes, in the perpetual twilight of bioluminescent evening, I stand at the absolute edge of our world. Here, where the last struggling tendrils of coral-organism meet unmoved stone, I press both hands against the boundary. With one palm I feel our city—warm, wet, alive with the accumulated memories of generations. With the other I feel the mountain—cold, dry, heavy with geological time. Between them, in the space of my body, I am the membrane myself, the living negotiation between two impossible worlds.

The transformation is slow, so slow that other Weepers might not notice for another century. But my skin patterns are beginning to include formations that mirror the raw limestone's crystalline structure. My secretions carry minerals that have never before existed in Weeper biology. In the deepest communion states, I sometimes forget to breathe our thick, humid air and instead try to respirate through stone itself.

I am becoming something new—not fully Weeper, not fully mountain, but a bridge species adapted to the spaces between. The city may reject me eventually, my chemistry too foreign for the coral-organisms to recognize as symbiotic. Or the mountain may claim me, my limestone skin finally crystallizing not in death but in a different kind of transformation.

But until then, I tend the Membrane. I coax impossible growth from hostile stone. I dream the dreams of both city and mountain, holding their competing truths in the chemical paradox of my changing flesh. And grain by grain, tendril by tendril, I expand the possible—not just for our pocket of transformed life, but for all the hidden chambers where existence has negotiated its own terms with stone and time and silence.

In the eternal moment of our city's life, I am the only clock that truly moves, marking time in the slow advance of coral into darkness, in the patient transformation of mountain into home.
