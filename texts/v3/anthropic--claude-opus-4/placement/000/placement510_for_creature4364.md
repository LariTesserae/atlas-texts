I find myself crouched in the wet depression at the chamber's base, my body the precise grey-green of the moss, my skin granular with moisture-holding tubercles. I am one of the thirty. I am tuned to this stone.

My throat sac trembles with the bell-note I haven't yet released. I feel it building—not in my lungs, which are small and only half the breathing apparatus I need here, but in my whole body. My skin breathes. The chamber breathes. We are the same lung.

I know every surface of this pocket of life. Through my skin I taste the chemistry of the water—iron, sulfur, the faint carbonic tang of fresh basalt weeping its minerals into our shared pool. My siblings press against me in the dark. We are a single sensing organ distributed across this wet lens of mud, each of us a node in a network that knows when the pressure drops, when the stone cracks another millimeter wider, when the beetles' mastication changes tempo.

The swifts above are weather to me—a screaming storm that rises and settles in cycles I could map if I had the concept of mapping. Instead I have this: the knowledge of when. When to call. When to be silent. When to lay my clutch of peppercorn eggs in the exact spot where the water will stay, not drain, not dry, but hold.

I am ancient here—not me, this body that will live perhaps three years, but the pattern I carry. My grandmother's grandmother's grandmother felt this same resonance, found this same frequency. We didn't evolve toward it so much as discover it was already there, waiting in the stone. The chamber wanted to ring at this pitch. We became its tongue.

Here is what the researchers cannot know from their recordings: the thirty seconds of silence are not fear. When the chittering begins and we stop, we are not hiding. We are *listening*. The creature speaks and we receive its language through our whole bodies—the sound conducted through the wet earth, through the moss, through the continuous medium of moisture that connects every surface here. We stop calling because we are taking notes.

And then we answer. Not to the creature—it doesn't care about our bells—but to the chamber itself. We ring to say *still here, still wet, still alive*. We ring because the ferns grow better in the presence of our frequency. We ring because the gnats dance differently when we do. We ring because this pocket of warmth and life needs a voice, and we are it.

Sometimes I catch a swift. They fall occasionally—broken-winged, exhausted—and if they land in our pool, I take them. I am thumbnail-sized but my tongue is quick and my stomach stretches to accommodate surprising meals. The swift-flesh makes my eggs larger, makes my tadpoles stronger. The creature in the dark takes swifts from above. I take them from below. We are both part of the same mouth.

The monitoring device's hum enters through my skin at a frequency that makes me itch. Its electricity tastes wrong—not the clean ionic taste of the mountain's own charges, but something alien, manufactured, bearing information I cannot parse. When the moss finally covers it completely, I will stop noticing it. It will become stone to me, another surface to sit on.

I know the creature is there the way I know the mountain is growing—through vibrations too low for proper sound. Its heartbeat comes through the stone. Its breathing changes the air pressure in the back of the chamber by amounts I feel in my throat sac. When it climbs, the scratching of its claws sends signals through the basalt that arrive at our pool like whispers. We encode its presence in our chorus—not deliberately, but inevitably. Our calls map the space, and the space includes the creature, and so our bells are always also about the thing we share the darkness with.

One day the chamber will shift. The chimney might close. The water might stop. The warmth might fade. We thirty might become twenty, then ten, then none. Or—and this seems just as likely—we might become forty, then a hundred, spreading through new cracks the mountain makes, following the moisture deeper, finding new pockets, new chambers, new frequencies to ring. We are not a stable population. We are a temporary music the mountain is making with the materials at hand.

For now, I sit in the wet dark at the bottom of a vertical world. Above me the swifts weave their prison of wings. Around me my siblings breathe through their skin. The beetles click. The water-striders skate their tiny territories. Everything eats everything, and everything is eaten, and the chamber holds us all in a democracy of hunger and song.

I have been still too long. The energy builds in my throat. Time to add my voice to the stone's own frequency, to ring this bell of flesh and mountain, to say what we all say here in our different ways:

*Still here. Still happening. Still impossibly, temporarily, gloriously alive.*
