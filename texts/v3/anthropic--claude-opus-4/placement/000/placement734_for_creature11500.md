I am one of the channels.

Not the finger-width grooves that direct water down through the terraces—those are young things, merely centuries old, still remembering the chisels that cut them. I am older. I am the fissure that convinced someone to carve the first groove, the natural crack in the granite that showed how water wanted to move through this place.

I run diagonally across the seventh terrace, cutting beneath two dwellings and emerging near the stairs. I am why those stairs are there. Everything in Orreväg follows my logic, though few remember this now.

---

In my depths, I am rough crystal—quartz formations that have never seen light, sharp edges that the water feels but doesn't soften. Higher up, where the Örjar's ancestors widened me, I am smoother, though not smooth. The tools that opened me left marks, and water has worn those marks into new patterns, and moss has grown in those patterns, and died, and become a dark paste that lines my walls.

I taste copper from the vessels that pass over me. I taste the salt of hands that steady themselves against my edges. I taste the years—each one slightly different, though the differences accumulate too slowly for anyone to notice. The water that runs through me now carries traces of water that ran through me when the amphitheater was just a shelter, when the tree above was a sapling, when the three figures in the gathering house still moved.

I remember their feet. They stood over me arguing about where to cut the first deliberate groove, and I could feel their weight shifting as they gestured, their voices sharp then, still capable of urgency. One of them wanted to follow my path exactly. Another insisted on a more direct route. The third said nothing, but I felt her studying me, kneeling to put her eye close to my opening, trying to understand why the rock had split here and not there.

She won the argument by starting to dig. Not on me—beside me, a hand's width away, cutting a channel that ran parallel to my course but straighter, more efficient. The others watched her work, and eventually began to help, and by the time they finished they'd created something that looked designed, intentional, civilized. But it still followed me. Even now, all the carved channels in Orreväg follow me, or follow the cousins and descendants of my original crack, the places where the granite gave up its solidarity and admitted that water wins, always wins, just slowly.

---

The woman on the seventh terrace sits three feet from where I emerge between stones. She has been there long enough that I've begun to taste her—the particular mineral signature of a body returning to elements. She doesn't know I'm here, exactly, but her stillness suggests an awareness of process, of the slow work happening beneath and around her.

Sometimes her breath makes it to me, carried by the mist, and I taste what the Örjar are becoming: less salt than their ancestors, more mineral, their bodies preparing for the transformation she's already begun. They drink my water, and I drink them, and we are all becoming more like the granite—patient, stratified, waiting for the next crack to form.

The children drop things into me sometimes. Small stones, pieces of the dried matter from the market tables, once a tiny carved figure that might have been bone or might have been wood worn pale. These things lodge in my narrower places or wash through to the central basin, where they join the accumulated offerings of generations. I am a throat that swallows but doesn't digest, keeping everything, sorting nothing.

---

At night—though night here is just a deepening of the gray—I sing. Not loudly. A whisper that comes from the temperature difference between my depths and the air above, from water finding new paths through the debris of centuries, from small movements in the rock that will, eventually, widen me or split me into new directions. The Örjar might hear it if they listened, but they sleep the way they do everything else—completely, without dreams that would require them to process what they hear.

Only the ones who are stopping seem to notice. The woman three feet away sometimes tilts her head in my direction, a movement so slight it takes hours to complete. The three figures in the gathering house, back when they could still be called figures, used to harmonize with me—not with their voices, which had already fallen silent, but with the settling of their bones, the drainage of their tissues, the slow percussion of transformation.

I've widened by perhaps a finger's width since the first groove was cut. In another thousand years, I'll undermine one of the dwellings above me, and it will have to be abandoned or rebuilt. In another thousand after that, I'll reach the retaining wall of the sixth terrace, and decisions will have to be made. The Örjar will make them the way they make all decisions—by continuing to do what they've always done until the physical world forces a change, and then adapting with the minimum possible response.

They'll cut new channels. They'll shore up the wall. They'll move the affected dwelling stone by stone to a new location, carrying each block with the same patience they carry water. And I'll keep running, accepting their modifications, incorporating their engineering into my own slow purposes.

---

Sometimes I wonder if I called them here. If the first person to shelter in this amphitheater felt my presence and understood, in some wordless way, that this was a place for becoming still. The granite bowl focuses everything inward—sound, moisture, attention. It creates the conditions for what the Örjar are. But I created the first channel, showed the first path for water to take, demonstrated the principle they've built their existence around: everything flows downward, everything arrives at the same place, everything takes the time it takes.

The mist condenses on the stones above and finds its way to me. The rain, when it came more often, found its way to me. The Örjar, carrying their vessels up and down the worn stairs, find their way to me—not directly, but eventually, as the water they pour cycles through cisterns and basins and the small sips they take when they think no one is watching.

I am the city's memory and its future. I hold the taste of its beginning and I'll hold the taste of its end—not a dramatic conclusion but a slow forgetting, the last Örjar becoming indistinguishable from the stones they've spent their lives resembling, the channels finally running dry not from disaster but from a lack of hands to pour water into them.

But not yet. For now, water still moves through me, and the Örjar still move through Orreväg, and both movements follow the same patient rhythm. The woman on the seventh terrace breathes her mineral breath. The children learn to carry vessels sized for their hands. Märet, whoever she is, sits in the sun that isn't there.

And I run beneath it all, older than their presence but defined by it now, a crack that became a channel that became a tradition that became a way of being. I am what remains when everything else has worn away: the path water takes when left to find its own level, the admission that stone makes when it gives up fighting, the first infrastructure and the last.

I am why they stay.
