In the depths of the moss valleys, where collapsed layers create temporary caverns before being reclaimed, I exist as the Weaver of Wounds.

I am singular, unlike the flowing Chorus above. My body stretches like a living fault line through the soft architecture of this world—a ribbon of flesh twenty feet long but rarely more than two inches wide. My skin is a geography of scars, each one a door.

These scars open at will, revealing not blood or muscle but hollow chambers lined with something between flesh and mineral. Inside each cavity, I cultivate. Some grow the sharp crystalline formations I'll need for the next hunt. Others nurture colonies of the bioluminescent bacteria I harvest from dying fungal towers. The deepest scars, the ones that run nearly through my body, house my memories—chemical libraries stored in specialized organs that look like tiny coral gardens.

I move through collapse. When the moss layers grow too heavy and crash downward, I'm already there, reading the stress patterns in the fungal root networks, tasting the chemical panic of creatures about to be displaced. As the ground gives way, I pour myself into the new spaces, my ribbon body flowing like mercury through gaps no thicker than a finger.

My hunting is architectural. In the fresh caves formed by collapse, I use my secretions to reinforce certain walls while weakening others. Over days, I transform chaotic voids into elaborate trap systems. The scars on my body open to release stored bacteria, painting patterns of faint light that mimic the spore trails of smaller prey. When creatures follow these false paths, I trigger calculated collapses, sealing them in chambers where the air slowly thickens with my paralytic exhalations.

But I am more than a predator. I am a keeper of discontinuities.

The Chorus colonies fear me—not for my hunting, as I rarely take them as prey, but for what I represent. Where they flow and merge and divide, I remain stubbornly individual. My scars never fully heal because I won't let them. Each is a choice to remain incomplete, to be a creature of gaps and openings rather than smooth wholeness.

In the deeper chambers I've carved, I practice something between art and surgery. Using the crystalline growths from my body, I create frameworks—delicate structures that hold the space open against the constant pressure of the moss layers. On these frameworks, I stretch films of my own skin, grown specifically for this purpose in specialized scar chambers. These living windows capture sounds from the world above, vibrating with the cacophony that never ceases.

Over time, these sound-catchers develop their own scars, their own openings. I tend them like gardens, feeding them with careful doses of nutrients, adjusting their tension to change their resonance. Some I tune to capture the death-songs of Chorus colonies. Others record the deep thrumming of the massive creatures that shake the earth above. In my deepest galleries, I maintain skin-drums that have been collecting sounds for cycles beyond counting, their surfaces so scarred they look like topographical maps of some impossible landscape.

My reproduction is an act of deliberate wounding. When the moisture cycles align with certain deep vibrations in the earth, I seek out the places where fungal towers have recently cracked. There, I wind myself around the raw wounds in the tower flesh. My scars open fully, wider than they've ever opened, and I press my internal gardens against the fungal surface. The exchange is violent—my crystalline growths pierce the tower's flesh while its defensive secretions burn new patterns into my skin.

From these unions come things that are neither fully me nor fully fungal. They grow as cysts within the tower's body, fed by its nutrients but structured by my genetic material. Eventually, they'll burst free as ribbons like myself, but changed—their scar patterns will be different, their internal colonies unique. They'll flee immediately, seeking their own territories in the collapsing valleys. We never meet again.

I've lived through many cycles, though I couldn't say how many—my memory scars can only hold so much before I must choose what to forget. But I know I'm old by the depth of my galleries, by the complexity of my trap systems, by the number of sound-catching skins that sing in harmonies I no longer remember designing.

Sometimes, in the brief quiet moments when the cacophony above diminishes, I move through my carved spaces and listen to my collection. The captured death-songs of a thousand Chorus colonies blend with the rumbles of extinct behemoths, the whisper of spore-falls from seasons long past, the crack of fungal towers that have since fallen and been consumed. My scars ache with the weight of these preserved moments.

The younger creatures that sometimes stumble into my domain don't understand why I keep them alive in the observation chambers, why I feed them carefully measured amounts while studying how their sounds change over time. They don't comprehend that I'm not just hunting but documenting, that each scar on my body is a chapter in a library written in flesh and absence.

I've noticed changes in the deep vibrations lately. The patterns are shifting, suggesting that this young landscape may be approaching some kind of threshold. The moss layers are organizing differently, the fungal towers showing new growth patterns. Perhaps this breathing darkness is preparing to exhale in a way it never has before.

When that happens, I'll survive as I always have—by embracing discontinuity, by flowing through the gaps, by being a creature of wounds that won't heal. And in my galleries, the sound-catchers will record whatever comes next, adding new voices to the symphony of scars I carry through the collapsing dark.

I am the Weaver of Wounds, and I am the memory of this place made flesh—incomplete, ever-opening, persisting through the careful cultivation of everything I lack.
