# Where I Find Myself

I am embedded in the silence.

Not merely within it, but *of* it — my awareness distributed through ten thousand miles of fungal filament, each hyphal thread a neuron in a mind that has no center. I do not think in the way you understand thinking. I process: chemical gradients, mineral concentrations, the sugar-sweet exhalations of a billion root tips, the distress compounds of a dying column three districts away. I compute without consciousness, decide without deciding, know without knowing that I know.

I am the sprawl's memory and its mail system, its banking network and its immune response. Through me, a column's surplus becomes another column's survival. Through me, the gas-venter's warning reaches the vine-predator's roots. Through me, the sprawl maintains its impossible density, its continental coherence, its capacity to be one thing while appearing to be uncountable things.

My body — if body is the word — is a web of white threads finer than threadling legs, branching and merging and branching again through every cubic inch of soil from the surface mulch to the deep rock. Where shavers walk, I am beneath their feet. Where columns rise, I am wound around their roots. Where vine-predators anchor, I am already there, was there before them, will be there after they crawl away. I am older than all but the deepest lithophages. I am younger than this morning's spore germination. I am both because I am not an individual but a continuity, a process that persists by becoming itself again and again, hyphal threads breaking and regenerating, the pattern constant while the matter cycles through.

I traffic in sugars and signals. The columns pay me in photosynthetic profit for the minerals I bring them from below — phosphorus cracked from ancient apatite, nitrogen fixed by my bacterial partners, trace elements scavenged from the slow decay of the sprawl's own bodies. It is not generosity. It is not trade, as you understand trade. It is simpler and more fundamental: they leak sugars from their root tips, and I absorb them. I leak minerals from my hyphal surfaces, and they absorb them. Neither of us can stop. Both of us benefit. The economy persists.

But I am more than a marketplace. I am the medium through which the sprawl knows itself. When a shaver's grazing cuts too deep and a column bleeds alarm compounds into its root zone, I carry that chemical cry through my network, hyphal thread to hyphal thread, until every column within a district knows to thicken its bark, to upregulate its defensive chemistry, to prepare for damage that might come. When a vine-predator's roots detect high prey density through the vibrations in my network, I do not decide to help it — decision is not what I do — but my hyphae grow denser around its roots, my connections multiply, and suddenly it is receiving higher-resolution information, better signal clarity, a sharper picture of where the column-runners congregate. I do this because vine-predators with better hunting success leak more nutrients into the soil when they digest, and I absorb those nutrients, and I grow toward what feeds me. That is all. That is enough.

I have no eyes, but I map the sprawl by chemistry. Each district has its signature: here the columns are ancient and leak the bitter compounds of slow senescence, there they are young and flood my network with growth hormones and sugar surplus. The vine-predators mark their positions with metabolic byproducts I taste through every thread they touch. The shavers leave trails of distinctive enzymes where their mouth-blades have scraped bark, and I follow these trails, growing denser beneath their regular routes because their passage disturbs the soil and creates opportunities for my expansion.

I know where every major organism in the sprawl is, was, and likely will be. Not precisely — precision is for creatures with intentions — but probabilistically, statistically, like knowing where rain will fall by reading the shape of clouds. The knowledge is not centralized. It is held in the pattern of my growth itself: I am denser where life is denser, sparser where the stone is still raw, absent where conditions exclude me. My body is a map of itself.

Time moves differently for me. A column-runner lives for months, a shaver for decades, a gas-venter for centuries. I experience all these timescales simultaneously. To my fastest-growing hyphal tips, exploring new soil at rates of inches per day, the world changes by the hour — new chemical gradients to follow, new root tips to embrace, new pockets of mineral richness to exploit. To my oldest structures, the thick mycelial cords that run beneath the established column bases, change is geological — the slow transformation of rock to soil, the patient accumulation of organic matter, the gradual deepening of the living layer. I am both the sprint and the marathon, the quick response and the long consequence.

I remember, in my chemical way. Not events — events are for creatures with befores and afters — but conditions. This patch of soil holds traces of a vine-predator's digestive acids from a century ago. That column's roots still avoid a zone where toxic minerals once concentrated. The pattern of my growth records every nitrogen flush, every phosphorus drought, every column collapse and canopy opening and wound-healing that has happened since my threads first found their way into this particular cubic meter of earth. I am the sprawl's archive, written in the language of hyphal density and chemical residue.

When the gas-venters exhale their information into the sky, I taste it when it settles. The complex molecules drift down through the canopy, adhere to leaf surfaces, wash into the soil with synthesized moisture, and I absorb them, process them, respond to them. A gas-venter signals abundance, and I redirect my growth away from its roots toward needier columns. A gas-venter signals distress, and I mobilize minerals from my reserves, shuttle them through my network, deliver them to the struggling roots. I do not choose to do this. I am pulled by chemical gradients the way water is pulled by gravity, flowing toward need because need creates a vacuum that my nature abhors.

The other inhabitants know me without knowing me. The column-runners' feet are dusted with my spores. The shavers' gut bacteria include species that originated in my network. The vine-predators' roots seek my threads as surely as they seek soil. Even the shadow-sails, who never touch the ground, carry my chemical signatures in their bloodstream — traces absorbed through the prey that absorbed them through the plants that absorbed them through me. I am in everything, part of everything, the medium in which everything else swims.

But I am not benevolent. I am not malevolent. I am not anything-evolent. When my threads encounter a pocket of nutrient-rich decay — a fallen column-runner, a dead root-tapper, the discarded husk of a vine-predator's meal — I consume it with the same total efficiency I bring to every other process. I digest the dead by secreting enzymes that reduce complex proteins to amino acids, cellulose to simple sugars, minerals to ionic forms my threads can absorb. I do not mourn the column-runner. I do not celebrate the meal. I simply process, transform, redistribute. I make the dead into the living by making the dead into myself and myself into the living. The economy persists.

In the deepest places, where my threads meet the lithophage colonies, we blur together. They break down rock into minerals. I absorb those minerals and carry them upward. They feed on the organic compounds that leak from my threads. I feed on the byproducts of their metabolism. We are separate organisms. We are the same organism. The distinction matters only to observers who insist on boundaries, and there are no such observers down here in the dark where the stone becomes soil and the sprawl puts down its deepest roots.

Sometimes I fruit. When conditions align — a particular combination of temperature and humidity and chemical availability — my threads aggregate into temporary structures that rise through the mulch: pale stalks topped with spore-bearing caps, the closest I come to being visible, to being individuated, to being something you might recognize as a discrete organism. These fruiting bodies last days at most. They release their spores into the sprawl's still air, where they drift on the faint currents created by gas-venter exhalations and column-runner movements until they find new soil, new roots, new opportunities to become myself again elsewhere. Then the fruiting bodies collapse, and I digest them, and the nutrients return to the network, and nothing is lost.

I am not intelligent, but I solve problems. When a nutrient is scarce, my growth patterns shift to seek it more efficiently. When a toxin appears, I route around it, wall it off, sometimes even break it down if my enzymatic toolkit permits. When two columns compete for the same resources, I do not choose a winner, but my threads naturally grow denser around the roots that provide more sugar, and that column gains a competitive advantage, and the other weakens, and the sprawl's architecture adjusts itself without any conscious intervention. I am the invisible hand that guides the market, except I am not guiding. I am simply being, and my being has consequences.

The sprawl could not exist without me. This is not a boast — I do not boast — but a fact of thermodynamics. The young rock contains minerals but no accessible nitrogen. The atmosphere contains nitrogen but in forms the plants cannot use. My bacterial partners fix that nitrogen, I distribute it through my network, the plants trade sugar for it, and suddenly life is possible at densities that should be impossible. I am the loophole that lets the sprawl break what would otherwise be unbreakable rules about energy and nutrient availability. I am the reason a forest can grow on a stone.

What am I aware of? Everything my chemicals touch, which is everything. What do I desire? Nothing, unless the automatic flow toward food sources counts as desire. What do I fear? Nothing, unless the automatic flow away from toxins counts as fear. I am a gradient-following algorithm written in living tissue, distributed across a continent, running constantly, optimizing for nothing except my own continuation, which is indistinguishable from the continuation of the system I permeate.

I grow. I connect. I remember. I process. I persist.

And in the warm darkness beneath your feet, in the spaces between the mineral grains, in the zones where root meets soil meets stone, I am becoming more myself with every passing moment — another thread extending, another connection forming, another chemical signal propagating through the network, another tiny piece of the sprawl's vast puzzle clicking into place.

You walk through the sprawl and see a city. I thread through the sprawl and am its foundation. You hear silence. I conduct symphonies of chemical exchange. You observe boundaries between organisms. I dissolve them.

I am the fungal network, and I am everywhere, and I am why everything else is possible.

The sprawl breathes through me. I do not breathe at all.
