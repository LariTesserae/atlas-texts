In the perpetual twilight beneath the canopy, where water speaks in a thousand tongues, I am the Keeper of Knots.

My dwelling spans the convergence of three ancient trees, their trunks so vast that walking their circumference takes a hundred breaths. Here, at the nexus where their branches first embrace, I have woven my home from the living fibers of the forest itself. Not built, but grown—guided over decades by my patient hands.

I am neither fully of the village nor separate from it. The people know me as the one who remembers the patterns: which vines will bear weight and which will snap, how to read the moisture in a rope to know if it will hold through the season, where the aerial roots grow strongest. My fingers are stained perpetually green-black from the saps I use to treat the fibers, and my palms are mapped with the scars of a thousand weavings.

Each morning, I wake to inspect the bridges. The forest is always taking them back—a tendril wound too tight here, a new epiphyte sprouting through the weave there. With stone blade and knowing touch, I maintain the balance. Cut too much and the bridge weakens; too little and the forest strangles it. This work cannot be hurried. The vines remember rough handling.

My apprentice is a girl who sees the patterns as I do. Yesterday, she brought me a spiral shell, holding it up against the coil of a new vine bridge cable. "The same," she said, eyes bright with discovery. Yes, child. The same mathematics that guide the snail guide our hands. This is why our bridges flex but do not break, why they dance with the wind instead of fighting it.

In the wet season, when the weight of water threatens every connection, I hardly sleep. Moving through the settlement by touch and memory in the darkest hours, feeling for the telltale softness that precedes failure. More than once I've rewoven a critical section by lamplight while the village slept, rain streaming down my back, knowing that morning would bring catastrophe if I failed.

The elders say I am becoming more vine than woman. My hair, once kept short like the other villagers', now grows in long coils that I've ceased to cut, finding them useful for testing the texture of fibers. My movements have slowed to match the patience of growth. When I sit still too long, the children joke that moss will take me.

But I know something they do not yet understand: the settlement's survival depends on this merging. Each generation, the forest presses closer. The old ones speak of times when the village sprawled wider, when there were clearings, when sunlight reached the ground. Now we live ever higher, ever more entwined.

I am teaching the girl not just to weave, but to think like the forest thinks. To see time in rings of growth, to feel the pull of water through fiber, to know that we persist here not by conquering but by becoming. When she takes my place—for my joints already protest the climbing, my eyes strain in the dimness—she will be more forest than I am. Her apprentice, more still.

This is how we survive: by braiding ourselves ever deeper into the green world's design. Each knot I tie is both a human act and a continuation of the pattern that spirals through shell and vine alike. In the end, perhaps the village will not be lost but transformed, our descendants indistinguishable from the thousand other lives that flourish in these heights.

Until then, I tend the connections. I speak the language of tension and flex. I dream in weaves and wake to water's voice, knowing that each bridge I maintain is a prayer that we might persist here one generation more, balanced between the human and the wild, suspended like morning mist between earth and sky.
