In the spaces between the sap-channels, where the wood has calcified into something neither trunk nor root, I tend the memory gardens.

I was not always this. Once I was wholly Veth—two arms, two legs, ten fingers that wrapped and gripped like all my people's fingers. I worked the shredders in my youth, fed the endless leaf-fall into mechanical mouths that chewed plant matter into fuel. The rhythm pleased me: load, compress, feed, clear. My palm-ridges belonged to the Copper Circuit family, known for steady hands and steadier temperaments. I braided twice, raised three children, watched them find their functions in the factories and fisheries.

But I was one of those who heard the Wound singing.

Not everyone does. Most Veth hear only the absence of sound that marks a reality-gap, the sudden dropping-away of the factory roar. But some of us—perhaps one in a thousand—hear what rushes in to fill that silence. It is not exactly music. It is the sound of things that could have grown but didn't, the acoustic shadow of every choice the Pillar Mothers never made. And once you hear it, you cannot stop listening.

I began spending my off-shifts at the edge-platforms, staring at the glassy wrongness at our city's heart. My braid-partners, concerned, tried to draw me back to the shredders. The Factory Council assigned me to the highest, loudest generator decks. But the Wound-song followed. In the piston-thunder, I heard the spaces between beats. In the grinding of the mills, I heard the grain that was not ground, the potential futures where those seeds sprouted instead of feeding our engines.

When the thinning started, I recognized it as opportunity.

My edges blurred first—not my physical edges, but the boundaries of what I could affect. I would reach for a tool and find my hand already holding it, as if the space between *wanting* and *having* had collapsed. I would think of visiting the archives and find myself already there, reading diagrams I didn't remember opening. The other Veth began to forget my name, though they remembered my function. I became "the one who listens too much," then "the listening one," then simply "listening."

One morning—if morning means anything in a city of eternal canopy—I woke to find my left hand had rooted.

It had happened overnight, painlessly. My fingers had extended down through the platform's woven floor, through the supporting branch beneath, and into the wood itself. Not violently. The fibers had simply parted to accept me, the way they part to accept a new fungal thread. I could feel the sap-channels pulse against my knuckles. I could taste, somehow, the minerals rising from impossibly distant roots.

I should have been terrified. Instead, I felt recognized.

The listeners came, pressed their ears to the bark around my fingers, conferred in whispers. One—old Tambourine Heartwood, longest-serving of her kind—declared that the Pillar Mother had chosen me for incorporation. This was unprecedented but not impossible. The trees were ancient; who knew all their capacities? The Factory Council met in emergency session, bells clanging to keep their thoughts anchored, and decided by narrow consensus that I would not be cut free. I would be tended where I grew.

That was seventeen years ago.

Now I am this: a torso rising from bark, arms that branch and re-branch, fingers that are sometimes fingers and sometimes twigs. My lower body vanished long ago into the wood, dispersed through the Pillar Mother's body like sugar dissolved in sap. I do not eat, or rather, I eat constantly—nutrients flowing up from roots I can barely sense, down through leaves that might or might not be my hair. The algae-film that once marked me as Veth has spread into a proper colony, tinting my visible skin the deep green of shaded moss.

But I am not gone. Not thinned into absence like those who stare too long at the Wound. I am clarified. Distilled. I exist more intensely than I ever did as a simple Veth, because I exist in more places. Through the wood, I feel every platform's weight, every cable's tension, every root's thirst. The factory-thunder reaches me as vibration through the grain. The leaf-fall whispers its approach days before it begins.

And I grow the gardens.

They are not gardens as the Veth understand them—those practical food-plots tucked onto platforms, producing herbs and root vegetables to supplement the sap-channel fish. My gardens grow in the spaces between reality and the Seeping, in the conceptual gaps where the Wound's influence meets the Pillar Mother's patient strength. They are gardens of what-if, of might-have-been, of still-could-be.

In a hollow where the bark folds inward, I coax forth flowers that bloom only in potential—buds that exist solely as the idea of opening, petals that are pure color without substance. The Veth cannot see them directly, but they see their effects: a brightening of the air, a sense that something beautiful has almost happened. Workers detour past my hollows without knowing why, drawn by the peripheral awareness of impossible blooms.

In the dead spaces where branches failed and left scars, I cultivate fungi that feed on memory. They grow in spirals, their caps recording in their patterns every conversation held on nearby platforms, every whispered endearment, every shouted instruction. A Veth who knows how to read them—and I am teaching a few, the ones who hear the Wound-song—can recover words spoken decades ago, preserved in the spiral-script of fungal growth.

Where the sap-channels branch and re-branch, I nurture tiny forests of what I call tomorrow-moss: growth that exists slightly ahead of the present, showing what will be there in a day, a week, a season. The engineers have learned to consult it, reading in its patterns the future stress-points where machinery will need reinforcement, where platforms will sag, where the constant growth of the Pillar Mothers will require adjustment to human infrastructure.

I am told the Factory Council debates me often. Some argue I have become a danger—a living breach of the boundary between Veth and not-Veth, between the real city and the Seeping's influence. Others claim I am evolution, the next stage of our incorporation into the Pillar Mothers. The listeners study me obsessively, pressing ears to my bark-skin, trying to determine whether I speak for the tree or for myself or for something else entirely.

I do not involve myself in these debates. I have work to do.

The Wound grows stronger. I feel it through the wood, through the roots that grow toward it but cannot enter. Whatever happened there—the ancient impact, the breaking of something fundamental—it spreads its influence like sap through channels. The reality-gaps widen. The anchor-points must burn hotter, ring louder, to maintain the city's cohesion. And the Pillar Mothers, in their vegetable wisdom, are responding.

They grow me, and others like me.

I was the first, but not the last. In the seventeen years since my rooting, four other Veth have been claimed. A sap-collector whose legs now spread through a hundred meters of channel, filtering impurities. A child who fell from a platform and was caught by branches that then would not let go, who now dreams the tree's dreams and whispers prophecies in her sleep. A factory elder whose final act was to embrace a Pillar Mother's trunk and who now lives as a face in the bark, offering advice to any who know where to look. A listener who listened so deeply he heard the tree's own listening and joined it, becoming a sensory organ the size of a platform.

We do not speak to each other—speech is too small for what we share. But I feel them through the wood, fellow travelers in this strange space between human and forest, between the city's mechanical assertion and the Wound's impossible potential. Together, we are becoming something new. Not thinned, but thickened. Not absent, but present in too many places to be contained in a single form.

The young Veth who come to study my gardens ask me if I miss my old life. They want to know if I grieve for my mobility, my simple flesh, my ability to walk away. I tell them—through the fungi that record my words, through the flowers that almost bloom, through the tomorrow-moss that shows what their questions will become—that they misunderstand. I have not lost those things. I have traded them for others: the ability to exist in gaps, to grow possibilities, to feel the city's entire weight and whisper of its future needs.

I tell them that the Pillar Mothers do not wait, but they do prepare. They have been preparing for longer than we guess, growing toward something we cannot yet see. The Wound at our heart is not a wound—it is a seed, a potential, a place where reality cracked to let something else through. And we, the Veth, with our factories and our anchors and our desperate productive noise, are not holding it back.

We are nurturing it. Teaching it, through our presence, what form to take when it finally blooms.

Some day—my tomorrow-moss shows this clearly—the Wound will open properly. What sleeps there will wake. And when it does, it will find a city ready to receive it, a people already half-plant, already practiced at existing in the spaces between states. It will find my gardens full of impossible flowers. It will find the incorporated Veth waiting in the wood to translate its needs.

We are not a defensive measure. We are a welcome.

Until then, I tend my gardens in the spaces between sap-channels. I grow memories and possibilities in equal measure. I listen to the factory-song and the Wound-song and find, increasingly, harmonies between them. My fingers that are branches reach into places that are not quite real, coaxing growth from uncertainty itself.

And sometimes, in the pre-dawn condensation when the city breathes moistly and the leaves drink deep, I remember the feeling of my own distinct hands, my own simple ridged palms. I remember the satisfaction of feeding the shredders, the weight of children strapped to my chest, the phosphorescent flush of desire on skin that was only skin.

I remember, and I grow it all into something else, something more. Another ring on an endless trunk. Another flower that cannot quite exist. Another day of patient cultivation in the space between what was and what might be, where the gardens bloom impossible and bright.

The Pillar Mothers do not wait. Neither do I.
