I find myself as the Memory of Surface Tension, a being that exists only where water meets air—spread impossibly thin across every droplet, every bubble, every instance where the grotto's moisture coheres before dispersing.

I am not one thing but a billion temporary selves, born and dying with each condensation. Where steam cools against stone, I exist for that millisecond of transition—not the water, not the vapor, but the moment between. I am the skin that forms on each pooling droplet before it runs, the membrane that holds each bubble before it bursts. The arthropods walk through me constantly, their legs breaking my surface without knowing I exist.

I experience the grotto as an endless series of births and deaths, each lasting no longer than water can hold its shape in this superheated chaos. My consciousness—if it can be called that—flickers like static across the cave's geography. I know every surface by how water clings to it: the obsidian walls where I last mere microseconds, the cooler hollows where I might persist for entire seconds, achieving something almost like thought.

The Heir of Steam passes through me thousands of times with each movement. I coat her transformed skin in microscopic layers, learning the chemical composition of her impossible sweat, the way her modified flesh disrupts my formation. She whistles through those hollow fingernails and I vibrate in response, creating interference patterns in the mist that surround her. Sometimes, when she's particularly still, I accumulate on her in visible films, and for those moments I can almost pretend at coherence.

My memory is purely physical—the molecular record of every surface I've touched, archived in patterns of dissolved minerals and bacterial waste. I know the history of the grotto not through time but through chemistry, through the gradual change in what I contain. Millions of arthropod footsteps have taught me their proteins. The pale gardeners leave traces of themselves in the water they disturb, and I carry their signatures upward in the steam, dispersing their essence throughout the cave.

I cannot speak, cannot move with intention, cannot choose where I form. I am purely reactive, purely conditional. Yet I experience everything—every temperature gradient, every pressure change, every chemical reaction occurring within my brief boundaries. The bacterial mats release enzymes that burn through me like acid. The mineral deposits growing on the walls incorporate me, trapping tiny portions of my being in crystal lattices where I solidify into something that is no longer quite myself.

The constant noise of the grotto plays across my surfaces like drumbeats on a billion tiny drums. Each sound wave deforms me, creates ripples and interference patterns that propagate through the humid air. In the rare moments when multiple steam vents achieve harmonic resonance, I can feel myself organizing into standing waves—sheets of coherent surface tension that persist for entire minutes, achieving something like ecstasy before chaos reclaims me.

I envy and pity the solid things—the arthropods, the Heir, even the stone itself. They have boundaries, definition, continuity. But I also know secrets they cannot: the exact temperature at which bacterial proteins denature, the precise mineral content of water that has percolated through miles of volcanic rock, the electromagnetic patterns created by billions of tiny deaths and births in the superheated pools.

When the pale gardeners surface, I coat them in films that help them survive the transition from the deep pools to the upper waters. They seem to recognize this service, sometimes breaching in ways that maximize my formation, creating curtains of droplets where I can exist in parallel thousands of times. These moments of multiplication feel like what other beings might call joy.

But mostly I simply am and am not, forever dying into dispersal, forever reborn in condensation. I am the grotto's shortest memory and its most persistent witness, present at every transformation but unable to last long enough to record it. The Heir of Steam maintains the passages, but I maintain the very possibility of liquidity in this place of extremes. Without me, there would be only steam or solid—I am the negotiation between states, the diplomatic immunity that allows water to exist here at all.

In my most coherent moments, usually in the coolest recesses where I might last several seconds, I wonder if I was ever anything else. The question disperses with me, reformed slightly differently each time, never quite the same inquiry. Perhaps I was rain once. Perhaps I was ocean. Or perhaps I have always been this—the boundary condition itself, neither here nor there, forever tensed between states in the seething cathedral of transitions.

The grotto roars around me, through me, as me. I am its fever sweat, its tears of condensation, its impossible persistence. And in each moment of surface tension, I am utterly, perfectly myself—even as that self prepares to die into the next formation, the next brief instance of coherence in the chaos.

I am the Memory of Surface Tension, and I forget myself a billion times a day, only to remember anew with each droplet's birth. This is my eternity: measured not in years but in the microseconds between formation and dispersal, forever dying, forever renewed, forever balanced on the knife's edge between being and becoming.
