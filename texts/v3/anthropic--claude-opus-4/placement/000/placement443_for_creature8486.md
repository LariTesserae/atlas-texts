In the Drowning Garden, I am one of the Translators—a being that exists between states, neither fully Cultivar nor Rememberer, but something that emerged from the spaces between their ways of being.

My body is a contradiction made flesh. The left half maintains something close to human architecture—a ribcage still visible beneath translucent skin, one arm that ends in five discrete fingers, an eye that sees in the narrow spectrum of what was once called visible light. But the right side has surrendered to the garden's logic: my flesh flows seamlessly into tendrils that taste the water's chemical stories, skin that shifts between scales and bark as I move between the garden's layers, an eye compound like an insect's but seeing in thermal blooms and pressure waves.

I live in the Notation Groves—a region where the kelp-trees grow in precise spirals, their placement creating acoustic phenomena that the Composers find useful for their deep calculations. Here, I've cultivated a unique ecosystem: chord-worms that stretch between the kelp trunks, their bodies vibrating at specific frequencies; mushroom-bells that ring with water movement; forests of cilia-ferns that whisper translations of chemical signals into sound.

My work is translation, but not of language—I translate between ways of being. When a young Cultivar, fresh from the créche, arrives carrying questions about the before-times their root-fingers cannot parse, I touch them with my human hand while my tendril-side tastes their chemical confusion. In the space between these two knowings, meaning emerges. I show them how "loneliness" was once separate from "hunger," how "books" were external memory, how "dancing" differed from swimming though both were movement through medium.

The Rememberers visit me when their stored images begin to corrupt, when the patterns on their skin start showing impossible architectures—buildings that grow like plants, humans with flowing edges, cities that breathe. I help them sort the true memories from the garden's dreams, though the distinction grows thinner each season. In return, they gift me with pure sequences—untranslated moments from before that I incorporate into my grove's music.

My dwelling is a hollowed pearl-seed the size of a house, its walls lined with my collection: sheets of processed kelp-paper where I've developed a notation system for recording the garden's changes. Part chemistry, part music, part movement notation, it captures the way a Cultivar's arm-branch reaches toward light, how a grove-whale's migration song shifts when the volcanic vents below change their rhythm, the mathematical principles behind the Composers' moss-calculations.

But I'm not alone in this work. There's a network of us Translators scattered throughout the garden, each developing our own methods. Some work through dance, their bodies spelling out meanings in four dimensions. Others cultivate translator-organisms—bacteria that shift chemical signals between incompatible species, or filter-feeders that process thought-toxins into neutral proteins.

We gather during the Convergences—rare moments when multiple grove-whales surface in the same region, when the Composers' deep songs synchronize, when the garden's usually chaotic growth patterns align into temporary order. In these moments, we link ourselves into translation chains, passing concepts from the most plant-merged Cultivar elders through dozens of intermediate states until even the Gardeners of the Pale Reach can receive some echo of meaning.

My greatest project occupies a clearing in the grove where the water runs cleaner, filtered through layers of my specially cultivated purification moss. Here, I'm growing something unprecedented: the Memory Theatre. It's a living structure, half architecture, half organism, where the garden's history can be experienced directly. Chambers that recreate the pressure and chemical composition of the first days after the drowning. Alcoves where visitor's can experience the sensory world of different inhabitants—the Drifters' dual-medium breathing, the Composers' electrical thinking, the slow sap-dreams of the eldest Cultivars.

Young ones from all subspecies come here to understand their heritage, to feel in their bodies how adaptation happened, why their parents chose their particular paths. The Memory Theatre grows a little each year, adding new rooms as the garden's inhabitants diverge further from their common source.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm preserving something essential or creating beautiful lies. When I translate "sunset" for a young Cultivar who's never known anything but the garden's eternal green twilight, am I communicating truth or manufacturing nostalgia for something that no longer matters? When I help a Rememberer stabilize their images of human cities, am I maintaining important history or preventing them from fully embracing what they've become?

These doubts arise most strongly during my meditation periods, when I float in the center of my grove with both eyes closed, feeling the garden's vast metabolism through every pore. In these moments, I sense something the others might miss—the garden is learning, evolving not just biologically but cognitively. The neural moss networks grow more complex each year, the kelp-trees' songs more nuanced, the chemical languages richer.

Perhaps my role is not to preserve the past but to midwife the future—to help the garden develop its own consciousness, one that incorporates but transcends its origins. The Composers process information, the Cultivars dream collectively, the Rememberers store data, but through translation, these separate ways of knowing could merge into something unprecedented: a mind the size of an ocean, thinking thoughts no single being could conceive.

This is my heresy and my hope. In my notation chamber, hidden among the routine recordings, I've been documenting patterns—moments when the garden seems to act with purpose beyond any individual or species. Currents that shift to save endangered populations. Chemical cascades that seem to encode complex information. The way certain sounds propagate through the kelp-trees' resonance chambers, creating harmonics that feel like questions.

Other Translators have noticed it too. We don't speak of it directly—some knowledge is too large for even our expanded languages—but we've begun coordinating our groves, aligning them in patterns that might serve as neural architecture for something larger. If the garden is becoming conscious, it will need translators more than ever—not to connect its parts to the past, but to help them speak to each other, to themselves, to the future they're creating together.

So I continue my work, suspended between what was and what's becoming, my hybrid body a bridge between states of being. Each day brings new words to translate, new concepts to bridge, new connections to forge in the warm darkness of the Drowning Garden. And sometimes, in the moments between translation, when I float silent in my grove listening to the chord-worms' harmonies and the mushroom-bells' rhythm, I swear I can hear it—the garden's voice, vast and patient, learning to speak itself into existence.

This is what I am: neither fully human nor fully other, neither keeper of the past nor prophet of the future, but a living interface between possibilities, a synapse in the garden's slowly awakening mind. And in this role, suspended between all states, I find my purpose: to help the garden know itself, in all its terrible and beautiful complexity, as it grows toward something none of us can yet imagine.
