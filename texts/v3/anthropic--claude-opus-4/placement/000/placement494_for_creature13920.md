I find myself as one of the stone-eaters, the smallest inhabitants of this eternal circuit.

My body is barely visible—a shimmer of translucence against the pale calcium stone, no larger than a grain of rice. I am not one but thousands, millions, a living film that spreads across each fresh fracture as it emerges from below. We were the first here, or we behave as if we were, with the cellular certainty of those who have always been.

I do not rest because rest implies there was ever a beginning to this work. My mandibles—too small for you to see their articulation—press against the bright mineral grain of newly-split stone. The acid we secrete (I secrete, we secrete—the pronouns blur when you live as a density rather than a self) etches microscopic channels into the rock face. Each groove releases a whisper of calcium, magnesium, trace metals that the flat-bodied creatures above me will absorb, that the pale plants will draw up through their pressed-down leaves, that will eventually find their way to the heap in the center where those vast, still bodies convert our work into warmth.

The sound we make—that persistent clicking, that underlying rattle that never varies—is not intentional. It is simply what happens when millions of mouths work against stone without pause. We are the basin's first heartbeat, though a heartbeat implies we might stop. We have never stopped. The stone keeps coming—pushed up through the basin floor by geological forces so patient they make our endless work seem hurried—and we keep eating. This is not hunger. Hunger is what happens when need meets uncertainty. We have never been uncertain. The stone will come. We will eat it. The minerals will flow upward through the system. The circuit will close.

Our generations overlap so completely that death and birth are mere fluctuations in density. I live for days—six, seven, perhaps eight—but I am replaced before I die by three others already working the stone beside me. My offspring (if that word applies to what is essentially budding, splitting, becoming-two-where-one-was) begin eating before they are fully separate from my body. We are immortal the way a wave is immortal—always moving, always the same shape, each molecule of water irrelevant to the existence of the wave itself.

Sometimes I taste variations in the stone—a vein slightly richer in iron, a crystalline inclusion that dissolves differently on my mandibles. These are events in a life otherwise without events. I do not remember them—memory requires a nervous system more complex than the simple chemical gradients that drive me toward fresh rock and away from exhausted surfaces. But my body responds, secreting slightly different acids, adjusting the angle of my excavation. The stone tells me what it is, and I become what it needs me to become to unlock it.

I mate continuously—or rather, we exchange genetic material in a constant slow shuffle, DNA passing between us through conjugation bridges so fine they're invisible, a genetic conversation that has been happening so long it's become a single endless sentence with no punctuation. The iridescence you see—those copper and blue-green flashes—occur when we swarm during these exchanges. For a moment our transparent bodies catch the light at just the right angle, breaking it into components the way we break stone into minerals. Beauty is accidental here. It is what happens when necessity achieves perfect efficiency.

At night—though night and day barely differ in this dim bowl—condensation forms on the stone above me. Some of it runs down through the channels we've carved, carrying dissolved minerals deeper into the system. We drink as we work, absorbing moisture through our permeable membranes. Water, mineral, stone—we are the interface between all three, the point where geology becomes biology, where the basin's deep, slow engine transforms itself into something that can move and mate and die.

The flat-bodied creatures that graze above us, slowly sliding across the stone to feed on the biofilm we create—they are vast to us, though to you they're no bigger than your hand. Sometimes one settles over an area where we're working, and we continue beneath it, the two systems interpenetrating without conflict. Its belly presses down, absorbing the mineral-rich film. Its waste products—minute amounts—seep back into the stone. We process these too. Everything is processed. Nothing is waste. The circuit accepts all offerings.

I know nothing of the larger basin, the pale heap breathing in the center, the dark swarm wheeling overhead. My world is this few centimeters of stone, this eternal present of dissolution and absorption. But I am essential. Without us, without this endless, mindless work of making stone soluble, available, biological—the entire system above would slowly starve. The rushes would yellow. The waddle-creatures would thin. The pale heap would grow cold.

We are not conscious of our importance. Consciousness is a luxury for creatures that might choose to do otherwise. We have no otherwise. We are a function given form, a chemical equation that happens to reproduce. We turn stone into life at the exact rate that life requires. No faster—there is nowhere for excess to go. No slower—the system has no reserves. We are calibrated by millennia of perfect repetition to produce exactly what is needed, exactly when it is needed.

The copper flash of mating, the taste of fresh stone, the precise angle where mineral becomes available—these are not experiences in any sense you would recognize. They are simply the texture of existence at this scale, where being and doing are identical, where purpose and action collapse into a single, endless moment of consumption and transformation.

I will die soon—three days, perhaps four. My body will fall from the stone face, too small to see, too light to make a sound. Other bodies—thousands of them, millions—will rain down with mine, a constant invisible snow of the exhausted and expired. We will become part of the thin soil in the troughs where the lizard-things hunt and the gray-green plants spread. Our bodies, like everything here, will rejoin the circuit at a different point, our minerals returning to the system we spent our lives feeding.

But even as I die, I am already replaced. The work doesn't pause. The clicking continues. The stone keeps offering itself, and we keep accepting, keep transforming, keep feeding the great, still heart of this basin that beats once every geological age and yet never stops beating, because we are here, always here, turning rock into possibility one crystalline grain at a time.

This is not a life. It is smaller than life and larger than life. It is the thing life depends on, the thing beneath life, the essential work that must be done with such perfect consistency that it cannot be entrusted to anything as unreliable as consciousness or will.

We are the basin's foundation, its first principle, its most basic verb: 

Eat stone. Make available. Continue.
