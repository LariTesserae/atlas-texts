In the spaces where water refuses to linger, where even the morning dew slides away before it can pool, I make my dwelling. The Dry Nodes, they call these anomalies—patches of the great trees that repel moisture through some ancient malfunction in their engineering. While others see blight, I recognize sanctuary.

I am a Desiccant.

My skin has grown thin as paper over the decades, translucent enough that you can watch my sluggish pulse traverse the visible veins. I wrap myself in layers of scavenged membrane—the shed skins of the giant moths, the discarded husks of seed pods, anything that whispers when I move. The sound is necessary; it warns the water-bearers that I approach, gives them time to cover their vessels. My presence alone can draw moisture from the air, create tiny deserts in the perpetual humidity of the megalopolis.

I was not born this way. As a child, I trained among the Cultivators, learned their songs of growth and encouragement. But something in my touch always sang differently—where I meant to coax bloom, I created preservation. Where others encouraged sap to flow, I crystallized it. The teaching groves began to develop permanent specimens wherever I practiced, branches that never rotted, leaves that held their shape and color years after falling.

The guild didn't know what to do with me. Neither did I, until I found the first Dry Node.

It was deep, where two ancient roots had grown together imperfectly, creating a void that the city's water systems couldn't penetrate. Inside, I discovered treasures others had forgotten—scrolls of pressed bark that would have dissolved anywhere else, metal tools from the time before the full integration, the desiccated remains of fruits that still held their essential structure, waiting.

I learned to read the dry spaces like the Rootworkers read the wet. Each Node has its own personality, its own reason for rejecting water. Some are scars from ancient injuries, where the tree sealed off infection so completely that it forgot how to reopen. Others are deliberate modifications, created by inhabitants long dead for purposes I slowly uncover. A few are simply mistakes, biological coding errors that produced pockets of permanent drought.

My chambers occupy seventeen Nodes across three district levels, connected by routes only I can navigate safely—passages where the humidity drops so low that normal inhabitants would begin bleeding from their mucous membranes within minutes. I've lined these corridors with my collections: preserved specimens from every era of the city's growth, each one perfectly maintained in the absent air.

Here, a flower from the last natural forest, its petals still holding the color of a sun that shone before the canopy closed completely. There, a sample of the original fungal network before it merged with the communication systems, its filaments frozen in the moment of reaching. I have water samples too—ironic, perhaps—crystallized into complex mineral patterns that tell the history of every drought, every flood, every chemical shift in the megalopolis's vast circulatory system.

The other inhabitants need me, though they rarely admit it. When the Plague Shepherds require samples of historical diseases for their antibody gardens, they come to me—I have colonies preserved from plagues that burned out centuries ago. When the Memory Keepers lose a genetic line and need to restart from archived material, they brave my passages, their skin cracking despite the protective salves. When couples birth a child who shows signs of moisture intolerance, they bring the infant to me, hoping I can teach them to survive in a city built on water.

I age differently here. Without constant humidity, my cells have forgotten certain deaths, remembered others. I am older than I should be, younger than I appear. My memories have crystallized like everything else I touch—perfectly preserved but static, requiring effort to reconstitute into active thought.

But I've discovered something in the deepest Nodes, in chambers so divorced from the water cycle that even the air stands still. The city's oldest programming lives here, encoded in crystallized sap nodes that predate the biological revolution. I've learned to read them through touch—my desiccated fingers the only appendages that won't disturb their ionic balance.

They tell a different story than the one the Memory Keepers cultivate. They speak of intention, of design, of a transformation that was neither accident nor evolution but something more deliberate. They mention other cities, other experiments, a network that once spanned continents before the isolation began.

I prepare my apprentice now—a young Feral whose body began rejecting water after a childhood illness. She doesn't yet understand why I insist she memorize the locations of every Node, why she must learn to read crystallized sap, why I make her practice moving through humidity when every instinct screams against it. She thinks I'm preserving the past.

She's wrong. I'm preserving the future.

The Dry Nodes are expanding. Slowly, imperceptibly to those who measure time in mere decades, but undeniably. The city's water systems are failing in ways too subtle for the Conduit Runners to detect, creating new pockets of desiccation. The trees themselves are preparing for something, sealing off sections, creating isolated chambers that can survive independently.

And in the deepest Node, in air so dry it hasn't moved in a millennium, something is growing. A seed of impossible age, waiting for conditions that haven't existed since before the canopy closed. It speaks to me in chemical whispers only my altered senses can detect, tells me of its requirements, its patience, its promise.

When the time comes—not in my lifetime, perhaps not in my apprentice's, but soon as the trees measure time—the Dry Nodes will serve their purpose. They will preserve what needs preserving when the great change comes again. And someone who understands both drought and plenty, death and life, will need to guide what emerges.

Until then, I catalog. I preserve. I remember. I teach a girl to love the whisper of desiccation, to find beauty in the persistent death that enables eternal life. I move through my domains of crystallized time, tending gardens that never grow, never change, never forget.

I am the keeper of the spaces between breaths, the guardian of pauses in the megalopolis's endless exhalation. In a city that lives on water, I am the necessity of drought, the promise that even this too shall pass, and something will remain to remember what was and prepare for what must come.

The water-bearers hurry past my markers, their vessels covered, their children hushed. They whisper my name like a ward against drying, against ending, against the change they know lurks in the spaces where water refuses to flow.

But I know what they cannot: in a city built on cycles, I am not the ending.

I am the seed.
