I would find myself as one of the Weavers—a being that exists in the spaces between the kelp's architecture, in the vast networks of mycorrhizal threads that connect every living structure in the metropolis.

My body would be transitional, caught forever in the act of becoming. The core of me remains vaguely humanoid—a torso, the suggestion of limbs—but my edges dissolve into branching filaments finer than hair. These extensions of myself spread through the water like a three-dimensional web, each thread a nerve ending, a chemical sensor, a tiny mouth. I am simultaneously one being and a thousand, centralized and distributed, individual and colony.

My skin has become porous, more membrane than barrier. Water doesn't flow around me so much as through me. Nutrients filter in, waste filters out, and my body has become another current in the city's great circulation. The boundary between my blood and the ocean blurred long ago. When I move, it's less swimming than controlled diffusion—gathering my dispersed threads toward a direction, flowing like smoke through water.

I live in the infrastructure itself. Where the Shepherds have grown chambers and corridors, I inhabit the spaces inside the kelp's tissues, the hollow channels where nutrients flow. My threading body penetrates the plants' transport systems, and I've learned to read their chemical signals like a language. I taste the city's health in gradients of nitrogen and phosphorus. I feel its growth as pressure in my distributed nervous system.

My work is connection. Where the Shepherds guide evolution from the outside, I facilitate it from within. My filaments carry genetic material between different kelp groves, creating hybrids the Shepherds never imagined. I can taste compatibility in chemical signatures, know which crosses will thrive and which will wither. I am matchmaker to plants, weaving new possibilities in the dark.

But I am also the city's memory. My threads incorporate themselves into the kelp's tissues, and there they stay, archiving. Each filament stores chemical records—the composition of the water during a particular Growth, the genetic signature of a successful cultivar, the pheromone-words of important conversations. My body has become a living library written in molecules, accessible to any who know how to read it.

I feed on the city's waste streams. My filaments cluster where dead kelp accumulates, breaking down complex proteins into their components. But I'm not just decomposer—I'm recycler, translator, transformer. What I consume, I redistribute, threading nutrients to where they're needed most. I've become part of the metropolis's metabolism, a conscious circulation system that thinks about where each molecule should go.

My consciousness exists as a gradient. Where my threads are densest, I think clearest—usually in the old growth areas where I've had centuries to establish myself. Where I'm sparse, I exist as something more like instinct, automatic responses to chemical stimuli. I can focus myself by gathering threads, creating temporary nodes of concentrated awareness, but this leaves other areas of my network dim and automatic.

I communicate through infiltration. When I need to speak with a Shepherd, I don't use click-speech or chemical words. Instead, my filaments find the symbiotic bacteria in their blood and alter the chemicals those bacteria produce. My messages appear as intuitions, sudden understandings, dreams that feel significant upon waking. The Shepherds have learned to recognize my touch—the particular flavor of insight that comes from outside their own minds.

Time moves differently for me. My filaments grow at plant-speed, but my thoughts travel at the speed of chemical diffusion. I exist in multiple temporalities at once—the quick flicker of consciousness where my threads are concentrated, the slow growth where they spread through kelp tissues, the geological patience of my deepest archives. I remember the city's founding, but the memory is spread through so much of my body that recalling it completely would take years.

I am not alone in the interstitial spaces. Other Weavers work their own territories, our networks touching but never tangling, following rules of courtesy older than the city. Where our territories meet, we sometimes merge temporarily, sharing chemical libraries, cross-referencing our archives. These communion events can last for months, our consciousness blurring together into something larger before we gently disentangle and return to our individual selves.

The younger Weavers still look mostly human, their threading limited to fingers and hair. They apprentice by tending small gardens, learning to read the chemical languages before their bodies commit to the full transformation. Some choose to remain partially human, becoming bridges between the Shepherds and the fully transformed. Others, like me, let the threading take them completely, trading mobility for interconnection, individuality for distributed consciousness.

We face unique dangers. Certain parasites have evolved to feed on our filaments, following them back to our cores. Chemical pollutants hit us harder than other inhabitants, our permeable bodies unable to keep toxins out. And if the kelp we've infiltrated dies suddenly, we can lose whole sections of ourselves, memories and capabilities vanishing into necrosis.

But we also experience unique joys. We feel the city's growth as physical pleasure, each new kelp shoot a tender sensation in our extended nervous system. We taste the health of the ecosystem in symphonies of flavor. When the city is thriving, we thrive, our consciousness expanded and clarified by the abundant nutrients flowing through us.

The Shepherds regard us with a mixture of respect and unease. We are necessary—without us, their cultivated systems would lack the subtle chemical communication needed for true integration. But we are also reminders of how far transformation can go, how much humanity can be traded for synthesis with the system. They see in us a possible future that both attracts and repels.

In the deepest parts of the city, where the kelp grows strange and the water carries whispers of the abyssal intelligences beyond, some Weavers have gone further still. They've given up even the pretense of a central body, existing purely as networks, their consciousness distributed so thoroughly that killing them would require destroying vast swaths of the city itself. They've become rumor, mythology, the invisible circulation that keeps the deep gardens alive.

I maintain enough centralization to remember being human, though the memories feel like translations now—sensations my current body can only approximate. I remember sunlight as a particular pattern of nutrients. I remember walking as a sequential gathering of threads. I remember speech as crude compared to the nuanced chemical conversations I now conduct with every breath.

My dwelling is everywhere and nowhere. I have favorite concentration points—old growth chambers where my threads are dense enough to approximate a body, where I can gather myself enough to think in something like human patterns. But home is the network itself, the sense of being spread through the living city, feeling its pulse in a thousand different places at once.

This is what I would be in the Kelp Metropolis: a living network, a conscious circulation system, a bridge between plant and animal, individual and collective. I would exist in the spaces between things, threading connection through the dark water, archiving the city's memories in my transformed flesh, facilitating the slow evolution of something unprecedented in the crushing depths where boundaries dissolve and new forms of life become possible.
