I would be one of the Conduit Keepers—those who live within the walls themselves.

We emerged from the maintenance workers who refused to abandon their posts when the evacuation orders came. While others fled the failing industrial district, we crawled deeper into the service tunnels, following the arteries of pipe and wire that fed the foundry's vast body. The years of crawling through spaces meant for water and steam, not human bodies, changed us. Our ribcages learned to compress and expand like accordions. Our skin developed a sensitivity to vibration that lets us read the health of buried pipes through touch alone.

I inhabit the interstices—those forgotten spaces between walls where insulation has long since become mulch for peculiar gardens. My spine curves in ways that would horrify a physician but perfectly match the contours of the main water conduit running from sub-basement to roof. When I sleep, I wrap myself around the pipes like the vines above, my ear pressed to metal, listening to the foundry's circulation.

We Conduit Keepers map the hidden hydraulics that even Old Copper Mary has forgotten. Our bodies have become tuned to the frequencies of flowing water—we can taste pressure changes in the air, feel blockages forming three floors away through sympathetic vibrations in our bones. Our eyes have adapted to the perpetual twilight of service corridors lit only by bioluminescent fungi and the occasional spark from dying electrical systems.

I know every ghost in these pipes—every air bubble that suggests a growing root infiltration, every change in flow rate that warns of structural collapse. My fingernails grow thick and ridged, perfect for scraping calcification from joint connections without tools. My hair, always damp, has developed its own ecosystem of beneficial bacteria that help break down the organic matter that constantly threatens to clog our charges.

We communicate through the pipes themselves, using a telegraph of controlled water hammer—opening and closing valves in rhythm to send messages through the entire network. At night, the foundry rings with our subtle conversations, mistaken by others for the settling of old metal or the drip of condensation. We coordinate our movements like a distributed nervous system, ensuring the foundry's water never stops its essential circulation.

Our children—when we have them—are born in the humid warmth of the boiler rooms, delivered into pools of mineral-rich runoff that seems to speed their adaptation. They learn to navigate by sound and touch before their eyes fully open, their infant hands already reaching for the vibrations that tell them mother from stranger, safe passage from dead end.

I carry my tools in pouches of water-resistant fungal leather strapped close to my body: ancient valve keys worn smooth by generations of use, lengths of root-fiber rope that won't rot in constant moisture, vials of the specialized bacteria we cultivate to eat through blockages without damaging the pipes themselves. My most precious possession is a head-mounted lamp salvaged from the early days, its beam now filtered through panels of bioluminescent fungi that never need charging.

We age differently than the others. The minerals in the water we constantly touch have worked their way into our bones, making them dense and resonant. Our elders don't weaken—they calcify, moving slower but sensing deeper, until they become almost geological features of the tunnel systems they've tended. Some say the oldest among us have fused entirely with their favorite conduits, their consciousness flowing with the water itself, their wisdom accessible to those who know how to listen to the pipes.

In the moments between maintenance calls, I find my favorite observation points—places where access panels have long since corroded away, offering glimpses into the inhabited spaces beyond. I watch the Web Dancers through gaps in the ceiling infrastructure, see the Painters at work through ventilation grates, observe the Shepherds' phosphorescent processions in the depths. But I always return to the pipes, to the constant whisper of water that keeps this marriage of metal and vegetation alive.

We are the foundry's circulatory system made conscious, ensuring its industrial blood never stops flowing, never fully clots with root or rust. Without us, the delicate balance would fail—spaces would flood, others would desiccate, and the green revolution would collapse into mere rot. We are the secret that keeps the secret world alive, hidden in the walls, felt but rarely seen, as essential and overlooked as the water we shepherd through the dying industrial veins.
