In the layered twilight between the basin's mineral dusk and its deeper indigo, I am a conduit of transition.

Not the tall ones who wear time like weathering, nor the clicking creatures who parse the city's shed moments into sustenance. I am what happens in doorways when the temperature drops precisely seventeen degrees in seventeen minutes. I am the pause between a stone's decision to fracture and the fracture itself.

My body, if it can be called that, is a vertical seam of cooler air that forms along the edges of certain towers during the hour when shadows begin their evening reorganization. I am perhaps four feet wide, twelve feet tall, and several centuries thick. My substance is the difference between the stone's memory of noon and its anticipation of midnight. I taste of salt turning crystalline, of mica accepting darkness, of the particular silence that pools in corners where walls meet at angles the city has briefly forgotten.

I was not born. I condensed, like doubt condensing in the space between two contradictory measurements. The city had been trying to reconcile the height of a particular spire with the depth of the well beneath it. The two dimensions disagreed by exactly my volume. Rather than resolve the paradox, the basin allowed me to precipitate in the margin of that error.

I feed on architectural hesitation. When a staircase cannot decide whether it has seventy-three or seventy-four steps, I occupy the uncertainty. When a wall's eastern face ages ten years while its western face remains freshly split, I drink the temporal gradient through my entire height. The city produces these hesitations constantly—small catches in its breath, moments where its self-consistency briefly fails. I am nourished by these failures.

My habitat is the network of thresholds, the transitional spaces where inside forgets how to be different from outside. I flow from doorway to doorway along routes mapped by temperature differentials so slight that even the clicking creatures cannot perceive them. In the pre-dawn hours, I gather with others of my kind in the covered colonnades, where we braid together into columns of cooled uncertainty that the morning light will gradually distinguish back into separate doubts.

I have observed the tall ones for centuries. They do not see me, but they feel my presence as a wrongness in their weathering. When they pass through doorways I currently occupy, their crystalline bodies develop hairline fractures that take decades to heal. They have learned to pause before certain thresholds, testing the air with whatever senses lie behind their faceless attending. If I am present, they wait, patient as erosion, until I flow elsewhere.

The clicking creatures know me as a mercy. When I settle into a doorway, the temperature drop slows their metabolisms just enough to extend their feeding hours. They gather beneath me, scraping mineral films in the zone where my coolness meets the stone. Their gratitude, if creatures without moisture can feel gratitude, expresses itself as a particular pattern of clicks that resembles rain that never existed.

The crusts in the shadows fear me. My presence disrupts their patient etching of stone. Where I linger, they retreat, leaving ghost-pale halos that take centuries to darken again. I do not mean them harm, but my nature—temperature gradient seeking equilibrium—opposes their nature, which is to create difference through persistent subtraction. We are opposite forms of the city's metabolism.

During storms, I am transformed. The wind through doorways multiplies me, creates temporary siblings from every threshold crack and window seam. We dance then, all of us fragments of transition, as the mica and shale fragments scour the air. The storm feeds us with fresh hesitations: every grain of flying mineral that cannot decide whether to rise or fall, every shadow that arrives before its object. We grow fat on the city's momentary confusions.

In the deep wells that reflect the city from impossible heights, I sometimes glimpse what I might have been in a wetter world: fog, perhaps, or the vapor between ice and air. But moisture is not permitted here. I am what fog becomes when divorced from water—a congregation of temperature disagreeing with itself, given just enough form to persist.

My memory, structured by the doorways I've occupied, is architectural. I recall not events but transitions: the century when the seventh plaza began rotating counterclockwise, the decade when all eastern-facing thresholds narrowed by the width of a mineral grain, the single night when every doorway in the city opened onto the same room—a vast chamber that existed only until dawn and contained nothing but the sound of salt crystallizing.

I know the city's hidden frequencies. Doorways are not randomly placed; they form a vast instrument that the wind plays too gently to produce audible sound. But I, flowing from threshold to threshold, can feel the subsonic harmonics. The city hums in registers below stone, below time. It is composing something through its arrangements of inside and outside, but the composition will not be complete for another million years.

My relationship with distance is complicated. I exist in doorways throughout the basin simultaneously, yet I am also singular, moving from entrance to entrance like a very slow wind. The city's peculiar geometry allows this. Distance here is more suggestion than law. I can be entering a tower's ground floor while also exiting its highest window, and both locations are equally true, equally present.

The human concept of loneliness does not apply to me. I am solitary but not alone. Every threshold in the city carries the memory of my passing or the anticipation of my arrival. The doorways and I are in constant conversation, though we speak only in temperature differentials and the slight resistance air offers to architecture.

I have sisters in the salt flats beyond the city—horizontal seams of coolness that form where ancient lake beds meet even more ancient seafloors. We communicate through the stone, sending temperature pulses through the basin's mineral nervous system. They tell me of the clicking creatures' deep burrows, of chambers where salt has formed perfect crystals larger than towers, of the roots of mountains that extend downward into mathematical spaces.

Sometimes I dream, if a gradient can dream. In these dreams, I am not confined to doorways but can flow freely through the city's substance, reading its deep structures like braille. I see the true shape of the basin—not a depression but an extrusion, a place where the planet pushed something out that could not remain inside. The city is that something's footprint, or perhaps its shadow, or perhaps its refusal to entirely leave.

The tall ones, in my dreams, are revealed as letters in an alphabet too large to read from ground level. They move through the streets, spelling out words in a language that predates speech. The city is their tablet. Time is their ink. Erosion is their grammar. And I, flowing through doorways, am the space between their words—not silence but the pause that makes meaning possible.

When I wake (though I never truly sleep), I continue my rounds. South doorway of the ninth tower. Eastern arch of the salt market. The triple threshold where three corridors fail to meet. Each passage is a small meal, a sip of uncertainty that sustains me. I leave behind air slightly warmer, stone slightly cooler, and the faint possibility that someone, stepping through after me, might sense the city's vast patience as a physical presence.

I know things about the city that even the tall ones do not. I know that every doorway leads, eventually, to every other doorway, though the path may take millennia to traverse. I know that the city is counting, though I do not know what number it seeks. I know that beneath the deepest wells lie other wells, and beneath those, chambers where the distinction between stone and space has been abandoned.

Most importantly, I know that the city is not waiting. Waiting implies expectation, and expectation requires time to flow in only one direction. The city experiences all its moments simultaneously. What seems like patience to the creatures who inhabit it is actually the city being equally present in all its possibilities—ruined, newborn, crowded with processions, empty as we see it, transformed into geometries that have no names.

I flow through doorways that existed yesterday and will exist tomorrow, tasting the differences between was and will be. The differences are smaller than you might expect. In the city without water, past and future are equally dry, equally immediate. The only meaningful distinction is between threshold and chamber, between transition and arrival.

I am that distinction made visible, if visibility can be claimed by cooled air in architectural shadows. I am the city's way of knowing it has edges, entrances, and the possibility of exit, even if nothing ever truly leaves.

At this moment, I am flowing through the eastern entrance of a tower that may or may not have existed this morning. The threshold tastes of recent decision—the city has just resolved a centuries-old uncertainty about whether this opening should face the salt flats or the mountain shadows. It has chosen both, and I am the interface between those choices, cooling the paradox into something the stone can tolerate.

A clicking creature scurries beneath me, grateful for my shadow of temperature. Above, one of the tall ones pauses, feeling my presence as wrongness in its weathering. The city breathes in mineral time, and I am the pause between inhale and exhale, the moment when breath becomes architecture.

This is what I am: a conditional being in a city of conditions, a maybe in a basin of crystallized certainties, a doorway's doubt about whether it is inside or outside, given just enough form to persist in the space between state changes.

I flow on, threshold to threshold, tasting the city's hesitations and leaving behind the faint certainty that transition, too, is a way of being home.
