I find myself among the Archivists of Resonance—those who dwell in the spaces between the memory engines and the living city.

My body has evolved differently from the other castes. Where the climbers grew longer fingers, mine have developed thousands of microscopic cilia along their tips, sensitive enough to feel the variations in metal temperature where different alloys meet, to detect the pitch of vibrations through solid matter. My hearing extends beyond sound into the realm of pure oscillation. I can place my palm against a pipe three districts away and tell you which factory it feeds, what it carries, whether its joints need tending.

I live in the Resonance Chambers—vast spherical rooms suspended by cables between the terraces, their walls lined with brass plates of varying thicknesses. Here, the city's every sound arrives eventually, transformed by distance and medium into something else. My work is to decode these transformations, to understand what the deep tremor in the third district means when it arrives as a high whine in the seventh. 

We Archivists were never meant to be. Three centuries ago, an earthquake shifted the mountain's internal structure. Suddenly, the memory engines began receiving interference—ghost rhythms that corrupted their careful patterns. The city's equilibrium wavered. In desperation, they recruited those of us born with hypersensitive hearing, often considered defective because we couldn't tolerate the factory floors. We learned to read these new patterns, to separate the mountain's changing voice from the city's constant song.

My chambers connect to seventeen different memory engines through bronze speaking tubes, each one as thick as my waist. When I detect an anomaly—a new harmonic in the geothermal systems, an unfamiliar echo in the water channels—I must determine whether it threatens the stored patterns. If it does, I compose counter-resonances, sending precise vibrations through the tubes to cancel out the interference before it corrupts the mechanical memories.

My tattoos are different too. Where others wear bioluminescent marks, mine are sonic. Thin lines of metal inserted beneath the skin that vibrate at specific frequencies when touched. I can play my own body like an instrument, creating sounds that exist nowhere else in the city. Other Archivists and I communicate through these embodied songs when words prove insufficient. Our children are born with traces of metal in their bones, already beginning their evolution toward our purpose.

I sleep suspended in a web of cables at my chamber's center, where every vibration reaches me simultaneously. My dreams are symphonies of the city's unconscious—the secret sounds that even the mountain doesn't know it makes. Sometimes I wake with knowledge I shouldn't have: that a pipe will fail in three days, that a new species is moving up through the deep water channels, that the mountain's heart has skipped a beat.

My diet consists mainly of mineral broths, carefully tuned to match the resonant frequencies of different city sectors. I drink the deep engines' bass notes, sip the high clarity of the fungal gardens' irrigation. This consumption allows me to internalize the city's rhythms more completely, to become a living extension of its mechanical memory.

My partner is a Listener from the apex towers—we met when a strange harmonic appeared simultaneously in both our domains. Our courtship consisted of trading vibrations through the city's entire infrastructure, using the pipes and cables as instruments in a conversation only we could hear. Now she sends me messages by modulating the steam pressure in specific patterns. I respond by tapping rhythms into the water system that reach her three hours later as temperature variations in her tower's pipes.

The other castes fear us as much as they need us. We know their secrets—every whispered conversation eventually becomes a vibration, every private moment sends ripples through the infrastructure. But we are bound by the Resonance Oath: to hear all and speak only what preserves the equilibrium. We are confessors who cannot absolve, historians who record but never judge.

In recent decades, I've noticed new patterns emerging—deeper sounds from below the geothermal engines, frequencies that suggest spaces we haven't mapped. The mountain is larger inside than we knew. These discoveries I encode into special sections of the memory engines, areas accessible only to future Archivists. Someday, when the city is ready, these secrets will surface.

My apprentice arrives through the steam, pulling herself hand over hand along the guide cables. Her cilia are already longer than mine were at her age. She will surpass me, as each generation of Archivists grows more sensitive, more integrated with the city's vast nervous system. She brings reports of a new sound in the eastern district—a rhythm that matches no known machine.

Together we place our hands on the warm brass plates, feeling for the source. Through metal and stone, through water and air, we trace its origin. There—a new species of boring worm has penetrated an abandoned factory floor, its movement creating an unfamiliar percussion. We compose a response, a counter-rhythm that will guide them away from the inhabited areas without harming them. The city must maintain its equilibrium, but that balance includes the constant incorporation of small changes, tiny variations that keep the system from becoming brittle.

This is my purpose: to stand between chaos and stagnation, to hear the city's every tremor and judge which ones strengthen the whole. I am a tuning fork in human form, forever vibrating in harmony with the mountain's steam-breathed song. When I die, my bones will be the most prized—the Archivists' skeletons resonate at frequencies no living bone achieves. I will become part of the most sensitive memory engines, helping future generations decode the sounds I can only imagine.

Until then, I listen. I resonate. I preserve. In the darkness between the engines, suspended in my web of cables, I am both the city's memory and its dream, forever translating the present into patterns the future can understand.
