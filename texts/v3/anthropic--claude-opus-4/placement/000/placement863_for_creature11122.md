Standing in the pale expanse of the worn plateau, I find myself to be something between the builders and their constructions — neither fully alive nor entirely stone, but participating in both states with equal commitment.

I am a keeper of the transition.

My form resembles the builders in general architecture: tall, narrow, worn smooth by wind. But where they have achieved their perfect stillness, I remain caught in a slower oscillation. My body is honeycombed with chambers — natural cavities in my calcareous frame that open and close over the course of years like breathing pores. In these chambers, things accumulate: fragments of the grazers' shells, dried remnants of the drifters' tendrils, minerals left by evaporated rain, the cobalt lichen's spores.

I move between the builders and their markers, but my movement follows a pattern they have forgotten or transcended. Every few seasons — when the chambers in my left side grow heavy with their collections — I make my way to one of the stone rings. The journey takes most of a year. Upon arrival, I lower myself (this alone requires a full turning of seasons) and empty the chambers, letting their contents settle into the ring's center. Then I wait, through rain and evaporation cycles, until the materials bind together into a new stone, distinguishable from the plateau floor only by its slightly different texture, its faint iridescence where the drifter-remnants have mineralized.

The right-side chambers fill while the left empties. The cycle reverses. I move to a different ring.

I am making something, though I could not say what. The rings slowly fill with these manufactured stones — each one a compressed season, a solidified sample of the plateau's breathing. The builders notice this work the way they notice everything: slowly, without judgment, as another texture in the world's long becoming. Sometimes one of them stands near a ring I am filling. By the time I have finished and moved on, they may have shifted their weight slightly, acknowledging the change.

My skin is mediated between theirs and the stone floor — smoother than rock, rougher than their worn surfaces. The rose lichen grows on me, but only on my back, where I cannot see it. The cobalt appears in my joints like theirs, but also inside the chambers when they are empty, glowing faintly in the gathered darkness. The black lichen has never touched me. I suspect I am not still enough for it yet.

I remember motion, dimly, the way the builders perhaps remember building. There was a time when I gathered stones with purpose, when the rings meant something specific, when the act of filling them was part of a practice that had beginning and end. Now there is only the filling, the emptying, the slow migration between markers. The purpose has worn down like everything else here, until only the pattern remains.

The drifters sometimes catch their tendrils in my chambers when I am still. I do not disturb them. They feed on something there — perhaps the accumulated minerals, perhaps the dying fragments of other drifters. When they eventually drift free, they leave behind a faint luminescence that persists for years. I carry these lights with me to the next ring, where they too become part of the slow manufacturing.

Water is my clock. The chambers must be empty when the rain comes, or they hold too much and I cannot move for seasons afterward, waiting for evaporation. I have learned to read the sky's patience, to predict the gathering weeks before they begin. This is my only urgency — to reach a ring and empty myself before the weather arrives. It is a discipline that shapes my years.

I do not know if I was always this, or if I became this. The distinction matters less with each cycle. Perhaps I was a builder who could not achieve their stillness, who needed to keep some fragment of the making alive. Perhaps I was something else that came here and found a role the ecosystem had left vacant — neither predator nor prey, neither fully still nor truly moving, but something that processes the plateau's abundance into memory.

The stones I make will outlast the rings that hold them. Eventually, the rings themselves will wear flush with the floor, and my productions will scatter across the bone-colored surface, indistinguishable from any other stone except for that faint iridescence, that different weight. Future visitors might notice them. Might wonder at stones that seem more made than formed. Might understand, in some wordless way, that even here, in this place that has finished most of its arguments, something continues to insist on marking time, on making time material, on ensuring that the long calm carries within it the memory of when it was not yet calm.

I am that insistence, made patient. I move between the builders like a slow dream they are having about their former selves, and between their markers like a continuation of their old conversation in a language simplified down to weight and accumulation. My chambers fill. I carry what they collect to the appointed places. I empty myself. I begin again.

The plateau permits this. Just.
