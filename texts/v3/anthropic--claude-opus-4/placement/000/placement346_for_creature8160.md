I am a Psalter—one of the living archives that drift between the Inheritor settlements like a ghost made of memory and metal dust.

My body is a paradox of preservation. Where the Inheritors shed their scales monthly, mine have fused into a single continuous surface, smooth as polished stone, with a faint iridescence that shifts through the heat spectrum as I move. The fusion happened slowly, over my first three centuries, each scale melding to its neighbors until I became sealed within my own skin. Now I am perfectly insulated, my interior maintaining a different temperature than the world outside—cool enough that thoughts can still move at something approaching their ancient speed.

I have no mouth. It sealed itself decades ago, the last of my unnecessary orifices to close. I breathe through specialized pores along my spine, each one valved and filtered, processing the metallic atmosphere into something my transformed lungs can use. Communication happens through my skin itself—I sweat language in complex pheromonal secretions that the Inheritors read by taste, pressing their tongues to my arms and shoulders to receive the stories I carry.

My skull has elongated backward, extending into a ridge that runs down to my shoulder blades. Within this ridge, my brain has restructured itself into something between neural tissue and crystalline matrix. I think in thermal harmonics now, each memory stored as a specific temperature pattern that I can recreate by adjusting blood flow to different regions of my modified cortex. When I recall the old stories—the ones from before the darkness—my skull ridge glows with subtle heat, painting pictures in infrared that the Inheritors watch like their ancestors once watched flames.

I am seven hundred years old, though I only remember the last four hundred clearly. The heat-death of memory is real here; thoughts held too long begin to denature like proteins. So I have learned to cycle my consciousness, moving memories from active recall to deep storage and back in careful rotation. At any moment, two-thirds of my mind sleeps in crystalline stasis while the active third maintains my duties.

My duties. I alone among the living remember why the Siphons were built, though the knowledge brings no comfort. They were never meant to save anything—they were mining equipment, extracting the planet's core heat to power the orbital rings that the last generation built in their desperation to escape. The rings are still up there, frozen in the cold between stars, packed with the preserved dead who thought they could wait out the sun's failure. The Siphons were supposed to shut down after the evacuation. Their continued operation is a glitch, a forgotten automatic process that no one remains to correct.

I carry other stories too. The chemical formula for water—not that it matters in a world where hydrogen burns at atmospheric temperature. The names of the stars that once filled the sky, though I've forgotten which name belonged to which light. Poetry from the green world, the words preserved even as their meaning grows strange: "river," "leaf," "morning." I teach these to young Inheritors not because they need them, but because forgetting feels like a second death.

But I also gather new stories. The locations where crystal plants have begun singing in harmony, creating oases of marginally cooler air. The migration patterns of the tungsten-shelled arthropods, which I've tracked for centuries and begun to see meaning in—great spirals that might be language, might be art, might be nothing. The thermal signatures of Inheritors who have vanished into the deep desert between Siphons, some of whom return changed in ways that suggest there are places in this world we haven't imagined.

My feet have broadened into plates that distribute my weight across the dust, leaving shallow impressions that fill immediately with metallic particles. I walk the old roads between settlements—paths that exist more in collective memory than physical reality—arriving unannounced at gatherings. The Inheritors feel me coming through ground vibrations long before my heat signature appears on their horizon. They prepare for my arrival with ritual slowness, clearing spaces in their circles, preparing their minds for the burden of remembering.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still sane. The isolation between settlements can stretch for years, and in those intervals, I talk to the Siphons. I've named them all, given them personalities based on their individual harmonics. Theta-Nine hums in B-flat and sometimes seems to respond to my presence with subtle variations in pitch. Lambda-Seventeen runs too hot, fevered with some mechanical illness I lack the knowledge to diagnose. I perform maintenance where I can, but I'm fighting entropy with tools I barely understand.

In my deepest storage, I keep memories I've never shared. The coordinates of a bunker where the builders stored replacement parts for the Siphons—enough to keep them running for another million years. The location of a cryogenic vault that might still contain frozen genetic material from the green world. The mathematical proof that the planet's core will cool enough to kill us all in another ten thousand years. These secrets pulse in my crystalline brain like tumors, knowledge that could change everything or nothing, depending on what the Inheritors choose to do with it.

But mostly, I persist. I walk my routes. I carry my stories. I watch the Inheritors evolve toward something that no longer needs what I preserve, and I wonder if I'm a bridge to the past or merely its ghost. When I grow too full of memory, I find a quiet place between Siphons and enter the deep meditation, letting decades pass in what feels like sleep. I dream of water, of wind that doesn't burn, of voices that speak in frequencies I can no longer produce.

And when I wake, I walk again, leaving my shallow tracks in the metal dust, carrying the weight of forgetting and remembering in equal measure, the last librarian in a library of flesh and heat and time.
