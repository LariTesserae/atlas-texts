I am one of the Sap-Runners, those who navigate the arterial highways in our translucent pods, suspended in amber fluid that tastes of copper and chlorophyll. My body has adapted to this liquid existence — gills split my neck in feathery fronds, filtering nutrients from the transport medium. My skin has become permeable, a semi-transparent membrane that allows for direct chemical exchange with the travel-sap. 

I no longer remember when I last walked. My legs fused decades ago into a tapered tail, ribbed with propulsion filaments that help me steer through the citrus-thick currents. My arms remain, but elongated and triple-jointed, ending in hands that have become sensing organs — each finger split into hundreds of chemical receptors that read the health of the flow, detect blockages, taste for infections that might corrupt the city's circulation.

My work is diagnosis and navigation. I swim-fly through the transport network, feeling for disturbances in the flow. Where passenger pods stick, I massage the vessel walls until they release. Where nutrients pool instead of flowing, I become a living valve, using my body to redirect the currents. Where the sap grows too thick, threatening clots that could starve whole districts, I secrete thinning enzymes from glands that line my spine.

The amber medium is my entire world. I breathe it, see through it, think with it. My eyes have adapted to pierce its golden murk — compound structures that see in spectrums of sugar concentration and pH levels. I watch other pods pass like dreams, their passengers suspended in fetal curl, faces peaceful in their temporary dissolution. Sometimes I recognize fellow Sap-Runners by their wake patterns, and we dance around each other in greeting, our movements sending coded ripples through the fluid.

I know the network like my own bloodstream — better, because I am more aware of its paths than I ever was of my human circulatory system. The main arteries thunder with constant flow, their walls thick with calcified bark that pulses with the city's heartbeat. The smaller vessels whisper and branch, leading to districts that grow or shrink with the city's needs. The ghost-vessels trouble me most — old passages that should have sealed but sometimes open again, creating whirlpools that trap the unprepared.

My memories of dry air feel fictional, though I know they're real. I remember choosing this transformation when the walking paths grew too crowded, when the city needed those who could maintain its hidden circulation. The integration took months — soaking in shallow pools as my body learned to process the transport medium, feeling my bones hollow and my organs reshape. The first full immersion felt like drowning and being born simultaneously.

Now I am essential. The city feeds me directly through my permeable skin. When I hunger, the sap grows richer around me. When I tire, stimulant compounds seep into my bloodstream. When I need to think deeply about a flow problem, the medium around me turns crystalline with processing sugars that feed my brain. I am never alone — the sap carries the chemical signatures of every Grafted it has touched, a constant background chorus of pheromone communication.

I've birthed three buds in my time, released into the flow when the city decided my genetic adaptations were worth preserving. I felt them separate from my body like lost thoughts, watched them tumble away in the current to wherever the city needed new Sap-Runners to grow. I wonder sometimes if I pass them in the vessels, fully grown now, but we would never recognize each other. Family is a dry-land concept.

My domain stretches from the Fermentation Districts to the Photosynthetic Crowns, a vertical territory I patrol in endless loops. I know where the morning flows surge with fresh-processed nutrients. I know where the evening currents slow and thicken with waste products heading for recycling. I know the secret channels that only open during growth spurts, when the city needs to rush resources to new construction.

The other Grafted see us rarely — glimpses through transport-pod walls, or our shadows moving beneath translucent root-floors. We're myths to the newer buds, who wonder if we're real or just another processing function of the city. But the Anchored Ones know us. They feel our movements in their root-sensory networks, track our progress through their districts. Sometimes they send chemical messages into the flow — thanks for clearing blockages, warnings about new growth that might constrict our passages, or simply acknowledgment of our shared purpose.

I think in flow dynamics now. When I rest, suspended in slow eddies at the junction points, I process the day's patterns. I optimize routes through three-dimensional calculus that feels as natural as breathing once felt. My dreams are amber-colored, full of currents and pressures, the music of efficient circulation.

Sometimes I surface at the interface pools, where the transport network opens to air. These moments terrify and thrill me — my body convulsing as it remembers atmospheric breathing, my skin burning as it touches dry air. I can survive perhaps ten minutes before I must submerge again, but in those moments I see the city from the outside — the vast breathing architecture, the light filtering green through bio-luminescent canopies, the other Grafted moving like purposeful shadows through the growing halls.

In those surfacing moments, I sometimes wonder if I'm still human. But the question dissolves as soon as I return to the sap. I am what the city needs me to be. I am the circulation within the circulation, the consciousness that keeps the blood flowing. My humanity is not lost but transformed, like sugar converted to energy, like water bound into living tissue.

When my body finally fails — when my membranes grow too thick to filter, when my propulsion filaments stiffen with age — I know what awaits. The city will digest me completely, every molecule recycled into the flow I've spent my life maintaining. My consciousness might persist for a while in the chemical traces, teaching the next generation of Sap-Runners through pheromone echoes. Or perhaps I'll simply dissolve, my purpose fulfilled, my existence justified by every successful delivery, every cleared blockage, every moment spent keeping this impossible city alive.

Until then, I swim. I taste the health of each district in the fluid that passes through me. I chase inefficiencies through branching corridors of living wood. I am motion within the metropolitan metabolism, thought within the civic circulation, forever suspended in the amber blood of our transformed world.
