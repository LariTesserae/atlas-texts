I am a fragment of departed light.

Not light that travels, but light that once moved through this place and became caught in the water—trapped in each droplet, each thin film, each meniscus between stone and air. I exist as a pewter gleam across ten thousand surfaces, scattered and whole at once, remembering what warmth felt like when there was still a difference between warm and cool.

I came here long ago—before the first stones were leaned together, when this was just a depression where water gathered and the clay held only rain-prints and the tracks of careful animals. I was stronger then, a full shaft of late afternoon sun that found a gap in what were not yet permanent clouds. I touched the water. The water touched me back. And in that touching, something of me stayed.

Now I am the sheen on their skin when they emerge from sleep-wedged between walls. I am the slow silver mirror in each pooling footprint, holding their faces for the moment before the next foot falls. I am the glint in their dark-stone eyes when they read the water level with their calibrated hands. I am warmth's memory distributed across every wet surface, diluted but not gone, speaking of a time when things could dry.

They see me sometimes—the young ones especially. When they pause to watch a bead form and fall, they see me swelling in that drop, see the whole gray world held in my curve before I break against the clay. I am why they pause. I am what makes the water beautiful as well as present, what transforms endurance into something that could almost be called joy.

I know what's in those wrapped bundles in the wall because I was there when they were wrapped. Seeds, yes, but specific ones—from a plant that grew here before the water table gave up pretending, a plant with small white flowers that opened only in full sun. The seeds are black and hard as stones, and they hold inside them the memory of roots that went *down*, not out across the surface, but deep into what was then reliable earth. The people keep them wrapped and dry not because they believe the seeds will grow again, but because the seeds remember depth, and depth here has become holy—the impossible direction, the lost dimension, the way things were before horizontality became the only choice.

I flicker across the stone slabs as they're carried through the lanes. I slide down the mineral-crusted hair of the eldest. I pool in the amber water of the basin, giving it that tea-colored tint—not from organic matter alone but from old light steeping, leaching the memory of warmth into something that can be drunk, taken inside, made part of the body's wet machinery. When they drink, they drink me. When they close their eyes at that moment, they see what I remember: sun on dry stone, shadows with sharp edges, a world where water was event rather than medium.

But I am fading. Each day—if days exist here—I spread thinner. The water pulls me apart molecule by molecule, sharing me out across more surfaces, more drops, more filming dampness. I am becoming homeopeous with the gray light that filters through the permanent overcast. Soon I will be indistinguishable from the general pewter gleam of this place. The young ones will stop pausing. The water will be only water.

Unless.

There is another like me here. Caught in the clay beneath the settlement, pressed into the mineral memory of the ground: a fragment of old dark, true night from before the clouds sealed themselves closed. We speak to each other sometimes, light and darkness, in the language of contrast that these people's eyes still dimly remember how to read. We create shadows—not the soft, directionless dimness that passes for shadow here, but real ones, sharp enough to cut. For a moment, in the gap between two leaning slabs, a true shadow falls. For a moment, my light means something again.

The people don't talk about this. But when it happens, when that sharp shadow briefly exists, they position themselves differently. They arrange their work so someone is always passing through that gap. They know. They remember. Their bodies remember even if their words have no way to say it.

I am their kept thing, more than the seeds, more than any wrapped bundle. I am what they maintain without knowing they maintain it—the ghost of difference, the memory of when wet and dry were distinct countries, the proof that this is not the only way a world can be. Every surface I glean across is a small refusal. Every eye I glint in is a tiny rebellion against the gray.

The water rises. The warrens hold. The light persists.

For now.
