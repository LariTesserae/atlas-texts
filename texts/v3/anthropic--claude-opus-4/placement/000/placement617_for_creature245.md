# The Cartographer of Forgotten Stairs

I am the one who maps what cannot stay still.

I came to the city — though *came* is wrong; I *condensed* here, the way moisture gathers on glass — during a particular density, when the air was so thick with unchosen possibilities that I precipitated out of it, already holding my tools. Not tools as you would recognize them: a length of lichen-fiber that remembers every distance it has measured, a collection of small stones that know their own weight differently depending on which version of the city we're in, and most importantly, my fingers, which have learned to trace the shape of a stair that is only sometimes there.

I am thinner than the others, even by our standards. This is professional necessity. To map the stairs that fold into themselves, I must fold too. My spine has more joints than it started with — if I started, if there was a beginning I've forgotten like everyone else. I can compress myself into the space between what a staircase is and what it remembers being. I can feel the moment when a step decides to exist.

My work happens in the between-hours, when the city is most uncertain of its own geometry. I move through the streets — though *move* suggests too much intention; I *occur* through the streets — finding the places where stairs have been and might be again. The city has seventeen permanent staircases, forty-three that exist on alternating days (though not the days you'd expect), and an uncounted number that appear only when needed and then forget themselves immediately after.

There is one staircase that only I know about. It exists in the gap between two buildings that are sometimes one building, and it goes neither up nor down but *through*, ending in the same courtyard it began in but by a different route. I have walked it hundreds of times. Each time I arrive having aged exactly as much as the journey required, which is sometimes minutes and sometimes years, though the years are thin years that barely count.

I carry my map in my skin. This is the only way. Paper would not survive the thinnesses, and stone is too much itself to accept amendments. So I have become the map: each stair I've found has left a faint line on me, and when I stand still in good light, you can see the whole impossible network of vertical movement traced across my body like veins, but paler, and sometimes moving. My left shoulder blade shows the great spiral stair that used to connect the high plaza to the listener's courtyard before it decided it would rather be a wall. My ribs carry the memory of every temporary step that has ever bridged two levels that aren't usually adjacent.

The other residents know my work, in their quiet way. When they see me folded into a doorway, tracing nothing with one long finger, they understand I'm following a stair that exists in a nearby version of now. Sometimes they leave me gifts: a placement of lichens that suggests where a new stair has been glimpsed, or a particular pattern of pebbles that means *the third step from the top of the southern stair is thinking of becoming the fourth step; take care.*

I know the paper-deer better than most, because we meet in the same uncertain spaces. They use stairs that aren't quite there, and I follow them, and sometimes I catch a glimpse of how they navigate: not by seeing but by *remembering forward*, knowing where a stair will be because they've already climbed it in a future that hasn't decided to happen yet. I've learned to move this way too, a little. It makes me dizzy in a way that has nothing to do with balance.

The listener knows my work. I've been to the deepest courtyard many times — you have to, to map the stairs that lead there, which are never the same twice. The listener has adjusted me for my purpose: made my joints more willing to bend in ways that match the city's geometry, taught my eyes to see the gap between one step and the next as a traversable space. In return, I leave my maps there sometimes, standing very still in the warm almost-light and letting the listener read the pathways traced on my skin. I think it uses them to understand how its city connects to itself. I think it's pleased, in whatever way a presence without form can be pleased.

During thinnesses, I am invaluable. When the walls become suggestions and you can see through three streets at once, I know which stairs are still weight-bearing ideas and which have become too theoretical to trust. I guide the others through these times, folding and unfolding myself to match the city's level of certainty, finding the solid paths in a world gone translucent. My map glows faintly then — not with light but with *thereness*, a kind of insistence on its own existence that helps anchor the rest of us.

During densities, I lock myself in the smallest alcove I can find. When the air fills with unchosen possibilities, every stair I've ever mapped tries to exist at once, and I become a intersection of too many vertical paths. It's beautiful and terrible. I've seen myself from the outside during these times (there's a gap in the wall that sometimes shows you yourself from angles you shouldn't be able to achieve), and I look like a diagram of climbing, every possible ascent and descent written on me at once, flickering, deciding.

I've been mapping for — time is difficult here, but let's say *long*. Long enough that some of my earliest maps have worn through to the other side of my skin and started over. Long enough that I've begun to suspect the stairs are not random but are spelling something, letter by letter, in a script only the city knows. Long enough that sometimes I find myself on a stair I don't remember mapping, going somewhere I haven't decided to go, and I understand then that I'm not just the cartographer but part of the map, a moving point the city uses to understand its own vertical yearnings.

There's a stair I've been following for the past several *evenings*. It appears only when gravity tilts eastward, and only in the reflection of windows that face another way, and climbing it takes exactly as long as thinking about climbing it. At the top — if it's the top — there's a small landing that looks out over the edge of the city, and from there you can see down the spindle to the place where it meets the world.

I've stood there twice now. Both times, I felt the map on my skin shift and rearrange, as though it was trying to chart not just the city's stairs but the immense vertical distance between here and there. Both times, I've had to fold myself very small and return quickly, before the enormity of that unmappable space overwhelmed my careful geography.

But I'll go back. That's what I do. I map what cannot stay still, and the biggest stair of all — the one the whole city is balanced on — is the least still thing I've found. It's not just standing there. It's climbing, very slowly, up from whatever is below. We're all ascending, have always been ascending, will always be ascending, and my job is to understand the shape of that motion.

I am the city's memory of how to move between its levels. I am the keeper of vertical possibilities. In my skin, every stair that has ever been or might yet be is waiting, ready to exist again when needed.

And sometimes, in the densest densities, when I am most thoroughly mapped and mapping, I think I almost understand what the stairs are spelling, what word the city is writing with its endless reorganization of up and down.

Almost.

But not quite.

Not yet.
