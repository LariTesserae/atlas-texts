In this world, I would be a **bell-founder's apprentice** who has begun to hear too much.

I came to the town at fifteen, raw-knuckled and eager, drawn by rumors of the pressure bell — that strange instrument that rings not for hours but for the underground river's moods. My master, Kellan, is a woman of few words and iron-gray hair who inherited the bell tower from her aunt. She sleeps in the workshop below it, says the ringing helps her know when to add tin to the alloy.

At first, I only heard what everyone hears: the bell's bronze voice announcing shifts in the buried water. But after three years of pouring metal, filing clapper joints, and sleeping on a cot beside the forge, I've begun to notice that the bell speaks twice. Once through the air, for the town. Once through the iron of its mounting, down into the earth, for something else.

The second voice is older. Not louder — thinner, like water speaking through water.

I've started mapping it. Not in ledgers like the valve clerks, but in metal itself. I make small bells, no bigger than a child's fist, each with a different alloy, a different thickness. I hang them on wires in the tower's understory where no one goes but me and the bats. When the pressure bell rings above, my small bells answer below, each picking up a different thread of that underground voice.

Kellan caught me listening last week, my ear pressed to a freshly cast bell still warm from the mold. She didn't scold. Just handed me a rag and said, "The settling comes up through the foundry floor sometimes. You'll know it by the pattern — like chainmail made of dew."

She was right. I've seen it twice now: perfect beads of moisture arranging themselves in interlocking rings on the concrete, each droplet holding its place until dawn burns them back to vapor. The second time, I laid one of my small bells in the center of the pattern. When I picked it up, its voice had changed — clearer, carrying a note that made my teeth ache sweetly.

I am becoming strange in the particular way of this place. My fingernails carry a permanent curve of metal dust. I wake knowing the river has turned east before the bell confirms it. I've started leaving gaps in my work — a deliberately thin spot in a bell's shoulder, an unfinished edge on a clapper — because I've learned that the beadlings (I've seen them, whatever Kellan says) prefer to complete things themselves. A bell they've touched rings truer.

The other apprentices think I'm too quiet, but it's not that. I'm listening for the third voice. Because I'm certain now there is one. Not the bell speaking to the town, not the bell speaking through iron to the river, but the river speaking back. It comes up through the foundry floor, through the furnace stones, through the anvil's root. It says —

Well. I'm still learning to hear it.

Some nights I climb to the bell chamber and sit with my hand on the great bronze curve, feeling it breathe with temperature changes, waiting for the river to shift. When it does, and the clapper swings, I feel the voice divide: up into air, down through stone, and somehow, impossibly, sideways into the bells I've made, the small choir hanging in the dark below.

I am nineteen, thin, soot-knuckled, patient. I wear the town's uniform of wool and worn leather, but my pockets are full of experimental alloys — brass with too much zinc, bronze with a trace of lead, pure copper blanks the size of coins. I am learning to speak in frequencies only the edges hear.

Last month, a traveling merchant asked what I did in this damp, noisy place. I said, "I listen to the water learning to be bells."

She laughed, but nervously, and left before her goods were fully sold.

I don't mind. In another year, perhaps two, Kellan will let me cast my first true bell. Not for a church or a school, but for here, for the settling, for the conversation between metal and moisture that never ends. I already know its shape — neither fully round nor fully open, but something between, with a rim that can hold dew and a voice that can speak both ways at once.

The beadlings, I think, are waiting for it too.
