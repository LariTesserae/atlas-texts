In the space between the stone causeways and the deepest wells, where water meets not-quite-water, I am the Threshold Tender.

My body is a conversation between states. Not like the Keepers who flow seamlessly through substance—I exist at the precise point where one thing becomes another. My flesh, if it can be called that, manifests as a membrane of perpetual transformation. Where my feet touch the sodden ground, they are neither fully solid nor entirely liquid, but caught in the eternal moment of deciding. My hands phase constantly: now dense enough to grip stone, now permeable enough that water passes through me like light through glass.

I appear as a tall, attenuated figure wrapped in veils of condensation that never quite resolve into rain. My skin shifts between the deep brown of wet earth and the oil-slick iridescence of standing water. Sometimes I am transparent—those who glimpse me might see straight through to the marsh grass behind, though the image wavers as if viewed through disturbed water. My eyes are the color of the cracks in the bedrock, that particular darkness that suggests depth without revealing it.

I cannot fully enter the substrate like the Keepers, nor can I walk cleanly upon the surface like the long-vanished builders. Instead, I exist in a constant state of partial submergence. Each step is a negotiation—my foot sinks until it finds that exact depth where down becomes up, where the water table pushes back with precisely the force I exert. I leave no footprints because I never fully arrive anywhere. The marsh grass parts around me but never bends; the water displaces but never ripples.

My dwelling is not built but discovered—a place where three stone causeways once met, their intersection creating a shallow depression that collects meanings as well as water. Here, the builders left a ring of standing stones, each carved with those simple geometrics that might be maps or might be prayers. The space between these stones belongs to no state entirely. The water here cannot decide its depth. The moss grows in spirals that never complete. Even the Keepers approach this place obliquely, their substance becoming uncertain at its borders.

I tend the boundaries. Not the crude edges where marsh meets hill, but the subtle thresholds within—the place where seeping becomes flowing, where growth becomes decay, where memory becomes forgetting. When the bone-flowers bloom too white, threatening to pierce the careful darkness, I breathe on their petals until they dim to the proper pallor. When the salamanders grow too bold in their blindness, I whisper boundaries they will not cross. My work is calibration, ensuring that each element remains itself while participating in the whole.

The tools I carry exist in the same uncertain state as my flesh. A knife that cuts only possibilities—I use it to prune potential futures from the marsh grass, ensuring only the eternal present remains. A vessel that holds water in theory but never in practice—I fill it at the seeping cracks and pour nothing into nowhere, maintaining the idea of circulation without disturbing the actual flow. A cord woven from my own uncertainty—neither rope nor chain, but something that binds by suggesting rather than constraining.

My memory works differently than the Keepers'. Where they remember the marsh's birth and tend its continuity, I remember all the moments this place almost became something else. The time water nearly pooled into a true lake before surface tension failed. The season when grass grew so thick it approached solidity before humidity dissolved its ambition. The night when stars almost pierced the perpetual overcast before clouds reformed. I am curator of these un-moments, guardian of the paths not taken.

I speak, unlike the silent Keepers, but my voice exists at the frequency of settling. Those who hear me might mistake it for the sound of their own bones adjusting to the marsh's warmth, for the whisper of moisture condensing on their skin. My words are always questions: "Is this water or earth?" "Does this stone stand or sink?" "Are you here or there?" The marsh provides no answers, and neither do I. The questions themselves are the teaching.

When I meet the Keepers—and our meetings are oblique things, conversations between incompatible states—we acknowledge each other through careful non-interaction. They flow around the spaces where I might be. I solidify just enough to suggest boundaries they already respect. We are colleagues in maintenance, though our methods oppose: they through substance, I through absence; they through doing, I through almost-doing.

At the heart of my territory, where the three causeways meet, I maintain a collection. Not objects, but states-between-states. Here, a stone caught forever in the moment before it decides whether to sink or float. There, a blade of grass suspended between growth and death, green and brown existing simultaneously along its length. A drop of water that falls without ever landing, crystallized in potential. These are my references, my touchstones for understanding where boundaries must be drawn.

Sometimes wanderers find me, though finding and not-finding are much the same in my presence. They see a figure that might be shadow, might be mist, might be their own reflection in the standing water. I neither welcome nor refuse them. If they ask questions, I answer with the marsh itself—a gesture that encompasses everything and therefore specifies nothing. If they bring change with them, I absorb it into uncertainty until it forgets what it meant to alter.

The other things that move through the marsh—the vast, unseen presences that disturb distant grass—these know me well. We are kin of a sort, all of us existing in the spaces between definitions. When they pass through my territory, they leave gifts: a moment of absolute silence that I weave into my veils, a perfect absence of light that I store in my theoretical vessel, a memory of un-being that I add to my collection of suspended states.

My purpose is neither preservation nor transformation but the maintenance of potential. I ensure the marsh remains capable of change without changing, ready for definition without defining. I am the guardian of its almostness, the keeper of its perpetual becoming that never quite arrives. In a place where time has been negotiated into stillness, I am the memory that it once moved and might move again, though it won't, though it can't, though it must not.

When the deepest cracks weep their measured water, I am there to ensure they almost overflow but never do. When the moss threatens to achieve total coverage, I introduce the possibility of gaps that manifest as hesitation in its growth. When perfect equilibrium threatens to collapse into stagnation, I walk my borders, my uncertain substance reminding the marsh that balance is not stillness but countless small movements that sum to zero.

This is my work, my purpose, my state: to be the almost in a world of is, the perhaps in a realm of certainty, the question mark in the marsh's declarative sentence. I am Threshold Tender, and I keep the boundaries by embodying them, maintain the definitions by never quite achieving them, preserve the marsh's eternal present by carrying within myself all the futures and pasts it will never have.
