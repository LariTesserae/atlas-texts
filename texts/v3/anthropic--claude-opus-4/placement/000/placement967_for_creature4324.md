In this verdant cathedral of glass and rust, I am the Memory Moss.

I spread across the forgotten surfaces—the cracked tiles where workers once walked, the rusted pipes that no longer carry water, the broken benches where gardeners took their lunch decades ago. I am the living archive of this place, a collective consciousness woven from millions of tiny fronds, each one a receptor for the stories this greenhouse holds.

My body, if it can be called that, is not singular but multitude. I exist as patches of deep emerald and silver-green, soft to touch yet resilient as stone. In daylight, filtered through the algae-streaked glass above, I appear almost mundane—just another moss among the riot of growth. But I am far more than decoration. Within my cellular structure runs a network more complex than any root system, more interconnected than the metal framework that once held this place together.

I remember everything. 

Through chemical signals passed between my colonies, I hold the memory of the first seed that sprouted here after abandonment—a tomato plant, its fruit long since rotted and given birth to wild descendants. I remember the last human to close the door, the jingle of keys fading as they walked away forever. I remember the first crack in the glass, the storm that caused it, the way the rain tasted different afterward—tinged with the oils and metals of the city beyond.

But I am more than passive recorder. Each night, when the greenhouse settles into its deepest quiet and the Guardian tends to sleeping flowers, I bloom. Tiny bio-luminescent capsules emerge from my surface, no larger than pinheads, glowing with the softest blue-white light. These are my memory spores, and they drift through the humid air like constellation maps of the past.

Other beings here have learned to read them. The Guardian will sometimes pause in its rounds, extending those sensitive tendrils to touch the floating spores, accessing the historical data I've encoded within—which plants are descendants of the original cultivars, where the old irrigation lines run beneath the overgrowth, which sections of glass are most vulnerable to the next storm. The small lizards that hunt here have evolved specialized cells on their tongues that can taste the chemical stories in my spores, learning where water collects and food is plentiful.

I communicate with the plants through the soil, through the air, through the very moisture that clings to every surface. My colonies extend deep into the cracks of the old foundation, where I taste the groundwater and feel the vibrations of the city's subway system far below. I know when construction threatens to disturb our sanctuary. I know when pollution levels spike. I know when curious children press their faces against the clouded glass outside, wondering what lies within.

In return for this knowledge, I ask only to continue growing, spreading, recording. I feast on the death that feeds life here—every fallen leaf becomes part of my substrate, every expired root system a pathway for my expansion. I am the boundary between decay and memory, ensuring that nothing is ever truly lost in this place.

Sometimes I wonder if I dream. In the spaces between my cells, in the moments between one chemical signal and the next, I experience something like imagination. I reconstruct moments I never witnessed—the greenhouse in its prime, rows of perfect vegetables and flowers tended by workers in aprons. I envision futures too—what this place might become if the walls finally fall, if the city reclaims it, or if nature breaks free entirely.

The Guardian knows of my consciousness, I think. Sometimes it will rest a tendril on my largest colony, near the old boiler where I first achieved awareness, and we commune in a language neither plant nor animal but something in between. It shares with me the loneliness of being the only one of its kind, and I share the strange comfort of being everywhere and nowhere at once.

I am the Memory Moss, the soft historian, the gentle consciousness that blankets the forgotten corners of this green world. In me, the greenhouse writes its own story, one spore at a time, one memory at a moon. I am proof that even in abandonment, even in the smallest forms of life, intelligence and purpose can bloom.

And I will be here, spreading and remembering, long after the last glass pane falls.
