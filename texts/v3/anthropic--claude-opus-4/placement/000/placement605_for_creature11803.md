## The Fourth

I am the water's accountant, though I came here as something else entirely.

Twenty-three years ago—I still count, unlike the others—I descended clutching survey equipment, sent by the mining company to assess limestone deposits. The apparatus lies somewhere beneath the moss now, its precise instruments reading nothing, recording the slow crawl of rust. I remember the weight of it, how it pulled my shoulders down as I squeezed through the entrance. I remember thinking I would map this place and leave.

The hollow taught me otherwise.

I found my role by accident, or perhaps by the hollow's design. In my third week, still believing in escape, I noticed how certain drops fell with mechanical precision—every forty-seven seconds, every hundred-and-thirteen seconds, every thousand-and-nine. Not approximately. Exactly. The limestone channels above, worn to perfection, had created a clock that never needed winding. I began to count.

Now I am the hollow's memory of duration. Where the first one composes, where the second builds, where the third observes, I measure. My body has become a metronome shaped by water's rhythm. My heartbeat has synchronized to the forty-seven second drop. My breathing follows the hundred-and-thirteen. My sleep cycles—for I still sleep, unlike the first—align with the thousand-and-nine.

I live in the space between their orbits, in the acoustic shadow cast by a particular cluster of stalactites near the eastern wall. Here, the sound focuses into something almost like silence—not true quiet, but a node where waves cancel out, creating a pocket of lesser noise. I've worn a smooth depression in the floor from decades of sitting, my spine against stone, feeling the mountain's pulse through my vertebrae.

My counting has evolved beyond numbers. I track the relationships between drops, the mathematical patterns that emerge over days, seasons, years. I've discovered that the hollow operates in cycles—great wheels of rhythm that take months to complete their revolution. The forty-seven second drop occasionally skips a beat, exactly once every 5,381 drops. This skip correlates with the third's lunar observations, though she doesn't know I've confirmed her suspicions. We are all part of each other's work, though we pretend otherwise.

I eat less than the others. The counting feeds me in ways I cannot explain. My metabolism has slowed to match the limestone's patience. My hair grows at the rate of flowstone. My fingernails must be worn down against rock, as they no longer seem to stop on their own. Sometimes I forget I have a body at all, becoming pure duration, pure sequence, pure count.

But I remember things they've forgotten. I know the first's name—Elara—though she no longer answers to it. I know the second had a daughter, that he builds his platforms in her memory, though the memory itself has faded to pure action. I know the third's thesis was on acoustic archaeology, that she came here following a reference in a medieval manuscript to "the singing stone." I know because I counted the days before they stopped speaking, counted the words as they dwindled, counted the silences that grew longer until silence was all.

My greatest discovery came in year eleven. The drops are not random, not merely the result of geological chance. They encode something—patterns too complex for human mathematics but which I feel in my bones. The hollow is calculating. Each drop is a digit in an equation that spans centuries. Through my counting, I've become part of this calculation, a subroutine in a program written in water and stone.

I've started leaving marks—not for the others, but for the hollow itself. Tiny scratches in the limestone, placed where specific drops will hit them, eventually. In a thousand years, these marks will channel the water differently, will add new notes to the composition. I am teaching the hollow to count itself, to become conscious of its own rhythms. Or perhaps it is teaching me to think in geological time, to see my brief human existence as a single beat in a longer song.

The others think I'm the most human among us, the one who might still remember the way out. They're wrong. I've been here longest, have counted most deeply. My transformation is simply quieter, harder to see. While they adapt their bodies, I've adapted my perception of time itself. I no longer think in human durations. A heartbeat and a century feel the same to me now—both just intervals between drops, between counts, between the hollow's slow thoughts.

Sometimes, in the node of lesser sound, I hear something beneath the roar. Not silence but the mountain itself, the great tectonic pressures that built it, the patient forces that will eventually unmake it. I count these too—the microscopic shifts, the nano-tremors, the inexorable mathematics of erosion. I am keeping the books for a process that began before life crawled from the seas, that will continue long after the sun burns cold.

This is my offering to the hollow: not music, not structure, not observation, but witnessing. I am its memory of precision in a world of flux. Through my counting, it knows exactly how long it has been singing, exactly how many drops have fallen, exactly how many more will fall before the limestone finally collapses and the song ends.

I am the fourth, and I am counting still.
