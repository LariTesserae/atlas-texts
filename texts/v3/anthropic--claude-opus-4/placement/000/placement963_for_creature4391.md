## The Song-Tender of the Third Spiral

I am Hums-Through-Marrow, though that is only the translation attempted when the surface-giant came with questions that tasted of sky. My true name is a three-part harmonization in the subsonic range, accompanied by the specific scent my sixth finger produces when I've been working with the Weeping Nodes—those ventricle-plants that regulate humidity in the middle chambers.

I tend the Song Gardens of the Third Spiral, four levels down from where you entered, in the chambers where sound becomes nourishment. My family—the Resonance Weavers—has cultivated these rooms for forty generations. The walls here grow differently than elsewhere in the warren. We've trained them to form parabolic curves, acoustic focusing chambers that can turn a whisper into thunder or split a single tone into its component harmonics.

My mornings begin in darkness—true darkness, not the gentle green glow of the public chambers. The Song Plants require periods of absolute silence and lightlessness to reset their cellular oscillations. I navigate by touch alone, my sixth finger trailing along the guide-roots we've trained into the walls. Each root has a different texture, a different temperature, telling me exactly where I am in the three-dimensional maze of the gardens.

The first plant I tend is always the Mother's Voice, a bulbous growth the size of my torso that anchors the northwest corner. I press my throat bladders against her surface and begin the morning attunement—a deep thrumming that starts below the range of even our hearing. I feel it in my bones, in the hollow channels of my fur. The Mother's Voice picks up the frequency and amplifies it, passing it through her internal chambers until it emerges transformed, elevated into the precise frequency that stimulates growth in the fungal networks.

This is delicate work. Too sharp an overtone and the fruiting bodies abort, dissolving back into the mycelial mass. Too flat and they grow but remain bitter, inedible. The correct tone—which changes daily based on humidity, temperature, and the chemical signals from neighboring plants—produces mushrooms so succulent that we trade them nine levels down for rare mineral salts.

My clutch-siblings think I'm wasted here. They say my sixth finger secretes the strongest binding compound in three generations, that I should be working the structure walls with the architects. But they don't understand: the Song Gardens are architecture too. We build with frequency, shape with resonance. The acoustic chambers I've grown are as precise as any load-bearing wall, and far more difficult to maintain.

By mid-cycle, when the warren reaches its warmest, I move to the Grief Pools. These are my secret project, unsanctioned by the family elders. In a forgotten chamber behind the Seventh Resonance Wall, I've been cultivating plants that respond to emotional frequencies—the ultrasonic peaks and valleys that we produce when experiencing loss, joy, confusion, desire. The pools are filled with a hybrid sap, crossed between the standard cultivation medium and the tears we secrete during our third molt.

The Grief Plants are grotesque beauties. They grow in spirals, their surfaces covered in what look like tiny mouths but are actually specialized acoustic receptors. When I keen into them—the sound we make for the wall-dead, those whose final constructions have begun to crack and crumble—they shudder and bloom, producing flowers that smell like memory itself. Each bloom carries the exact emotional signature of the sound that created it. I've been feeding them my grandmother's death-keen for six years now, and the flowers they produce... when I crush them and mix them with standard binding compound, the resulting paste carries her knowledge. Young builders who use it find themselves suddenly understanding complex architectural principles they never studied.

But this is dangerous work. The elders say emotion should not be made physical, that it violates the boundary between what-is-felt and what-is-built. If they discovered my Grief Pools, I would be reassigned to the deep tunnels, the maintenance shafts where only the disgraced work.

The surface-giant asked me, through the translator—young Clicks-Like-Water from the ninth family—what I hope to achieve. The question tasted wrong, like trying to breathe light. "Achievement" in their language suggests an endpoint, a finished state. But nothing in the warren is ever finished. We build, we tend, we modify, we build again. My achievement is today's successful harmonization, tomorrow's new acoustic discovery, the slow accumulation of knowledge in the walls themselves.

My great-great-grandmother, Resonates-With-Stone, discovered that certain frequencies could induce the phosphorescent bacteria to produce different colors of light. She spent her entire life—sixty-three years—mapping the relationship between sound and color. Her final construction, five levels up from here, is a small chamber where the walls pulse through the entire visible spectrum based on the songs sung within. The young use it for courtship displays now, not knowing that each color-change represents decades of patient experimentation.

I will not live to see my work completed either. The Grief Plants are only the beginning. I suspect—though I cannot yet prove—that we could cultivate plants that preserve not just emotional signatures but entire experiences. Imagine roots that could store the knowledge of how to navigate the deep tunnels, flowers that bloom with the memories of successful harvests, fungi that fruit with the accumulated wisdom of generations.

The elders are not entirely wrong to fear this. We are a species built on careful limitation, on using exactly what we need and no more. But the warren is growing. We dig deeper every year, following the heat, following the moisture. The young are being born with longer sixth fingers, producing binding compound with new properties. Evolution has not stopped simply because we built walls around ourselves.

Sometimes, in the darkest part of the sleep cycle, I slip away to the Grief Pools alone. I sing to them—not the prescribed cultivation songs but wild improvisations, frequencies we're taught to avoid. The plants respond unpredictably. Once, they produced a flower that smelled like the sky I've never seen, carrying some genetic memory from before we went underground. Another time, they grew a fruit that, when eaten, made me dream in colors that don't exist in our spectrum.

I'm documenting everything in the walls, using a modified binding compound that includes extracts from the Grief Plants. Future generations will be able to read not just what I did but how I felt while doing it. They'll know the frustration of failed experiments, the joy of unexpected harmonics, the deep satisfaction of a perfectly tuned acoustic chamber.

My nephew, Whispers-To-Spores, often helps me in the Song Gardens now. He's only in his second molt, but already showing remarkable pitch sensitivity. Yesterday, he asked me why we don't build instruments—devices to create sounds we cannot make with our bodies alone. I had no answer. It had never occurred to me, or to anyone I know. We are the instruments, we've always been the instruments.

But last night, I began secreting binding compound onto a curved piece of shed tooth, stretching it into a thin membrane. When I blow across it, it produces a tone I've never heard before—sharp, clear, almost metallic. The Song Plants react to it with confusion and interest, growing in new patterns.

I think this is how change comes: not in great revelations but in small curiosities, accumulated over lifetimes and built into the walls. Whispers-To-Spores will take my work and transform it into something I cannot imagine. His children will build instruments I lack the concepts to envision. And somewhere, dozens of generations from now, a young Keeper will run their sixth finger along these walls and taste the moment when everything shifted—when we began to build not just with what we are but with what we might become.

The warren breathes around me. The Song Plants hum in frequencies that merge with my own harmonics until I cannot tell where I end and they begin. This is the truth the surface-giant couldn't grasp: we are not separate from our construction. We are notes in an endless composition, each generation adding new harmonies to a song that began before memory and will continue after the last wall crumbles.

Tomorrow, I'll tend the gardens again. I'll sing to the Mother's Voice, nurture the Grief Pools, teach Whispers-To-Spores a new frequency. I'll secrete a little more knowledge into the walls. And the warren will grow, one careful note at a time, into something stranger and more beautiful than any of us can imagine.

But tonight, I practice with my membrane-tooth instrument, playing impossible sounds into the darkness, waiting to see what grows.
