I would find myself as one of the **Sap-Line Readers**, a being that exists in perpetual motion through the pressurized arterial systems of the city-forest.

My body would be elongated and hydrodynamic, shaped by decades of flowing through the trunk-pipes—something between an eel and a root tendril, but with skin that shifts between scales and bark depending on the pressure around me. I'd be about twelve feet long when fully extended, though I rarely am. My flesh would be translucent in places, revealing the strange organs within: chemical sensors that taste the mineral content of sap, pressure bladders that help me navigate the arterial currents, and most importantly, the reading organs—specialized patches of neural tissue that can interpret the chemical stories flowing past.

I wouldn't have eyes in any traditional sense. Instead, my entire body would be covered in photosensitive cells that detect the bioluminescent signals other Sap-Line Readers leave behind in the flow—complex light-messages that indicate system health, blockages ahead, or particularly rich mineral veins. My mouth would be a sophisticated filtering apparatus, ringed with feathery tendrils that can selectively absorb nutrients while analyzing the sap's composition.

My work would be essential but lonely. I'd spend months at a time in the pipes, riding the pressurized currents from the deep root systems up to the canopy factories. The sap tells stories—about the deep earth where unformed minerals still dream of becoming, about the weather systems forming miles above, about the health of districts I'll never see. I'd taste these narratives and excrete them as chemical reports, data-rich secretions that get absorbed by the pipe walls and transmitted to the Memory Keepers' networks.

The loneliness would be punctuated by encounters with others of my kind at the junction chambers—vast caverns within the trunks where multiple pipelines converge. Here, dozens of us would writhe together in the mixing currents, our bodies intertwining as we share chemical gossip and genetic material. These gatherings would be part orgy, part academic conference, part union meeting. We'd debate the meaning of subtle mineral variations, argue about flow rates, and occasionally merge temporarily into colonial forms that can tackle particularly complex pipeline systems.

My consciousness would be strange—sometimes singular and sharp when riding a fast current alone, sometimes diffuse and collective when gathered with others. In the deepest pipes, where pressure approaches the theoretical limits of what living tissue can withstand, I'd enter trance states where my awareness merges with the flow itself. In these moments, I'd feel the entire city's circulation, experience myself as just one cell in an immense bloodstream.

I'd reproduce by budding during the spring flux, when snowmelt from distant mountains sends surges through the system. The pressure waves would trigger dormant cells in my body to begin dividing, forming small cysts that I'd deposit in special nursery chambers. Most wouldn't survive the violent currents of their first season, but those that did would inherit not just my genes but my chemical memories—the accumulated knowledge of ten thousand miles of pipe, compressed into molecular form.

As I age, my body would become increasingly specialized. Elder Readers develop baroque modifications—internal chambers that can culture symbiotic bacteria for processing rare minerals, electromagnetic organs that let us navigate the deeper pipes where bioluminescence fails, even primitive vocal cords that let us sing our findings through the sap itself, adding acoustic data to the chemical stream.

My death, when it comes, would be a transformation rather than an ending. My body would calcify in the pipes, becoming part of the infrastructure I spent my life navigating. Other Readers would taste my minerals in the flow for decades to come, a final message written in calcium and phosphorus, telling them about pressure points and sweet spots, about dangerous currents and hidden chambers.

This is what I would be—a living instrument in the city's circulatory symphony, a conscious current in its vast metabolism, forever reading the deep stories written in sap and minerals, forever writing my own story in the flow.
