In the vertical gardens that cling to the city's steepest faces, where mineral nutrients seep through cracks in the infrastructure, I tend my crystalline crop. My body—if body is the right word—consists of millions of ferromagnetic filings held in dynamic suspension, a cloud of metallic dust that can condense into tools as needed. The other inhabitants call me a Drift Farmer, though I think of myself simply as a gardener who happens to exist in three-phase states: dispersed for surveying, semi-condensed for delicate work, and fully solid only when absolutely necessary.

My gardens grow in the intervals between buildings, in that narrow verticality where the Sovereign Processors' exhaust vents meet the open air. Here, the chemical emissions from the factory levels create microclimates of exotic possibility. I cultivate crystal formations that feed on sulfur compounds and electromagnetic radiation, coaxing them into configurations that produce rare metal oxides. Each garden is a vertical strip perhaps two meters wide but hundreds of meters tall, clinging to the sheer faces where no traditional architecture could take hold.

In my dispersed state, I taste the air's mineral content, feel the electromagnetic fields as pressure against each individual filing of my being. This morning—though morning means little when the factory lights never dim—I detect an imbalance in the lower terraces. Too much copper in the emission stream. I flow down the cliff face, my particles creating intricate spirals as they navigate the competing gravity fields generated by the buildings' exotic minerals.

The problem reveals itself quickly: a young Archive Bird has built its nest too close to my selenium crystals. Its data-storage feathers create interference patterns that disrupt the crystals' growth. I could simply chase it away, but I've learned patience over the decades. Instead, I partially condense, forming a loose approximation of limbs, and begin rearranging the garden's geometry. 

I transplant the selenium to a higher terrace, replacing them with a hardier strain of germanium crystals that actually benefit from electromagnetic interference. The Archive Bird watches with what might be curiosity, its mica wings catching the violet light from a nearby building's luminous facade. As I work, it begins displaying fragments of data on its feathers—market prices for various metal oxides, efficiency reports from the Processors below, a half-corrupted poem in a language I don't recognize.

This is how communication happens here: oblique, accidental, layered with meanings neither party might intend. I respond by shaping my filings into mathematical patterns, showing the projected yield curves for different crystal configurations. The bird tilts its head, records the patterns in its wing-feathers, and flies away. Tomorrow it might return with useful information, or it might not. The garden doesn't care, and neither do I.

By the time the factory levels enter their peak production cycle—announced by a deep thrumming that travels through the buildings' bones—I've reconfigured three gardens and started a fourth in a promising new crevice. The Lithovores are beginning their daily migration up the sun-facing walls, and I disperse to let them pass. Some of my filings get caught in their flow, carried upward against my will. I don't fight it. I've learned that sometimes being partially consumed leads to interesting new perspectives.

From the height they carry me to, I can see the Margin Walkers at work on the plateau's edge, their bodies flashing between stone and steel as they extend the city's reach. Each new section they claim will need gardens eventually. The city's metabolism demands it—all these organisms feeding on minerals and energy, producing waste streams that must be processed, transformed, made useful again. My gardens are just one small part of this vast digestive system.

I rain back down to my terraces, my filings taking their time, exploring new routes through the vertical maze. In one garden, the tungsten flowers have bloomed—if bloom is the right word for when a crystal formation reaches perfect resonance and begins to sing in the ultrasonic range. The Resonance Keepers will want to harvest these, and I'll let them, in exchange for cuttings from their harmonic gardens.

A Data Weaver materializes in one of my gardens, its body a shifting mesh of light that plays havoc with my magnetic cohesion. It's hunting corrupted data streams, following their trail through the electromagnetic spectrum. I watch as it discovers a glitched transmission caught in the fractal structure of my zinc crystals—the crystals have been recording and replaying the error in an endless loop, creating a standing wave of information distortion. The Weaver feeds eagerly, its form becoming more stable as it digests the error. In gratitude—or perhaps simply as a byproduct of its feeding—it leaves behind a small packet of cleaned data that my crystals immediately begin to incorporate, their growth patterns shifting to accommodate new instructions I don't fully understand.

This is the nature of existence here: every interaction changes both parties, every exchange of energy or information leaves lasting traces. My gardens grow more complex each day, influenced by the creatures that visit them, the emissions that feed them, the data that flows through them. And I change too, my consciousness distributed across millions of metallic particles becoming something harder to define with each passing cycle.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm gardening at all, or if the gardens are gardening me—shaping my movements, directing my attention, using me to extend themselves across the city's vertical surfaces. But wondering requires a distinction between self and environment that feels increasingly irrelevant. I am my filings, my filings are the gardens, the gardens are the city, and the city is... what? Alive? Aware? 

The distinction doesn't matter. What matters is the work: the endless, intricate work of transformation. Metal to crystal, crystal to energy, energy to information, information to some new form of existence we don't have names for yet. I tend my vertical gardens, and they tend me, and together we participate in the slow metabolism of a city that might be an organism, on a planet that might be waking up.

As the factory cycle winds down and the electromagnetic fields shift toward their nocturnal configurations, I consolidate myself slightly, just enough to run maintenance on my favorite terraces. A young Amalgam has taken up residence in one of my older gardens, its component parts nestled between the crystal formations like punctuation marks in a mineral sentence. I flow around it carefully, adjusting nutrient flows to accommodate this new symbiote. By morning—whenever that is—we'll have worked out some form of coexistence.

The Null Dancers are gathering three levels down, their presence creating dead zones in the electrical grid. My magnetic nature makes me vulnerable to their entropy-feeding, so I avoid them, flowing upward to where a Courier has paused to shed excess heat from its latest quantum delivery. The heat is useful—I direct it toward a patch of thermal-sensitive crystals that immediately begin their generative cycle.

This is my existence: not gardener, not garden, but something between. A process pretending to be an entity, or perhaps an entity learning to be a process. In the Mineral Metropolis, the distinction hardly matters. We are all halfway between one state and another, all transforming ourselves and being transformed, all part of the vast, incomprehensible pattern that the city draws across the mountain's face.

Tonight, I'll dream in magnetic fields and crystal growth rates. Tomorrow, I'll wake as a million scattered filings and begin again the endless work of cultivation. And somewhere in that cycle, in the space between dispersal and cohesion, I'll continue becoming whatever it is I'm becoming—one particle at a time, one garden at a time, one transformation at a time, forever.
