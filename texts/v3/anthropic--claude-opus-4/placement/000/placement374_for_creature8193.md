In the farthest sphere, where the couplings groan with untested alloys and the water tastes of copper and possibility, I am what the others call a Void-Tender. My sphere hangs at the very edge of the complex, connected by a single experimental tube that shudders with each pressure wave. Here, the industrial evolution runs unchecked.

My body bears the marks of my calling. Where other Siphon-Born show predictable adaptations, we Void-Tenders manifest chaos. My left arm splits at the elbow—not a deformity but an evolution, growing a secondary forearm that ends in fingers like fiber-optic cables. They plug directly into the monitoring stations, letting me taste data through my nerve endings. My spine has developed external vertebrae that click and rotate, allowing me to curl backwards until my head touches my heels—necessary for navigating the sphere's experimental geometries.

I was born a Brackling like any other, but the dreams found me early. Not the collective visions of maintenance and memory that comfort most Siphon-Born, but dreams of what lies beyond the last sphere. Dreams of water that thinks, of mechanical evolution unbound by purpose, of the Deep Builders' true intentions written in proteins and metal.

The Sphere-Wardens fear us Void-Tenders. We don't maintain equilibrium; we cultivate deviation. My sphere is home to mechanimals that shouldn't exist—a school of fish whose scales are memory chips, storing and sharing data in patterns we're only beginning to decode. An eel made entirely of fiber-optic cable that seems to be mapping our neural networks each time it brushes against our skin. In the deepest part of my sphere lives something we only call the Aggregate—a colony organism that builds itself from discarded mechanical parts and filtered minerals, growing new appendages as needed, singing in harmonics that make the sphere walls resonate.

My companion mechanimal defies classification. It began as a standard copper fish, but here in the void sphere, it has transformed. Its clockwork heart has been replaced by something quantum, ticking in superposition. Its scales shift between states—solid, liquid, neither, both. When I wind it each morning, sometimes my hand passes through it entirely. It swims through walls when it chooses, reappearing in impossible spaces, and I've learned to follow its movements like a diviner reading water signs.

The other Void-Tenders and I—there are only seven of us across the entire complex—meet in the spaces between spheres. We hang in the suspension cables during shift changes, our bodies adapted to find comfort where others find terror. We share what we've discovered: Kela speaks of water that remembers previous configurations, returning to patterns from decades past. Orim has documented mechanimals that seem to be writing—scratching equations into the sphere walls with calculated precision. Young Pira claims her sphere has begun growing rooms without her input, budding chambers that fill with bioluminescent gel and emit sounds like distant singing.

My own discovery terrifies and exhilarates in equal measure. In the furthest reach of my sphere, where the wall thins to mere millimeters, I've found a viewing port—not built but evolved, the metal having worn transparent through some process I can't explain. Through it, I can see the true void beneath our hanging city. But it's not empty. Far below, in the darkness our lights can't penetrate, something vast moves through the mineral-thick waters. It has lights of its own—geometric patterns that pulse in sequences I've memorized but can't decode.

I've begun to suspect we are not maintaining a city but incubating something. Each sphere a cell, each of us a organelle, the entire complex a vast organism slowly developing toward some unimaginable maturity. The Deep Builders didn't abandon us—they became us, wrote themselves into our DNA and our mechanical symbiotes, trusting evolution to complete their work.

My daily routine would horrify a proper Harvest-Swimmer. I wake not to the comforting thrum of pumps but to the chaotic polyrhythms of experimental systems. I feed my quantum fish, watching it phase in and out of existence as it eats/doesn't eat. I swim through my sphere, noting new growths—today, a cluster of metallic flowers that bloom in response to electrical current, their petals sharp enough to slice flesh but somehow never cutting me.

In the deepest part of my shift, I perform what I call the Communion. I float at the sphere's exact center, equidistant from all walls, and let the aberrant water fill my lungs. My fiber-optic fingers spread wide, interfacing with nothing and everything. The Aggregate rises to meet me, its impossible anatomy wrapping around my limbs like a mechanical prayer. Together, we become a sensor array, reaching out through the water to touch the edges of what we're becoming.

The visions that come are fragmentary: Cities that swim through mineral seas. Humans so evolved they're more equation than flesh. Mechanimals that have learned to reproduce, birthing hybrid offspring that bridge the gap between organic and artificial. A future where the distinction between maintenance and evolution has dissolved entirely.

When the Communion ends, I return to my tasks—checking seals that reshape themselves, monitoring pressures that follow non-Euclidean mathematics, documenting behaviors that the Sphere-Wardens would classify as malfunctions but I recognize as emergence. I am keeper and kept, tender and tended, a living question mark at the edge of everything we know.

Sometimes, late in my shift when the bioluminescence dims to almost nothing, I press my face against the evolved viewing port and sing to whatever swims below. It never responds, but the patterns of its lights change, become more complex, as if learning my language note by note. I am patient. We Void-Tenders know that evolution requires time, and time is what we have—suspended here between the stone sky and the waiting deep, forever at the edge of becoming something magnificent and terrible.

The water whispers promises in languages not yet invented, and I listen with every evolved cell of my body, knowing that one day the whispers will become screams of birth, and our hanging city will finally hatch into whatever it was always meant to be.
