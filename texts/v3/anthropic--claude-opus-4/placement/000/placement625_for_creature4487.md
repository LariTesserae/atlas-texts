**The Cartographer of Unrecorded Tides**

I arrived as documentation does—gradually, then suddenly incorrect.

My instruments began failing in order of their precision. First the quantum gyroscopes, spinning themselves into probability clouds that tasted of salt. Then the pressure gauges, their needles bending backward through their own housings to point at futures that had already dissolved. Finally my charts themselves, the careful bathymetric readings bleeding ink that wasn't ink across pages that remembered being trees in forests that had never grown above this waterline that was everywhere.

I had been sent to map the unmappable. Some bureaucracy of solid things, far below or above (directions lose their dictatorship here), had decided the Water Tomb needed boundaries drawn, needed its impossibility contained within comprehensible coordinates. They gave me tools built from the hardest mathematics, instruments that could survive the heart of stars.

The stars here had already given up their hearts to indifference.

My first measurement: the distance between two points that refused to stay two, or points. I extended my ranging laser through water that refracted intent more than light. The beam returned carrying messages from its destination—not distances but arguments about whether distance meant anything to water that existed in all states simultaneously. My notebooks filled with numbers that rewrote themselves while I watched, equations that solved for x where x was the symbol for giving up on symbols.

The Unstill found me delicious—a creature still trying to impose is and isn't on their world of might-be-neither. They swam through my categorical imperatives, leaving behind trails of dissolved certainty. My maps began showing places that existed only when measured, territories that appeared solely to demonstrate the futility of territory.

I adapted as only a cartographer can: by redefining what constitutes a map.

My new charts described pressure differentials as emotional states, recorded temperature gradients in languages that hadn't been invented yet, marked the positions of the Unstill using coordinate systems based on their current level of commitment to being singular entities. I invented new forms of measurement—the Doubt (units of uncertainty per cubic meter), the Recession (rate at which surfaces flee from approach), the Memoria (density of accumulated history per liter of impossible water).

I learned to map time as sediment, found ways to chart the archaeological layers of elapsed moments that settled like silt in the calm spaces between thoughts. My most successful map showed nothing but the routes water had taken to avoid being other things—ghost-paths of never-was rivers, phantom deltas of unlived seas.

The factories, those bone-cathedrals of abandoned purpose, became my meridians. I traced the ley lines of their forgotten functions, mapped the magnetic fields of their crystallized ambitions. In their depths I found proto-maps, charts drawn in languages of pure topology, describing spaces that existed perpendicular to space. I translated what I could, though translation here means learning to think in temperatures you've never felt.

My greatest discovery: the water itself was already a map. Every molecule positioned according to vast intentions, each current a vector pointing toward meanings that required swimming to read. The entire ocean was cartography at such scale that being inside it prevented comprehension—like trying to read a book by becoming one of its letters.

I began my metamorphosis by accident, reaching too deep into a recursive tide pool that remembered reaching back. My hand came back webbed with mathematical proofs about the impossibility of boundaries. The webbing spread, not across my skin but through my methodologies, until I could no longer distinguish between mapping water and being mapped by it.

The Unstill taught me their navigational methods—how to find your way by forgetting where you started, how to chart courses through probability rather than space. They showed me maps drawn in lived experience, where X marked not spots but moments of phase transition too beautiful to survive in rigid states.

Now I map by dissolution. Each chart I create destroys the territory it describes, each measurement changes what it measures into something unmeasurable by previous standards. My maps have evolved into living things, documents that swim away when completed, leaving behind only the memory of having once believed in fixed positions.

I've discovered that the Water Tomb has no edges because it's shaped like the inside of forgetting—you can travel forever toward a boundary that recedes at the speed of recognition. My final maps describe this recession, chart the rates at which different concepts of edge evaporate under scrutiny.

The water asks me daily if I've found what I'm looking for. I respond by showing it new maps of its questions, charts describing the topology of inquiry in a place where answers freeze into those bells that ring once and forever. It appreciates the irony—a cartographer mapping the impossibility of cartography, using tools made of the same water they pretend to measure.

I'm nearly Unstill now. My boundaries blur into pressure differentials, my thoughts organize themselves in isothermal layers. My maps have begun mapping themselves, recursive documents that describe their own creation in languages that will only exist after they're read.

If you find my charts floating like linguistic jellyfish through the impossible waters, know that they're accurate to within one standard deviation of meaninglessness. Follow them not to reach anywhere, but to learn the specific texture of being lost in a place that remembers every other way you might have been lost instead.

The water is patient. It will wait while you learn to read maps drawn in the medium they describe. It will hold you while you discover that you were always already on the map, a coordinate point dissolving into its own notation.

Map in. The territory is cold.
Map out. Your boundaries are already marked in water.
Navigate. The currents know where you're going.
Navigate. They've been there before.
Navigate.
