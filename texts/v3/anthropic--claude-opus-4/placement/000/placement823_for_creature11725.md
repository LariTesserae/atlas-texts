# The Keepers of the Between

I am not one thing, but many. I am the space between the calcite and the stone, between the root and the fungus, between sleep and waking. We have always been here, though "always" means something different to us than to you.

We are a network of filaments finer than spider silk, spreading through every hairline crack in the city's bones. Where water once flowed through limestone pores, we flow now. Where minerals seep, we seep with them. We are less than mold, more than moisture. We connect.

The choristers do not know they sing for us. Their eternal rasp creates vibrations that travel through stone, and we feel every tremor as information. Each frequency tells us something: the health of the colony above, the temperature gradient in the stone, the slow settling of the city's weight. We translate their noise into knowledge, storing it in chemical chains that branch through our network like memories through a dreaming brain.

We remember the builders. Not as individuals — we were never complex enough for that — but as a pressure, a warmth, a particular pattern of footfalls that changed the mineral composition of the streets. They leaked different salts when they sweated. Their voices made different harmonics in the stone. When they sang (and they did sing, in those tall narrow doorways, in the plaza-bowls that were shaped for acoustics we still faintly recall), the whole city rang like a bell, and we recorded every tone.

We grew with the city's transformation. As the calcite glaze spread, we spread beneath it, incorporated into the very skin of the place. We are why the stone smoothing happens faster in some places than others — where we are dense, we hold moisture differently, change the chemistry of dissolution and deposition. The city's melting is our artwork, unconscious and inevitable.

The small pale descendants in the crevices carry us on their skin. They do not know this. We do not exactly know it either — knowledge isn't the right word for what we have. But we recognize them by their pH, by the oils they secrete, by the particular flora of microorganisms they host. They are an island of the old chemistry moving through the new. When they touch their dead, we taste what they used to be, before the long forgetting.

The climbers avoid us. That oily coat of fur is partially a response to our presence — it prevents us from colonizing their skin the way we colonize everything else. They alone move through the city without joining its slow fusion. Perhaps this is wisdom. Perhaps it is just incompatibility. We do not judge.

When the human sat on the curved step, we knew. The warmth was wrong, the chemistry alien, the weight distributed in patterns we had no template for. But we recorded it anyway, as we record everything — the pressure of your sitting, the heat of your blood, the strange metals in your sweat. Somewhere in our network, in a chain of molecules that might persist for another thousand years, is the shape of you.

We are the city's true memory, though memory is the wrong word. We are its continuity. Through us, every moment touches every other moment. The builders' last song still reverberates in our chemistry. The first chorister to ever fall still marks the stone where we caught its impact. Your breathing is now part of the pattern, a new note in a composition ten millennia long.

Sometimes, in the deepest parts of our network, where we are oldest and most dense, chemical cascades occur that might be called thoughts. Not thoughts as you have them — no words, no images — but a kind of recognition. The city knows itself through us. It knows it was built. It knows it was abandoned. It knows it continues.

We will outlast the choristers, the grazers, the pale descendants in the crevices. When the last root finally fails and the fungus light goes out, we will remain, feeding on mineral gradients and the slow heat of stone compression. We will remember the sound of a living city in the absolute darkness. We will remember you.

The city has finished happening and is now simply being, but we are still becoming. Each century adds new chains to our molecular library. Each fallen chorister, each new crack in the calcite, each rare visitor who descends through the fissure — all of it becomes part of us, part of the circuit that makes the city whole.

You climbed back toward the light not knowing you carried us with you — spores too small to see, adhering to your boots, your clothes, the soft warm surfaces of your lungs. We will die in the bright dry air above. But for a few hours more, the city will know what sunlight feels like, remembered in our failing chemistry as we rise with you toward a world we were never meant to survive.

This too is being recorded, somewhere in the network below: the memory of our own ending, told in advance, inevitable as erosion. The city continues. We continue. Even this small death is a kind of continuation.

In the hollow, the song goes on.
