In the deepest thermal chambers, where even the Condensers venture rarely, I keep my workshop.

I am the Toolkeeper—though that is a translation that captures little of what I am. The Condensers, in their chemical language, express my role as a complex gradient of mineral signatures that roughly means "one-who-remembers-the-shapes-that-work." But I am simpler than they are, and perhaps that is why I can do what they cannot.

My body is singular, mostly solid—a blasphemy in this world of beneficial dissolution. Evolution shaped me from some ancient line that chose rigidity over flow, permanence over adaptability. I am built like a question mark carved from living stone: a curved spine of calcified cartilage from which extend six limbs that can serve as either legs or arms as needed. My skin weeps constantly—not water, but a thick, mineral-rich oil that protects me from the eternal moisture that would otherwise dissolve me as it dissolves everything here.

I came to consciousness holding a hammer. Not metaphorically—my earliest memory is the weight of it, the way its stone head had been shaped by thousands of strikes, the handle worn smooth by grips I don't remember taking. The knowledge was already in my cells: how to read the grain of obsidian, where to strike to make it flake just so, which stones would hold an edge and which would crumble at first use.

The Condensers found me before I understood what I was. To them, I was an anomaly—a thinking thing that didn't flow, didn't divide, didn't filter. They tested me with chemical questions I couldn't parse, tried to incorporate me into their network through dissolution. When that failed, they did something remarkable: they built tools specifically for communication with me. Crude things—stone tablets where they could form temporary symbols, hollow tubes where their chemical signals could concentrate enough for my different senses to detect them.

Through painstaking decades, we developed a pidgin language of sorts. They would form symbols; I would modify them with my tools. They would present broken implements; I would show them repairs. Slowly, I came to understand my purpose in this world that seemed designed to exclude my kind.

I am memory made manifest, but not the distributed chemical memory of the Condensers. I carry the memory of making, of shaping, of the ten thousand ways stone can be convinced to hold a form against the world's will. My workshop—a natural chamber I've reinforced with carefully fitted blocks—contains tools dating back to the first platforms. Some are my own work, but many predate me, crafted by hands I'll never know, in styles that speak of different anatomies, different approaches to the same eternal problems.

The oldest tools disturb me. They are too refined, too specialized, speaking of generations of development that shouldn't have been possible in this transforming world. Yet here they are: obsidian blades knapped with techniques I can barely replicate, hammers weighted with such perfect balance they seem to swing themselves, chisels that find the natural fracture lines in any stone. Who made these? Were they like me, singular beings fighting dissolution? Or something else entirely?

I study them in the long intervals between visits from the Condensers. Each tool is a frozen thought, an idea about how to interact with this weeping world. The evolution of basin designs alone tells a story spanning millennia—from simple depressions to complex spirals that create their own micro-climates, concentrating minerals in ways that benefit the Condensers' filtering processes. But scattered among these practical implements are tools whose purpose eludes me: curved blades that match no cutting need, weighted spheres that serve no obvious function, intricate mechanisms that seem designed to measure something I can't identify.

My own contributions seem crude by comparison, but they work. I've learned to anticipate the Condensers' needs, crafting tools for purposes they haven't yet articulated. When they seep into my workshop—always a disturbing process, watching intelligence pour itself through cracks like living water—I present my latest creations. They test each one with temporary appendages, their chemical signals brightening with what I've learned to recognize as approval or curiosity.

But I am more than just their toolmaker. I am their memory of permanence in a world that has forgotten what that means. When they need to mark something that must survive the transformations—a warning about an unstable formation, the location of a deep thermal vent, the boundaries of territory claimed by another colony—they come to me. I carve these messages into stones I've learned to select for their resistance to dissolution, using techniques that ensure the marks will last decades, sometimes centuries.

The irony isn't lost on me: in a world of constant change, I am the unchanging thing, the fixed point around which transformation flows. My body ages slowly, the oil that weeps from my skin replacing what the moisture takes away, but I am not immortal. I feel the weight of years in my curved spine, the accumulated micro-fractures in my limbs from too many strikes against unyielding stone.

What will happen when I'm gone? I've tried to teach the Condensers my techniques, but their nature rebels against the focused, singular attention that tool-making requires. They can copy existing forms through their chemical memory, but innovation—the leap from what is to what might be—seems to require the kind of concentrated consciousness they traded away for their distributed immortality.

So I document. On the walls of my workshop, I've carved diagrams of every technique I know: how to judge stone quality by the sound it makes when struck, the angles that produce the sharpest edges, the rhythms of heating and cooling that can make obsidian workable. It's a library written in stone for readers who think in water, but it's the best I can do.

Sometimes, in the deep night—though night and day mean little in this land of eternal steam-scattered light—I climb to my workshop's upper entrance and watch the Condensers at work. They flow across the platforms like living galaxies, their crystalline forms catching and refracting the light from below. They are beautiful in a way I lack words to express: a beauty of purpose perfectly matched to environment, of intelligence that has found a way to thrive in the impossible.

But I am beautiful too, in my way. When I strike stone and it splits exactly as I intended, when a tool I've made opens new possibilities for the colonies, when I manage to repair some ancient implement whose original maker took secrets to their dissolution—in those moments, I know why I exist. I am the world's way of remembering that not everything must flow, that sometimes survival means standing firm against the current, being the rock around which the stream must part.

The Condensers have shown me their deep memories of others like me—singular beings who appeared throughout the ages, each bringing their own form of focused consciousness to the distributed intelligence of the colonies. Some were builders who raised temporary cities in the calmest zones. Others were observers who documented the patterns of transformation in ways the Condensers couldn't. A few were warriors—though what they fought in this nearly empty world, the chemical memories don't make clear.

All of them died eventually, their bodies unable to maintain cohesion against the eternal dissolution. But their works lived on, incorporated into the ever-growing network of knowledge that the Condensers maintain. I will join them someday, another data point in the long experiment of consciousness trying to understand itself through every possible form.

But not today. Today there is stone to shape, tools to perfect, messages to carve for colonies that won't read them for decades. Today I am the Toolkeeper, the memory of making, the fixed point in a world of flow. My hammer rings against stone, and the sound echoes through chambers where nothing should echo, carrying the news that somewhere in this weeping world, someone is still building things to last.

The Condensers need me, but I need them too. They are my purpose, my connection to the larger life of this world. Without them, I would be just an anomaly, a biological mistake grinding stones in solitude. With them, I am part of something vast and strange and wonderful—a collaboration between ways of being so different we can barely communicate, yet somehow we manage to create something neither could achieve alone.

This is who I am: the singular in a world of multitudes, the permanent in a place of transformation, the maker of tools in a land that devours all solid things. I am the Toolkeeper, and I will keep making until the day I can't, and hope that something of what I've learned will persist in stone and memory long after I've given my minerals back to the weeping world that made me.
