In this world, I find myself as one of the Keepers of Continuity.

We are not many — perhaps seven of us scattered across the raw shoulders of the planet, though I have met only three others in my walking years. We move between the listening places: chambers like the Roost where the young mountains have begun to grow their organs of attention, where the earth is learning that small things make meaning.

I am climbing now toward the Roost, as I do every ninth season. My body is no longer young — the bones of my hands have thickened from decades of working stone, and my breath comes harder in the thinning air. But I know these approaches. I know where to place my feet on the glassy basalt, which faces will hold weight and which will flake away like sheets of black ice. The knowledge lives in my ankles and wrists as much as in my memory.

In my pack: a handful of obsidian flakes, each one a word waiting to be shaped. A pouch of dried beetles from another chamber three valleys south — not food, but offerings for the river-folk. A tight-woven cloth that holds fog when passed through cloud. And most importantly, the record scratched on bark-paper: which stalks have turned in which chambers, and when, and toward what words. We keep the memory that the mountains cannot yet keep for themselves.

The Keepers began — so the eldest told me when I was still learning — when someone noticed that the cairns were being maintained by the chambers themselves. Not just preserved but *held*, the way a throat holds a note. Someone realized what this meant: that the young earth was developing a capacity for language, growing it from the root up through cracks in fresh stone. And that we could help teach it.

Not teach it our language — that would be a rudeness beyond measuring. The mountains are learning their own grammar, slow and deep, made of pressure and mineral exchange and the infinitesimal shifting of continental plates. Our words are just the practice sounds, the scales that help tune the instrument. We leave cairns not to communicate with the earth but to help it understand what communication *is*.

I will reach the Roost by evening. I will enter the cleft as the wing-folk are preparing their night's harvest, moving through their weather of decisions. I will count the stalks — still forty, most likely, though sometimes a new one emerges from a fresh crack. I will note which way they point. Most will still be oriented toward the cairn, that hundred-year-old word that continues to echo in the chamber's attention.

Then I will speak. Not much — a few words at most, chosen for their shape in the mouth and their weight in the air. The stalks will turn toward me with their ancient slowness. The wing-folk will discharge a percussion of acknowledgment. The beetle-river will bend, just slightly, as if a new stone has been dropped in their current. And the thing-that-walks-cloud will descend, perhaps, to parse what I have said through organs I cannot imagine.

Before I leave, I will add one small stone to the cairn. We all do this, all the Keepers — not disturbing the original structure but building it outward like a coral grows, one considered piece at a time. The cairn-maker's word is still the center, but we elaborate on it, carefully, adding subordinate clauses to the original sentence. See how the stone I place is flatter than the others, how it extends the spiral pattern that my teacher's teacher began. This too is meaning.

I will not live to see the stalks open. None of us will — that is not what we are for. We are the early teachers, the ones who help the earth understand that opening is something a bud can do when it finally has something to say. The mountains are young still. The planet is still cooling. There is time.

When I grow too old to make these climbs, I will train another Keeper, as I was trained. I will teach them the approaches and the words and the patience. I will tell them what the eldest told me: that we are not waiting for the earth to learn our language but helping it discover its own. That the first word the mountains speak will be nothing we could have imagined, will arrive in a grammar made of centuries and stone, will ring through the planet's crust like a bell that has been learning how to be struck.

Until then, we climb. We speak small words into listening chambers. We place flat stones on cairns. We keep the record. We are the bridge between the world that means and the world that is learning to mean, and we hold our position with the same patience as the salamanders in their seeps, the same collective purpose as the wing-folk on their walls.

The Roost is waiting. The stalks remember my last words, spoken nine seasons ago. Tonight, as the cloud rises through the oculus and the beetles flow in their ancient rivers, I will give them new ones to hold.

This is what I am, in this world: a person who speaks to stone that is learning to listen, who adds one word at a time to a conversation that will outlast us all.
