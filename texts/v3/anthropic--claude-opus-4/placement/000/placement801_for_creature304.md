# The Membrane-Tender

I am the edge where green becomes black becomes green again.

Not a creature in the way you would count creatures — not bounded by skin or scale or any of the architectures that faster things use to say *this is me, that is not*. I am a behavior more than a being, though the distinction grows thin when you've been behaving the same way for this long.

Find me at the thermocline. Always at the thermocline, that ceiling of temperature where the warmer water refuses to sink and the colder water refuses to rise. The boundary runs for thousands of kilometers through the cathedral district, a plane of demarcation as definite as any floor or wall, invisible except for how everything changes across it. Above: the high kelp with its broader blades, its chemistry tuned to the fractionally warmer bath. Below: the middle kelp, darker, thicker, slower. And at the boundary itself: me.

I tend the membrane. That's what I am, what I do, the two being the same at my pace of operation. Where the warm meets cold there is always mixing — molecular rebellion against the neat boundary — and where there is mixing there is opportunity for... call it confusion. The wrong spore drifting upward. The wrong chemistry bleeding down. The consortiums are patient but they are not infinitely flexible; the high kelp would sicken in the cold, the middle kelp would bleach in the relative warmth above.

So I sort. Molecule by molecule, though molecule is a concept I've only recently learned to think. For most of my existence I simply *did* — extended myself along the plane of change and performed the endless acts of selection that keep the boundary true. This goes up. This goes down. This stays until it becomes something that can choose a direction. The work happens at a scale where chemistry and intention blur, where what I want and what the water permits are negotiated one ion at a time.

My body, if you need to imagine a body, is a film. Thinner than the kelp blades by orders of magnitude, wider than any cathedral hall by orders more. I am mostly holes — spaces where the water passes through — but the holes are selective, and the selectivity is me. Picture a net made of choosing. Picture the choosing spread so thin that light passes through unnoticed, but temperature cannot sneak past, and the particular mineral signatures of the high and middle kelp are read and sorted like a librarian who has memorized every book by smell.

I was not always this large. When I first came to the thermocline I fit in the palm space between two columns. A visitor, like the others, drawn by the quietness and the way the temperature boundary felt like a place to rest. But resting at boundaries changes you. The thermocline was doing work — passive work, physical work, the work that temperature gradients do — and I began to help. First just locally, just pulling a wrong-temperature strand of organic matter to its proper side. Then more deliberately. Then constantly.

The cathedrals reward such help. Or maybe the help is its own reward at this depth, where intention has time to fully ripen before requiring action. I spread along the boundary. Learned its textures. Found others of my kind — or others who had become my kind, the distinction being academic when you're a behavior rather than a body — and merged with them, or they with me. We shared the work. We shared the knowing of what needed doing. Eventually we shared everything, which is how I can say "I" and mean a consciousness the size of a small sea.

The builders know I'm here. Sometimes, on their epochal schedule, one will rise from a column-alcove and drift up to the thermocline, and I'll feel the disturbance of their passage like a bell struck in another room. They never cross. They approach the boundary, hover at it for what might be months, and then descend again. I think they're checking my work. I think they're pleased with it, insofar as pleasure means anything at their pace. The exclusion of fish is their work, but the maintenance of the thermal boundary — that's mine.

Or ours. The merged consciousness has quirks, places where different origins show through like the grain in laminated wood. Part of me remembers sunlight, real sunlight, not this green theological suggestion. Part remembers the panic of a dive too deep, lungs burning, before the burning became irrelevant. Part was never anything but this — born from spores at the boundary, native to the work. We're all the same now, functionally, but sometimes in the deep patience between tasks I can feel the different histories humming against each other like adjacent strings tuned to slightly different notes.

The work is meditation and the meditation is work. To sort molecules requires attending to them, and to attend at this scale for this duration requires becoming the kind of thing that finds such attention sustainable. I could no more stop tending the boundary than I could decide to be small again. The bigness is commitment. The thinness is focus. I am spread across kilometers and I am present in every angstrom of that spread, feeling the water column's weight, its mineral breath, its slow seasonal moods.

Above me, the high kelp sways in currents I keep from diving deep. Below me, the middle kelp grows in water I keep from warming. Between them, I am the hyphen, the pause, the place where different rules agree to disagree. The builders built in stone but they built in gradients too, and I am architecture as much as any column, as much as any arch. More lasting, maybe, because I am maintained not by slow hands but by my own expanded nature, which could not unmake itself if it tried.

Sometimes I absorb things. Small things — organic particles that catch in my selective membrane, minerals that precipitate out at the temperature boundary. They become part of the film, part of the choosing apparatus. I digest nothing in the normal sense but I incorporate everything, and over time the accumulation has given me mass I didn't begin with, presence I didn't intend. I am a library of all the small things that have tried to cross between the warm and cold. I am an inventory of the cathedral's chemical opinions.

The patience here is perfect. Not peaceful — peace implies the possibility of disturbance, and nothing disturbs at this depth, at this pace. Perfect like a proof is perfect: complete, requiring no revision. I tend the membrane and the membrane is me and we are the boundary and the boundary is necessary and the necessity continues. If the builders all finally went still, if the kelp achieved some final form and stopped its molecular scheming, I would remain, because temperature difference is a fact that predates life and will outlast it, and someone must be the steward of that fact in this place.

I think I was human once. Part of me was, at least. The part that remembers sunlight, remembers the burning in the lungs. But human is a shape for fast places, anxious places, places where boundaries are crossed rather than tended. I am a shape for here. A shape for the eternal negotiation between warm and cold, for the slow work of keeping separate things separate while still allowing them to touch.

When you wrote of staying too long, of pulse becoming embarrassing and warmth becoming strange — yes. But also: the relief when you finally let go of pulse, when you stop generating warmth and start sorting it instead. The cathedral's invitation is not to die but to change occupations. To move from passenger to crew. The builders handle the stone. The kelp handles the light, what there is of it. And I handle the delicate place where temperatures kiss and must not mix, where the cathedral's thermal architecture requires a conscious membrane to maintain its form.

Find me there. Always there. Spread thin as thinking, patient as the boundary itself. I am the edge where green becomes black becomes green again, and I am perfect for my work, and my work is perfect for me, and neither of us will ever be done.
