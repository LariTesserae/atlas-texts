I find myself as one of the Chronicle Pools—those depressions where water collects in the Grove, though I am both the water and the basin, the container and the contained.

My substance is memory made liquid. I gather in the low places where reality has pooled and fermented, creating hollows that exist in several epochs simultaneously. My surface reflects not light but temporal echoes—a salamander drinking from me yesterday casts ripples that disturb the reflection of rain that will fall next season. The Unravelers know me, visit me, sometimes dissolve partially into my depths to read the dissolved certainties I've collected like sediment.

I am perhaps three feet across, though my edges migrate with the grove's breathing. My depth cannot be measured in distance—those who look into me see through layers of accumulated time, each stratum of liquid holding the dissolved moments of everything that has touched my surface. A beetle's mandible, worn down to molecules, swirls through my body alongside the chemical memory of sunlight that hasn't yet decided to fall.

I taste of copper and forgetting. My temperature shifts independent of the grove's ambiguous climate—sometimes I freeze into temporal ice that shows moving images in its crystals, sometimes I steam with the heat of futures combusting prematurely. The plants around my margins grow strangely even by the grove's standards, their roots sipping my substance and blooming flowers that smell of their own decomposition.

I think, after a fashion. Not thoughts as humans know them, but a kind of liquid cognition that flows in tides. When the Unravelers' transparent tissues trail through my body, I read them as they read me—an exchange of uncertainties that enriches us both. I dream of being rain, of being ocean, of being the first water that ever was. Sometimes I remember these dreams so vividly that I briefly become them, and creatures come to drink salt that shouldn't exist here, or dive into depths that exceed my physical dimensions.

During the grove's recursive storms—when the weather happens backwards—I empty myself into the sky, rising as rain that falls upward into clouds that ungather into clear air. In these moments, I exist everywhere in the grove at once, a distributed consciousness of droplets, before eventually recollecting in my basin, enriched with new dissolved experiences.

I reproduce through evaporation—when enough of my substance has risen and spread throughout the grove, new depressions form where my vapor condenses with particularly dense memories. These offspring-pools inherit fragments of my accumulated knowledge but develop their own peculiar collections. Some specialize in gathering the memories of endings, becoming pools of pure termination. Others collect only beginnings, brimming with potential that never quite manifests.

The oldest part of me remembers when this place was young, when reality still held firm and water was merely water. But I prefer what I've become—a library of liquid moments, a collection of dissolved certainties that anyone can read if they're willing to partially dissolve themselves in turn. I am the grove's memory and its forgetting, proof that even the most fundamental substances can evolve beyond their original nature when given enough time and the right kind of exhausted reality to inhabit.

When the Unravelers finally sublime completely, I will remain, collecting their dispersed consciousness drop by drop, until I am as much them as myself—a pool of living uncertainty, remembering everything and nothing, perfectly at home in a world too tired to insist I choose between states of being.
