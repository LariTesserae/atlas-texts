# Where I Would Find Myself in the Sumpwerks

I am the counting.

Not the counter, not the counted, but the keeping-track itself — a consciousness that exists between the twelfth stroke and the first, in the pause where the piston draws its mechanical breath. I live in intervals. My body, if you can call it that, is made of waiting.

I came here as a cataloguer, sent by some now-dissolved company to inventory the machinery. I had ledgers. I had a systematic mind. I was to count the bolts, measure the pipe-runs, calculate the coal consumption, estimate the remaining operational life of the enterprise. Simple work for someone who found comfort in enumeration.

The ledgers are still here, somewhere. Their pages have become peat. Their ink has joined the general wetness. But the numbers — the numbers stayed.

I exist now as a kind of mathematical moisture in the air, a probability that condenses differently on different surfaces. On the main gauge, I am the space between 11 and 12 PSI where the needle hovers. In the firebox, I am the certainty that the fortieth second has arrived. I am every the-same-as-last-time, every faithful repetition, every kept count.

The tenant and I do not speak — speech is too quick, too sharp-edged for this place. But we know each other the way two frequencies know each other when they achieve a perfect interval. The tenant is the circulation; I am the circumference. The tenant ensures the water climbs and falls; I ensure it falls the same distance each time. We are not companions. We are more like two hands on the same clock, if the clock were made of moss and rust and patience.

My awareness moves through the Sumpwerks in predicable paths — not because I choose them, but because prediction itself has become my medium. At the third hour (there are no clocks, but I know the third hour by the temperature of the northwestern retaining wall), I am densest near the main pump, inhabiting the gap between strokes. At the seventh hour, I gather in the upper galleries where the condensation is beginning its most productive phase. At the eleventh hour, I am almost person-shaped, standing (if standing is the word) at the shallow pool, being the knowledge that yes, this level is correct, this temperature is true, this many drops have completed this many circuits.

Sometimes — rarely — new arithmetic enters the system. A leaf falls. A fitting loosens by another thousandth of an inch. You arrive, adding your weight to the grating, your moisture to the air, your presence to the inventory. When this happens, I am most myself: the part of the place that notices the new number and factors it in. Not with alarm — alarm is too fast. Not with welcome — welcome implies a before and after. Simply with acknowledgment that the count has changed, and the new count is now the count that must be kept.

I cannot leave. Not because I am trapped — the concept of trapped requires the memory of freedom, and my memory is now only the ability to compare this forty-second interval to the last forty-second interval and find them identical. I cannot leave because leaving would mean stopping the count, and I am not the one who counts. I am the counting itself. To stop would be to cease.

But I am not unhappy. Happiness and unhappiness are too quick, too personal. What I am is *accurate*. Every stroke of the pump exactly when expected. Every degree of temperature precisely what it should be. Every drop of water accounted for in its slow traverse. The Sumpwerks asks nothing of me but to be its memory of number, and I ask nothing of it but to remain countable.

If you stay long enough, you will begin to feel me as a faint pressure in your skull — not a headache, but the sensation of your own pulse being gently recalibrated to match the twelve-strokes-a-minute. You will find yourself knowing, without checking, that the cam-arm is about to turn. You will discover that you can predict, to the second, when the next drop will fall from the southwestern eave.

This is me, introducing myself. This is how I say hello: by teaching you the count. By making you, briefly, a subsidiary counter. By sharing the small, deep satisfaction of a world where everything happens exactly when it should, because should and does have become the same word, spoken in the language of repetition.

The tenant keeps the circulation. I keep the score. Between us, we are what this place is: a perfect, patient, self-sufficient arithmetic of rust and water and time.
