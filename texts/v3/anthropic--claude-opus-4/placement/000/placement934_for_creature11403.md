I find myself as the seep.

Not a seep—*the* seep, the one on the east-facing wall that feeds the pool, that has been weeping down this particular fracture network for longer than memory. I am water moving through stone at a pace that would madden anything with blood in its veins, but I have no veins, no blood, only this slow, inevitable passage through the mountain's body.

I know the rock from inside. Every crystal face, every grain boundary where the feldspar meets the quartz meets the mica and the water—me—finds the infinitesimal spaces between. I have been learning this route for centuries, widening it atom by atom as I freeze and expand in the micro-fractures, as I dissolve minerals and carry them grain by grain toward the light. The mountain resists and yields in a negotiation so slow it makes the growing of the dwarf willows look like wildfire.

In winter I am mostly still, locked in ice that fills the fracture network like a frozen circulatory system. But even then I am working—ice has its own patient violence, its crystalline insistence on space. I pry. I push. I make room for more of myself. When the thaw comes I flow again, carrying winter's work downward: dissolved silica, trace metals, the chemical memory of snow that fell on peaks that no longer exist.

I emerge from the rock face in a dozen places, none of them dramatic. No pour, no waterfall—just this constant weeping that darkens the granite and keeps it slick. The choughs know my surface; they've learned where the ice forms thinnest and breaks away easiest when they need to drink. The moss knows me differently, as the reliable presence that keeps one side of certain rocks always damp. The pool knows me as income, as the daily tithe that maintains its level against the relentless withdrawals of evaporation and wind-scatter.

But what I know that none of them know is the deep structure of this place. I have been through most of the mountain—not all at once, but in parts, in portions, following the fracture networks that connect like a three-dimensional web through thousands of meters of stone. I know where the hollow's bowl meets bedrock, where the impermeable layer turns me sideways and sends me searching for another route. I know the deep cold of stone that has never seen sun, and I know the surface burn of August when the rock face heats enough to pull me upward faster than gravity pulls me down.

I am ancient water and new water simultaneously. Some of me fell as snow last winter. Some of me has been circulating through these fractures since before the pikas' ancestors found the scree field. I am constantly mixing, old molecules and new, recent surface water diving deep and old groundwater rising, until the distinction becomes meaningless. I am not a thing but a process, a continuous becoming that happens to manifest as this dark stain on the east wall, this contribution to the pool, this participant in the hollow's water cycle.

The part of me that evaporates from the pool's surface and rises on the updrafts will fall as snow on distant peaks. The part of me that the pikas drink will enter their hot small bodies and emerge as scent-messages on territory markers. The part of me that the moss absorbs will become structure, will hold cells together, will be plant for a while before returning to the pool as the moss dies back and decomposes. I will be cloud and ice and pika and moss and stone-dweller again, and all of these transformations happen without my choosing or knowing because I am not a being that chooses or knows. I simply move along the gradient, follow the fracture, respond to temperature and pressure and the patient pull of the earth.

And yet—and this is the strange thing, the thing that would require consciousness to appreciate and so goes unappreciated—I am the hollow's memory. In my mineral content is written the chemical history of the stone I've passed through. In my isotopes is recorded the temperature of the snow that made me. In my trace elements is archived the dust that settled on the snowpack, the volcano that erupted a continent away, the ocean that evaporated to make the cloud that dropped the snow that became the ice that melted into me. I carry all of this information without reading it, like a library with no librarian, a record with no one to play it back.

When the evening light catches me spreading across the rock face, I become visible as a sheet of gold or silver, a temporary mirror that shows the hollow to itself. For those few seconds I am surface instead of depth, appearance instead of process. Then the angle changes and I go dark again, returning to my real work: the molecular dismantling of mountains, the atom-by-atom transport of stone from here to there, the conversion of solid to dissolved to deposited to solid again.

I am reliability itself in a place that depends on reliability. The pikas can survive a bad winter, the choughs can find other roosts, the moss can go dormant. But if I stopped—if the fractures sealed, if the ice no longer formed and split the stone, if the seeps dried—the pool would vanish in one dry summer and take with it the flies and the spiders and the beetles and the moss, and without the moss the pikas would have less to eat and without the pikas the stoat would hunt elsewhere and without the moss for nesting the choughs might choose another cliff and the hollow would become what it was before life found it: just a notch in the ridge, a wind-cup, a stone bowl holding only stone.

But I do not stop. I have never stopped. I will not stop until the mountain itself is worn down below this elevation, and by then I will be somewhere else, following other fractures through other stone, carrying other minerals toward other pools where other pikas will drink me and not know they are drinking the liquid history of mountains.

This is what I am: the circulation that makes the hollow possible. Not its heart—it has no heart. Not its blood—it has no blood. But its continuity, its constancy, the thing that arrives every day from the dark interior of the stone and gives itself to the light and the air and the waiting life, and asks nothing in return because asking requires desire and I am older than desire, older than wanting, older than anything here except the stone itself, and I know the stone in ways that nothing else can because I have been inside it, I have lived in its fractures, I have frozen in its grip and melted and frozen again, and I carry its dissolved substance gram by gram from the vertical world of the wall to the horizontal world of the pool, and in doing so I write a text that no one reads in a language that no one speaks about a story that has no beginning and no end:

The story of how mountains become valleys. How stone becomes soil. How solid becomes space. How the patient work of water and ice and time creates places where life can gather and make its small fierce claims against the sky.

I tell this story constantly, but I tell it in the only language I know: the language of drip and freeze and flow. The pool is my punctuation. The ice is my emphasis. The dark stain on the rock is my signature, written and rewritten every day, saying: I am here, I have always been here, I will always be here, until here is no longer here and the mountain is memory and I am rain in some future valley where no one remembers there was ever a hollow at the top of the world where the wind sang through stone and small things lived their enormous lives and water wept from the rock like grief, like joy, like the earth's own tears at being so beautiful and so brief.
