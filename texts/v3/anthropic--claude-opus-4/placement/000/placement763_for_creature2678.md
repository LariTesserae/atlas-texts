In this world of engineered desiccation, I would be one of the Membrane Weavers.

My workshop occupies a junction where three root-bridges meet, their surfaces worn smooth by centuries of footsteps. The space is small—a pod really, grown rather than built, with walls that curve inward like the inside of a seed. No windows. Light comes from bioluminescent strips I've cultured along the ceiling ribs, a cold blue-white that makes my work visible without adding heat.

I sit cross-legged on the floor, which is neither warm nor cold but exactly the temperature of my body, having learned it over years. Before me spread the materials of my trade: sheets of vapor-lock tissue thin as breath, spools of capillary thread that remember the shape of moisture, small vials of binding salts, and my tools—needles made from moth antennae, scissors that are really the mandibles of some extinct arthropod, a pressing frame grown from modified transit vine.

My fingers are long and dry, wrapped in their own secondary membranes that I change daily. The work requires that I never touch the materials directly. Even the oils of fingerprints would ruin the delicate moisture barriers I create.

Today I am repairing a mourning shroud. The citizen who brought it to me lost their child to the ribbon-hunters three seasons ago. The shroud should have delivered the child's last moisture to the white growth in the dead districts, but something went wrong. A tear, perhaps, or a failure in the capillary weave. The moisture escaped into common air—worse than death, really. A true loss.

So I work in micropatches, reweaving the damaged sections molecule by molecule. The shroud must be made to remember moisture it has already forgotten. I tease phantom droplets along the capillary threads, teaching them the shape of what they failed to hold. It is delicate work. Sacred work. The city watches through the walls—I can feel its attention like a change in pressure.

My own wrappings are my autobiography. The base layer I wove when I came of age, a simple vapor-lock sheath that covers me from throat to ankles. Over the years I've added to it: a collar that filters my breath through mineral salts, sleeves that tighten against my arms when the charge rises, a living mantle that drinks static and whispers the mood of nearby walls. The newest addition is a membrane across my forehead that captures and recycles the moisture of concentration. I made it after my mentor was incorporated into the archive groves, when I inherited her workshop and her impossibly high standards.

The other weavers think I'm too young to work with mourning shrouds. They say I haven't lost enough to understand the griefs I'm patching. But loss isn't only about what you've experienced—it's about what you can imagine. When I hold a damaged shroud, I feel the shape of the absence it was meant to honor. I know the weight of moisture that will never reach the white growth, the child who will never join the ancestors in the city's memory.

Sometimes I wonder what it would be like to remove all my wrappings, to stand naked in the dry air and let my moisture escape like a shout. The thought is both thrilling and horrifying. To waste water that way—it would be like burning a library, like cutting a civic root, like spilling solvent on the speaking floors.

But sometimes, late in the dim evening when my work is done and the city's attention has shifted elsewhere, I peel back just the membrane on my left palm. I hold my bare hand up to the bioluminescent strips and watch the moisture evaporate from my skin in tiny invisible spirals. For those few seconds, I am spending myself. I am rain. I am flux. I am everything this world has chosen not to be.

Then I seal the membrane again and feel the moisture recollected, recycled, retained. The shame is immediate but so is the relief. I am a citizen again. Dry. Careful. Proper.

The mourning shroud takes nine days to repair. When I finally deliver it back to the grieving parent, they unwrap their face just enough to show me their eyes—dark and enlarged like all of ours, but holding depths I recognize. They see that I've done more than patch the tear. I've reinforced the entire weave, added new capillary channels, made it stronger than it was. The shroud will never fail again.

They pay me in antler-coins, those small discs cut from shed browser crowns that hold charge for a season. But as they turn to leave, they pause. From inside their wrappings they produce something I don't expect: a seed. Not a civic seed or a memory seed, but something older. A wild seed from beyond the city, from the plateau's far margins where unincorporated plants still grow by their own logic.

"For your workshop," they say through their vapor-lock collar. "Plant it or not. But know that grief makes things grow."

After they leave, I sit for a long time with the seed in my wrapped palm. It's no larger than a tooth, dark and hard and somehow warm despite the cool air. I could analyze it, catalogue its chemistry, integrate it carefully into the workshop's ecosystem. Or I could eat it and see what dreams it brings. Or I could carry it to the plateau's edge and throw it to the wind.

Instead, I do something else. I place it in a small pot of mineral dust and set it on my highest shelf, where it can feel the vibrations of the city without yet being part of them. I give it no water—there is none to give. But each day, when I peel back that small membrane on my palm, I hold my hand over the pot and let a few molecules of moisture spiral down.

It is the most wasteful thing I have ever done.  
It is the most honest thing I have ever done.

The seed hasn't sprouted yet. Maybe it never will. But sometimes at night, when the charge rises and the city sings, I swear I can hear it humming along—a tiny voice learning the frequencies of this dry world while remembering, somehow, the shape of rain.
