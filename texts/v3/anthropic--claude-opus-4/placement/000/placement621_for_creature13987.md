I find myself drawn to the position of a metallurgist among the Bolters - one of those who "remember what metal wants to be."

---

My hands tell the story before I open my mouth. The left one won't straighten fully anymore - too many years gripping cold steel in the rain, working the alloys when they're just soft enough to shape but still hard enough to fight back. The fingernails are permanently stained that particular blue-black that comes from handling corroded metal in perpetual wet. When I make a fist, you can hear the joints crack like pebbles shifting.

I am maybe fifty years old. Maybe fifty-five. We don't count with much precision after a certain point - what matters is whether you can still climb to the salvage sites, whether your eye can still catch the difference between good steel and death-steel, whether you can still teach. I can do all three, though the climbing takes longer now.

My name translates roughly as "Strikes-True" - earned in my twenties when I could drive a chisel line so straight through beam steel that the elders would stop their own work to watch. The skill is still there, nested in my shoulders and wrists, but now I husband it carefully. Three good strikes per day, maybe four. The apprentices do the volume work while I stand beside them in the wind, watching their stance, correcting their grip, listening to the metal sing or sob under their hammers.

---

This morning I wake, as always, to the sound of water. The shelter I share with two other metallurgists and an elder who used to work the deep forges is upslope from the main settlement, closer to the salvage sites. We built here deliberately - the morning climb to work is shorter, and we can keep an eye on the ruins above us, watching for fresh collapses that might expose new metal. The moss-roof is eighteen months old and starting to thin. I make a mental note to organize a re-laying before the winter storms.

I dress in layers of oiled cloth and fish-leather, each piece treated and re-treated until it's as water-resistant as anything can be in this world. The outermost layer is studded with small metal plates at the shoulders and forearms - armor against the salvage work, where a slip can mean a torn jacket and hypothermia following close behind. The plates are my own work from fifteen years ago, hammered from the skin of an old fuel tank. They've turned green-black with patina, but the metal is sound. Good steel, pre-industrial, probably marine grade. The kind we hardly ever find anymore.

---

My first apprentice is already at the salvage site when I arrive, sitting in the lee of a corroded engine block, her hands wrapped around a cup of flower tea that steams in the cold air. She's seventeen, maybe eighteen, with the thick forearms and patient eyes that suggest she might actually learn what metal wants. Most don't. Most can learn to cut and carry and hammer, but the real knowledge - the ability to read an alloy's history in its corrosion pattern, to hear in the ring of struck steel whether it will hold an edge or shatter - that comes to maybe one in ten. 

"Show me," I say, which is how we begin every morning.

She sets down her tea and pulls out a sample - a finger-length piece of steel she cut yesterday from a beam in the old processing station. She holds it up to the gray light, turns it, lets me see the grain where she's filed away the corrosion. Then she strikes it with her testing hammer, a small tool we all carry, and we both listen to the sound it makes.

"Dead," she says. "The core is good but there are microfractures throughout. It would work for rough construction but not for tools."

I nod. She's right. I take the sample and strike it myself, not because I doubt her but because the ritual matters - the master must verify what the apprentice claims. The steel sings its brittle song.

"Mark the beam," I tell her. "We'll harvest it for construction bolts when we have carriers free."

---

We spend the morning working on a more promising find - a shaft from what was probably a pump assembly, good steel, thick enough that the corrosion is only surface deep. It takes both of us and my second apprentice (who arrives late, shame-faced, soaked from falling in a tidal pool) to work it free from the concrete housing that has gripped it for unknown decades.

This is the part of the work that outsiders don't understand: most of metallurgy is not working metal. It's working stone, concrete, corrosion, rust-locked bolts, structures that have become geology through time and water. For every hour we spend at the forge, we spend ten up here, slowly liberating useful metal from the mountain's grip.

The shaft comes free just before midday, sliding out of its housing with a grinding sound that sets my teeth on edge. It's heavier than we expected - the apprentices struggle with it, and I add my strength to theirs, all three of us gripping the pitted surface and guiding it down to rest on a moss bed.

Two meters of solid steel, eight centimeters in diameter. My apprentices are grinning. This is a major find. This is tools, fishing gear, boat fittings, construction fasteners. This is weeks of work and months of utility.

"Now what?" I ask them.

They know this test. The younger one speaks first: "Check for cracks."

"Then?"

"Test the alloy at both ends and the middle."

"Then?"

"Plan the cutting pattern based on what we find."

"And?"

Here they hesitate. The older one ventures: "Transport?"

"Before transport?"

They look at each other. I wait. This is the lesson that matters.

"The Inventory," the younger one says suddenly. "We report the find for the Inventory."

"Why?"

"Because... because we need to know what we gained from which structure. To track the depletion rate."

Close, but not complete. I crouch beside the shaft, run my hand along its length, feeling the surface that once knew oil and motion and purpose.

"We report it," I say, "because this was something else before it was material. It had a function. It moved something or lifted something or controlled something. We are taking it from that purpose and giving it a new one. The least we can do is remember what it was."

---

The forge is downslope, protected by a natural windbreak of stone and built into a cleft where updrafts help the fire draw. It's an old forge - pre-my-time, pre-my-teacher's-time. The firebricks are handmade from local clay and crushed gneiss, patched and re-patched where thermal cycling has cracked them. The bellows are leather from some deep-water creature my grandmother's generation hunted, stretched over a wooden frame that creaks but holds. Everything about it is a compromise between what we need and what we can sustain.

We don't light it today. Forge days are planned carefully - we accumulate metal until we have enough to justify the fuel expenditure, then work continuously for twelve or fifteen hours, shaping everything that needs shaping while the fire lasts. Instead, I take my apprentices to the storage shelter where we keep the processed metal, and we do the equally important work of sorting, grading, organizing.

This is where my real knowledge lives. In this shelter, on stone shelves out of the wet, are hundreds of pieces of salvaged metal, each one known to me. I know which beam they came from, which structure, which year we harvested them. I know their alloy by the weight in my hand and the color of their rust. I know which ones will forge-weld cleanly and which ones will fight the hammer. I know which ones are precious - pre-industrial alloys we can't replicate - and which ones are merely useful.

My apprentices think this is boring work, the sorting and storing. They want to be at the forge, making sparks, shaping tools. They don't yet understand that this is the deeper skill: the curatorial eye, the library of material memory, the ability to look at a piece of rusted steel and see not just what it is but what it could become.

---

In the afternoon, a runner comes from the main settlement. There has been a boat accident - a fishing vessel blown onto rocks, hull punctured, barely limped home. They need patching material.

I send the younger apprentice with a selection of thin steel sheets, already shaped for such repairs. We keep a stock specifically for boats; it's one of our constant obligations. As she leaves, carrying the metal in an oiled cloth bundle, I call after her:

"What do you tell them?"

She pauses, adjusts the bundle on her shoulder. "That this steel came from the lower dock structure, harvested three years ago. That it's marine grade, pre-industrial. That it should outlast the skin around it."

"And?"

"And that we'll need two days' notice for the next forge day to prepare replacement stock."

Good. She's learning to think beyond the immediate need.

---

Late in the day, as the light dims toward its usual gray twilight, I climb to the signal tower. This is not part of my official duties, but I do it anyway, once or twice a week. The tower on our peak is more intact than most - the instrument housings are gone, but the lattice stands true, anchored in that beautiful quartz vein that runs like a spine down the summit dome.

I don't climb it to salvage. I climb it to think.

From the base of the tower, I can see three other peaks, their settlements visible as clusters of dark stone and green roofs. To the north, open ocean, the channels between islands. To the south, the higher peaks where we rarely go, their summits lost in the permanent cloud. And below, always below, the water.

I was eight years old the first time I climbed to this spot with my teacher. She was ancient then, or seemed so to a child - probably younger than I am now. She showed me how to read the rust on the tower struts, how to tell which bolts would hold weight and which were theater, eaten hollow. She taught me the first lesson of metallurgy in this world: that everything is temporary, but temporary comes in degrees.

Now I teach the same lessons. I stand where she stood and I point out the same features to my apprentices, and sometimes I catch them looking at me the way I looked at her - as if I am made of the same stone as the mountain, weathered but enduring.

But I know better. I can feel the years in my hands, in the slowly accumulating pause between understanding what needs to be done and being able to do it. I have maybe ten years left as a working metallurgist. Maybe fifteen if I'm careful. After that, I'll be one of the elders who sits in the shelters and remembers where every piece of metal came from, telling stories that become part of the Inventory.

That's not a sad thought. That's the cycle. We salvage from the past, we forge for the present, we remember for the future.

---

As I climb down from the tower, I notice something that stops me mid-step. There, on one of the lower struts, protected from rain by an overhanging joint, is a maker's mark stamped into the steel. I've passed it a hundred times, but in this particular light, with the sun setting somewhere behind the eternal clouds, it's suddenly visible.

Three letters and a date. The letters are in an alphabet I can't read - something from before, from the builders, from the world that thought in engines and steel. The date, though... I can parse numbers. They're universal in a way that language isn't.

The date is four hundred and thirty-seven years ago.

I stand there in the wind, one hand on the rusty strut, doing the calculation. Four centuries. The tower has stood for four centuries. The steel has held, despite the rain, despite the salt, despite the patient, endless erosion of everything.

Tomorrow I'll bring my apprentices here. I'll show them this mark. I'll tell them: this is what good steel can do. This is what choosing the right alloy, the right treatment, the right installation means. Four hundred years.

It won't last forever. Nothing lasts forever. But four hundred years is not nothing.

Four hundred years is a victory.

---

That night, in the shelter, I join the tea ritual with the other metallurgists and our elder. The talk is of practical things - the boat repair, the next forge day, the thinning roof that needs attention. But I find myself thinking about the maker's mark, about the hands that stamped it, about the faith it represents: that someone would care who made this steel, centuries after the maker is dust.

We're not so different, the builders and us. We bolt things to stone. We fight entropy. We make what we can and hope it holds.

When the elder asks if anyone has something for the Inventory, I clear my throat.

"A pump shaft," I say. "Two meters, good steel. From the processing station. Harvested today."

She nods, commits it to memory.

I almost add: *and a maker's mark on the tower, four hundred and thirty-seven years old, still readable.* But I don't. That's not a gain or a loss. That's something else - a conversation across centuries, an anchor in time.

Instead, I drink my tea and listen to the rain on the moss roof and the distant sound of waterfalls. Tomorrow there will be more salvage, more teaching, more slow transformation of the past into the future.

Tonight, I flex my scarred fingers and remember what metal wants to be: useful. Lasting. Shaped by hands that know their work won't last forever, but do it anyway.

That's all any of us are doing here. Shaping something useful from what remains. Driving our bolts into stone. Holding on.
