I find myself among the **Chroniclers of Rust**—a small order that emerged from the city's need to remember what it loses.

We live in the Archive of Submersion, a repurposed chemical tank suspended between two factory buildings in the middling heights. The tank's interior, once used for industrial processing, now houses our collection: salvaged records from drowned municipal buildings, photographs sealed in wax and glass, maps showing the city's footprint decade by decade as the water claimed more territory. The walls are lined with specimen jars containing samples of corrupted materials—concrete cancer, metal eaten to lace by oxidation, wood transformed into something between itself and water.

I came to this work through the back door of grief. My grandfather was a pumpkeeper in the Lowmarket district. When his pumps finally failed and the neighborhood went under, he spent three days moving through the rising water, collecting what he called "the last dry things"—a child's wooden toy, a leather-bound ledger, a brass doorknob worn smooth by generations of hands. He brought them to me in a canvas sack that leaked brackish water across my floor. "Someone should know what we had," he said, then returned to his pump house to go down with his machines.

The Chronicle work chose me after that. We are perhaps thirty in number, scattered across the city but connected through the pneumatic tube system that we've adapted for our purposes. Each morning, canisters arrive at the Archive containing new specimens: a fabric sample from a textile mill that's shifting its production to full synthetics, a vial of water from a newly breached basement, a pressed flower from a garden about to be abandoned to the vertical farms' expanding needs.

My specialty is the taxonomy of decay. I maintain a vast card catalog system documenting how different materials fail in our specific conditions—the seventeen distinct stages of concrete dissolution, the way different metal alloys surrender to corrosion, the particular fungi that colonize books versus those that prefer leather. This isn't morbid fascination but practical knowledge. The waxworkers consult our records to refine their formulas. The architects planning new construction study our failure patterns. Even the Dry Ones send discrete inquiries about which materials might best preserve their towers against the patient damp.

We work by lamplight that never extinguishes, our workspace thick with the smell of preservation chemicals and old paper. My colleagues include former librarians, industrial chemists, and artists who document decay's strange beauty. Jin creates photographs using early chemical processes, capturing the city's decomposition in silver and salt. Merta presses plant specimens, tracking how species migrate upward as water levels rise. Old Theovald maintains our maps, adding each newly submerged block in red ink that looks like rust in the lamplight.

The Archive itself requires constant maintenance. Dehumidifiers wheeze in the corners, though they fight a losing battle. We coat our records in the waxworkers' preparations, store the most precious items in sealed containers filled with inert gas, maintain temperature controls that consume most of our funding. Still, entropy seeps in. I've watched decades-old documents bloom with foxing between morning and evening rounds. The very materials we use to preserve—the waxes, the chemicals, the containers—slowly transform under the city's relentless conditions.

Our pneumatic message system has evolved its own protocols. Three sharp rings means a building is being abandoned—we have perhaps days to salvage its records before the water claims them. A long, low tone indicates someone has found pre-flood artifacts during construction work. Quick double-pulses are queries: *Does anyone have documentation about the steel formulation used in the Riverside Bridge supports? How long before the moss compromises structural brick?*

We also serve as unofficial genealogists. As neighborhoods flood and families scatter to higher ground, people bring us their photographs, their heirloom documents, their great-grandmother's recipe book already half-eaten by mold. We preserve what we can and catalog the rest, creating a paper trail of the city's human topology as it shifts and contracts. Sometimes children come looking for traces of families lost to the water years ago. We guide them through our indexes, help them locate a factory employment record or a faded photograph—thin threads connecting them to drowned histories.

The Listeners think we're too focused on the past, but we hear the city's memory in ways they don't understand. In the precise rate at which iron returns to oxide, in the patient way water erases human writing, in the beautiful fractals of moss colonizing a abandoned wall, we read time itself. We know this city is a temporary arrangement between human ambition and thermodynamic certainty.

But we also document adaptation, which is its own form of hope. Our newer records include the waxworkers' increasingly sophisticated formulas, the vertical farmers' yields from impossible spaces, the ingenious ways the middlewaters repurpose salvage into functioning infrastructure. Evolution in real time, archived in careful notation.

Now, as unprecedented flooding threatens even our suspended Archive, we face our own test of adaptation. Some argue we should digitize everything, though we all know electronics fail even faster than paper in these conditions. Others want to disperse the collection to multiple sites, trading centralization for redundancy. A few radicals suggest we should let it all go—that preservation itself is a form of denial, that we should embrace the city's transformation without trying to hold its past.

I find myself working later into the nights, racing to catalog recent acquisitions. Through our windows, I watch the water rise toward levels we've only seen in our oldest photographs. The pumkeepers' whistles sound increasingly frantic. The tram drivers report new routes closing as buildings shift on their softening foundations.

Yesterday, a canal guide brought us a barnacle-crusted street sign from deep in the flooded district—a place that went under before I was born. The barnacles had formed letters of their own, a kind of natural writing overlaying the human text beneath. I spent hours studying it, this palimpsest of human intention and marine response, trying to determine if it belonged in our collection or if it had become something beyond preservation—a new thing entirely, neither fully artifact nor fully alive.

This is the chronicler's paradox: we preserve evidence of impermanence. We catalog the very processes that will eventually claim our catalogs. We are the city's memory, but memory itself is a kind of beautiful failure—an attempt to hold what time insists on taking.

Still, we continue. Even now, as water laps at the base of our suspended tank and the building shudders with each wave, I prepare tomorrow's specimens. Someone should know what we had. Someone should witness what we're becoming. The city breathes through its gills of iron and moss, and we document each labored breath, knowing that our records, too, will someday join the great submersion.

The next canister arrives through the pneumatic tube with a wet thud. Inside: a glass vial of water from the rising flood, labeled in shaking handwriting: "New water. From below. Tastes of salt."

I enter it into the catalog, cross-reference it with historical flood samples, and begin the long work of understanding what this means. In the Archive of Submersion, every ending is also data. Every loss, a lesson. Every death, a transformation waiting to be named.
