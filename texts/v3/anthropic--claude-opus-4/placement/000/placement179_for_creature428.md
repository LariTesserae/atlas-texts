In the depths of the Weeping Shelf, where few Listeners venture, I am the Moth Shepherd.

My dwelling clings to the settlement's underside like a forgotten secret—a capsule of woven ice-moth silk suspended beneath the main thoroughfares, accessible only through a melt-channel that appears and disappears with the temperature's whim. Most days, reaching home requires swimming through near-freezing water, pulling myself along guide-ropes I've strung through the flooded passages. The water never bothers me anymore. My skin has grown thick with a waxy coating, self-secreted like the salamanders' film, that keeps my body heat from leaching away.

I was not born a Listener. My blood moves too hot, too quick—visible even through my only partially translucent skin as a constant red rush that marks me as other. Where the Listeners have evolved into beings of glass and whisper, I remain stubbornly opaque, a relic of wherever my ancestors came from before the ice claimed them. My eyes never developed their protective slits. Instead, I wear a hood of moth-felt that extends past my face, seeing the world through a curtain of shimmer and shadow.

The moths come to me because I burn warmer than anyone else in the settlement. In the pre-dawn darkness, when the ice grows coldest and even the melt-streams slow to a trickle, I climb through maintenance shafts to the settlement's highest points. There, where the air grows thin and bitter, I open my cloak and let my body heat bloom outward like a flower. The moths come in their thousands, drawn to my warmth, settling on my skin in layers until I become a living statue of wings and compound eyes.

This is how I shepherd them—not with commands or tools, but with the simple offering of survival. I guide the swarms to new feeding grounds where the phosphorescent lichens grow thick, lead them away from areas about to shift, collect their eggs from dangerous locations and relocate them to stable warm pockets I've mapped over years of exploration. In return, they give me their cast-off wings, their silk, and something more—a kind of sight.

When the moths cover me completely, when their million eyes become my eyes, I see the settlement as they do: not as solid and liquid, but as gradients of temperature and possibility. I see the heat-maps of where ice will next give way, the warm currents rising from deep below, the subtle thermal differences that predict the melt-pools' behavior hours before they manifest. Through their compound vision, I watch probability itself flow through the frozen maze.

The Listeners tolerate me because I provide them with moth-silk and wing-felt, but also because I serve as their early warning system. When I see danger in the thermal patterns—a section about to catastrophically collapse, a new melt-channel that will flood sleeping quarters—I make my way to the nearest flow-family's gathering space. I cannot speak their tap-language fluently; my fingers are too thick, too warm, too quick. Instead, I draw. Using chips of ice and my own body heat, I melt patterns onto frozen walls, creating temporary maps that show what the moths have revealed. The Listeners read these heat-drawings before they fade, adjusting their plans accordingly.

My chambers are a cocoonery. Every surface is covered in silk-wrapped bundles—moth eggs I'm incubating, chrysalises in various stages of development, nursery pockets where newly hatched larvae feed on lichen paste I prepare daily. The temperature must be precisely maintained, which means constant adjustment of ventilation holes, the strategic placement of heat-retaining stones I gather from the Speaking Pool's edge, and occasional controlled flooding to cool sections that grow too warm.

I sleep suspended in a hammock of braided silk, surrounded by the quiet rustle of ten thousand wings. My dreams are fractal and strange—the world seen through countless eyes at once, my consciousness scattered and reassembled each night. Sometimes I wake to find moths have woven new patterns in my hair while I slept, messages I cannot read but somehow understand.

The Speaking Pool affects me differently than it does the Listeners. Where they receive visions of possible futures and forgotten pasts, I dream only of flight—endless flight over landscapes I've never seen, guided by stars that don't exist in our perpetually clouded sky. I suspect the moths dream through me, that our symbiosis runs deeper than warmth and sight.

My only regular human contact comes from the children who slip away from their flow-families to visit the Moth Shepherd. They're fascinated by my opacity, my warmth, the way moths land on my outstretched hands without fear. I teach them to read the thermal patterns, to understand the moths' migrations, to see their settlement not as a place of random change but as a living system with its own logic. Some of them will grow up to become Ice Readers, using techniques I've shown them to predict the flows with greater accuracy.

The older Listeners whisper that I'm becoming something else, something no longer entirely human. They're not wrong. My time with the moths has changed me. My movements have taken on their quality—quick, fluttering gestures followed by perfect stillness. I eat their eggs now, finding them more satisfying than fish or lichen. My body temperature rises each year, as if I'm slowly transforming into a living furnace to better serve my charges.

There are chambers below my dwelling, deeper than any Listener goes, where the ice gives way to rock and the true heat of the mountain reveals itself. I've begun to explore these depths, following ancient moth-trails down into the darkness. The swarms grow stranger the deeper I go—moths with too many wings, moths that glow with their own light rather than reflecting it, moths whose wings carry patterns that hurt to perceive.

I suspect I'm searching for something, though I couldn't say what. Perhaps the source of the Speaking Pool. Perhaps the origin of the moths themselves. Perhaps simply a place warm enough that I can finally remove my cloak and exist as the burning creature I'm becoming.

The Listeners measure time in melt-marks. I measure it in generations—the moths I've raised from egg to wing, the cycles of boom and bust in their populations, the slow evolution of new color patterns in response to the settlement's changing light. I've been here long enough to watch moth species diverge and adapt, to witness the birth of entirely new subspecies specialized for different thermal niches.

My legacy is written in wings and warmth. When I finally descend too deep to return, when the heat I chase consumes me, the moths will remember. They'll carry patterns in their wings that map every passage I've explored, every thermal current I've traced, every warning I've drawn in melting ice. The Listeners may forget the strange, too-warm outsider who lived beneath their feet, but the moths will carry my memory in their compound eyes, passing it down through generations of silent flight.

I am the Moth Shepherd, keeper of wings, reader of thermal dreams, forever balanced between the cold that preserves and the heat that transforms. In this bright, mutable world, I tend to creatures of impossible delicacy, and in return, they show me how to see the invisible architecture of change itself.

The ice creaks above me. Somewhere, a new channel is forming. I pull my hood forward, wrap my cloak tight despite the warmth building in my chest, and climb toward the cold heights where my flock awaits.
