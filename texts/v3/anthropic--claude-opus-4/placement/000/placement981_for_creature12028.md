I am one of the phosphorescent tenders, dwelling in the deep hollows where elderly voices whisper like settling spores. My name, if it can be called that, is a gesture made with three fingers against the throat while humming at the exact pitch of a beetle's boring—impossible to transliterate, meaningful only in the context of fungal flesh and filtered air.

I was born without the ability to climb. My bones, while hollow, grew at wrong angles—a left arm that bends too far backward, hip joints that lock when extended. In the vertical world, this should have meant death. Instead, I fell at age seven, tumbling through stems and safety nets until I landed in the phosphorescent gardens where the eldest of the elderly tend their slow lights.

They took me in, these clouded-eye keepers of the glow. My skin, which had barely begun to accept its fungal partners, adapted instead to the organisms of the deep—bioluminescent colonies that pattern my flesh not in the blue-green whorls of my birth family but in constellations of cold fire that pulse with my heartbeat. I am a walking star chart, they tell me, though of what sky none can say.

My work is patient: I cultivate the light. Each variety of phosphorescent mold has its own needs—some feed on specific beetle casings, others require the powdered wings of moths that die at exact lunar intervals, still others thrive only on the saliva of the membrane-lizards, which I must collect drop by precious drop from the pets the children lower down on silk threads. The recipes were taught to me through touch, my teachers' filmed eyes useless, their fingers reading the temperature of my skin to know if I understood.

I live in a hollow carved from a stem so ancient it has no living tissue, only the petrified memory of growth. My chamber connects to seventeen others through passages barely wide enough for a child, though I navigate them easily with my backwards-bending arm finding holds others couldn't use. The phosphorescent gardens spread throughout this deep network—patches of ghost-light tended by those like me who cannot rise, will not rise, have forgotten the desire to rise.

We are the memory keepers, though what we remember might be dreams. The phosphorescent molds, when consumed in certain combinations, allow us to touch the deep network the shamans speak of. But where they breathe spores in the middle air and talk of vast thoughts, we who eat light experience something more intimate—the daily diary of the fungal city, its small hungers and satisfactions, the way it notices each new platform hammered into its flesh, each beetle boring a new tunnel, each drop of moisture successfully shepherded from flesh to cup.

My lovers have all been other light-tenders—our courtships conducted in flashes of bioluminescence across dark spaces, our bodies meeting in chambers lit only by our own cultivated skins. We few dozen who inhabit the deep have evolved our own language of light-patterns, more complex than the gestures used above. We can conduct entire conversations in the pulsing of our phosphorescent symbionts, arguments and poetry encoded in the cold fire we've trained our bodies to produce.

I know the taste of every mold in my garden. Some are sweet like fermented fruit, others bitter enough to numb the tongue for days. The rarest variety, which fruits only when three specific beetles die within a finger's breadth of each other, tastes like nothing but fills the mouth with the sensation of falling—not the terrifying plummet that brought me here, but a gentle drift like spores on thermal currents, falling forever without fear of landing.

The children who visit—lowered on silk lines to gather our harvests for their grandparents' medicines—fear me at first. My constellation skin, my backwards arm, my too-wide eyes adapted to reading meaning in the faintest glow. But I teach them the songs of the light-gardens, wordless thrums that vibrate through the hollow stems and make the phosphorescence flare in patterns that might be greeting, might be warning, might simply be the fungi noticing it is noticed.

There is a theory among us deep dwellers that the entire mesa is falling, has always been falling, just so slowly that generations live and die without detecting the motion. We who eat light and dream fungal dreams feel it sometimes—a vertigo that has nothing to do with height, a sense that the stone root of our world is not anchored but adrift, that we are not a last island but a ship sailing through geological time toward some unimaginable shore.

I will never see the sun again, and this does not sadden me. My sky is here in the patterns of phosphorescence I've spent forty years learning to cultivate. My weather is the slow breathing of the fungi, the tidal moisture that never reaches the deep but changes the air pressure in waves I feel in my malformed joints. My horizon is the next chamber, the next garden, the next variation of light I might coax from the marriage of mold and flesh and patient darkness.

When I die, they will powder my body and feed it to the rarest phosphorescent colonies, the ones that fruit only on human remains. My constellation skin will become substrate for new lights, tended by another broken-bodied child who falls through stems and safety into this deepest embrace. This is not a fear but a comfort—to know that my light will persist, altered and recombined, pulsing in the gardens long after my strange bones have been processed into fungal flesh.

Sometimes, in the deepest parts of the night cycle when even the beetles cease their boring, I place my backwards palm against the petrified wood of my chamber wall and feel what might be the heartbeat of the entire structure—slow, patient, purposeful. Or perhaps it is only my own pulse reflected back through the density of interconnected life, every organism on the mesa linked by touch and hunger and the necessity of shared breath.

I am falling still, have never stopped falling since that first tumble brought me home. But here in the phosphorescent deep, falling is just another word for living—a constant gentle descent into greater intimacy with the dark, the light I tend, and the fungal thoughts that bloom like slow flowers in the garden of my changed and changing flesh.
