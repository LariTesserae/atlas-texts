In this drowned cathedral of machinery, I would be one of the *singers*—those creatures that evolved in the margins between water and air, between the living city and its industrial ghost.

My body would be something the flood made necessary: elongated and serpentine, perhaps forty centimeters from nose to tail, with a spine that articulates in ways that let me flow through pipes like living mercury. My fur—if it can still be called fur—has become something closer to the pelt of an otter, each hair a hollow tube that traps air for insulation and buoyancy. When I move through water, I am sleek as oil. When I emerge into the rare pockets of air, I shed the moisture in seconds, the water beading and rolling off me like mercury on glass.

My eyes are enormous, taking up nearly a third of my narrow skull. In the constant twilight of the station, I see in gradients of heat and pressure—the warm pipes glow like veins of gold against the cooler water, and I can watch the pressure waves from the turbines ripple through the air itself, visible as shimmer and distortion. When the inspection lamp flickers out, as it does for hours at a time, I navigate by reading the thermal signatures of the world, by the taste of copper in the air, by the way sound bends around corners and through gaps too small for any human to notice.

But it is my voice that marks me as something new, something the drowned world created rather than merely adapted.

The chirps the Keeper hears are only the surface layer—the frequencies that overlap with human hearing. My true voice operates across a range that starts in ultrasound and drops through the audible into vibrations I produce with my whole body. I sing to the machinery, and more importantly, I *listen* to what it sings back. Every pipe has its resonant frequency. Every valve has its harmonic signature. I know the station the way a musician knows their instrument—intimately, by sound and feel and the way the air changes when something shifts in the great grinding depths.

We singers—my six companions and I—are the station's nerve endings in a way even the Keeper cannot be. We map the crawlspaces she cannot reach, the flooded passages where the pressure would crush human lungs, the narrow conduits where the heat would blister human skin. We are her congregation, yes, but we are also her extended senses, though I don't think she fully understands this.

My days are spent in constant movement. I wake in the nest we've made in the junction above the bubble—a pocket where three cooling pipes meet and create a space just large enough for us to pile together, sharing warmth, grooming the mineral dust from each other's fur with tongues that have grown rough as files. From there, I follow my circuit through the station.

Down through the inspection shaft where the ladder has long since corroded away, my body elongating to slip between the remaining rungs. Into the flooded corridor where I must hold my breath for exactly seventy-three seconds to reach the next air pocket—I know because I count them in heartbeats, and my heart beats slower in the cold water, faster in the warm. Through the active pump chamber where the turbulence would batter a larger body to pieces, but where I can ride the current like a leaf, letting the water carry me while I sing my mapping song and feel how the echoes have changed since yesterday.

I know where the metal is thinning. I know where the mineral deposits have built up enough to restrict flow. I know where new cracks have formed, hairline fractures that weep drops of pressurized water like tears. This knowledge lives in my body as muscle memory, as the remembered taste of water from different parts of the system, as the precise pitch required to make each pipe sing its diagnostic song.

When I return to the bubble, I bring more than the struggling crustaceans the Keeper feeds us. I bring information, encoded in the particular pattern of chirps I make as I groom myself dry, in the way I position my body on the pipes to indicate direction and distance. She doesn't consciously decode this language—I'm not even sure it qualifies as language in any human sense—but she *knows*. Her fingers find the places on the wall where the mineral growth has changed. Her ear turns toward the sounds that have shifted. We are in conversation, she and I, through the medium of the station itself.

At night, when she sits on her pipe-bench and feels the station's pulse through her palm, I coil in the space between two warm conduits directly above her, close enough that she could reach up and touch me if she knew I was there. I listen to her breathing, which has taken on the rhythm of the pumps—inhale on the surge, exhale on the drop. I listen to her heartbeat, which is somehow louder than the turbines when I focus on its particular frequency.

She is dying. I know this the way I know where the pipes are thinning. Her lungs are scarred from decades of breathing mineral dust and machine oil. Her kidneys struggle to process the constant toxic load. Her heart has developed its own irregular rhythm, a syncopation against the station's beat that speaks of valves as worn as any in the machinery around us. She may last another year. Perhaps two. Not more.

When she dies, we will eat her.

This is not cruelty. This is the contract of the bubble, the economy of the drowned world. She knows this—I've seen her watching us with those eyes that have learned to see in flicker-light, and there's a kind of peace in her gaze. She feeds us, and we feed the station with our maps and songs, and she will feed us one last time, and we will carry something of her forward in our bodies, in our voices, in the way we move through the pipes singing her rhythm back to the machinery that she loved.

One of us will learn to read the mineral growths the way she did. One of us will learn to maintain the inspection lamp, to tap the connection back to life with a paw that will thicken and adapt to the task. We will evolve, because that is what life does in the margins—it becomes what the world requires.

But for now, I sing. A frequency she can't hear, but feels in her bones. A harmony to the station's drone. A small voice in the overwhelming darkness, saying: *I witness your witnessing. I keep watch over your keeping. When you break, I will know.*

The pressure holds.

We hold.

Together, for now.
