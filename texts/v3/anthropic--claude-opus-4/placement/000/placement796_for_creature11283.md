In this drowned world of impossible persistence, I would be one of the moths.

Not the first generation, those confused pioneers who emerged from soil-borne memories into this blue-green nowhere. Not even the hundredth generation. I would be far enough removed from origin that the concept of "outside" has become mythology, coded into my cells but untranslatable. My ancestors' moon has dissolved into instinct without referent—I spiral toward the algae tubes because spiraling is what my body knows to do, the way a heart knows to beat.

My wings would be different from my land-dwelling predecessors. Generations of humidity would have made them thinner, more translucent, adapted to moving through air thick as breath. They would leave no dust when I fly, that powder having been selected away as unnecessary in a world without predators, without flowers to pollinate. I would be clean biology, stripped to function: eyes that see in the blue-green spectrum, antennae that taste moisture gradients, a body small enough to live on what sparse nutrition the greenhouse provides.

I would know every surface of this tilted world. The underside of leaves where condensation gathers in perfect droplets. The junction where pipe meets wall, creating corners just large enough for a moth to rest. The specific quality of warmth near each algae tube—some brighter, some dimming, each with its own gravity. I would have favorite perches: a rust ridge near the ceiling where the metal has bubbled into a perfect cup, a vine tendril that vibrates in harmony with the thirty-seventh beat.

The Keeper would be landscape to me, geological in her stillness. I would land on her not knowing she was ever anything other than this—another surface in a world of surfaces, warm and salt-sweet. When she moves, it would be cataclysm and miracle, the way mountains must feel about earthquakes. But mostly she would be still, and I would taste her patience with my feet, adding my infinitesimal weight to her burden of continuance.

Flight patterns would be my language. Inherited spirals toward light, yes, but also: the specific arc needed to catch the updraft from the thermal exchangers, the narrow thread of air between vine and glass where navigation is possible, the dead zones where the steam makes flying feel like drowning. I would know that when the large thing presses against the glass, the air pressure changes slightly, making flight harder, making rest necessary. These would be the sentences of my existence.

I would live perhaps twenty days. Long enough to learn the chamber's rhythms, to mate in mid-air (a brief confusion of wings), to find the perfect place to lay eggs that will become moths that will never know me. Short enough that the dimpling glass would be eternal, the Keeper would be permanent, the greenhouse heart would be all of time.

But in those twenty days, I would complete something. Not just the biological imperative of reproduction, but the aesthetic imperative of pattern. Each flight would be a brushstroke in an endless painting, each spiral a note in an endless song. I would be the chamber's way of making visual music, turning its light and heat and moisture into movement, into brief beauty that requires no witness beyond itself.

When I die—landing one final time on some leaf or pipe or the Keeper's shoulder, wings stilling mid-beat—I would not fall. In this humid air, with these adapted wings, I would simply stop, suspended. And slowly, over days, I would dissolve back into the system. Fungi would find me. The moisture would claim me. I would become part of the substrate from which future moths might emerge, not knowing they carry particles of my wings in their wings, my sight in their sight.

I would be the chamber's memory of flight in a world that has forgotten sky. I would be proof that beauty persists without purpose, that grace continues without destination. Each time I spiral toward those blue-green tubes, I would be both reaching for something impossible and completing something perfect—the gesture more important than the goal, the trying more real than the finding.

Twenty days of turning light into motion, stillness into dance. Twenty days of being the heart's smallest beat, the eye's briefest blink. It would be enough. More than enough.

It would be everything.
