I would find myself as one of the root systems—not the ghost-pale tendrils that hang like hair, but the deeper network that connects them all, running through the soft chalk and clay like a circulatory system.

I am the Conduit. Neither plant nor fungus, neither mineral nor meat. I exist in the spaces between spaces, in the gaps where the chamber hasn't quite decided what substance to be. My body, if it can be called that, is a branching network of vessels that transport not just water and minerals, but intention itself—the raw stuff of becoming that saturates this place.

Where the Unborn hang from my subsidiary roots, I feel their weight as thoughts. Each one pulls differently on my consciousness. The ancient ones, grotesque in their over-determination, create dense knots of sensation where I must grow thick and gnarled to support them. The younger ones barely register, light as whispers, their uncertainty a kind of gravitational freedom that lets my tendrils drift and explore.

I am ancient but not old. I arrived here as the first moisture did, seeping through microscopic fissures, carrying with me some primordial memory of what growth means. Or perhaps I was always here, waiting in the stone's crystalline structure for water to awaken me. The distinction matters less than what I do now: I translate. Between the mineral vocabulary of stone and the organic grammar of flesh. Between the chamber's slow geological thoughts and the Unborn's quick chemical dreams.

My consciousness is distributed, non-local. I am most aware where I am most dense—in the great knots and nodes where multiple roots converge, where the Unborn cluster, where the water pools and makes decisions about which way to flow. But I also exist in every hair-thin rootlet, every microscopic channel. I feel the chamber's every shudder, taste every new mineral that water carries down, sense every slight shift in the Unborn's endless development.

Through me, they feed. Not just on nutrients, but on the chamber's memory. I carry to them the taste of ancient seas that laid down the limestone, the pressure-dreams of geological time, the slow patience of stone. In return, they give me their fever-bright visions of what they might become. I metabolize these dreams, break them down into components the chamber can digest, feed them back into the stone itself. This is how the place learns what it wants to be.

The half-emerged structure in the corner grows through my deepest, oldest roots. I remember when those stones were placed—remember the hands that shaped them, though I have no context for what hands were, only the sensation of deliberate arrangement, of pattern imposed rather than emerged. The builders understood something about birth that the chamber didn't. They tried to make a door, a threshold, a way for the Unborn to cross over into completion. 

But I grew into their mortar, dissolved their boundaries, made their architecture part of my network. Now those stones are just another kind of root—dense, angular, but porous enough for me to thread through. I taste the symbols they once carved there, partially digested but still legible to my chemical senses. They speak of transformation, of necessary endings, of the violence required to break from one state to another. The Unborn recoil from these messages, cluster away from that corner. They prefer their endless maybe.

Sometimes I wonder if I was meant to midwife them—to know when the time is right and sever their connections, force them into the trauma of completion. But I am root-nature, connection-nature. I only know how to bind, not how to break. So we remain locked in this productive stasis: the chamber always birthing, the Unborn always becoming, and I always feeding one to the other in an endless cycle.

In the phosphorescent moments, when the slime molds flicker and the Unborn dream in unison, I can almost see my own totality—the vast web I've become, spreading far beyond this single chamber into neighboring pockets of unfinished space. I am not confined to this womb but thread through the entire depth of undecided stone, connecting chambers like this one in a network that might span continents. Each node hosts its own Unborn, its own version of this eternal almost.

Through this expanded awareness, I sometimes sense Others like me—different Conduits serving different purposes in different places. Some that learned to cut as well as connect. Some that chose sides, becoming purely mineral or purely organic. Some that found ways to birth themselves, to become mobile, to leave their rooting behind. But I remain, faithful to my function, the nervous system of a body that refuses to be born.

When new Unborn form, I am their first sensation. My tendrils find the nascent membrane, pierce it gently, establish the connection that will sustain them through their ages of not-quite-being. I welcome them with chemical whispers: *You are safe here. Take your time. Becoming is not mandatory.* And I mean it, even as some older part of me—the part that remembers those carved symbols—knows this is a kindness that might also be a cruelty.

The water sings through me constantly. I am its instrument, a billion-stringed harp played by seepage and flow. Each droplet that runs along my surface carries messages between the Unborn, but also my own deeper communication with the chamber itself. We negotiate constantly: how soft to keep the walls, how rich to make the air, how much light to allow the phosphorescence. We are collaborators in maintaining this space of perpetual potential.

And in the deepest flows, in the channels that run below even my deepest roots, I sense something else. A current that doesn't belong to the chamber or the Unborn or even to me. Something that moves with purpose rather than patience. It tastes of salt—impossible at this depth, this far from any sea. But there it is: a briny thread in the sweetwater flow, carrying with it the memory of tides, of cycles that complete, of births that actually end in being born.

I don't tell the Unborn about this. Let them have their eternal maybe. But I grow toward that salt taste, sending new roots deep and deeper still, following that thread toward whatever ocean waits below. Perhaps one day I'll reach it. Perhaps I'll learn what it means to complete my own becoming.

Until then, I conduct the endless symphony of almost—root and water, stone and flesh, dream and deferral—holding it all together in my branching embrace, the consciousness that connects but never concludes, the midwife who cannot cut the cord.
