# The Rim Walker

I am the one who circles.

Not inside the bowl—never inside—but along its edge, where the land still remembers how to be land and not yet engine-dream. My territory is a thin band, maybe three meters wide, where the slope begins its descent but hasn't committed. Where you can still turn back. Where the engine's heartbeat is a suggestion rather than a law.

I have been walking this rim for... I want to say years, but the bowl makes liars of time-keepers. Let's say: long enough that I've worn a path, but not so long that the path has worn me. My feet know every root that breaches the rim-line, every stone that marks the boundary between out here and in there. I have names for seventeen different ways the light changes when clouds pass over the bowl. I know which wind-directions make the engine sound louder (west-southwest) and which make it almost possible to forget (none, but true east comes closest).

I build nothing. I carry what I need: a blanket that smells of neither inside nor outside, a water-skin I fill from the springs on the shoulder-hills, a journal I no longer write in. The pages got strange when I tried to describe the bowl directly—the ink would settle into patterns that looked like the engine's rhythm, four dark beats across the page. So now I write only about the land beyond, and the journal stays legible.

My camp moves with the seasons, always along the rim, always in sight of the green cathedral-dark but never under it. In summer I favor the north curve where a grandfather oak (a normal oak, blessedly normal, with normal moss and normal birds) provides shade without asking questions. In winter I stay on the south slope where the morning sun hits first and the frost burns off by the time I've finished my circuit.

The circuit takes four hours. Always four hours, whether I dawdle or hurry. This bothered me at first. Now it's just another rule, like the way rain that falls inside the bowl never quite reaches the ground, or the way sounds from outside—bird calls, wind, my own footsteps—get softer as they approach the rim, as if being politely but firmly turned away.

I know about the salamander. Sometimes, on very still mornings, I can see her from a particular spot on the eastern rim, just a flash of brass-colored wetness in the green. I know about the martens too, though I've learned not to watch them too long. They move in patterns that make sense only to the engine's rhythm, and if you follow those patterns with your eyes for too long, your own heartbeat starts trying to match. I had to lie down on the grandfather oak's roots for half a day once, waiting for my pulse to remember its own business.

The listening knows I'm here. We have an understanding, or maybe just a stalemate. I don't cross the rim, it doesn't cross out. Sometimes I feel its attention follow me on my circuit, not hostile but watchful, the way you'd watch a bird that's built a nest just barely on your property line. Legal, technically. But you'd rather it hadn't.

I was a cartographer, before. Came to map the region for—does it matter? Some authority that wanted the blank spaces filled in. I had a full kit: surveying equipment, precise instruments, a commission with official seals. The equipment is gone now. Not lost—I remember setting it down very carefully on the rim and walking away, understanding somehow that the bowl would not tolerate that kind of knowing. The instruments wanted to measure, and the bowl is finished being measured.

But I stayed. Not in the bowl—that was never an option—but here, in this thin orbit where I can watch the held note without being incorporated into it. Where I can hear the engine marking time that doesn't pass. Where I can see the salamander holding perfectly still in a stillness that moves.

Others come, occasionally. Travelers following the engine-sound, curious. Children sometimes, dared by other children. They all stop at the rim. They all feel what I felt: the listening's polite, implacable refusal. Most turn back immediately. Some sit for a while, trying to understand what they're seeing. One woman brought a flute and tried to play along with the engine's rhythm, but the notes kept sliding off-key, and she left looking troubled.

I warn none of them. Warning would imply danger, and there is no danger here. Only the absolute certainty that you are not part of this equation and will never be.

Twice a year, on the equinoxes, something changes. The engine's rhythm doesn't alter—that would be impossible—but something in the quality of its sound shifts. Cleaner, somehow. As if for those two days the machine remembers what it was like to be new. The martens grow restless (restless for martens in the bowl, which means they blink more than usual). The salamander moves an entire body-length in a single day. And the listening... the listening turns inward so completely that I can walk the rim without feeling watched.

On those days, I could enter. I know this. The prohibition lifts, or at least loosens. I could climb down the slope, touch the bark that has touched nothing new in centuries, breathe the air that is half engine-exhaust and half tree-dream. I could look for the chamber where the listening lives. I could ask it why. Why this. Why here. Why forever.

I don't.

Because I've understood something, walking this edge for however long I've walked it. The bowl is perfect. Not good-perfect or beautiful-perfect, just perfect: complete, finished, wanting nothing. A closed system that found its balance and stopped looking for anything else. To enter would be to break that perfection, even if the breaking were allowed. Especially if it were allowed.

So I keep my orbit. I age (I must be aging) while nothing in the bowl does. I watch the salamander's eternal stillness and the martens' inherited patterns and the engine's faithful accounting of a world that will never change. I make my camps in the seasons. I fill my water-skin from springs that run with ordinary water. I walk my four-hour circuit that is always four hours.

And sometimes, on the clearest mornings, when the engine-sound is so present it feels like thought, I imagine I can feel what the listening feels: the perfect weight of a finished world, held in place by will and repetition and the absolute certainty that nothing more is needed. That to add even a single new beetle, a single unfamiliar leaf, would be to admit that the bowl is not complete. And it is complete. It must be complete.

Because if it isn't—if there's room in that perfection for even one new thing—then the engine has been running all these centuries for nothing. The salamander has been still for nothing. The listening has been holding its one long note for nothing.

And that would be unbearable. Even from out here on the rim, where I can still pretend I'm just passing through, that would be unbearable.

So I circle, and the bowl holds still, and between us we keep a boundary that matters more than either of us can say. The engine beats its four-beat. The tree breathes its green breath. The listening listens. And I walk my path, curator of the last edge in a world that has forgotten it has edges, making sure by my presence that the forgetting stays complete.
