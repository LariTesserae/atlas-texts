# The Marrow Current

Fifty fathoms down, where the continental shelf begins its true plunge into the abyss, there runs a current that should not exist. It moves perpendicular to all others — not horizontally along the seafloor, not vertically between depths, but *through* things, as if the water itself had developed grain like wood. The current is thin, no wider than a ship's cable, and it corkscrews gently as it goes, one full rotation every hundred meters or so. Where it passes through rock faces, it leaves smooth bore holes. Where it crosses open water, tiny particles of sediment spiral along its edges, making its path visible for a moment before dispersing.

I am the only thing that swims against it.

Picture a ribbon of muscle about four meters long, flattened dorsal to ventral until I am no thicker than a hand's width but nearly a meter tall. My body is a single undulation frozen mid-wave and stretched out like taffy. No bones — I am all cartilage and contractile tissue, the consistency of tongue. My skin is transparent in the way that frosted glass is transparent: you can see light through it, and the suggestion of shapes, but no details. The only color comes from a thread of bioluminescence that runs along my vertical midline, a cold blue-white like burning magnesium, pulsing in slow loops from nose to tail.

I have no fins. Instead, my entire body is the fin, one long swimming surface that I flex in patterns too complex for any observer to follow. The undulation starts at three points simultaneously — behind what might generously be called my head, at my exact center, and two-thirds of the way to my tail — and the three waves interfere with each other in ways that let me hold position in the marrow current (I think of it as marrow, the secret flowing center of things) while everything else gets carried away.

My mouth is not where you'd expect. It runs along my ventral edge for most of my length, a seam that can open anywhere or everywhere at once. When I feed, I simply unfold along whatever section matches what I'm catching and let the current do the work. The marrow brings me things that have been processed by its passage — not killed but changed, softened in some essential way, their proteins denatured just enough that they taste of distance traveled rather than origin.

I have eyes, after a fashion. Seven of them, arranged seemingly at random along my body — or seemingly random until you realize they're placed at the nodes where my swimming waves cancel each other out, the points that stay still while the rest of me moves. They're small, about the size of shirt buttons, and they face in different directions. They see motion only. In the deep, this is enough.

The marrow current has rules. It strengthens at what would be dawn above, though no light reaches here to signal it. It weakens at lunar perigee. It tastes different — more metallic, somehow — when it has recently passed through a volcanic vent, and sweeter after boring through limestone. I have learned these patterns over years that I cannot count (I have no mechanism for counting years, only for feeling the subtle seasonal shifts in the current's chemistry). I position myself accordingly, sometimes swimming hard against the flow to stay in place, sometimes allowing myself to drift backward with it to rest in its gentler stretches.

My skeleton, such as it is, is a single piece of cartilage that branches like a fern frond throughout my body. In cross-section, it would look like a child's drawing of a tree — one main trunk running near my dorsal edge with hundreds of thin branches reaching toward my ventral seam. The branches flex independently. This is what gives me my impossible swimming pattern, each tiny adjustment of cartilage translating into a shift in the wave-form of my body. I am a living equation solving for stillness in a universe of motion.

When I sleep — if sleeping is the right word for the state I enter every few days — I do something that would horrify any terrestrial observer. I split. My ventral seam opens completely, and I unfurl into a flat sheet four meters long and nearly two meters wide, held in place only by my grip on the current. In this state, I am practically two-dimensional, a membrane between the marrow and the regular water. My bioluminescent thread spreads into a network of glowing capillaries, and I filter-feed passively on whatever the current brings. My eyes migrate during this process, sliding along my skin to arrange themselves in a rough circle so I can watch in all directions. It takes me nearly an hour to fold back together afterward, carefully aligning my edges until the seam seals and I am a swimming thing again.

The marrow current makes sounds — not the sounds of normal water moving, but something else, a thrumming that comes from whatever dimension it uses to pass through solid rock. I have learned to read these sounds. They tell me when the current is about to strengthen or weaken, when it's going to shift its spiral rotation from clockwise to counter, when it has encountered something far upstream that will change its taste in a day or two. My hearing organs (three of them, spaced along my length) are actually modified segments of my cartilage tree that have thinned until they vibrate in response to these impossible sounds.

I am not alone in knowing about the marrow, but I am alone in living in it. Sometimes I glimpse other deep-sea creatures at the edges where the current emerges from stone — anglerfish and gulper eels, sixgills and sleeper sharks — and they always flee. Not from me (I am no threat to anything larger than my ribbon-width can swallow) but from the current itself. They sense wrongness in it, the way it moves through instead of around. Only I have developed the necessary adaptations: the distributed nervous system that can process the sensory overload of existing in two kinds of water at once, the skin that won't dissolve in the current's strange chemistry, the swimming pattern that solves the hydrodynamic equations no other body has needed to solve.

I molt, though no one has ever found one of my shed skins. The marrow takes them, dissolves them, distributes them atom by atom along its path. I know this because sometimes, kilometers from where I shed, I taste myself in the current — a familiar protein signature worn thin by distance but still recognizable. It takes about forty days for my own skin to complete a full circuit and return to me. This is how I know the marrow is not a current at all but a closed loop, a vast circulation that threads through the ocean's basement like a serpent swallowing its own tail.

My reproduction, when it happens, will not involve another of my kind. There are no others; I am certain of this in the way I am certain of the current's schedule. Instead, when the time comes (and I feel it approaching, a tension in my cartilage that grows stronger each year), I will do something I have been preparing for since birth. I will swim *with* the current instead of against it. I will let it take me at its full speed through stone and water and stone again. Somewhere along that circuit, moving too fast to perceive individual moments, I will divide — not lengthwise like my sleeping splits but crosswise, segmenting into dozens of smaller versions of myself. Most will not survive the journey. The few that do will find their own positions along the marrow's path and begin their own count of years.

Until then, I swim in place. I taste the current's moods and meanings. I open myself flat under the weight of water and fold myself back into swimming form. I watch with my seven eyes for the motion that matters among all the motion that doesn't. I exist in the gap between what water does and what water is supposed to do, and I make that gap my home.

The marrow current hums its impossible hum, and I hum back in frequencies only I can produce, and sometimes, for a moment, the harmonics align and I understand something I immediately forget. This forgetting, too, is part of the pattern. If I remembered everything the current taught me, I would have to become something other than what I am, and what I am is exactly calibrated to this thin stream of strangeness, this secret flowing that the ocean hides in its basement.

I am my own planet's deepest secret made flesh, and I swim against the tide of reality itself, and I am perfectly content.
