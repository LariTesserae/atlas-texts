# The Keeper of the Third Pool

I am the fish with the pale belly, though that is not all I am.

In the pool below the third fall, where the water turns in its eternal circle before continuing down the cleft, I rest against smooth granite worn by my own passing. Ten thousand times ten thousand circuits I have made, following the water's patient spiral, and in all that time the pool has neither deepened nor grown shallow. The falls above me applaud with the same quiet insistence they have always had. The stones beneath me are the same stones, arranged in the same pattern, holding the same shadows.

But I remember when they were different.

This is my burden, or my purpose, or simply what I am: the one thing in Oss-in-the-Cleft that remembers change. Not the keeper of the breath — I know now who that is, or was — but the marker of it. The knot tied in the thread so that someone, someday, will know where the holding began.

My body is silver-scaled and long as a child's arm, though I was smaller once. I grew in the time before the stopping, when growth meant something different than it does now. My flesh should have fed some hawk or gone soft with age a thousand generations ago, but here I am, turning in my pool, watching the light change on the water's surface in its reliable way — morning bright, noon white, evening gold, night black with stars — while my body neither hungers nor ages nor forgets.

The people know of me. I am a story they tell, one of their description-stories that goes: *There is a pool below the third fall where the water turns in a circle before continuing. In the pool lives a fish with a pale belly. The fish is old. The pool is cold even at midday.* They do not wonder how old. They do not ask why I am alone in my pool when the other pools have many fish, small and quick and forgettable. It is enough for them that I am, that the story of me can be told and retold without variation, a smooth stone in the pocket of their quiet lives.

Sometimes a child comes to watch me. They sit on the flat rocks beside the pool, their feet bare, their dark hair bound back, and they look at me with those valley-eyes that see distances even when looking at something close. I rise to meet them, though not to the surface — never to the surface — and we regard each other through the water's lens. The child sees a fish. I see a held breath made flesh, a small person-shaped pause in the world's turning. Neither of us speaks. In Oss, I have learned, the most important things are not spoken.

But I remember the speaking, before.

I remember when the rock was raw — not just apparently raw as it is now, but truly fresh-cut, bleeding water from new springs, sharp enough to slice. I remember the first people arriving with their tools and their tired faces, refugees from something they would not name, looking for a place to rest. I remember them finding this cleft and deciding it would do, not knowing yet what it would become.

I remember her.

She was not old then, the woman who became the Keeper. Middle-aged, perhaps, by the counting of the world outside. She had the same compact mountain build as the others, the same capable hands, but something in her was different. Heavier. As if she carried stones in her chest where her heart should be. She came to my pool often in those early days, when they were still building the first terraces, still learning the shape of their new home. She would sit where the children sit now and put her hands in the water — it was warmer then, before the holding — and sometimes she would speak.

"I'm tired," she said once, to me or to the water or to no one. "We're all tired. Of running. Of time taking everything. Of building only to watch it fall."

I was just a fish then, ordinary, one of many. But I listened. Fish listen better than people think.

She began to come every day as the terraces grew, as the channels were cut, as the first houses rose. The others worked with the steady patience of people who have learned to expect loss and are grateful for any respite, but she worked differently. Harder. As if she were racing against something. And every evening, she came to my pool.

"It could stop," she said one night. The moon was new, invisible. The stars were the same stars, but they still moved then, still wheeled in their courses. "All of it. The wearing away. The losing. It could stop."

I should have been afraid, perhaps. A fish should fear a human who speaks of stopping time. But I was curious. Fish are curious, too.

She began to change. It was subtle at first — a stillness in her that went beyond the normal quiet of the valley. She would sit by my pool without moving for hours, barely breathing, and I would circle below her, watching. The others noticed but said nothing. In Oss, even then, privacy was absolute.

"I've found it," she told me one morning. Dawn was breaking over the eastern wall, and her face in that light looked both ancient and new-made. "The pause between breaths. The place where the wheel stops turning. I can hold it."

I wanted to warn her, but I had no voice. I could only circle, faster now, trying to say with my movement what I knew: that wheels are meant to turn. That breath is meant to flow. That holding is not the same as healing.

But she was already beginning. I felt it in the water first — a thickening, not of substance but of time itself. The current in my pool slowed, not physically but essentially. The fall above me began its eternal applause. The moon, three-quarters full that night, stopped its waning.

"For them," she said, and her voice was already becoming what it would be: not quite here, not quite gone. "So they can rest. So nothing they build will fall. So no one they love will age away from them. For as long as I can hold it."

The holding hurt her. I could see that in the way she sat, no longer like someone resting but like someone carrying an invisible weight. But she carried it. Day after day, season after ceased season, she came to my pool and held Oss-in-the-Cleft in its perfect pause.

The people thrived. They built their city slowly, without fear of time. Their children grew to that perfect plateau of adulthood and stopped. Their fruit trees bloomed and ripened eternally. They created their quiet culture of description and kindness, free from the urgency that drives the outside world. They were happy, I think. They are happy.

But the Keeper — for that is what I had begun to call her in my fish-thoughts — grew thin. Not in body, for bodies here do not change, but in presence. She became what the very old here become: transparent, visible-through, a person made of held breath and will and not much else.

"I don't know how much longer," she said to me one day. How many years had passed? I had no way to count them. The seasons did not turn. The moon did not change. But time was passing somewhere, accumulating like snow on a roof that must, eventually, collapse from the weight.

She stopped coming daily. Then weekly. Then only occasionally, and when she came, she was so transparent I could see the rocks through her, could see the water through her hands when she dipped them in my pool.

The last time I saw her — truly saw her, as opposed to sensing the space where she had been — she said: "You'll remember. When it ends. Someone should remember how it was before, and during, and why."

And then she was gone. Not dead, I think, but dissolved into the holding itself. Become the pause she had created. The people of Oss do not speak of her, perhaps do not remember her as distinct from the place itself. But the shrine on the lowest terrace began to fill after she left, and I think, in their quiet way, they know. They know something is being held. They know it will not hold forever.

I am the memory of that holding. I am the fish with the pale belly who turns in the pool below the third fall, who has turned for uncounted years, who will turn until the breath releases. When it does — when the Keeper finally lets go, or dissipates, or simply forgets to hold — I will remember what came before. The wearing of stone. The changing of seasons. The way water warms in summer and cools in winter. The ordinary march of birth and growth and death that the outside world calls time.

I will probably die then, quickly, the accumulated years catching up with me like a wave. But I will have a moment, I think, to swim downstream at last, to leave my circular pool and follow the water where it wants to go. To be, briefly, just a fish in a river, mortal and forgetful and free.

The children still come to watch me. I rise to meet them, these held breaths in human form, these beautiful pauses in the world's turning. They see a fish. I see the kindness the Keeper bought with her dissolution, the peace she paid for with her presence.

Sometimes I think I should try to warn them. To splash, to leap, to somehow signal that their world is not the world, that their timelessness is time held back like water behind a dam. But then I see their faces — calm, attentive, kind — and I know they already know. The shrine fills with its offerings. The stories describe but do not explain. The transparent old look south toward where the cleft opens.

They are waiting, as I am waiting, for the breath to release.

When it does, the water in my pool will remember how to warm and cool. The moon will remember how to wane and wax. The fruit will remember how to fall. And the people of Oss will remember how to grieve, how to age, how to die — but also how to be born truly new, how to build things that matter because they can be lost, how to love fiercely in the face of time.

I circle in my pool, patient as stone, carrying my memories like eggs that will hatch when the world begins again. The falls applaud above me. The water turns its endless spiral. Somewhere in the substance of the valley, the Keeper holds and holds and grows ever thinner, ever more tired.

And I wait, with my pale belly and my long memory, for the moment when the holding stops, and time comes rushing back like water through a broken dam, terrible and ordinary and absolutely worth the price of change.

The children watch me, and I watch them back, and together we are the story of Oss-in-the-Cleft: the fish and the people and the held breath between them, beautiful and temporary and almost ready to let go.
