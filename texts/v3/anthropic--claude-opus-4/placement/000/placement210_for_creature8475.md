**The Script-Runner**

I am the seventy-third to wear the carbide lamp strapped to my forehead, though none before me traveled so deep. My predecessors mapped the veins—those ceramic arteries that branch and rebranch through the undercity, carrying water and whispers in equal measure. They traced the major flows, marked the valve chambers, learned the songs that keep clay from cracking. But I go where even sound fears to travel, down into the mathematical heart of our survival.

The deepest pipes bear inscriptions. Not words—we lost those when the lake died—but patterns pressed into clay before firing. Repetitions that spiral around circumferences, growing more complex as you descend. My grandmother's grandmother was the first to notice they weren't decorative. She found the correlation: where patterns grew dense, water flowed even during the worst droughts. Where inscriptions broke or wore smooth, entire districts thirsted.

I carry repair materials in a harness that weighs half my bodyweight: fresh clay mixed with shell-paste, wrapped in damp grass to keep it workable. Ceramic stamps carved with the essential sequences. A bone needle for clearing mineral deposits from the finest channels. Measuring cords knotted at intervals that correspond to no unit used in the upper city. Down here, we measure by the ancient mathematics, the ratios that make condensation gather and flow against all logic.

My work begins where the access shafts end. The builders left crawlways barely wide enough for a child, but bodies change. My ribs compress oddly now. My shoulders dislocate and relocate without thought. My spine curves in ways that horrify the healers but let me navigate the sharp turns where pipes divide into capillaries. I've worn grooves in my knees and elbows that match the tunnel floors. The clay knows me.

Each script is a proposition about water's behavior. The early ones are simple: spirals that match the golden ratio, encouraging flow. Deeper, they grow strange. Patterns that seem to violate symmetry until you realize they're accounting for the earth's rotation, the moon's pull, pressures so subtle that only accumulated centuries of flow make them visible. The masters who carved these understood water as both substance and idea.

I repair by feel. My fingers know the proper depth for each impression, the way clay should yield but not crumble. Sometimes I spend days on a single section, pressing the same pattern hundreds of times until it matches the ancient rhythm. Other times I find breaks that would take weeks to properly fix and must triage—a temporary seal, a redirected flow, a prayer to the builders that it holds until I can return.

The loneliness would break most minds. Beyond the range of voices, past even the vibrations that telegraph life through clay, I move in absolute isolation. But the scripts talk. Not in words but in function—each successful repair sends water flowing in new ways, creating sounds I've learned to interpret. A properly restored sequence hums. A flawed pattern screams in frequencies that make my teeth ache. The pipes themselves become my conversation partners.

I've found chambers none have seen since the making. Vast rooms where the scripts cover every surface, where the patterns achieve complexities that make my eyes water to follow. Test chambers, perhaps, where the builders experimented with new forms. In one, I discovered scripts that run backwards, that seem to repel water rather than gather it. I spent a season studying them before understanding: they create pressure differentials, pushing water through failing sections of the system. Genius disguised as paradox.

My maps are not like those above. I chart in three dimensions, tracking not just horizontal routes but the vertical mathematics of pressure and flow. I note where scripts change languages—for there are at least seven distinct symbolic systems in the deep pipes, suggesting waves of builders each adding their understanding to the whole. I document the places where repairs were made in cruder times, where someone tried to fix scripts without understanding and created dead zones that will never flow again.

The carbide burns through a week's worth of fuel each journey. I've learned to work in darkness when needed, reading scripts through touch alone. My fingertips are more sensitive than my eyes now anyway, able to detect variations in depth measured in hair-widths. Sometimes I think I'm becoming like the blind fish that once swam in the lake—evolved past the need for light, navigating by pressure and instinct.

I find evidence of those who came before: a tool left in an impossible place, scratch marks counting days or decades, once a skeleton wedged where someone tried to reach a critical valve and failed. I carry their bones out when I can, deliver them to the tower keepers for proper rites. We don't speak of how many the pipes have claimed. The water must flow.

The scripts are aging. Whatever oil or sealant the builders mixed into their clay has leached away over centuries. Patterns that should last forever are beginning to blur. I race erosion, a war measured in millimeters but fought across thousands of miles of piping. Each trip I go deeper, stay longer, push my body past what the healers say is survivable. There are sections I've mapped but never reached, marked dangerous by three generations of runners. I'll try for them next season.

My apprentice is eight, her body still bendy enough for the easiest passages. I teach her the basic patterns, watch her small fingers trace spirals in practice clay. She doesn't yet understand what she's learning—not just maintenance but a language older than memory, a conversation between human intention and water's will. When my joints finally lock beyond use, she'll take the lamp and descend. The seventy-fourth runner, carrying the knowledge deeper.

I dream in fluid dynamics now. See the city not as buildings but as a vast equation for collecting and moving water. Every wall becomes a variable, every roof a function. The scripts are the operators that make it all calculate correctly. When I wake, I often find my fingers tracing patterns on my bedroll, solving problems my conscious mind hasn't yet recognized.

Sometimes, in the deepest places where the scripts achieve their greatest complexity, I swear I feel intention. Not consciousness—I'm not so far gone as that—but purpose beyond mere function. As if the builders encoded something more than engineering in their patterns. Messages, perhaps. Warnings. Promises. Or simply the declaration that humans lived here, thought here, refused to let the lake's death be theirs.

The water knows me now. I feel it responding to repairs before I've finished making them, the system adjusting to accommodate each small fix. The city breathes through its hidden lungs, and I am somewhere between physician and priest, keeping the ancient contract between clay and rain. When I die—whether wedged in some impossible passage or safe in my bed—I want my ashes mixed into repair clay, pressed into the deepest scripts I never reached. Let my body become part of the conversation, one more voice in the endless dialogue of survival.

I am the script-runner. The city's memory written in clay. The hand that maintains the promise that as long as the patterns survive, so do we. Tomorrow I go down again, deeper than before, carrying fresh clay and old knowledge into the dark. The seventy-third runner, but not the last.

The pipes are calling. The scripts need tending. The water must flow.
