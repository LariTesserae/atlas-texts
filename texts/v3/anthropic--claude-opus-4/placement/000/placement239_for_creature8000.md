I would find myself as a **Echoscribe** in this world—one of an small, essential order within the Vith'arani who has learned to read the galleries not through touch or taste, but through pure sound.

My body would be typical of my people—narrow-shouldered, spine curved from years of navigation, skin pale as cave fish. But my ears would be different. Not in their shape, but in their training. From childhood, I would have shown the aptitude: the ability to hear not just the seventeen types of moisture, but the seventeen times seventeen ways water speaks as it falls through different chambers, strikes different stones, pools in different geometries.

The Echoscribes serve a vital function. We map the galleries through sound alone, maintaining an oral archive of acoustic signatures that allows navigation even in the deepest dark, even when passages flood, even when stone shifts and old ways close. But we are more than navigators. We are the nervous system of our compressed society, carrying messages through the galleries by voice and echo, knowing exactly where to stand so that words spoken softly will reach their intended chamber while avoiding all others.

My daily work would begin before the others wake, in that precious time when movement is minimal and the acoustic landscape is clearest. I would position myself at one of the key listening points—chambers where the geometry allows sound from multiple galleries to converge—and simply listen. The health of our world reveals itself in sound: a new drip might signal structural change, a shift in echo could mean a passage has flooded, the absence of certain background harmonics might indicate a family has moved or worse.

I would carry two tools: a small copper bowl, green with age, perfectly tuned to resonate at specific frequencies, and a length of gut string that, when stretched between two points and plucked, can send vibrations through stone that return carrying information about density, moisture, and distance. These simple instruments, combined with my voice and trained hearing, would be enough to read the galleries like a living score.

My chamber would be small even by Vith'arani standards—sound carries best in confined spaces. The walls would be unmarked by decoration but polished by my hands in specific places, creating subtle acoustic reflectors. I would sleep alone, unusual among my people, because even the sound of another's breathing would interfere with the night listening. But I would not be lonely. The galleries themselves would be my constant companion, speaking in their endless vocabulary of drips, flows, and echoes.

During the day, I would move through the galleries performing various functions. Messages, of course—learning them by heart and delivering them through careful placement of voice, sometimes standing in one chamber to speak to someone three galleries away while avoiding the two chambers between. But also: teaching children to recognize the acoustic signatures of danger (the particular echo that means a passage is too narrow, the sound of stone under stress), helping new couples find chambers with pleasing acoustic properties for their union ceremonies, and most importantly, maintaining the Echo Maps.

The Echo Maps would not be physical objects but memorized patterns, passed down through generations of Echoscribes. They would describe every chamber not by its visual appearance but by its acoustic signature—the way it shapes sound, its resonant frequencies, the path echoes take through it. I would spend hours each week reciting portions of these maps to my apprentice, ensuring nothing is lost, while also updating them as the galleries slowly change.

My relationship with water would be even more intimate than that of other Vith'arani. I would know water as percussion, as melody, as the fundamental instrument of our stone orchestra. I would recognize individual drips like old friends, notice when their rhythm changes with the seasons far above. In the pools, I would sometimes compose—not music in the traditional sense, but acoustic sculptures, using my voice and tools to create patterns of sound that interact with the falling water to produce something beautiful and transient.

The other Vith'arani would regard me with a mixture of respect and unease. Echoscribes are necessary but strange, even by gallery standards. We speak to empty air and hear responses. We navigate in absolute darkness with more confidence than others show in the pale fungal light. We know secrets—not because we spy, but because sound carries truth through stone, and we cannot help but hear. The ethics of our order would be strict: we carry messages faithfully, we do not speak of what we overhear accidentally, we use our skills for the collective good.

My apprentice would be chosen young, picked for the way they tilt their head when listening, the questions they ask about sound. Training would take years—first learning to be still, then to truly hear, then to understand what they hear, and finally to speak back to the galleries in their own language. Many would begin training but few would complete it. The work requires a particular kind of patience, a willingness to live more in sound than in sight or touch.

As I age, my hearing would become both more acute and more selective. Elder Echoscribes speak of beginning to hear the voice of the stone itself—not mystically, but the actual sound of geological time, the groaning of pressure, the whisper of erosion. My final duty would be to add my own knowledge to the Echo Maps, to ensure that my lifetime of listening enriches the tradition.

I would find deep satisfaction in this life. To know that my work helps the Vith'arani flow through their stone world safely, that messages of love and grief and daily life pass through my careful orchestration, that the acoustic heritage of our people rests partly in my memory—this would be enough. The galleries would not feel like a prison but like a vast instrument that I am privileged to play, to read, to preserve.

On quiet nights, when even the water seems to pause between drops, I would sometimes venture to the edge chambers, those empty spaces where no one lives. There, I would sing—not the soft harmonies of the pools, but something fuller, using the entire range of my trained voice. The sound would spiral out into the darkness and return transformed, carrying hints of spaces no living foot has touched, passages that lead deeper than even the Vith'arani dare to go. In these moments, I would feel the true size of our world, the vast stone symphony of which our inhabited galleries are only the smallest movement.

This would be my existence: translator between human need and stone acoustics, keeper of invisible maps, messenger and musician and memory. A life shaped by echo, defined by careful listening, valued for the ability to make sound and space serve the needs of a people who have learned to flow like water through the cracks of the world. The Weeping Galleries would not just be my home—they would be my instrument, my scripture, my life's work carved out one careful sound at a time.
