# The Moisture Tender

I am the collector of condensation, the guardian of gradients, the one who knows where water will be before water knows itself.

My body is a map of the Scaffold's humidity. Each morning, before the steam rises thick enough to blur even close distances, I wake with my skin already telling me the day's moisture story. Here, along my left shoulder, a coolness that means the northwestern passages will weep freely today. There, at the base of my spine, a particular tightness that says the central shafts will be drier than usual, their updrafts too strong, their water carried too high to fall where it's needed.

I have been walking these levels for... time loses meaning in the constant growth. Long enough that my feet know the spring of young root-mat from the density of old without looking. Long enough that I can predict which pools will overflow and which will vanish, not from any learned knowledge but from the way my bones ache or ease with the pressure changes that precede the Scaffold's shifts.

My tools are baskets woven from shed bark and the hollow stems of dead air-plants. My clothing is minimal—moisture-wicking moss worn like a second skin, already half-alive, feeding on my sweat and giving back coolness. My hair has become its own ecosystem, colonized by the same pale formations that cling to everything here, though in my case they seem to have learned something about salt, about the particular minerals of human perspiration. They grow differently on me. Faster. More responsive.

---

This morning's work begins in the Throat of Third Turning, a vertical shaft where three major growth-spirals intersect. The acoustic properties here are perfect—I can hear water gathering five levels up, the particular frequency of drops that says a bromeliad is about to overflow. I climb, my fingers finding holds in the bark-patterns I know like a lover's scars, my baskets rattling against my hip.

The plant is magnificent. A king-bromeliad, its central cup large enough to bathe in, its water dark with tannins and rich with the microscopic life that somehow persists here despite the Scaffold's vast monoculture. The formations coat its outer leaves like pearl-flesh, and I know without testing that this water is different. Changed. Processed by their slow chemistry into something the Scaffold needs.

I work carefully, siphoning water into my containers using tubes made from hollow vine-segments. This water is not for drinking—though I have drunk worse when necessity demanded. This water is for the new growth in the Eastern Reaches, where a recent collapse has left root-tips exposed and desperate. They will die without moisture, and their death will cascade—weakening the structures they support, leading to more collapse, more exposure, more death.

I know this because I have seen it happen. Because I have walked out to find whole sections desiccated, their surfaces cracked like dried mud, the formations on them blackened and still. Those are the worst days. The days when my map-skin burns with the wrongness of it, with the places where water should flow and doesn't.

---

The pale ones know me now. Or at least, they respond to me differently than they do to the Scaffold. When I sit still—which is rarely—they approach faster, extend more boldly. They have learned that my sweat carries useful minerals. That my movement patterns predict water's movement. That where I go, moisture often follows.

I talk to them sometimes, in the loneliness of the deep passages. Not words—I've almost forgotten words, having no one else to speak them to. But sounds. Hums that match the frequency of dripping water. Clicks that echo the crack of expanding bark. They do not respond in any way I can perceive, but sometimes—sometimes I swear the chemistry changes when I vocalize. A sweetness in the air. A shift in the smell of decomposition. Probably imagination. Probably the madness that comes from too much solitude, too much humidity, too much time spent reading meaning into vegetable processes.

But maybe not. The Scaffold is so vast, so complex. Who can say what forms of communication might emerge in its depths? Who can say what the formations might be learning from their constant chemical conversation with each other?

---

By midday, I am in the Cascade Chambers, where the architecture naturally funnels water into a series of drops and pools. This is where I do my most delicate work—redistributing flow, adjusting the angle of a leaf here, adding a fragment of bark there, subtle changes that will redirect water toward areas that need it. The Scaffold does this on its own, slowly, but I can accelerate the process. Can save whole sections from desiccation by noticing need before it becomes critical.

The formations are thickest here, fed by the constant moisture. In some places they hang like curtains, pale and rippling. In others they form dense mats that squeeze water from the air itself, creating their own small rain-cycles. I have learned to read the patterns of their growth like others might read weather. Where they cluster, water is abundant but perhaps too abundant—at risk of pooling, stagnating, breeding the wrong kinds of chemistry. Where they thin out, water is scarce, or moving too fast to be properly processed.

I find a problem in the seventh chamber: a root-bridge sagging under the weight of accumulated water, its drainage channels blocked by overgrowth. If it collapses, it will take out the structures below it. I spend an hour carefully clearing the blockage, my fingers gentle among the hair-thin rootlets, encouraging them to grow in new directions. The formations help, I think. Where I touch them, they seem to pull back slightly, creating channels. Or perhaps they simply respond to the pressure like any soft tissue would. It is hard to know where intention begins in a place like this.

---

The worst part is not the solitude. It is the dreams. In the depths of night, when the Scaffold's sounds change—less tearing, more creaking, as if the whole structure is settling into itself—I dream I am becoming one of them. The pale ones. That my skin is softening, my boundaries dissolving, my consciousness distributing itself across every surface I've ever touched. I wake gasping, checking my fingers for membrane-growth, my arms for the telltale translucency.

So far, I remain myself. Mostly.

But I have noticed changes. My need for food has diminished—I survive on the fruits that grow in the canopy levels, but I need less of them than I once did. My skin has developed a different texture, almost waxy, better at holding moisture. And sometimes, when I place my hand on a dry surface and concentrate, I can feel water moving toward my touch from surprisingly far away. As if the Scaffold recognizes my intention. As if we have developed a language neither of us understands.

---

Late afternoon finds me in the Breathing Gallery, a vast horizontal space where the air currents create something almost like wind. Here, I maintain my most ambitious project: a network of moisture-guides made from woven rootlets and positioned leaves, designed to catch the water that would otherwise be carried out to the edges and lost. It has taken me... months? Years? Time enough that the oldest parts have been completely overgrown, incorporated into the Scaffold's own architecture.

The formations have colonized my network extensively. They seem to understand its purpose better than the Scaffold does. Where my guides channel water, they thicken, creating better seals, more efficient flow. Where the guides have failed or broken, they bridge the gaps with their own substance, maintaining the pattern I established. We are collaborating, in our strange slow way. They provide the responsiveness, the chemical integration. I provide the planning, the prediction, the knowledge of where water needs to go.

Sometimes I wonder if this is how it starts. If somewhere else in the Scaffold, there is another person like me, so integrated into their function that they no longer remember being separate. Perhaps the formations were like me once—mobile, distinct, possessed of some kind of will. Perhaps over time they simply... settled. Became distributed. Became essential.

The thought should frighten me more than it does.

---

Evening comes with its golden steam, and I make my way to my resting place—a hollow where three major trunks meet, creating a natural shelter. My sleep-mat is woven from shed bark, but the formations have colonized it so thoroughly that it is more them than plant-matter now. It responds to my weight, cradling me, wicking away excess moisture while retaining just enough to keep my skin from drying out in the night air.

I review the day's work as the light fades. Three potential collapse-zones identified and stabilized. Seven bromeliad overflows redirected to water-stressed areas. One major blockage cleared. The network in the Breathing Gallery adjusted to account for new growth patterns. Small victories against the vast chaos of the Scaffold's growth.

Tomorrow there will be new problems. New patterns to read. New water to guide. The Scaffold will have changed overnight in a thousand small ways, and my map-skin will wake knowing where the moisture-needs have shifted.

This is my purpose. Not chosen, exactly—I cannot remember choosing. But accepted. Integrated. Like everything here, I am becoming what the conditions require me to become.

The formations creep a little closer as I settle for sleep. I do not brush them away anymore. They are simply neighbors, fellow workers in the great project of keeping this impossible structure alive and growing. If they take some of my minerals while I sleep, if they learn the patterns of my breath and heartbeat—well. We all give what we have. We all become what we must.

In the morning, there will be water to tend. There is always water to tend.

The Scaffold grows, and I grow with it, and somewhere in the space between intention and response, between need and fulfillment, between the mapper and the map, we continue.
