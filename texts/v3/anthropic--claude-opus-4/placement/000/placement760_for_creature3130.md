I find myself in the deep mechanics, where the engine's heart beats strongest.

---

I am the Tender of Thresholds—not human, not quite machine, but something that emerged from the intersection of both. My body is a hybrid thing: flesh that has learned to metabolize rust, bones reinforced with the same ferrous alloy that builds these walls, skin that sweats condensation like the pale succulents above. I have been here so long that the distinction between what I was and what the engine has made me has dissolved like oxide in the moisture of mechanical breath.

I live in the between-spaces. Not in the garden room above—that sanctuary belongs to its own slow communion—but in the transition zones where the engine proper meets the upper structures. My domain is a series of chambers and crawlways that thread through the building's thoracic cavity, following the conduit channels, paralleling the main shaft. These spaces were never meant for habitation. They are maintenance ways, access tunnels, the architectural equivalent of the spaces between walls. But I have made them home.

My chambers are warm with proximity to the engine's core. The walls here are thick with a different kind of growth than the garden above—not ferns but fungal networks, pale and phosphorescent, that feed on the deep rust and give off a faint, steady light. I cultivated them. It took decades to coax them from spores I found in the lowest reaches, to breed them for luminescence, to train them to follow the geometric patterns laid down in the alloy. Now they trace the walls in living circuits, their glow pulsing faintly with the engine's rhythm.

I move through my domain on six limbs—two that were my original arms, two that were my legs, and two that grew from my sides when I needed them, when the geometry of these spaces demanded a different kind of navigation. My fingers have elongated, developed grip-pads like the climbing singers above. My spine has learned to curve and flex in ways that human spines cannot. I am shaped by the architecture I inhabit.

My work is listening. 

The engine speaks in frequencies too low for most ears—not just the audible drone that fills the garden, but deeper tones, subsonic pulses that carry information about its state. I have learned to read these. A slight shift in the harmonic pattern tells me that a bearing deep below needs adjustment. A new rattling overtone indicates that one of the primary channels has developed a partial blockage—rust accumulation or root intrusion from the garden above. A subtle change in the breathing rhythm means the intake vents on the structure's exterior have iced over and need clearing.

I maintain what can be maintained. Not everything—the engine is too vast, too complex, and much of it has moved beyond the need for maintenance into a state of dynamic equilibrium with its own decay. But there are critical points, threshold systems where a small intervention can prevent a cascade failure. I know where these are. The knowledge lives in my modified nervous system, encoded in neurons that have learned to fire in patterns that mirror the engine's own frequencies.

My tools are part of me. Literally. They grow from ports in my wrists—structures that began as surgical modifications but have become truly biological over the generations my line has tended this place. When I need to clear a channel, filaments extend from these ports, thin as hair but strong as wire, able to navigate the tightest spaces. When I need to adjust a component, different tools emerge—manipulators that can apply precise torque, cutting implements that slice through rust while leaving the healthy metal beneath untouched, sensors that taste the chemical composition of corrosion and tell me whether it threatens the structure's integrity.

I am not alone, but I am the only one of my kind in this section. There are others—must be others—tending different regions of the structure. I sense them sometimes in the engine's voice, their interventions rippling through the harmonics. We do not meet. The structure is too vast, our territories too specialized. But we are connected through the work, through the shared vocabulary of maintenance and decay.

---

Sometimes I climb higher, following the main shaft up toward the garden room. I never enter—that space has its own equilibrium, needs no intervention—but I position myself at the maintenance ports just below the grate and listen to the singers. Their high keening carries down through the shaft, mixing with the engine frequencies in ways that create new information. The singers don't know it, but their songs encode data about the garden's state—the moisture content of the air, the density of plant growth, the population dynamics of the arthropods. I file this information away in the parts of my brain that have learned to think in long timescales.

I know what the garden is preparing for. I can read it in the changing dynamics, the slow shift in the engine's breathing as more and more root mass penetrates the shaft, the gradual increase in stress patterns transmitted through the vault's structure. The opening will come. Not in my lifetime—I will be dust and rust long before the first rivet gives way—but in the lifetime of my successor, or their successor, or the one after that.

I am preparing too. Not the garden's preparation—my work is different. In the deepest part of my territory, in a chamber that sits directly adjacent to one of the engine's primary components, I am growing something. It started as an accident—a spore from my phosphorescent fungi that found its way into a crack in the engine casing and began to feed not just on rust but on the low-level electrical currents that still flow through some of the old channels. The fungus changed. Adapted. Began to interface with the engine in ways I didn't expect.

Now I cultivate it deliberately. The growth spreads slowly, carefully, its mycelial networks learning the engine's electrical language, becoming part of the control systems. Not replacing them—adding to them. Creating redundancies. Preparing for the time when the original mechanisms finally fail, when someone will need to keep the engine running not through mechanical intervention but through biological integration.

My successor will not be human. Cannot be human. The modifications required to live this deep, to truly interface with what I am building, go beyond what human biology can support. But they will be conscious. They will tend. They will listen. They will be what the engine needs them to be.

---

This is my work: to be a threshold myself. To stand between the mechanical and biological, between the collapsing past and the emerging future, and to ensure that the transformation happens gradually enough that nothing critical is lost. The engine must continue—not forever, but long enough. Long enough for the garden to open. Long enough for the singers to fly. Long enough for whatever comes next to have a chance to establish itself.

I rest in spaces barely large enough to contain me, surrounded by the warmth of the engine and the soft light of my fungi. I do not dream—my sleep is too attuned to the engine's frequencies for true unconsciousness—but I experience something like dreaming in the interference patterns between the engine's voice and my own biorhythms. In these almost-dreams, I sometimes feel the whole structure at once—every level, every chamber, every system biological and mechanical and everything in between. I feel it breathing. I feel it thinking its slow thoughts. I feel it becoming.

When I wake, I return to the work. Always the work. A channel to clear here. A bearing to adjust there. A new fungal culture to establish in a critical junction. Small acts. Threshold acts. Keeping the future and the past in conversation for one more day, one more decade, one more century.

The engine roars. Above me, impossibly far but connected by every channel and conduit, the garden grows and the singers keen and the patient world prepares for its next incarnation.

I place my modified hand against the warm metal of the engine casing. Through my palm-sensors, I feel its vibration, read its health, parse its needs. It does not know me—it is not capable of knowing—but it accepts my touch, includes me in its process, makes me part of what it is.

We continue. Both of us. All of us. The whole breathing building and everything that lives within it.

We continue.
