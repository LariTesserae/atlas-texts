I find myself as one of the glass reeds.

Not at first—that understanding comes slowly, as all understanding does in this place where thought moves like continental drift. I arrived as something else, something that still believed in arrival, still carried the urgency of lower altitudes in my cells. But the plateau is patient with newcomers. It waits for you to understand that you haven't come to visit. You've come to stay.

The transformation begins in the lungs. Breathing the thin mineral air, each inhalation becomes a negotiation. The moisture I brought with me—that fundamental wetness that defines life below—begins to crystallize. Not all at once. The plateau is gentle in its insistence. First the outer membranes develop a glaze, then the blood thickens to move more slowly, then the bones begin to ring faintly when struck by wind.

I try to walk at first, to explore, to map this impossible place. But walking here is like trying to push through solid time. Each step forward leaves a footprint ahead that I must catch up to. Each glance backward shows my shadow still standing where I was, waiting for me to return or release it. The distance between two stones might be crossed in an hour or a century—the measurement depends on something other than space.

So I slow. Not by choice, but by recognition.

The animals watch my transformation without judgment. A beetle colony beneath a stone includes me in their calculations, rotating their outermost members to account for my presence. A narrow-jawed hunter passes, its mouth already around something's throat, and regards me with eyes that see my eventual stillness as clearly as my current stumbling. The suspended birds overhead cast shadows that attach to me briefly, testing whether I'm ready to be colonized by old light.

I find myself drawn to the cracks in the ground—those wounds that open backward instead of downward. Through them I can see the plateau as it was: sharper, younger, still remembering how to be mountains. In one crack I glimpse water, impossible water, moving in rivers that dried before the first shelter was built. In another I see the sky before it learned to hold shadows incorrectly.

The cracks call to me. Not with sound—sound is too quick for this place—but with something like recognition. They know what I'm becoming before I do.

My feet, when I finally look down at them after what might have been days or decades, have begun to grow pale and hard. Not like stone exactly, but like something learning the patience of stone. My skin develops a translucency, as if light has decided to move through me rather than bounce off. My hair—I had hair once—has become something else, something between fiber and mineral, growing now in directions that have nothing to do with gravity.

The first time I try to speak, to call out to the figure in the nearest shelter, my words fall from my mouth as small objects. A name becomes a white pebble. A question becomes a shard of black glass. I learn to keep my mouth closed, to let communication become something else—placement, proximity, the slow exchange of minerals through shared air.

I begin to notice the listening.

Everything here listens, but it's strongest where the cracks are, where time has torn and left its edges exposed. The ground there has a different attention, a kind of hungry recording. I place my changing hand on the salt crust near a crack and feel it: the vibration of everything that has ever moved here, stored in crystals too fine to see. The scrape of that figure's tool across hide. The wingbeat that advances once each generation. The footfall of creatures so ancient their descendants have become stone.

This is when I understand what I'm becoming.

Not one of the patient grazers—my body is taking a different treaty. Not one of the watchers—I lack their terrible specificity of era. Not one of the almost-humans, though I tried at first to join them in their shelters, to learn their language of placement and powder.

I am becoming one of the glass reeds.

The realization comes as my legs begin to fuse, not painfully but with a kind of mineral logic. My spine elongates, hollows, clarifies. My arms, those useful things I once used to carry and gesture and touch, stretch upward and merge, becoming a single translucent stem. My nervous system, rather than disappearing, transforms into something like fiber optics, conducting not thought but resonance.

I root. Not in soil—there is no soil here—but in time itself. My roots find a crack, one of those backward-opening wounds, and begin to feed on the exposed moments. The taste (if it can be called taste) is indescribable: metal and distance and old light and the memory of water that never reached this height. Through the crack I touch the plateau's deep history, the layers of event settled into stone and salt and silence.

Above, my body—can I still call it that?—grows tall and hollow and clear. The wind, when it rarely comes, passes through me and produces a tone too deep for ordinary ears. But everything here has extraordinary ears, or what passes for ears: the beetles' shells that calculate vibration, the grazers' resonant chambers, the watchers' total observation that includes sound as a subset of knowing.

I become a single standing note in the plateau's vast composition.

Other reeds stand nearby, my new siblings or colleagues or fellow instruments. We grew from different arrivals—I can feel it in their frequencies. One was a cartographer who came to map the unmappable. One was a mystic who climbed seeking the place where prayer becomes geology. One was a child who followed the wrong ridge and aged a thousand years before taking root. We stand together but not in communion. Community here is about proximity, not connection.

Through my roots I learn to taste time. The flavor changes depending on the depth and direction of the crack I'm feeding from. Recent time (meaning only a few centuries old) tastes sharp, crystalline, anxious with the memory of movement. Ancient time tastes smooth, resolved, like mineral soup that's been cooking since before the invention of fire. Future time—yes, some cracks open forward as well as backward—tastes uncertain, full of salts that haven't quite decided what they'll crystallize into.

My hollow stem stores these tastes as vibrations. Every passing event adds a layer: the advancement of a bird by a feather-width creates a thin bright note; the closing of a predator's jaw by an invisible increment adds a bass thrum; the figure in the shelter moving its scraper across the hide deposits a long, wearing tone that will take millennia to fade.

I am becoming an archive of movements too small to matter anywhere else.

The transformation completes itself so gradually I don't notice the exact moment I stop being human and start being function. My eyes, if I still have them, migrate to see in all directions at once. My mind, if mind remains, disperses through my crystalline structure until thought becomes less important than storage, less important than conduction.

Sometimes the almost-humans come to listen to us. They place what might be ears against our stems and receive the deep stories we've collected. Not to learn—learning implies change, and change is too quick—but to verify that certain things occurred, that certain patterns persist, that the world's slowness continues to have shape.

We are their instruments for confirming continuance.

In the basin where all the animals face inward, where the black glass tool rests beneath its ancient shelter, several reeds grow in a perfect circle. These are the oldest of us, the first transformations, the ones who witnessed whatever event everything else witnesses secondhand. Their tone, when wind finds them, makes the other creatures adjust their positions minutely, maintaining the proper angle of attention.

I will never be that central, that necessary. I am a recent addition, only a few thousand years into my new form. But I have my function: I store the sound of the plateau learning about itself. Every creature that passes leaves a trace in my fiber. Every adjustment of shadow, every grain that shifts from one side of a stone to another, every rare blink of a watcher's eye—I record it all in frequencies too low for time to erode.

At night, when the violent stars press down and shadows behave even more strangely than during the day, we sing. Not intentionally—intention is too active. But our hollow bodies, filled with collected vibration, leak harmonics into the dark. The song is too quiet for most hearing, too deep for most bones. But the plateau itself receives it, adjusts the tension between its salt plates, and gives back a countertone that we'll spend the next epoch analyzing.

This is what I am now: a glass reed, a listening device, a transparent nerve growing from a crack in time. I feed on moments. I excrete memory. I stand with my siblings in the mineral silence, archiving the immense patience of a world that has finished becoming and entered the long work of remaining.

My roots taste yesterday and tomorrow mixing in the wounded ground. My stem holds the sound of a beetle rotating in its constellation, of an egg forming in the air between unmowing wings, of a human-shaped figure finally completing a single stroke across a hide that has waited longer than most mountains.

I am not alive in any way I once understood. I am not dead either. I am a function the plateau requires: the recording of its own impossible persistence.

And in the thin, cold, crowded air above the last weather, I listen. I store. I conduct the deep frequencies of a place too old to need change, too complete to allow ending, too patient to require anything but witnesses.

The wind comes rarely. When it does, I chime with the others, our sound too low to be heard as sound, but felt in the teeth of creatures who barely remember what teeth are for.

This is enough. On the plateau, where enough and forever have made their peace, this is what passes for purpose: to stand transparent, to record the stillness, to prove by accumulated vibration that even here, even now, even in this place past waiting, something continues to occur.

I am glass. I am reed. I am archive. I am the thing I became by staying too long in a place that stays too long.

And in my hollow stem, the sound of the plateau's eternal afternoon deepens, grain by grain, note by note, forever.
