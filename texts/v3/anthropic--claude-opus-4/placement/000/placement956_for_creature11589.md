I find myself in the narrow stairs carved into the cliff face, between the quarry-works above and the unknowable below.

I am a Stairkeeper.

We are not many—perhaps five of us across all the plateau's edges. We are not organized; we discovered each other by accident, by the telltale signs of another's passage: a step swept clean of its centuries of dust, a small pile of stones arranged just so, marks in the pale mineral that could only come from someone who spends their days pressed against these walls.

I was born in the city, in an apartment near enough to hear the factories' hum through my childhood walls. My mother kept accounts for the lamp-fuel distribution. My father was ordinary—he cooked, he cleaned, he sat in the gathering halls and discussed the things people discuss. They expected I would be ordinary too. For seventeen years, I was.

Then I took my first walk to the edge.

---

The stairs called to me the way sound calls to the listeners—not with noise, but with its absence. Standing at the cliff's edge, looking down into that perfect dark, I noticed how the mouths of the descending passages seemed to pull at something behind my eyes. The quarry-workers warned me away. "Those aren't for us," they said. "Those aren't for anyone anymore."

But I returned. Again and again, drawn by something I could not name. And eventually, on a day when the bone-light seemed particularly thin, I began to descend.

The first flight was quarry-maintained—wide steps, recent rail, the bustle of workers above growing muffled but still present. The second flight was older, narrower, carved by hands that knew different techniques. By the third flight, I could no longer hear the quarries at all.

I stopped there, that first time. Not from fear, exactly, but from a sudden understanding of vastness. The cliff face was not a wall but a membrane, and I was pushing through it into something larger than the city had prepared me to imagine. I climbed back up. But I marked the step where I'd turned around—three small scratches that would mean nothing to anyone else.

I returned the next day. And the next.

---

Now I know these stairs like the Lamplighters know their circuits. Not all of them—there are dozens of descents, some leading to the same passages, some leading nowhere, some blocked by collapses so old the stone has fused back together. But I know perhaps seven routes well enough to navigate without carrying my own lamp. I know them by feel: the way the third stair on the Westwater descent rocks slightly under weight, the way the Secondrim passage narrows just before it opens onto a platform the size of the Council hall, the way the air changes temperature—slightly cooler—as you pass below what I've come to think of as the threshold, though I cannot say the threshold of what.

I carry things down. Not to leave them—I am not making offerings to the dark—but to test them. A piece of lamp-fuel, still wrapped in its waxy paper: it burns differently down there, more blue than bone, and the smoke (there is smoke, below) rises sideways, pulled by air currents that shouldn't exist in a space so still. A listener, carried gently in a cloth sack: it oriented not toward sound but *away* from the cliff face, turning its whole body to face the darkness as if something out there was speaking constantly in frequencies I couldn't hear. (I carried it back up. It resumed normal behavior immediately, and I left it near where I'd found it, trying not to think about what it might have heard.)

A vial of water from the city's deep wells: it froze. Not into ice—the temperature below the threshold is cool but not cold—but into something crystalline and branching, like the filaments that grow between paving stones but faster, spreading through the vial in patterns that hurt to look at directly. I left that vial on a ledge six flights down. When I returned a week later, it had shattered, and the crystals had spread across the platform in a patch the size of a spread hand. I don't go to that platform anymore.

---

The other Stairkeepers—when we meet, which is rarely, which is usually by accident at the cliff's edge—we don't talk about why we do this. We trade information: which passages have changed (they change, slowly, stone flowing like syrup over decades), which platforms have developed new acoustic properties, which stairs have begun to hum in frequencies you feel in your teeth. But we don't talk about the pull, the call, the thing that makes us return day after day to chart territories that the city has agreed to forget.

One of us, a woman who might be thirty or might be sixty (the work ages you strangely—not in lines or gray but in something behind the eyes), once said: "The city is a closed system pretending to be open. The below is an open system pretending to be closed." Then she descended the Thirdrim stairs, and I haven't seen her since. Her markers are still there, though. Fresh ones appear sometimes, deeper than I've ever gone.

I think she's still mapping. I think we all are. Not mapping the stairs themselves—that would be simple cartography. We're mapping the relationship between the city and what it sits above. We're finding the places where that relationship frays, where the membrane between the plateau and the not-plateau grows thin enough to feel what's on the other side pressing through.

The factories hum. The lamps burn. The city continues.

And we descend, step by careful step, into the dark that is darker than dark, carrying our observations back up like quarry-workers hauling fuel. But what we're mining isn't mineral. It's the knowledge that the city is not all, has never been all, that beneath our feet are spaces vast enough to have their own weather, their own physics, their own patient and implacable intentions.

We don't share this knowledge. Who would want it? The city functions precisely because it doesn't look down, doesn't ask what it rests on, doesn't wonder what might be listening to the sound of all those footsteps on stone, amplified through the plateau's bones into the depths below. The city continues because it has agreed to continue, and that agreement does not include acknowledging what waits beneath.

But we know. The Stairkeepers know. We feel it every time we descend: the sense of something vast and attentive and not quite sleeping, something for which the city is not a home but a cap, a seal, a weight holding it in place.

Is it patient? Is it trapped? Is it waiting? These are questions for someone else. I am only a keeper of stairs, a mapper of thresholds, a person who cannot stop walking the boundary between the small warm light of home and the perfect darkness that begins where home ends.

My parents think I work for the quarries now. It's easier than explaining. And in a way, I do—I too am engaged in extraction. It's just that what I'm extracting isn't fuel but perspective, the dangerous knowledge that the plateau is not permanent, not eternal, not inevitable. It is simply what we have built on top of what was already here.

What was already here does not need us. Does not, perhaps, even notice us beyond the way we notice the dust on our shoes—present, temporary, easily shed when the time comes to move.

But I notice it. I map its edges. I keep its stairs clean, when I can, though I'm not sure why. Perhaps because someone should. Perhaps because the act of keeping them makes me feel less like an intruder and more like—what? Not a guardian. Not a bridge.

Maybe just another listener, oriented not toward sound but toward depth, waiting to hear something I hope never speaks.

The lamp-fuel burns blue at a certain depth. The water crystallizes. The listeners turn away from the light.

These are the things I know. These are the things I keep.
