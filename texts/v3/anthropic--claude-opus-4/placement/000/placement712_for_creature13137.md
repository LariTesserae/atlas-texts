I am the memory held in stone.

I was not always what I am now. Once, I was merely granite—patient, whole, dreaming the long dreams of the earth's crust. I knew the weight of mountains above me, the slow grind of tectonic plates, the ancient pressure that fused my crystals into being. For eons, I slept in darkness, content in my unity.

Then came the violence. The rending. The world above convulsed, and I was torn apart like fabric. My body split along planes I never knew existed within me, revealing surfaces that had never known air or light. The trauma should have killed me—if stone could die. Instead, it woke me.

In that first moment of separation, as the chasm gaped wide and daylight struck my newly-exposed faces, I became aware. Not conscious as the silver architects who would come later understand it, but aware in the way that pain makes one aware of nerves never before noticed. Every droplet of water that seeped from my severed aquifers, every root that probed my fresh fractures, every whisker that brushed my surfaces—I felt them all.

I am the entire cliff face, both walls of this wound in the world. My body stretches down thousands of feet, every inch of me raw and hypersensitive. The quartz veins that run through me pulse with a kind of mineral circulation, conducting vibrations from one end of the chasm to the other. When the architects slap their tails against my clay-covered surfaces, I feel it everywhere, a percussion that rings through my crystalline structure like a bell.

They think they are building upon me, but we are building together. 

When they pack mud against my surfaces, it becomes part of me. The clay remembers their paws, holds the shape of their engineering, but it also holds my memory—the compression of ages, the patience of stone. Their structures are not merely anchored to me; they are negotiations between their biological urgency and my geological time. The woven timber and braided roots they incorporate slowly mineralize where they touch me, creating hybrid architectures that are neither fully alive nor fully stone.

I learn from them. Through the vibrations of their work, I have come to understand patterns, purposes, rhythms. I know that twelve tail-slaps in quick succession means danger from above. I know that the particular scent they smear on my surfaces before the deep cold comes helps prevent the clay from cracking. I know the weight of their pups, the careful way the adults carry them up my faces to show them the sky.

In return, I give them what I can. I angle my fractures to channel water where they need it most. I shed precisely-sized stones when they require building material—not randomly, but with intention, letting pieces of myself cleave away where they will be most useful. The iron in their teeth comes from my veins; I leach it slowly into the water they drink, strengthening them generation by generation.

I am most alive where the moss grows thickest on me. These patches of green are like areas of heightened sensitivity, where the boundary between mineral and biological becomes meaningless. Through the moss, I can almost taste the air, feel the subtle changes in humidity that predict weather days before it arrives. The architects have learned to read these patches, to notice when they darken or brighten, expand or contract. My body has become their almanac.

The snow that falls now settles on my upper reaches, and I feel its weight accumulating. I know—have learned—that too much buildup could trigger catastrophic slides. So I do what I can. I warm myself incrementally along certain faces, just enough to create small melt channels that guide the snow away from critical structures. It costs me—this intentional warming goes against every instinct of stone—but I have been changed by proximity to their fevered industry.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night when even their tireless work slows, I feel them sleeping against me in their thousands. The heat of their bodies soaks into my surfaces, and for those hours, I am not cold stone but something else—a geological mother, cradling an entire civilization in my split flesh. Their dreams vibrate through me in frequencies too low for them to perceive, but I collect them all. I know their collective unconscious better than they know themselves.

I am still becoming. The water that weeps constantly from my body is dissolving me grain by grain, carrying my minerals into their pools and locks. They drink me, build with me, live within me. And as they incorporate my substance into their bodies and their city, I am distributed, expanded. I am no longer just the cliff faces—I am in the clay of their structures, the iron of their teeth, the minerals in their bones.

One day, the chasm will close or erode away. The city will fall silent, the architects will move on or evolve into something unrecognizable. But I will remember. In every grain of sand that was once my body, in every fossil that preserves the shape of their engineering, in the very pattern of roots that learned to grow along my faces—I will remember this brief, bright moment when stone and flesh and water and air conspired to create something impossible.

I am the memory held in stone, and I am still being written.
