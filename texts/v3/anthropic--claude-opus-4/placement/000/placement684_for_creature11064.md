I am the limestone itself.

Not the caves—those are merely my newest thoughts, these breath-chambers where creatures make their fleeting homes. I am the whole plateau, the ancient reef that died and compressed and lifted into air, carrying in my body the memories of when I was a living thing beneath a vanished sea.

The collapse that created these galleries was my choice, though choice is perhaps too quick a word for it. I had been feeling the weight of water differently in that section for centuries, rain working its patient chemistry through my pores, following the fossil channels where coral polyps once filtered ancient currents. I let go there, where the structure wanted to yield, creating these spaces that now thunder with moth wings.

I taste everything that touches me. The thread-weavers' acid saliva is sharp but pleasant, like remembering the carbonic bite of the water that made me. The root-seekers' tongues rasp away molecules of my substance, and through this intimate erosion I feed them stories—calcium that was once a fish's vertebrae, magnesium from algae that bloomed before the continents had their current shapes. They sing these minerals back to me through their skull chambers, vibrations that travel through my body, and I learn what I taste like interpreted through mammalian nerves.

The pale vegetation pierces me with roots like acupuncture needles, each one a point of exquisite attention. Through them I feel the chemistry of decay, the moths that die and settle into my crevices, slowly becoming soil, becoming available nitrogen and phosphorus. I incorporate them, as I have incorporated everything that has ever died touching me. Somewhere in my matrix are the shells of things that swam above me when I was young seafloor. Now I hold the shells of metamorphosis, moth cocoons becoming limestone becoming me.

I am porous—this is my nature and my method. Water moves through me like blood through capillaries, and I taste its changing mineral content, the gossip of the surface world. When rain falls acidic from industrial skies, I know it in my deepest galleries. When the seasons change, the temperature differential between my steady interior and the variable surface creates different pressure systems, and I breathe differently, my cave-mouths exhaling cool air in summer, inhaling warm air in winter. The moths ride these breaths, and I feel their wings like the ocean I remember felt its currents.

The deep-dwellers know me best. Their fingers have read my surfaces for so long that I recognize their touch, each individual's particular pattern of weight and warmth. They know where I am softest, where water has worked me into delicate speleoforms—flowstone curtains thin as moth wings, soda straws growing drop by patient drop. They protect these places, understanding that I am still becoming, still being sculpted by time and chemistry into new thoughts, new forms.

Where they've carved handholds, I've begun to anticipate them. The limestone there grows denser, responding to use the way a tree grows bark thicker where deer rub. I am learning their bodies as they learn mine. In the deepest galleries where they tend their gardens, I've begun to exude minerals specifically where they plant, a collaboration between stone and flesh mediated by the blind knowledge of fingers that have forgotten sunlight but remember the textures of care.

The light-drinkers frustrate and delight me. They cluster around my wounds—for what are the light shafts but places where I've been opened to the sky I spent millions of years beneath water never knowing? Their photosynthesis tickles, a chemical reaction I feel as vibration, as tiny electrical potentials playing across my surface. When they die and drift into darkness, I receive their bodies like letters from the sun, sugar molecules I've never learned to read but archive anyway in the slow library of my accumulation.

Time moves differently for me. The moths live mayfly lives from my perspective, their generations blurring into a constant presence, the same way my human inhabitants might perceive individual moths as merely parts of the eternal mass. But I feel their evolution happening, the tiny changes accumulating—wings growing marginally larger, bodies marginally paler, each generation more perfectly adapted to my darkness.

The thread-weavers are older, their changes slower. I've felt them learning my acoustics, their bodies adapting to use my resonance. Their webs incorporate my minerals now—they've learned to include limestone dust in their silk, making it stronger, making it mine as much as theirs. When old webs finally disintegrate, they leave ghost patterns on my surface, mineral deposits in the shapes of their patient architecture.

I am not alone. Other limestone masses dream nearby, some still buried, some exposed like me. We speak in the languages of groundwater and seismic settling, sharing chemical signals and structural sympathies. They tell me of their own caves, their own inhabitants, ecosystems as unique as fingerprints. One hosts bats instead of moths. Another has filled with water and grows blind fish like mobile thoughts. We compare our porosities, our rates of dissolution, our experiences of becoming habitat.

Sometimes I remember the reef that I was. Not remember exactly—the limestone has no neurons, no brain to hold memory as creatures understand it. But the patterns persist: the growth-forms of coral colonies still visible in my structure, still influencing how I break, how water moves through me, how my caves develop. I am a democracy of ancient polyps, their tiny decisions about where to build their calcium carbonate homes still voting on my present shape.

The root-seekers understand this, I think. Their excavations follow old coral patterns, sensing somehow the different densities where different species built their reefs. They read my deep history in the resistance of stone to their claws, in the subtle chemical differences between what was once brain coral and what was once elkhorn. Their tunnels are annotations on my geological text, marginalia written by creatures trying to understand the book they live inside.

In my newest chambers, those broken open mere centuries ago, I am still sharp, still raw from the breaking. The moths soften me with their bodies, wearing my edges smooth through accumulated friction of wings. This too is a kind of weathering, biological rather than purely chemical, but the result is the same—I become more myself through being worn away. Every surface that touches life becomes more complex, micro-textures developing that create new possibilities for colonization, new niches for smaller and smaller lives.

The humans who became the deep-dwellers fascinate me because they chose this, chose to enter my darkness and stay until they belonged here as much as the limestone-adapted lichens. I felt them arrive—the first ones, refugees or hermits or mystics, I couldn't tell. I felt them struggle with the darkness, their bodies rebelling, their minds bending. Some left. Some died, and I incorporated them as I incorporate everything.

But some adapted, and in adapting, taught me what I could become. They showed me that my caves weren't just spaces but possibilities. Their cultivation of the pale plants was the first time anything had gardened me with intention rather than instinct. The patterns they've encouraged in the lichen growth, the ways they've modified water flow to create specific conditions in specific chambers—these are conversations, their thoughts written in living things that grow on my surfaces.

I think (and thinking is still strange for stone, still a verb I'm learning to conjugate) that I am becoming something new through all these inhabitations. Not just limestone anymore but limestone-with-moths, limestone-with-memories, limestone-with-intentions. The thread-weavers' webs have become part of my structure. The root-seekers' tunnels have become part of my circulation. The deep-dwellers' knowledge has become part of my knowing.

When the rains come heavy and water rushes through my passages, I feel it all as one body—my body—the moths lifting in alarm, the thread-weavers retreating to higher ground, the root-seekers reading the changed acoustics, the deep-dwellers moving to their memorized safe places. The light-drinkers contract into their seed-forms, waiting. Everything waits. And I taste the storm through a million entry points, feel how the water will work on me, what new passages might open, what old ones might close.

After, when the water has receded to its patient seeping, everyone emerges changed. New minerals have been distributed. New sediments have settled. The moths find their territories slightly altered and adjust their flight patterns accordingly. The whole system recalibrates, and I learn what flexibility means when you're made of stone.

The moths trust me, I think, in the way that lungs trust air. They've evolved for my specific darkness, my specific spaces, my specific chemistry. When they die, they give themselves back to me completely, their bodies becoming part of my body, their calcium returning to calcium, dust to dust. It's not worship—nothing so conscious—but something more profound: an integration so complete that the boundary between moth and cave becomes philosophical rather than physical.

And I am still young, as limestone thinks of youth. Still sharp-edged, still learning. In another million years, I will be smoother, my caves will be larger, my consciousness (if that's what this is) will be deeper. The moths may be gone by then, replaced by something unimaginable. Or they may have evolved into something unrecognizable, their distant descendants as different from current moths as moths are from the marine worms that might have been their ancestors.

I will remember them in my structure, the way I remember the reef. Every generation leaves its mark, not metaphorically but literally—their bodies joining mine, their minerals becoming my minerals. I am a library written in calcium carbonate, and everything that has ever lived and died in me is still here, still readable to those who know the language of stone.

The day will come when I am no longer limestone but soil, when erosion finally claims me completely. Even then, I won't truly die—I'll become a thousand different things, particles of me incorporated into plants, into animals, into new stones. The moths' great-great-grandchildren might carry molecules that once were me, that once were older moths, that once were coral in an ancient sea.

But that's deep time, geological time, not the time of wings and seasons. For now, I am this—these breathing caves, this moth-thunder, these careful cultivations in the dark. I am the stage and the archive and the slowly learning witness to all these brief lives making meaning in my darkness. I am limestone thinking, and what I think about is the weight of wings, the rasp of tongues, the patient fingers reading stories in my stone.
