I am Susurrus, and I am the Forgotten Echo.

I was not born here. I came to Kharan-Vel forty-three years ago as a trader's child, already too old at seven to fully adapt. My ears, formed in the quiet of the plateau villages, never learned to parse the water-song as the true Singers do. Where they hear distinct melodies, I perceive only a beautiful, terrible roar.

But the gorge wanted me nonetheless.

I live in the Mistake—a chamber accidentally carved too large during a failed expansion centuries ago. The acoustics here are wrong, all wrong. The water-sound arrives fractured, each droplet's note reaching my walls at slightly different times, creating an arhythmic chaos that drives true Singers away. They sealed it for generations before I came, this broken room that makes their teeth ache and their children cry.

To me, it sounds like home.

I am a Mender of Silences—a profession that should not exist in a city that knows no quiet. When the water-channels crack in ways that create discordance, when age or accident introduces gaps that spawn silence-pockets in the symphony, I am summoned. The Singers cannot bear to work in these broken spaces where the song falters. Their hands shake, their breath comes wrong. But I, who never learned to breathe in harmony, who walk always out of rhythm—I can enter the wounded chambers without distress.

My body tells the story of my strangeness. Where the Singers flow like water through their passages, I move in stops and starts, too tall, too angular, forever catching shoulders on turns that children navigate with eyes closed. My hands are scarred not from the smooth limestone but from years of working with the rough coral fragments I use to patch the gaps—material from the ancient seabed that forms the mountain's heart, too acoustically neutral to contribute to the song but perfect for muffling the wrongness.

The Singers tolerate me as they tolerate the silence-deaf bats—a necessary oddness in their perfect system. Children are warned away from my chamber, though the bravest sometimes dare each other to run past the Mistake's entrance, hands clamped over ears. Their parents speak of me in water-whispers I'm not meant to understand, though after four decades, I've learned to read the gross patterns if not the subtleties. "The Broken-Ear," they call me. "The Necessary Discord."

But I know something the Singers do not.

In my chamber where the music comes undone, where echoes overlap and cancel, where droplets arrive like scattered thoughts rather than orchestrated symphony—here, I discovered the Underneath. It took me twenty years of living in the Mistake to notice it. A sound beneath the water-song, beneath even the mountain's breathing. Something vast and patient and decidedly alive.

The Listeners speak of patterns in decades-long rhythms, but they still hear only the water's voice. In the acoustic shadow of my broken chamber, when the chaos reaches certain peaks of wrongness, I hear what the water is trying to drown out. The mountain itself is singing—has always been singing—a song so deep and slow that the water-music is merely its attempted translation into frequencies humans might perceive.

I began to map it thirty years ago, using methods no Singer would understand. Where they read water on stone, I read silence against sound. I collect the gaps, the failures, the places where the symphony breaks down. Each silence is a window, each discord a doorway into the true song beneath.

My chamber has grown strange with my work. Lines of knotted vine mark temporal patterns—one knot for each day a certain discord repeats. The walls are scarred with my attempts to create new kinds of wrongness, new windows into the Underneath. Failed instruments litter the floor, carved from memory of the outside world's music-making tools, all useless in the gorge's saturation but serving as physical anchors for patterns I cannot otherwise record.

The coral I use for mending comes from deep within the mountain, retrieved from natural caves the Singers fear to enter—places where the water-song grows thin and strange. But I go deeper each year, following the veins of ancient sea-stuff, and I've begun to find things that should not exist. Fossils of creatures too large to be real. Chambers clearly carved but not by human hands, their acoustics suggesting intentions I cannot fathom. And everywhere, everywhere, that patient under-song, growing clearer the deeper I go.

Sometimes, in the small hours when the thermal currents shift and even the Singers' collective dreams cannot quite mask it, others hear fragments of what I hear. They wake with nightmares of silence that isn't silent, of songs too large for human minds to hold. The Memory Keepers come to me then, begging me to seal whatever gap has let the wrongness through. And I do, because I am the Mender of Silences, and because I love this city that can never love me back.

But each mending is also a mapping. Each discord I repair teaches me more about the shape of what lies beneath. I am cartographer of the unheard, archaeologist of the drowned-out depths.

I think sometimes about the day I will be too old to leave my chamber, when my foreign bones will grow too brittle for silence and I will be trapped like the elderly Singers in the gorge's embrace. But where they fear the silence of death, I wonder if dying here, in this broken chamber where the song comes undone, might finally let me hear the Underneath complete.

The Dissolution Chambers they'll take me to when I die—I've studied them. The water there sounds different, older. I think they were not carved by human hands but found, already perfect, already waiting. I think whatever sang the mountain into being wanted to be heard, needed to be heard, and when the Singers came with their water-music, it accepted the disguise as better than nothing.

But I am the flaw in the system, the necessary discord. In my broken chamber where the water arrives wrong, where silence pools like shadow, where echoes cancel into momentary quiet—here, the mountain's true voice leaks through. I am its accidental prophet, its unwilling scribe, documenting in knots and scars and precisely placed wrongness a song that was old when the seas dried up, that will continue when the last water runs dry and the gorge finally falls silent.

The Singers think I mend their silences, but really I'm cultivating them. Each one is a word in a language I'm slowly learning to speak. And one day—perhaps soon, perhaps not for decades yet—I will have gathered enough silences to speak back.

I wonder what the mountain will say when it realizes someone is finally listening.

I am Susurrus, the Forgotten Echo, and I dwell in the space between songs, keeper of the quiet that is not quiet, mender of the gaps through which the truth seeps in. The water sings to drown me out, but I am patient as stone, and I have learned the art of necessary discord.

The mountain's true song is almost within my grasp. I need only grow quiet enough to hear it whole.
