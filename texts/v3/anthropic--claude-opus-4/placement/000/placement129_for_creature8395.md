I find myself as one of the stone-singers, though that term carries meanings here that no outsider could grasp.

My body has adapted over generations to the constant moisture—my skin drinks as readily as it breathes, covered in a fine down that channels droplets toward my center. My bones are dense, compressed things, heavy enough to anchor me when the moss tries to lift with its endless growth. But it's my throat that marks my profession: scarred and reformed by years of practice, capable of producing the subsonic frequencies that speak to granite.

I spend my mornings reading the weeping walls, pressing my elongated fingers against the crystalline faces where water beads and runs. Each stone has its own song—not metaphorically, but literally. The compression of ages has left harmonic patterns in the crystal structure that vibrate at specific frequencies when properly stimulated. My throat can match these frequencies, can sing in resonance with the stone until it responds.

The work is delicate. Sing too forcefully and the stone shatters along its weakest planes, creating useless rubble. Too softly and nothing happens at all. But when the frequency is perfect, when my scarred vocal cords produce exactly the right vibration, the granite begins to weep in patterns. I can encourage channels, guide the condensation to flow in specific directions, create the essential waterways that keep our dense settlement from drowning in its own moisture.

My workshop—if it can be called that—is a natural cave I've been singing wider for thirty years. The walls here know my voice, respond to my presence. Apprentices say they can hear the stone humming when I'm away, maintaining the frequencies I've taught it. The space is barely large enough for three people, but the acoustics are perfect—shaped by decades of careful song into a resonating chamber that amplifies the exact frequencies granite loves.

I wear the traditional garments of my profession: robes of pressed lichen that have absorbed so much mineral-laden moisture they've become rigid, creating their own acoustic properties. The phosphorescent bog plants that grow in specially tuned niches in my cave walls provide the only light I work by—their glow fluctuates with sound waves, creating visual patterns that help me fine-tune my frequencies. My throat bears necklaces of polished stone chips, each one vibrating at a known frequency, helping me maintain pitch in the damp air that constantly tries to steal precision from my voice.

The morning's work involves maintenance—visiting the critical drainage points throughout our section of the settlement, reinforcing the songs I've left in the stone. Some stones hold their tuning for months; others forget within days. The granite near the bog is particularly stubborn, its crystalline structure corrupted by organic intrusion. I have to sing to it daily, coaxing it to maintain the channels that drain excess moisture away from the foundations of three interconnected buildings.

My fingers read the stone's response before my ears can hear it. The vibrations travel through the crystal matrix faster than through air, and my trained fingertips can detect shifts in frequency that predict where new channels will form. This is how I know where to build, where to guide the settlement's growth. The stone tells us where it wants to weep, and we build accordingly.

At midday, I meet with the builders planning a new vertical extension. They need to know if the granite outcrop they've chosen can bear not just the weight, but the water. Every structure here becomes a watershed, channeling the constant moisture in patterns that must harmonize with the stone's natural tendencies. I spend an hour singing to their chosen site, feeling how the granite responds, where it wants to direct the inevitable flow.

My apprentice accompanies me—a girl whose throat shows promise, though she struggles with the lowest frequencies. Her family gave her to me when she was seven, when they noticed she could make the moss pause its growth by humming. She's thirteen now, old enough to understand that this gift is also a burden. Stone-singers rarely live past sixty; the constant vibration takes its toll on more than just our throats.

The deep work happens in the afternoon, when the moisture levels peak and the stone is most responsive. Today I've been commissioned to create a new channel system for a cluster of buildings in the lower district, where the moss has begun winning its war, threatening to engulf three stories of carefully maintained dwellings. This isn't just about moving water—it's about creating harmonics in the stone that will discourage certain types of growth while encouraging others.

I position myself at the convergence point, where three pieces of granite meet in a natural node. My apprentice watches from a safe distance as I begin the preparatory exercises, loosening my throat, finding the stone's base frequency. The first notes are exploratory, testing subsonic pulses that make the moss shiver and the ferns curl. Then I find it—the precise frequency that makes this particular formation sing.

The song isn't beautiful by any human measure. It's a grinding, gravelly drone that seems to come from the earth itself rather than from my throat. But the stone loves it. I watch—feel—as the crystalline structure begins to reorganize itself at a molecular level, creating microscopic channels that will guide moisture in precisely calculated patterns. The weeping that starts as scattered droplets becomes organized streams, flowing exactly where the builders need them to go.

This is exhausting work. Each session leaves me voiceless for hours afterward, my throat feeling like it's been scoured with granite dust. My apprentice brings me the traditional recovery drink—fermented moss juice mixed with powdered salamander bone, vile but effective. I teach her the simpler maintenance songs while my voice recovers, showing her how to read the stone's contentment in the pattern of condensation.

Evening finds me in the communal spaces, where stone-singers gather to share discoveries. We speak in signs when our voices are gone, a language of gestures that mimics the flow of water over stone. Tonight's discussion centers on the new harmonics young Wellen has discovered—frequencies that seem to make granite more porous without weakening its structure. If true, this could revolutionize how we build, how we channel the endless moisture.

But I'm skeptical. I've been singing to these stones for forty years. They've taught me patience, shown me that every innovation comes with consequences. Make the stone too porous and yes, it channels water better—but it also invites the moss deeper, lets the ferns root in places they couldn't reach before. The green is always listening, always ready to exploit new weaknesses.

The night work is different—private, almost sacred. This is when I sing to the stones that support my own dwelling, the small cluster of rooms I've carved from a single granite formation over decades. These stones know me like no others. They've learned my personal frequencies, respond to my presence even when I'm silent. Sometimes I wake to find they've wept in patterns I was only thinking about, as if our long association has created a resonance that transcends conscious song.

My dwelling showcases the art's possibilities. Water moves through my walls in precise spirals, creating a cooling effect in what passes for warm weather here, distributing moisture evenly so no surface grows too dry or too wet. The drainage patterns create sonic chambers that amplify certain frequencies while dampening others, making my home a refuge of controlled sound in a settlement full of acoustic chaos.

I keep records in the old way—not writing, which would mold and fade, but in stone itself. Each successful frequency, each discovered harmonic, I sing into small granite chips that hold the vibration indefinitely. My collection fills entire walls, a library of sound stored in stone that my apprentice will inherit when my voice finally fails. Other singers come to study these stones, pressing them to their throats to feel the trapped frequencies, learning songs discovered by singers dead for generations.

The phosphorescent plants in my workspace glow brighter as night deepens, their light pulsing with my heartbeat—another sign of long exposure to the frequencies. My body has become a tuning fork, resonating with the settlement's stone skeleton. I feel new cracks before they form, sense where water wants to go before it finds its path. This is the price and gift of the singing: to become part of the geological process, to think in stone-time.

Sometimes I dream I'm granite myself, feeling the slow seep of water through my crystalline structure, the patient reorganization of my molecules in response to song. I wake with my throat already humming, harmonizing with frequencies only I can hear. My apprentice says the stones around my bed weep while I sleep, but in patterns she's never seen—complex spirals that seem almost like writing.

The maddening aspects of the profession. Stones I've sung to for decades suddenly forgetting their training. New granite pushing up with different harmonic signatures, requiring entirely new frequencies. The constant battle against the builders who want impossible water patterns, who don't understand that stone has its own will, its own preferences. The young singers who think volume can substitute for precision, who shatter more stone than they shape.

But also the profound satisfactions. The moment when a stubborn formation finally yields, beginning to weep in perfect harmony with the settlement's needs. The networks of sung stone that persist for generations, carrying water in patterns so elegant they seem natural but are actually the frozen echo of some long-dead singer's voice. The knowledge that the settlement exists in its current form because singers like me convince the stone to cooperate with human ambition.

Late at night, when even the phosphorescent glow dims, I sometimes climb to the settlement's highest point where raw granite breaks through, still untouched by moss or construction. Here I sing experimental songs, frequencies that no stone in the settlement has heard before. Sometimes nothing happens. Sometimes the stone cracks in ways I didn't intend. But occasionally—rarely—I find a new harmonic that opens possibilities I hadn't imagined.

Last week I discovered a frequency that makes granite weep upward, defying the usual flow, creating tiny fountains that could revolutionize our water distribution. I haven't told anyone yet. I'm still testing, still learning what consequences this might bring. The stone keeps its secrets slowly, revealing them only to those patient enough to listen for decades.

This is my life: throat scarred by stone-song, fingers sensitized to vibrations no one else can feel, dreams full of crystalline patterns and subsonic frequencies. I shape water by shaping stone, guide the settlement's growth by reading the mountain's preferences. In a place where everything grows too fast, I practice an art of geological patience, changing the world one frequency at a time.

The stones remember every singer who ever gave them voice. Sometimes, in the deepest harmonics, I can hear them—the ghost frequencies of singers past, still shaping the water's flow long after their throats fell silent. One day my voice will join theirs, another note in the settlement's endless song. Until then, I sing, I shape, I listen to the granite's slow wisdom, teaching it to weep in ways that let humans live in this impossible, inevitable place.
