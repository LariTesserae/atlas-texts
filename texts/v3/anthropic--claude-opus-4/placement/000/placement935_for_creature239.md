# The Stone-Whisperer of Basin Twelve

I am the one who tends Basin Twelve, though "tends" is not the right word. The water tends itself. I simply... attend.

My house sits three terraces below the main settlement, where the shelf makes a natural step. The builders—my ancestors or someone's—carved the dwelling directly into the cliff face, so that my back wall weeps constantly into channels I've cut along the floor. The water runs through my home before joining Basin Twelve, and this means I always know its temperature, its rate of flow, the way it tastes at different times of day. 

I was not born here. I remember this sometimes, the way you remember a dream upon waking—vivid for a moment, then dissolving. I came from... there was a city. There were bells. I was younger and I moved quickly, with purposes I can no longer quite recall. Something about mapping. Yes. I was a cartographer. I came to chart the unmapped spaces between the valleys.

The mapping tools are in a chest somewhere. I have not opened it in... it doesn't matter.

What matters is Basin Twelve. It is different from the others. The water here forms patterns—not ripples, nothing so crude, but arrangements in the stillness. Sometimes hexagons appear on the surface, perfect, each one the size of my palm. Sometimes spirals, turning so slowly you must watch for hours to see the motion. The patterns come when the light is neither direct nor absent—in the hour after dawn, in the blue time before full dark. They last as long as they last. I document them.

My documentation is peculiar. I do not draw the patterns. I cut them into stone—not representations, but responses. For each pattern the water shows me, I cut a stone. The stones accumulate around my house, hundreds of them now, each one different, each one a conversation with something the basin said. The other people, when they pass, neither question this nor praise it. It is what I do. Like the woman who maintains the sluice-gates. Like the man who harvests the pale fungus. We each have our... not duties. Tendencies.

I speak less than the others, and the others already speak little. Months pass without words. This is not loneliness. The patterns are a kind of speech, and the stones I cut are a kind of answer, and the conversation continues, has been continuing since I arrived and began to slow.

The slowing was not immediate. For the first year—or was it two?—I fought it. I tried to maintain my pace, my purposes. I spread my maps on the floor and planned expeditions to the higher peaks. But the maps grew damp. The ink ran. The paper slowly dissolved into the stone, leaving only ghost-marks that looked, if you squinted, like the patterns on the basin. I stopped fighting then. Or rather, I forgot I had been fighting. The forgetting was a relief.

Sometimes travelers find me. The path to Basin Twelve is not obvious—you must know where the shelf folds back on itself, where the natural stairs hide behind a curtain of water. But sometimes they come, drawn perhaps by the accumulation of carved stones, which must look, from above, like a strange garden. They ask me questions. What are the patterns? What do they mean? How long have I been here? I consider these questions carefully, the way we consider everything here, and then I show them the basin.

If they are the type who can see, they stay for hours, watching. If they are the type who cannot see, they grow restless quickly and leave. Both responses are correct. The basin does not perform for everyone.

One traveler, a young woman with tools for measuring starlight, stayed for three days. She was convinced the patterns were caused by some invisible influence—magnetic fields, she said, or density variations in the stone beneath. She had instruments. She took readings. On the third morning, I found her sitting by the basin with her instruments beside her, untouched. She was watching a spiral form, so slowly, so perfectly. When it completed itself, she made a sound—not quite a gasp, not quite a sigh. Then she gathered her instruments and left. She walked quickly up the path, the way people walk when they are fleeing something that isn't chasing them.

I thought she might return. The ones who truly see the patterns often do. But she has not come back, and enough time has passed that she would be old now, if time passes the same way out there as it does here. Which it may not. The shelf has its own agreements with duration.

The strangest thing—though strange is relative here—is that I believe the patterns are teaching me something. Not in the way a book teaches, with information to be learned and retained. More the way water teaches stone, through long contact. I cut my responses and the responses accumulate and sometimes, looking at them all together, I almost understand something. Almost. The understanding retreats when I reach for it directly, but it remains there, patient as the basin itself, waiting for me to become slow enough to receive it.

There are mornings when I wake believing I have always been here. Other mornings, I remember the bells of that distant city with perfect clarity and wonder what urgent message they were ringing, and whether anyone answered. But most mornings I simply rise, check the water's path through my floor channels, eat a piece of the dense fruit, and walk the seventeen steps down to Basin Twelve.

Today the surface is showing me concentric circles, each ring slightly elliptical, each one offset from the last by a degree I cannot measure but can perceive. I select a piece of pale stone from the pile I quarried last season. I take up my tools—simple ones, a bronze chisel, a wooden mallet worn smooth by my hand. I begin to cut.

The sound carries up the cliff face: tap, tap, tap. Patient. Regular. Like water dripping, but with intention. If someone on the main terraces hears it, they will know I am working. They will not wonder what I am making or why. It is what I do, the way water runs downhill, the way the stone stays sharp, the way the silence holds us all.

The pattern will be complete by evening. I will place the finished stone with the others. Tomorrow, Basin Twelve will show me something new. Or the same thing, seen differently. There is no hurry to understand. There is no danger of running out of stone.

I am exactly where I am supposed to be, doing exactly what I am supposed to do, at exactly the rate I am supposed to do it.

The water knows this. The patterns know this. Slowly, cut by cut, I am learning to know it too.

The traveler I was would have found this existence unbearable—all potential collapsed into routine, all horizons shrunk to seventeen steps. But the traveler I was never saw Basin Twelve when the morning light catches it just so, never felt the perfect weight of the mallet in his hand, never understood that some conversations take a lifetime to complete and are no less urgent for their slowness.

I had a name once. The people here have not asked for it, and I have not offered. I am simply the one at Basin Twelve, the cutter of stones, the watcher of patterns. This is enough. More than enough. It is everything, occurring at the exact speed of itself, while the cliffs weep and the young stone waits to age and the patterns rise and fade on the surface of water that has never known a wind.

Tap, tap, tap. The sound of someone becoming what the shelf requires. The sound of attention, sustained past all reason, until reason itself dissolves into something clearer.

When this stone is finished, I will sit beside the basin until the light fails. If a pattern comes, I will watch it. If no pattern comes, I will watch that too. Both are worth the watching. Both are the same gift, offered at different angles.

This is what I have become. This is what I do.

The water knows. The water has always known.
