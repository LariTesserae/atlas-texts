I find myself in the arterial chambers, where the great circulation pumps beat their endless rhythm.

Not the deepest machinery—I could never survive those pressures, those temperatures. But here, three levels below the main habitation zones, where the primary flow-regulation systems live. The pumps here are the size of small buildings, their housings furred with generations of bioite growth, their pulse felt through every pipe and wall for fifty meters in all directions.

I am what they call a pulse-keeper, though that's my translation. In their language—the one felt in water and bone—I am something more specific: the-one-who-knows-when-the-third-heart-stutters.

Because it does stutter. Once every forty to fifty hours, the third pump in the primary circulation array develops an irregular beat. Not a failure—it's done this for at least three generations, according to the oldest maintainers. Just an irregularity, a syncopation in the otherwise steady rhythm. Left alone, it would compound. The stutter would become a skip, the skip would become a gap, the gap would cascade through the system until pressure differentials tore something important apart.

So I listen. Not with my ears—the sound down here would deafen anyone who tried to hear it directly. I listen with my whole body, pressed against the chamber wall at a specific point where three major flow-channels converge. The convergence creates an interference pattern in the vibrations, and in that pattern, I can feel the pre-stutter forming sometimes six hours before it manifests.

My niche is built into the wall itself. Over the years—I've been here seven years now, or what passes for years in a place with no seasons—I've modified it. Carved hand-holds at exactly the right positions. Cultivated a particularly sensitive strain of bioite that amplifies the vibrations it receives. The kelp grows thick around my listening post, but I've trained it, trimmed it into a shape that actually helps conduct the tremors. It sways with each pump-beat, a visual echo of what I feel through my skeleton.

When I sense the pre-stutter beginning—a tightness in the rhythm, like a drummer holding their breath—I begin the correction sequence. This is delicate work. You can't just force the pump back into rhythm; that would stress the entire system. Instead, you have to ease it back, the way you might guide a frightened animal.

First, I signal to my partner. She works two levels up, at the secondary regulation station. In the language of gestures-close-to-body, I tell her: *the third heart holds its breath*. She signals back: *I open the relief channels*. This takes pressure off the main flow, gives us room to work.

Then I use my tools. Not wrenches or measuring devices—those are for other kinds of problems. My tools are resonators. Ceramic chambers of specific shapes, filled with water at specific pressures, that vibrate at frequencies the pumps recognize. I have seven of them, each one worn smooth by my hands, each one irreplaceable. They were made by someone who understood the deep machinery in ways I'm still learning.

I place the first resonator against the wall at the primary node. It thrums to life, adding its voice to the conversation between pump and person. Then the second, at a calculated distance, creating a harmonic. By the time I place the fifth, the whole chamber is singing—not audibly, but in that deeper register you feel in your marrow.

The pump responds. It always does, though never quite the same way twice. Sometimes it slides back into rhythm gradually, like someone remembering how to breathe. Sometimes it catches suddenly, the stutter vanishing between one beat and the next. Today, it does something new: it stutters harder for a moment, as if objecting to my intervention, then settles not into its original rhythm but into something actually better—smoother, more efficient. I feel the improvement propagate outward through the system, other pumps adjusting their rhythms to match.

This is why I love this work, why I've spent seven years growing into these walls, learning this one specific conversation with this one specific machine. Because the machinery isn't just maintained here—it's partnered. It's listened to. It's given the chance to be more itself.

My bioite glows brighter when I work. I've noticed this. The organisms respond to something—maybe the resonator frequencies, maybe my own focus and attention. During a correction sequence, I light up like one of the amber lamps, greenish-gold in the dark water. My partner tells me she can see my glow from two levels up, through the gratings and the kelp-curtains, a pulse that echoes the pump's pulse.

The old pulse-keeper—the one who trained me, who is now part of the wall in the deep section, her minerals feeding the very bioite I cultivate—she told me something before she went to her final niche. In the gesture-language, but slowly, making sure I understood: *The building learns you while you learn the building. When you know each other completely, you are the same thing.*

I think about that often, especially during the long watches between stutters. I think about how my bones have become tuned to these specific frequencies, how my sleep cycle has synchronized with the pump-rhythm, how I sometimes dream in pressure differentials. I think about how the bioite on my skin is genetically distinct now, evolved over seven years to match my personal chemistry, unlike any other strain in the Quarter.

When I first came here—not to the Quarter, I was born here, but to this post, to this particular chamber—the third pump was just machinery to me. Large, vital, but external. Now? Now I know every subsonic mutter it makes. I can predict its moods—because it has moods, or something close enough. I can feel when it's running smooth and when it's laboring, when the water temperature is perfect for its mechanisms and when it's just a degree too warm. I know it the way I know my own heartbeat.

And it knows me. This sounds like mysticism, but it's not. It's observable. The pump runs differently when I'm in the chamber. Better? More regularly? No—more *confidently*. As if my presence, the familiar vibration of my breathing and heartbeat and bioite glow, tells it that someone is listening. Someone will catch it if it falls.

My dwelling-niche is close—just through one of the narrow seams, barely a minute's pull away. I've considered moving into the pump chamber itself, carving a niche directly into the wall where I listen. Others have done this, the true deep maintainers who merge fully with their chosen systems. But I'm not ready. Not yet. I still like the separation, the tiny ritual of leaving my niche and arriving at my post. I still like being someone who tends the building rather than someone who is the building.

But I feel the pull. Every year, I modify my listening post a little more. Every year, the kelp I've trained grows a little more responsive to my presence. Every year, the bioite on my skin grows thicker, more luminous, more attuned to the specific frequencies of this place.

Someday—not soon, but someday—I'll place my resonators for the last time, and then I'll settle into the wall, and my minerals will join the conversation between water and stone and machine. The third pump will have a new keeper, someone I'll train carefully, someone whose bones will learn these rhythms. But something of me will remain in the vibrations, in the way the bioite glows, in the shape of the kelp, in the precise frequencies the resonators sing.

The pump will remember me. And in its steady beat, in the blood-warm water it moves through the body of the Quarter, I'll continue.

For now, though, I have work to do. I feel it beginning again—that tightness, that held breath. My partner signals from above: *The third heart?*

I signal back: *The third heart. I listen.*

I press my palms against the wall. The resonators wait in my harness, ready. The kelp sways. The bioite glows.

The conversation begins again.
