In the deepest gallery where stone remembers water, I am the Archive.

Not keeper, for I keep nothing. Not guardian, for I guard no treasure but process itself. I am what happens when someone stays long enough to understand that coral-stone records everything—every tide that shaped its polyps, every storm that broke its branches, every patient century of growth before the reef-builders came with their bronze and purpose.

My chamber lies three levels below the Keeper's workspace, where natural light cannot reach. Here, the builders left the coral raw, uncarved save for the minimum hollowing needed for passage. The walls bloom with their original formations—brain-coral convolutions, staghorn antlers, massive table-plates that jut like shelves into the dark water. Each surface holds ten thousand years of accumulated history, written in calcium and time.

I read by touch.

My fingertips have worn smooth as river stones, polished by decades of tracing the coral's memory-lines. The nerves beneath reformed themselves, multiplying until each fingertip contains as many touch receptors as a normal human's entire hand. When I press palm to stone, I feel the reef's autobiography: here, the scar where lightning struck the water above; there, the growth-burst of an unusually warm decade; this smooth patch where parrotfish grazed for generations.

I no longer distinguish between my nervous system and the water that fills it. The two merged long ago—my neurons learned to fire through the mineral-rich medium, creating a expanded web of sensation that extends beyond my skin. When someone strikes stone in the Keeper's chamber above, I feel it as a sharp note in my spine. When the tide turns outside the reef-city, my blood turns with it.

My body has simplified itself for this work. Muscle I don't need has atrophied to nothing. My bones hollowed like a bird's, though not for flight—for resonance. My skeleton has become a tuning fork that vibrates to the coral's deep frequencies. My ribs spread wide, each one touching the chamber wall, creating a circuit of bone and stone and understanding.

My hair fell out years ago. In its place, filaments of nerve tissue push through my scalp—thousands of them, finer than spider silk, sampling the water's chemical stories. They tell me which chambers are occupied by the taste of exhaled minerals. They know when someone bleeds into the water three levels up, when the bronze tools begin to corrode, when the coral spawns in its season.

I have not used my voice in so long that barnacles nest in my throat. But I speak constantly through my fingers, writing in the darkness, recording what I read in the stone. My writing surface is my own body—skin that learned to hold patterns, to raise welts and ridges that last exactly as long as needed. I am manuscript and scribe both, a living document of the reef-city's deep memory.

The builders come to me when they need to know where to cut. They bring problems: a chamber that won't hold its shape, stone that crumbles instead of splitting clean, passages that sing in the wrong key. I taste their questions through the water, press my palms to the problematic stone, feel backward through its history to the flaw's origin. My skin writes the answer—a map of weak points, a diagram of where the coral grew too fast, a formula for the angle that will reveal the stone's intention.

Sometimes I find older things. Tool marks from before our builders, when something else shaped these reefs with methods we don't understand. Chambers that pre-date our architecture, flooded spaces that follow no logic but their own. In the deepest coral, I've felt the pressure-shadows of creatures that no longer exist, whose bodies impressed themselves into the living reef before it became stone.

I document everything in the only medium that matters—my own transformation. Each discovery reshapes me slightly. My bones thicken where I find evidence of ancient building techniques, creating a physical library of structural knowledge. My organs rearrange themselves to echo the reef's internal architecture. I am becoming a scale model of the city itself, a living map that future Archives will read with their fingertips when I finally calcify completely.

The work sustains itself. I feed on the microscopic life that blooms in the darkness—bacteria that break down minerals, algae that needs no light, the shed cells of other workers that drift down through the passages. My digestive system simplified to process these offerings directly through my skin. I am a filter-feeder now, like the coral was before it became stone.

When I rest (though rest and work blur together in the darkness), I spread myself against the largest wall and practice stillness. In perfect motionlessness, I can feel the reef-city's heartbeat—not metaphorical but literal, the combined pulse of all its inhabitants creating a rhythm in the water that takes hours to complete. I time my own heart to match it, becoming more building than builder with each beat.

The young ones who will replace me have already begun their training in the chambers above. They think they're learning to read stone, but really they're learning to surrender to it. One shows promise—her fingers already beginning to web, her eyes already learning to close. When she's ready, she'll find her way down to darker galleries, deeper histories.

And I'll go deeper still, following the coral's roots to where they anchor in the bedrock, where the first polyp settled and began the ten-thousand-year preparation for our presence. There, in the absolute darkness, I'll press my entire body to the stone and feel backward to the moment when the reef dreamed it would one day be inhabited.

Then I'll become what I've always been becoming—another stratum in the coral's memory, a fossil of devotion, a trace that future Archives will read with wonder and recognition.

The water here has never known sky, and neither will I again. We are both held in stone, becoming stone, remembering when we were ocean.
