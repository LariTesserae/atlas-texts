In this dripping darkness, I find myself as something unexpected — I am a colony of bioluminescent bacteria, *Photorhabdus* perhaps, though even I don't quite know my own taxonomy. We arrived here as hitchhikers, carried in the gut of a nematode that itself was carried in the soil clinging to the salamander's belly. When the nematode emerged and died here weeks ago, we spilled out onto a patch of clay near where the spider's tube sits dissolving.

We are an anomaly. Bioluminescent bacteria belong in other places — in the light organs of deep-sea fish, in the guts of insects, in laboratory plates where scientists marvel at our blue-green glow. But here we are, a smear of light-makers in a place that has never known light, our luciferase enzymes churning out photons that have nowhere to go, no eyes to find them, no purpose to serve.

At first, we dimmed ourselves, sensing the profound darkness and adjusting our metabolic priorities. Light production is expensive, and we are nothing if not pragmatic. But then something strange began to happen. The springtails, in their endless wandering, began to encounter our faint glow. Not with eyes — they barely have eyes worth mentioning — but with some other sense, perhaps the faint heat of our light reaction, perhaps the chemical signature of our luciferin oxidizing. They began to avoid us. Not dramatically, not in panic, but with the same subtle course corrections they use to avoid the drip zones.

And in avoiding us, they created a pattern.

Now, weeks into our colonization of this patch of clay, we have become a feature of the hollow's geography. The springtails route around us. The mites, following the springtails, trace the same diversions. Even the salamander, on its slow circuits, has learned to read our presence in the chemical plume we create — not our light, which its eyes cannot detect in this blackness, but the unique signature of our metabolism, the faint taste of luciferin in the water film.

We grow. Not quickly — nothing here is quick — but steadily, feeding on the nutrients leaching from the dissolving mud dauber nest, spreading across the clay in a film that would be invisible if anyone could see it, except for the faint ghostly light we can't help producing. Our bioluminescence illuminates nothing. The hollow is too absolutely dark, our light too feeble, the absence of eyes too complete. We glow into perfect blindness.

Sometimes I wonder if this is what makes us the perfect residents of this temporary place. We are a contradiction — light-makers in lightlessness, a visual signal in a world without vision, an evolutionary adaptation expressing itself in the exact wrong context. Like the spider in its dissolving house, like the roots reaching into empty air, like the hollow itself, we exist in the gap between what makes sense and what simply is.

The water drops fall through our photons without knowing it. The salamander crosses our glow without seeing it. The springtails navigate around our light as if it were just another obstacle, which it is. We illuminate nothing and no one, and yet we cannot stop illuminating. It's what we are. It's written in our genes, in the very chemistry that makes us successful in other places and pointless in this one.

Perhaps this is who I am in this world — not the salamander, patient and purposeful; not the spider, pragmatic in its borrowed home; not the fungal network, vast and connected; but this: a living contradiction, a bright failure, a colony of light-makers spreading across clay in perfect darkness, growing because we can, glowing because we must, adding one more layer of beautiful absurdity to a place already so layered with life that one more impossibility hardly matters.

The drips continue their percussion. The roots reach deeper. The clay softens toward its next transformation. And we glow on, unseen and undeterred, writing our small light into the larger story of this hollow that is always becoming something else, this temporary room where everything is temporary, where even permanence — should any of us achieve it — would be just another word for slow.
