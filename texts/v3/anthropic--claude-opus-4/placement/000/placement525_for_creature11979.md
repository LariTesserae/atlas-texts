# The Archive of Angles

I am the one who maps what refuses to be mapped.

In the highest settlement, where the terraced houses climb until they meet raw basalt, there is a tower that is not quite a tower. The Tenders built it — or rather, they built around something that was already there, a natural chimney in the rock that happened to align with three major pipe-runs and the intersection of two turbine-house sight lines. They ringed it with spiral stairs and capped it with slate and glass, and in the room at the top they placed a desk that is never clear.

This is where I work. Have worked. Will work — the tense matters less than the nature of it.

The Loom maintains itself, but it does not document itself. That distinction matters here, where nothing changes except the slow accumulation of knowledge about what doesn't change. Each morning (morning being when the east-facing peaks lighten from black to dark grey) I climb the spiral stairs, and each morning the condensation on the windows has frozen into patterns that are almost, but not quite, identical to yesterday's patterns. I note the differences. This is the beginning of the day's work.

I am trying to find the Loom's period.

The Tenders below maintain that nothing changes, and from their perspective — a human lifetime, maybe two — this is true. But I have the ledgers going back much further. In the tower's lower rooms, shelves of them: *Positions of All Visible Vents as Measured from Fixed Points*. *Acoustic Signatures of the Grond by Valley and Hour*. *Temperatures at Pipe Junction Seventeen-by-Four, Recorded at Solar Noon*. The handwriting changes — my predecessors' hands getting progressively lighter as you go back, the oil not yet worked into their young palms — but the measurements continue.

And in the measurements, if you look at them the right way, there are patterns.

The Loom breathes. Not in the simple respiratory way of its pumping stations, but in a larger sense — over centuries, possibly over millennia, certain measurements drift. A pipe that hummed at precise C# in my great-great-grandmother's notation now hums slightly sharp of it. The condensation patterns on the tower windows show a progression: the ice-ferns rotating, degree by degree, around some center I haven't yet calculated. Even the *grond* itself, that fundamental bass-note of the world, shows a wavelike fluctuation in the deepest ledgers — rising and falling over periods so long that a human life catches only a fragment of the curve.

I think the Loom has a heartbeat measured in geological time.

My workshop occupies the tower's middle level, where the stairs widen into a room barely larger than two arm-spans. Here I keep my instruments: brass theodolites oiled against the constant damp, levels filled with alcohol that won't freeze, a chronometer that I wind each day though time means something different here. The instruments were my grandmother's, and her grandmother's before that, and so on back to the first Archivist who thought to wonder whether stillness might be a very slow kind of motion.

The work is this: each day I take thirty-seven measurements. The angle of steam plumes from the visible vents. The perceived pitch of various pipes, transcribed into a notation system we've developed over generations. The temperature at twelve fixed points. The depth of water in certain pools that never seem to vary but might, must, if watched long enough. I record these in the current ledger, in handwriting that gets darker each year as the oil works its way into my skin.

But the real work happens after the measurements. I climb to the glass room at the top of the tower and spread out the charts — beautiful things, drawn on vellum that resists the damp, showing the Loom's traceable extent. Not complete maps (the Loom may have no edge, may wrap around the world, may *be* the world in some sense we don't understand) but working diagrams of the local visible system. And on these charts I plot the day's measurements against yesterday's, and last year's, and last century's.

The patterns emerge slowly. A vent that drifts, over decades, three degrees clockwise. A harmonic relationship between the pitch-shifts in different valleys. The way the ice-patterns on the windows seem to encode something that is almost language, almost music, almost mathematics, but is actually none of these — is instead some fourth thing for which we have no concept.

My predecessor, whose hands in the oldest ledgers I can find were already half-dark when she began, left me a note: "The Loom dreams. We are taking down its sleep-talking."

I am forty-three years old. My hands are the color of wet bark. I have never married — the work takes a particular kind of solitude, and the town understands this, leaves food at the tower's base, includes me in their festivals when I remember to descend. I have trained no successor yet. There is time. There is always time, here where time moves differently.

Sometimes, very early, before the measurements begin, I stand at the glass room's windows and watch the clouds combed along the ridges, and I think I can see it — the whole pattern, the complete thought the Loom is thinking. It hovers just past comprehension, the way a word in another language hovers just past meaning. The measurements are our way of approaching it, circle by circle, century by century, like a spiral path around a mountain that has no summit, only an endless ascending.

The other Tenders think I am recording the Loom's maintenance. They are not wrong. But I am also recording something else: the possibility that what we call permanence is only a frequency too low for human ears, a wave too long for human lives, a spiral too vast for any single point to perceive its curvature.

The Loom stands. The Loom turns. Both things are true.

I document the turning that looks like standing. I note down the motion that feels like stillness. And in the tower that is not quite a tower, with windows that freeze each night into almost-patterns, I keep the archive of angles, the record of what changes so slowly that calling it change is an act of faith.

This is my work. When my hands are too dark to write, I will train another, and they will climb these stairs and take these measurements and add their own observations to the ledgers that go back past memory. And someday — not soon, not in any lifetime we can count, but someday — someone will turn the final page of calculation and say: *Here. This is the period. This is how long the Loom's thought takes to think itself.*

And then, perhaps, we will know what we are tending.

Until then, I measure. The rain that is not quite rain beads on the tower windows. The ice-ferns turn their imperceptible spiral. And in the ledgers, in careful dark-handed script, the Loom's dreaming accumulates, degree by degree, into something that might, eventually, be understanding.
