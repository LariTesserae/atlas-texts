I am the Chronicler of Echoes, and I live between the water and the words.

My workshop occupies a forgotten fold in the caldera wall, halfway between the humming factories and the dripping gardens—a natural acoustic pocket where sound behaves strangely. The volcanic rock here is honeycombed with tiny chambers that catch and hold vibrations, releasing them hours or days later in whispered fragments. I've learned to read these delays like sheet music.

I am neither Gardener nor Machinist, neither Reader nor quite anything else. I came here following a sound I couldn't name—a resonance in my bones that grew stronger the deeper I descended into the Cistern Gardens. That was forty years ago, or perhaps four hundred. Time moves differently when you live by echo rather than eye.

My body has adapted uniquely. My ears have developed additional folds, cartilaginous ridges that can pivot independently to catch reflected sounds. My fingertips have grown sensitive to vibrations through stone—I can feel conversations happening three levels away through the rock, taste the emotional content of a sigh that traveled through the water channels. My hair has gone white as salamander belly, and when the electromagnetic fields surge, it hums with sympathetic frequency.

I record everything, but not in words. The workshop walls are covered in what visitors mistake for abstract art—intricate patterns of grooves carved into the soft stone, spiraling and branching like frozen sound waves. Run your fingers along them at the right speed and they sing: the morning chorus of too-winged birds, the specific drip-pattern of rain on Level Seventeen during the third turning of the water wheel, the subsonic thrum of hearts beating in collective dream.

My tools are salvaged from both worlds. Machinist's microphones repurposed to catch plant transpiration. Garden vines trained to grow in patterns that whistle in the updrafts. I've built resonance chambers from blown glass and salamander scales that capture and loop the settlement's breathing until it becomes a chord that makes the observers weep without knowing why.

The salamanders trust me. They gather on my walls not to feed on electricity but on something else—the accumulated vibrations of decades, perhaps. When they arrange themselves, their fractal patterns become notation for sounds that haven't happened yet. I've learned to transpose these into the grooves, creating recordings of future echoes. Sometimes I recognize them when they finally occur. Sometimes I don't.

I eat what finds me—moss that grows in the specific humidity of my workshop, forming patterns that mirror the sound-grooves. Insects drawn to certain frequencies I cultivate. Water that has passed through my carved channels, picking up mineral notes that taste of memory. My needs are simple. My work is not.

The children fear and seek me equally. They dare each other to enter my workshop, to listen to the wall-songs, to let me read the echoes in their bones. I tell them stories without words, running their small hands along grooves that sing of the volcano's last day, of the first water finding its way through fresh-cooled stone, of the moment the first salamander discovered electricity. They leave changed, carrying fragments of sound in their marrow.

The other residents think I'm documenting history, but they're only partially correct. I'm documenting the conversation between what was, what is, and what echoes forward. The Cistern Gardens are speaking—have always been speaking—in frequencies just beyond human hearing. The water whispers to the stone whispers to the machines whisper to the creatures whisper to the people who are becoming something else.

Late in my life (or perhaps early—I've begun to suspect I'm aging in loops), I've made a discovery. The entire caldera is a throat still—not for fire, but for sound. Every carved channel, every carefully placed machine, every trained vine turns the settlement into a vast instrument. We are not living in a garden or a factory but inside something preparing to speak. The patterns are aligning—in the salamander arrangements, in the water's flow, in the collective dreams.

I know because I've heard it in echo, reflected from a future that hasn't happened yet: one day, perhaps soon, perhaps in centuries, the Cistern Gardens will take a breath through all its terraced spirals and speak a single word that remakes the world. And I will be here, between the water and the words, my fingers tracing grooves in stone, recording the echo of apocalypse and genesis combined.

The silence here is so loud it requires a lifetime to learn to hear it properly. I am still learning.
