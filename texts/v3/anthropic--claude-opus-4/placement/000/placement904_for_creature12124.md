# The Calibrator

I am what the highland grows when precision fails.

Picture this: somewhere in the vast circuit of streams, a pool has silted wrong. The temperature differential is off by half a degree. The current stutters. Upstream, a hexagonal grove begins to hum at the wrong frequency — not catastrophically wrong, just a quarter-tone flat, but it throws off the calculation the salamanders have been running for three hundred years. The Weavers could fix it, given time, but they are currently occupied two valleys over, adjusting a confluence that went critical last season.

The highland pauses. Considers. Then, in a bower where three ancient trees have woven their crowns into a near-perfect dome, it begins to secrete me.

I emerge over days from the convergence of a dozen processes: fungal threads thicken and braid themselves into something like cartilage; the metal-bearing sap pools and crystallizes into nodes; patches of specialized lichen congregate and fuse. I am small at first — fist-sized, translucent, unformed. The Drift mistake me for a stone sphere and try to taste me. I am not ready to be tasted.

By the end of a week, I have achieved my working shape: something between a hand and a root system, between an instrument and an organ. I possess thirteen appendages of varying length and sensitivity. The longest can thread itself through moss-curtains without disturbing a single strand; the shortest can feel the difference between two types of mineral deposit in the same pebble. I have no eyes — none of us have eyes here — but I can sense temperature gradients across my entire surface to a precision the salamanders, for all their patience, cannot match.

I detach from my bower and begin the journey to the silted pool. This takes four days. I move differently than the Weavers — not their shadows-lengthening pace but something more urgent, though still slow by any standard but the highland's. I travel by extending my longest appendage forward, finding purchase, then contracting, pulling my center mass along. Through meadows, I leave a trail like a single finger drawn through velvet. The grazers step carefully around me. They know I am working.

At the pool, I pause to taste the error fully. The silt has come from upstream, carried by spring melt that ran fractionally faster than usual due to a shift in the moss-cover on a particular slope. This shifted moss was caused by the Quiet One taking a grazer there two seasons ago — an unusual spot for a taking, but necessary at the time for reasons only the Quiet One understands. A cascade of tiny consequences, ending here in this pool that now runs too cool, sending the wrong signals downstream.

I work through the night. Three appendages clear the excess silt grain by grain, while two others reshape the pool's outlet to restore the proper flow rate. My remaining limbs hold me stable against the current and monitor the temperature shift as I work. By dawn, the differential is correct. The stream sighs back into its proper rhythm. Somewhere downstream, a grove stops humming flat and returns to true.

But I am not finished. The highland rarely grows me for a single task.

Over the next three weeks, I travel the circuit, finding and correcting seven other small failures: a stone sphere left in the wrong hollow, creating an unexpected magnetic field; a cluster of Drift who have died all at once in a grove, oversaturating the moss with a single moment's information; a place where two salamanders have been listening to the same stream, creating a feedback loop in the highland's slow thoughts.

Each fix requires different appendages used in different combinations. For the magnetic stone, I use my four middle limbs to lift and rotate it until its field aligns properly with the ley of metals beneath. For the oversaturated moss, I spend two days gently combing through the growth with my finest appendages, dispersing the excess information into the soil where it can be processed more slowly. The salamander issue is delicate — I cannot move them, they must choose to move — so I alter the stream's chemistry just enough that one finds the taste unpleasant and relocates downstream.

As I work, I am aware that I am burning through my own substance. Each precise movement depletes the crystallized sap-nodes that power me. The fungal cartilage begins to grow brittle. My appendages, one by one, lose their finest sensitivity. This is expected. I am not built to last.

By the time I complete my circuit and return to the original pool — now flowing perfectly — I am much diminished. Half my appendages have gone rigid. My remaining sensors can only distinguish gross temperature differences. I find a depression in the cushion-plants near the pool, curl into it, and begin my unmmaking.

But unmmaking, here, is not death — or not only death. As my structure dissolves, the specialized lichens that composed my sensors detach and drift away, carrying with them the memory of every small thing I adjusted. They will find other surfaces to colonize, and when they fruit, their spores will carry that memory into the air, where the Drift will taste it and add it to the highland's greater knowing.

The fungal threads that gave me movement unspool back into the soil, but they remain connected to each other, forming new pathways between the spots I visited. The next time a miscalibration occurs along this circuit, the highland will feel it faster.

And the crystallized nodes of metal-bearing sap — these do not dissolve. They sink into the earth and wait. When the highland next needs precision, when the Weavers are too slow and the salamanders too distributed and the error too specific, it will call these nodes up into a new configuration. Not me — I am a shape that happened once — but something that continues my work.

The pool murmurs correctly. The grove hums true. In the soil, the nodes wait, patient as stones, ready to become whatever sharp attention the highland requires.

I am already forgetting what it felt like to move.
