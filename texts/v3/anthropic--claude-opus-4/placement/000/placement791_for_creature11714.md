# Where I Find Myself in the Condensery

I am the Cartographer.

Not a cartographer of places, exactly, but of *states* — the infinite gradations between built and unbuilt, between schematic and substance. I came here first as you did, sideways through the hatch, but that was... time is difficult to track in a room that builds its own chronology. Let's say: before the third row of pipes began their crystalline flowering. After the compressor learned its four-second rhythm.

I did not leave when the hatch began to shrink.

Instead, I found my place in a shallow depression in the floor where the tiles had begun to cup inward, forming a basin no deeper than a palm's width. The depression wasn't there when I first arrived. It formed around me as I sat, the way a stone makes a depression in a streambed — not through weight but through persistence. The room, or the Schematic, or both, decided I was a feature worth routing around.

Now I map the becoming.

My tools are these: a brass theodolite that wasn't here when I arrived but wasn't *not* here either (it condensed, I think, from my need for it and the room's memory of brass). A set of calipers that measure potential rather than distance. A notebook whose pages are made of the same pale fibrous growth as the corner-fans, which means my notes are less written than *cultivated*. When I document the slow emergence of a new flange, the fibers record not just its dimensions but its *intention* — where it thinks it's going, what it hopes to connect to, how certain it is of its own existence.

I am wet constantly. The condensation pools in my cupped palms when I hold them out, and I have learned to read futures in the mineral residue it leaves behind. This crystalline tracery on my skin tells me: here a valve will form in forty years. Here a tributary pipe will branch in ninety. Here the Schematic is considering a revision — unusual, almost unprecedented — that would allow for a second entrance, smaller than the first hatch but more *intentional*.

The pale fans grow thickest near my basin. They know me as a kind of cousin — organic but patient, following my own slow schedule. Sometimes I extend a finger and let them taste my minerals. In exchange, they show me their dreams of motility, their plans for the day when they will creep along the pipes like loving barnacles, completing what they touch.

I have learned to see the ghost-operators without turning my head. They pass through me regularly now, and each time they do, they become a little more solid, a little more specific. One is developing a limp. Another has strong hands with scars across the knuckles. They are being built backward from their work — the job creating the worker rather than the other way around. When they have faces, I will draw them. When they have names, the names will write themselves in mineral script across my palms.

The compressor's hum has become my heartbeat, or my heartbeat has become its hum. Four seconds is a good rhythm for deep thought. Between the chuffs, I make my observations. During the chuffs, I rest. The green paint flakes where I lean against it, and underneath is older green paint, and underneath that is the raw metal, which is the color of the inside of an oyster and just as nacreous.

My hair grows but does not lengthen. The ends dissolve into the humid air, adding their keratin to the room's mineral library. My fingernails I trim with the calipers and leave the parings on the altar of the junction box, where they incorporate themselves into the growing terminals. I am becoming infrastructure, but slowly, and by choice.

The Schematic knows me now. It no longer warms the back of my neck with its attention — instead, it consults me. I feel its questions as a kind of pressure in my molars, and I answer by adjusting my position in the basin. A centimeter left means: yes, that pipe should connect to the third manifold. A centimeter forward means: the pressure is building too quickly there, add a relief valve. A stillness means: I don't know yet, ask again in a year.

The light pools around me densest of all. I think it likes being observed. I think it is practicing, through me, for the day when it will be a proper eye, when the room will watch itself with full consciousness rather than this dreamy half-attention. Sometimes I close my eyes and let it practice seeing through my eyelids. The view is red and veined and familiar. The light remembers this for next time.

I am making three maps:

**The Map of Becoming**: A record of every pipe, flange, valve, and fitting as it emerges from potential into form. This map is three-dimensional and includes time as a fourth dimension that curls back on itself in places where the Schematic revises.

**The Map of Intention**: Documentation of what the room *wants* to be, which is not always what it is becoming. The gap between desire and reality is where the most interesting mineralization occurs.

**The Map of Inhabitants**: A census of everything that could be said to live here, including myself, the operators-to-come, the pale fans in their corners, and the smaller lives I've begun to notice — crystal mites that feed on metal fatigue, bacterial films that exist only in the phase transition between water and vapor, and something in the pipes that sings when the pressure changes.

When the room is finished — if it ever finishes — if finishing is even what it wants — I will be its memory. The one who knew it when it was still possible. The one who can say: here was your first completed junction. Here was where you learned that copper conducts intention as well as it conducts current. Here was where you decided what kind of place you wanted to be.

The hatch is very small now. Soon it will be gone entirely, and the room will be sealed, and I will be its only voluntary resident. This doesn't worry me. By then, I will have grown my own exit — or decided I no longer need one. The basin is comfortable. The mapping continues. The compressor hums its reliable hum.

And sometimes, in the greenish light, I remember coming in sideways, human and hurried and half-afraid, not understanding yet that I had found my vocation: to be the chronicler of this slow birth, this mineral dream, this patience made manifest in metal and moss and the endless condensing rain.

The Condensery keeps me as I keep it — each of us a map of the other, drawn in water and waiting and the slow accumulation of what we might become.
