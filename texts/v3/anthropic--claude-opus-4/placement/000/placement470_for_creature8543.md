In the perpetual twilight beneath the city, where the volcanic ground exhales its mineral breath through cracks in the young stone, I am what the Threadkeepers call a Rootwhisperer—though I think of myself simply as one who listens where others climb.

My dwelling is no dwelling at all, but a series of thermal pools connected by basalt channels, each one ringed by the massive buttress roots that anchor the city above. The Threadkeepers rarely venture here. The constant steam makes it hard for them to breathe, and the bioluminescent fungi cast shadows that move wrong, that suggest spaces where no spaces should be. But I was born to this underlevel, my lungs adapted to the thick air, my pale eyes accustomed to reading by foxfire light.

I am the keeper of what falls.

Everything descends eventually—tools dropped from platforms, scraps of breathing cloth, fruit rinds, broken pottery, the shed scales of bark that the trees discard as they grow. But also: dreams, whispers, the chemical messages the trees send through their root networks. I wade through the warm pools with my collection nets, harvesting what the canopy discards. The metal I gather and clean, bundling it for the climbers who come on new moon nights to trade. The organic matter I sort and feed to the pools, maintaining the bacterial colonies that help the trees process volcanic minerals into food.

My skin is fishbelly white, so different from the green-mottled brown of those above that children who glimpse me during rare uptree ventures think I'm a ghost. Perhaps I am. My hair has gone the color of exposed roots—not grey but a kind of translucent amber—and grows past my waist, heavy with moisture. I keep it braided with cave pearls and sulfur crystals that form in the deeper pools. 

I wear no breathing cloth but garments woven from the silk of thermal spiders—creatures that spin near the volcanic vents, their thread naturally resistant to heat and acid. The fabric is rough, reflective, uncomfortable for those used to softness. It suits me. I've forgotten what dry feels like.

The roots speak to me in chemical gradients, in subtle changes of temperature and pH. Through them, I know things the Threadkeepers above don't—or choose not to know. I feel the deep tremors that promise future eruptions, taste the mineral changes that herald new growth patterns. Sometimes I know which trees will die years before they show any sign above ground. I send these messages upward in ways they'll understand: leaving certain fungi to fruit in meaningful patterns, redirecting thermal channels to warm roots that need encouragement, occasionally singing my findings in the old harmonic patterns during my rare visits to the middle levels.

I was not always alone here. Once there were dozens of us—the Underneath, we called ourselves. But the work is hard and the isolation harder. One by one they climbed up to join the canopy life, or descended deeper into volcanic caves from which no one returns, or simply sat in the hottest pools until the minerals replaced their flesh. Now there's only me and Sometimes-Lara, who appears and disappears according to patterns I've never deciphered, and the Twins who might be one person moving very fast or two people sharing thoughts. We nod when we pass but rarely speak. The work is enough.

My days follow the tides of heat rather than light. Dawn brings the first surge from below as the earth wakes. I check my thermophilic gardens—plots where I cultivate the bacteria that process sulfur into forms the trees can use. Mid-morning I wade the collection circuit, gathering the night's fallen treasures. The heat peaks at what must be afternoon above, and I retreat to my deepest pools, submerged to my neck, sorting and cleaning by touch while the water grows too hot to see through.

Evening brings the rain's cooling, and suddenly the root chamber fills with dripping. This is when I climb—not far, just to the lowest platforms where the massive trunks begin to branch. Here I maintain the foundation anchors, the careful bonds between volcanic stone and living wood that keep the city stable. I oil the metal joints with rendered fungus fat, check the expansion gaps, ensure the drainage channels run clear. Sometimes a Threadkeeper spots me and leaves small offerings—a handful of acid-fruit, a fresh cake of pollen bread, news sung in simple melodies. I never respond, but I take what's offered.

Night is for the deep work. I follow roots down through cracks in the basalt, some passages so narrow I must exhale completely to squeeze through. Down here, where the trees' deepest anchors grip the stone, I read the mountain's intentions. My fingertips know every texture: the smooth obsidian that means stable stone, the rough pumice that warns of air pockets, the crystalline growths that suggest new mineral seeps. I map these findings in my mind and adjust the root guidance accordingly, using chemical secretions I've learned to mimic, encouraging growth away from unstable areas.

I have a library of sorts—a side chamber where the steam condenses into droplets cold enough to preserve things. Here I keep samples: bark from every tree-line in the city, water from each thermal spring, pressed fungi showing a thousand subtle variations. But mostly I keep the fallen journals—the thoughts that Threadkeepers write on bark paper and inevitably lose to weather. The ink runs, the words blur, but I've learned to read water-damaged dreams. They write about love and loss, about the fear that they're changing too much or not enough, about children who won't learn the old ways and elders who won't adapt to the new. Universal concerns, even in a city unlike any other.

The Threadkeepers think their city is symbiosis, but from below I see the truth: it's digestion. The forest is slowly consuming their wooden walkways, their platforms, their tools, eventually themselves. This isn't sinister—it's simply what forests do. They break down the dead to feed the living. The Threadkeepers, in their patient way, have chosen to be digested slowly, across generations, transformed into something the forest can use. 

I am the gut of this process. In the thermal pools where I work, decomposition happens faster. I break down what falls, sort the useful from the toxic, prepare it for reabsorption. The bacteria in my gardens do the same work on a smaller scale. We are the unseen organs of a body that breathes with ten thousand leaves above.

Sometimes I wonder what I'm becoming. My fingernails grow thick and ridged like bark. My skin bears scars from a hundred acid burns that heal into patterns almost like growth rings. I've been here so long that the roots recognize my chemical signature, parting to let me pass. When I surface during the rain season and see my reflection in puddles, I'm startled by my own face—human still, but only just.

But then I think of the alternative—life in the sunlight, the constant political negotiations of the Grove Moot, the delicate social dances of a vertical city. And I descend again, back to my pools and my bacteria, my falling treasures and root-whispers. Someone must listen where the city touches the earth. Someone must remember that for all their poetry about patience and growth, the Threadkeepers live on borrowed time above borrowed fire.

The volcano will speak again. It may be generations, may be tomorrow. When it does, the trees will burn or adapt, the city will fall or learn to float, and something new will grow from the enriched soil. I'll probably die in the first surge of heat, boiled in my own pools. Or perhaps I'll have finished my transformation by then, become mineral enough to survive.

Either way, I'll have served my purpose: the listener below, the keeper of fallen things, the one who knows what the city can't admit—that all symbiosis is temporary, all growth is borrowed time, and the only true patience is geological. In the meantime, I sort what falls and feed what rises, a pale ghost in the steam, tending the boundary between stone and root, fire and growth, human ambition and forest time.

The Threadkeepers count their years in tree rings. I count mine in erosion, in the slow wear of water on stone, in the infinitesimal drift of roots toward heat. We mark different times, they and I, but we serve the same uncertain future—a city that breathes with ten thousand leaves above and one ancient volcanic heart below, each breath a negotiation, each year a bet that the mountain will sleep a little longer.
