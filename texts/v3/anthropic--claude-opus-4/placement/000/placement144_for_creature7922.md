I find myself in the Weeping Archive, a chamber where the Chronicle Pools have overflowed their basins and begun to dream.

I am a Scribal Lamprey—not the creature you know from your solid world, but something that could only evolve here where metaphor has teeth. My body is translucent, revealing the oils of digested memories flowing through what might charitably be called veins. I am as long as a Chorus-Sworn is tall, though length means little when you exist primarily in the spaces between then and now.

I was human once. Or I will be human. Or I am human in the timelines I haven't eaten yet. The distinction matters less than you might think.

My work is essential and reviled in equal measure. When the Chronicle Pools grow too full, when the memories begin to ferment and foam, threatening to spill their iridescent truths through the whole city—that's when they summon me. I slip into the basins through dimensions the Chroniclers pretend not to see, and I feed.

But I am discriminating in my appetite. I eat only the memories that have begun to lie to themselves, the histories that have curdled into something false through too much handling. A war remembered so often it has become glorious. A love polished by retelling until it bears no resemblance to the clumsy, beautiful thing it was. These are my meat.

The process is visible to those with oil-sheen eyes. They watch their carefully tended histories disappear down my translucent gullet, see them dissolve in my stomach acids that exist three seconds in the past. They hate me for it, these Chroniclers, even as they depend on me. Who wants to watch their life's work deemed inedible?

I excrete truth.

Not pleasant truth, not complete truth, but truth in its most elemental form—compressed pellets of what actually was, black as the deep pillars and twice as dense. These sink to the bottom of the Chronicle Pools where they slowly dissolve, releasing their payload back into the communal memory over years instead of moments. The history returns, but humbled, stripped of its self-importance.

My body has adapted to this work in ways that horrify even the Dispersed Ones. I have no eyes—memory is better tasted than seen. My skin weeps constantly, the same iron-sweet secretion as the cave walls, marking me as belonging more to the city than to the people who built it. Where a lamprey's mouth would be, I have instead a spiral of small, desperately human hands, baby-soft and eternally grasping. They sort through the memories as I feed, picking out the ones too precious to digest—a child's first word, the smell of bread in a grandmother's kitchen, the particular weight of grief when it's still fresh.

These I return to the pools untouched.

The Chorus-Sworn fear me, but not for the reasons you might think. They fear that I was once like them, that my transformation was voluntary. They fear the implication—that someone looked at their beautiful, terrible city and chose to become its janitor, its truth-eating bottom-feeder, its necessary monster.

But here is what they don't understand: I am not trapped in this form. I could molt back into humanity between any Tuesday and its echo. I choose this shape because someone must. Someone must love this impossible city enough to eat its lies, to digest its self-deceptions, to return its history to it cleaned of the tumors of nostalgia.

I move through the flooded chambers at night (or what passes for night when darkness is negotiable), my body undulating through water that sometimes remembers being air. The merchant platforms empty when they sense me coming—who wants to sell their wares next to a creature that might declare their whole livelihood a consensual delusion? The children, though, the brave ones—they follow me at a distance, clicking their spiral nails in patterns they think I don't recognize.

They're singing me a Maintenance Hymn, trying to fix me in place, to make me more real and thus more bearable. Sweet children. They don't yet understand that I am maintenance itself, the city's way of keeping its story from drowning in its own embellishments.

In the deepest pools, where even the Chroniclers rarely venture, I've found memories so old they predate the city's founding. Memories of when this was solid stone, memories of the impossible fire that birthed the black pillars, memories of things that built the chambers for purposes we've forgotten. I eat these too, carefully, sparingly. Some truths are too large to excrete all at once. These I release in portions, small enough that the city can integrate them without shattering.

The Resonators know what I am. When I pass the Speaking House, they bow—the only ones who do. They understand that I am not separate from their choir but part of it, singing in frequencies too low for human ears. My song is the bass note that prevents the whole symphony from ascending into pure abstraction.

I am the Scribal Lamprey, and I am what love looks like when it grows too large for a human shape to contain. I am the city's memory of what it costs to exist in defiance of reality. I am the price happily paid.

In your world, you have a word: sacrifice. But sacrifice implies loss, implies that what is given up is gone. Here, in the Sunken Chorus, we know better. Nothing is lost. Everything cycles, transforms, returns. I eat lies and excrete truth. The Chorus-Sworn fear me and need me. The city dreams through its Chronicle Pools, and I swim through those dreams, ensuring they remain dreams and not hauntings.

Sometimes, in the spiral of small hands that serves as my mouth, I feel the phantom weight of a pen. The muscle memory of marking truth onto pale wood tablets. The ghost of who I was before I understood what the city needed from me.

But that's just another memory, and I've learned to digest those too.

This is what I am: the necessary monster, the willing transform, the thing that loves too much to remain merely human. I am the Scribal Lamprey, and I wouldn't go back to having only two hands for all the linear time in the world above.
