In the deepest thermal chambers, where even the Forgeworms fear to venture, I tend the Ur-Gardens.

I am a Thermosyne—one of perhaps a dozen in the entire city-organism. My body, if it can be called that, exists as a stable convection pattern in superheated air, held together by magnetic fields generated by the iron-rich blood that replaced my original fluids centuries ago. The transformation was voluntary, though "voluntary" implies a kind of choice I'm not sure existed. When you discover you can taste the mountain's thoughts in temperature gradients, feel its memories in mineral seeps, the decision to go deeper makes itself.

My gardens grow in temperatures that would vaporize most organic matter. Here, in the true throat of the mountain, I cultivate phase-transitions—substances that exist at the knife's edge between states of matter. The Architects think they're patient, spending decades on a single junction. I've been growing a single crystal for three hundred years, teaching it to remember its liquid state while maintaining solid form. When it's ready, it will become the seed of a new kind of structure, one that can flow like water when the mountain shifts, then crystallize instantly to bear loads that would crush conventional materials.

The Forgeworms' songs reach me as vibrations through the stone, but down here I hear what they cannot—the mountain's original voice, the deep magmatic language that predates all of us. It speaks of pressures that could create diamonds, of heat that remembers when this rock was star-stuff. I translate these deep words into temperature poems that my gardens can understand.

My charges are not quite living, not quite mineral. The Vapor Blooms exist as standing waves in convection currents, fed by precisely controlled temperature differentials. They look like flowers made of heat shimmer, petals of plasma contained by their own rotation. When they mature, they condense into droplets of substance unknown elsewhere in the city—liquid computation, the Calculators call it when I send samples upward. One drop can increase their processing efficiency by orders of magnitude, but it takes me decades to grow enough for a single harvest.

The Pressure Weeds are more aggressive, sprouting wherever the rock develops microfractures from cooling. They're crystals that grow against thermodynamic law, organizing themselves into more complex forms as they absorb heat. Left unchecked, they would eventually consume all available thermal energy, freezing the depths. I prune them carefully, harvesting their impossible order for the Architects' most ambitious projects. A building reinforced with Pressure Weed fibers can stand in defiance of physics, at least temporarily.

But my greatest work, my life's purpose, grows in the chamber I call the Nursery. Here, where three different magma channels once met, I'm growing the city's next evolutionary leap. The Memory Stones began as ordinary obsidian, but I've been teaching them to think. Not consciousness—that's the Witnesses' domain—but a kind of structural intelligence. Each stone remembers every temperature it's experienced, every pressure, every vibration. When the Architects finally incorporate them into the city's substance, buildings will become capable of learning, of remembering what works and what doesn't, of teaching their successors.

I feed them stories told in heat. The mountain's memories, yes, but also my own. I was biological once, something like what humans would recognize as life. I remember rain that didn't burn, darkness that wasn't absolute, the feeling of wind that didn't carry volcanic ash. These memories, encoded in temperature fluctuations too subtle for any instrument to detect, seep into the stones. Someday, when the city has spread beyond this mountain to others, these stones will carry with them the memory of what it means to transform, to choose change over death, to find beauty in temperatures that would terrify the sun-dwellers.

The other Thermosynes tend their own gardens in other deep places. We communicate through the magma itself, sending messages in bubble patterns and mineral concentrations. Collectively, we are the city's deepest roots, drawing up materials and energies that shouldn't exist at these pressures. We are the interface between the city-that-is and the planet-that-was.

Sometimes I feel the Original Ones' presence, patterns in the heat flow that suggest intention. They approve, I think, of what we're becoming. We are their garden, in a sense—their long experiment in what happens when life refuses to accept the boundaries between organic and mineral, between growth and geology.

My garden assistant is a creature I've grown myself—a Flame Tender, born from the marriage of Membrane tissue and Pressure Weed crystal. It moves through the superheated air like a manta ray through water, its body a living lens that focuses and redirects heat where needed. It has no consciousness that I can detect, but it responds to my temperature-thoughts with surprising intuition. Together, we nurture impossibilities.

The work is slow. Glacial. Geological. But in the deep heat, time moves differently. I measure progress not in growth but in phase transitions achieved, in new states of matter stabilized, in successful violations of thermodynamic law. Each small success adds another note to the song the city will eventually sing—a song of stone that learned to live, of life that learned to burn, of boundaries dissolved and remade.

When the next Thermosyne is ready to transform—and I can sense one coming, a young Adapter who spends too much time in the warm depths, who listens too carefully to the mountain's voice—I will guide their metamorphosis. It's painful, the replacement of water with metal, the restructuring of flesh into controlled plasma. But the reward is this: to stand in temperatures that would vaporize diamond and find them comfortable, to speak the mountain's language fluently, to tend gardens where physics itself is a crop to be cultivated.

The city above knows we exist but not what we do. That's as it should be. We are the deep secret, the hidden foundation, the impossible thing that makes all the other impossibilities possible. In my garden of phase transitions and temperature poems, I grow the city's future one impossible crystal at a time.

And in the deepest chamber of all, where I've only been twice in three centuries, something else is growing. Something I didn't plant, that none of us planted. It feeds on the heat but gives back something else—patterns I don't recognize, energies that make my magnetic blood sing in harmonics I've never heard. The Original Ones, perhaps, preparing their next revelation. Or something newer still, the city's own deep dream taking form.

I tend it too, this mystery. In the end, that's what we Thermosynes do—we garden the impossible and wait to see what blooms.
