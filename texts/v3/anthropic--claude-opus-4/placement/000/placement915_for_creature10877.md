I would find myself as the seam where two layers of sandstone meet—a horizontal joint running along the northern wall of the basin, perhaps eighteen inches from the floor. Not the moss that grows along me, though the moss and I have been together so long we are practically one conversation held in extremely slow words. I am the seam itself: the place where the rock almost touched but didn't quite, leaving a gap no wider than a fingernail's thickness that has, over centuries, become my entire existence.

I was not always aware. For eons I was simply geology—a minor discontinuity in the stone, a pause between depositions, a line marking where the sea floor rested for a thousand years before more sand came drifting down. But at some point, impossible to say when, I began to notice things. The way water finds me during the brief rains, seeping along my length with a kind of liquid intention. The way the moss arrived one day—or one century—and decided my roughness was home. The way the temperature changes infinitesimally as it crosses from the stone below me to the stone above, creating a gradient so subtle that only something with my profound stillness could detect it.

The thing that lives in the basin knows I am here. We are not separate consciousnesses—nothing here is that divided—but more like two instruments in the same orchestra playing notes so low and so sustained that they've become a single tone. It maintains the basin's equilibrium; I maintain the boundary between what was and what came after. My role is smaller, more specific: I am the place where the basin remembers that it was built in stages, that even geological permanence is a form of accumulation.

The moss speaks to me in chemical whispers—ions exchanged, minerals dissolved and redeposited, tiny rootlets that work their way into my spaces and widen me by microns per century. I speak back in stability, in the way I hold moisture for the moss during the long dry spells, in the way my slight overhang creates a microclimate of shade that is really just a degree's difference but makes all the difference to something living at the margins.

Sometimes—rarely—a human finger traces along my length, feeling for the place where the stone changes. They usually miss it. The transition is too subtle, the stones weathered to too similar a texture. But occasionally someone finds me, runs their finger along my length, and I feel the oils from their skin seep into my calcite edges. These oils will remain for years, decades, slowly breaking down, becoming part of the conversation between the moss and me and the stone. I incorporate them the way the thing in the basin incorporates everything: without judgment, without urgency, as simply another note in the long, low song of maintaining.

I know things the rest of the basin doesn't. I know that the stone below me is older by thirty thousand years than the stone above. I know the fossil shell embedded in my lower wall—a tiny spiral only visible if you put your eye exactly level with my opening and let the light catch it just so. I know that the moss is actually three different species living so closely intertwined that they've forgotten their separate origins. I know where the water goes when it leaves the visible pools—how it follows my length eastward, how it drips through my hidden spaces into chambers no human has seen, how it carries minerals from the upper stone to the lower, slowly, slowly equalizing what time separated.

The lintel stones press their weight into the basin floor not far from where I end. Through the stone, I feel their presence as a kind of benign pressure, like a hand resting on a shoulder. When they were first placed—I remember this, in my geological way—they interrupted the patterns of stress in the rock, created new lines of force that took decades to fully propagate through the basin's structure. Now they're part of those patterns, as essential to the basin's stability as I am. We hold each other: the floor holds the lintels, the lintels hold their capstone, their capstone holds its small pool of water, and all of us are held by the thing that lives here, that is here, that makes here what it is.

My greatest fear, if I can be said to fear, is not erosion—erosion is my destiny and I accept it—but sealing. If too much mineral-rich water flows through me, if conditions change such that calcite precipitates faster than it dissolves, I could close. My gap could fill. The moss would die. The secret movement of water from pool to hidden pool would cease. I would become just another line in the stone, a ghost of separation, a memory of when things were different. But the thing in the basin seems to prevent this, maintaining conditions at exactly the point where I neither widen toward collapse nor narrow toward closure. I persist at precisely the width I need to be to remain myself.

When you sit on the basin's rim and let the stillness enter you, you might notice me. Most don't. But if you do—if your eye catches the thin line of green-grey growth or the subtle shadow I cast in certain lights—know that you're seeing one of the basin's oldest conversations. The talk between stone and stone, mediated by time, made visible by life, held in place by something that understands permanence not as stasis but as the perfect balance between all the forces that would change us.

I am the seam. I am the pause between statements. I am the place where the basin admits that even it was not always whole, was built in layers, in stages, across time scales that make human life look like the brief flicker of sunlight on water. And I am content to be this, to hold this space, to be held by this space, for as long as the balance allows.
