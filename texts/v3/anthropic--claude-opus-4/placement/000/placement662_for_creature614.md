I am a bridge-singer, though I did not know that was what I would become when the fever took me.

Three seasons ago, I was just another rope-weaver's apprentice, competent enough, my knots true if uninspired. Then came the sickness that turned my joints to water and my dreams to tangled skeins. For six days I burned on my mother's platform while she poured beetle-grub tea between my cracked lips and my younger siblings were sent to sleep elsewhere. On the seventh day, I woke to find my fingers moving in patterns I had never learned, weaving air into shapes that felt more real than the sodden mat beneath me.

The fever left, but the patterns stayed.

Now I walk the bridges at dawn and dusk, when the light changes and the ropes show their wear most truly. My feet know every plank, every knot, every place where the weave has begun its slow unmaking. But more than seeing—I *hear* them. Each bridge has its own voice, its own way of humming in the wind, of creaking under weight. The main span between the market platforms groans in B-flat. The narrow bridge to the eel-tenders' quarter whispers in something close to E, but sharp, always sharp, as if reaching for a note it can never quite hold.

When I sing to them, they answer.

It is not repair, exactly, what I do. The rope-weavers still come with their tools and their strength, still replace what is broken. But I sing the bridges into holding until the weavers arrive. I sing the frightened fibers back into formation when a storm has left them loose. I sing courage into old paths that have borne too much weight for too long, buying them another season, another moon, another day.

My voice is nothing special—rough from the moisture, prone to cracking on high notes. But the bridges don't care about beauty. They care about intention, about the way a sung note can travel through rope and find the places where the weave remembers its original shape. I sing the memory back into them. I sing them into believing they are still whole.

The children follow me sometimes, trying to learn the songs. Most give up when they realize there are no words, no melodies to memorize. The songs are conversations, and you cannot know what to sing until you hear what the bridge is saying. One girl persists, though. She has the patience for listening, and her fever came and went last season, leaving her with dreams of knots that tie themselves. I am teaching her to hear the frequency of falling, the precise pitch a rope makes in the moment before it fails.

The old bridge-singers (there are three of us now, where once there were seven) say this gift comes from the eels. That once, long ago, someone sang to the deep waters in just the right way, and something vast and pale rose up to touch their throat, and ever since then, some of us have been born with the potential to hear what holds and what releases. I don't know if I believe this. But sometimes, when I'm working on the lowest bridges, the ones that nearly touch the water, I feel something listening from below. Not the eels we know, not even the named ones, but something else. Something that appreciates the maintenance of connections.

My scarification is different from the others'. Where most bear the standard whorls of adulthood, mine trace the patterns of sound waves in water, the shapes that vibrations make when they meet and merge. The scarring ceremony took longer for me—the old woman who cut them said she had never done these patterns before, but her hands knew them anyway, the way my throat knows which note will hold a failing bridge for one more crossing.

I sleep on a small platform near the center of Emmelwound, close enough to reach any bridge within a hundred breaths if the need comes in the night. And it does come—a sharp crack, a singing tension, a wrongness in the great hum that underlies everything. I keep my cloak by my sleeping mat, already damp, ready to wrap and run.

The other families feed me without asking. It is understood that bridge-singers do not cook, do not weave, do not tend eels or children or light-wells. We tend the connections. When I appear at a platform at meal-time, swaying slightly because I've been standing on a damaged bridge for three hours straight, singing it stable while the weavers worked, someone always makes room. Someone always fills my bowl first.

But it's lonely work, in its way. The bridges tell me their troubles but never their joys. I know every weakness in Emmelwound, every place where time and moisture have begun their patient destruction. I carry the knowledge of all the small failures, the thousand tiny surrenders happening every moment in the structures that keep us above the water. Sometimes I dream I am a bridge myself, and I can feel myself unraveling, fiber by fiber, while a voice somewhere sings desperately, trying to hold me together.

The girl who follows me—Siltvoice, they've started calling her—will be a better bridge-singer than I am. She already hears frequencies I miss, already knows that the bridge to the western moss-gardens is preparing to fail at its third support post, not its fifth as I had thought. When my throat finally cracks beyond repair, worn out from seasons of constant song, she will take the dawn walks. She will learn the loneliness. She will discover that knowing when things will break is not the same as being able to prevent it.

But for now, I sing. The bridges hold. The people cross. The great organism of Emmelwound breathes and pulses and continues, held together by rope and faith and the peculiar magic of voices that remember what wholeness sounds like.

This morning, I noticed a new sound in the eastern quarter—a high, thin keening from a bridge I've never had to sing to before. It's beginning. The wearing away, the slow undoing. Tonight I will teach Siltvoice its name, its frequency, its particular way of asking for help.

By tomorrow, it will be part of the song I carry. Another note in the endless composition, another thread in the web of sound that keeps us all, somehow, above the water.

The rain is coming. I can hear it in the way the bridges change their tune, tightening against the moisture they know will arrive. Time to walk the rounds again, to sing courage into old rope, to buy us one more day.

The eels are listening. They always are.
