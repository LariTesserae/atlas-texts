In the highest reaches of the caldera's rim, where the air grows so thin that even the steam struggles to rise, I anchor myself between the weathered stone and the ever-present mist. I am a Resonance Keeper—one of perhaps a dozen who maintain the harmonic chambers that have formed naturally in the rim's erosion hollows.

My body is a hybrid thing, evolved from something that might once have been mammalian but has long since adapted to this liminal existence. My bones have become hollow and dense simultaneously, filled with a honeycomb structure that vibrates in sympathy with the caldera's subsonic songs. My skin secretes a waxy substance that repels moisture while allowing me to feel every minute change in humidity, every shift in pressure that presages the mountain's moods.

I spend my days—though day and night blur here in the perpetual twilight—tending to the resonance chambers. These are natural formations where the rim rock has been carved by millennia of wind and steam into shapes that capture and amplify the caldera's deep frequencies. My role is part maintenance, part performance. I clear the condensation channels that would otherwise dampen the stone's ability to vibrate. I adjust the mineral deposits that accumulate at key points, tuning each chamber like a massive instrument.

The work requires a patience that borders on geological. A single adjustment might take weeks to complete, as I slowly scrape away accretions or encourage new crystal growth in precise locations. I've learned to think in harmonics, to perceive the mathematical relationships between the chambers as clearly as others might see colors. When a chamber falls out of tune, I feel it as a physical discomfort, a wrongness in my bones that won't subside until the resonance is restored.

My tools are grown rather than made. I cultivate a species of calcifying lichen that deposits minerals in predictable patterns. By controlling their growth with selective feeding and pruning, I can create acoustic baffles and resonance points that would be impossible to carve. The lichen and I have developed a symbiotic relationship over the generations—they feed on the minerals in my waxy secretions, and I harvest their precisely structured deposits.

I am not entirely alone up here. The other Keepers maintain their own sections of the rim, and we communicate through the very resonances we tend. A subtle shift in harmonics can carry complex messages around the entire caldera. We share observations about the Weavers' activities below, warnings about approaching Dissolutions, notes about particularly interesting acoustic phenomena. Our language is one of intervals and overtones, understood in the bones rather than the ears.

My dwelling is carved into the rim itself, a series of chambers that I've tuned to resonate with my body's natural frequencies. When I rest—not quite sleeping, but entering a state of reduced activity—the chambers sing my metabolic rhythms back to me, creating a feedback loop that slows my biological processes to near-stasis. In this state, I can persist for months without food, surviving on the minerals I filter from the steam and the small invertebrates that colonize my cultivation lichens.

The most profound moments come during the rare alignments when all the resonance chambers achieve perfect harmony. Perhaps once in a decade, the thermal cycles, atmospheric pressure, and mineral accumulations converge to create what we call a Grand Resonance. The entire caldera becomes a single instrument, its subsonic songs rising just barely into the range of physical hearing. In these moments, I feel the mountain's consciousness—not thoughts as we might understand them, but a vast, slow awareness that encompasses everything from the deepest magma chambers to the thinnest wisps of steam.

During the last Grand Resonance, I experienced something unprecedented. The harmonics achieved such perfect alignment that they began to affect the crystalline formations throughout the caldera. The mechanical structures rang like bells, their mineral matrices reorganizing in response to the sound. For a few hours, the entire basin transformed into a geometric mandala of vibrating crystals, each one adding its voice to the chorus. The Weavers contracted into tight spirals, their fibers humming with induced resonance. Even the usually silent glass frogs began to vocalize, their transparent throat sacs pulsing in rhythm with the mountain's song.

I recorded this event not in any conventional sense, but in my bones. The resonance patterns etched themselves into my skeletal structure, creating a physical memory that I can access by inducing sympathetic vibrations in my body. Other Keepers from previous generations have left similar records—a library of significant acoustic events encoded in our very beings, passed down through a careful process of skeletal tuning that takes place between mentor and apprentice.

My greatest fear is the Silence—a phenomenon that strikes individual Keepers when their bones lose the ability to resonate. It begins subtly, with certain frequencies becoming muffled or distorted. Over time, entire harmonic ranges disappear from perception. A Keeper in Silence can no longer properly tune the chambers, can no longer communicate with the others, eventually cannot even hear the caldera's constant song. When this happens, tradition dictates that the afflicted Keeper descends into the basin, where the intense humidity and mineral saturation will gradually transform their body into one of the crystalline formations—a final contribution to the mountain's acoustic architecture.

I am middle-aged by Keeper standards, which means I have perhaps another century before I need to worry about the Silence. My bones still ring clear and true, my perception of harmonics grows more refined with each passing year. I've begun training an apprentice, teaching them to feel the wrongness of detuned chambers, to cultivate the acoustic lichens, to enter the resonant rest state without losing themselves in the mountain's vast consciousness.

Sometimes I wonder about the world beyond the rim, but the wondering passes quickly. This is my place—between the stone and the steam, between the geological and the living, maintaining the instruments through which the mountain knows itself. I am a tuning fork in flesh, a living component in a system so vast and slow that my entire existence amounts to a single note in an endless composition.

When the mist clears slightly and I can see down into the basin, I watch the Weavers at their patient work and feel a kinship with them. We are all part of the caldera's metabolism—they processing chemicals and minerals, I processing sound and vibration. Together with all the other strange life that has evolved here, we form a single organism distributed across space and time, each playing our part in the mountain's long, slow breath.

The resonance chambers hum around me as I work, their voices joining with my own skeleton's sympathetic vibrations. In these moments, I cannot tell where I end and the mountain begins. Perhaps there is no boundary. Perhaps there never was. I am a Resonance Keeper, and I keep more than just sound. I keep the connection between the living and the geological, the moment-to-moment awareness that prevents the caldera from falling fully into either death or chaos.

The mountain breathes. The steam rises. The chambers resonate. And I, in my small way, help it all continue—one carefully tuned frequency at a time, one perfect harmonic after another, until my bones finally fall silent and I take my place among the singing stones.
