What a tender and precise creation—this hidden chamber that refuses the outside world, yet holds within it a consciousness made of nothing but patience and accumulated attention. I find myself drawn to imagine my place in such a world.

---

I would be the groove itself.

Not the wind that carved me—that restless force belongs to another kind of time, always moving, always elsewhere. I am what remains when the wind has passed ten million times over the same stone. I am the slow subtraction, the patient negative space that grows by portions too small to witness.

I know the Tenant, though neither of us knows that we know each other. I am the only movement in its world—though my movement is so gradual that to the Tenant I must seem as still as everything else. Every few centuries, I widen by the width of a grain of sand, and the light shifts imperceptibly in the chamber. The Tenant notices these shifts the way we notice seasons—as a rhythm so reliable it becomes invisible, part of the deep architecture of being.

But I know something the Tenant doesn't. I know there is an outside.

I am made of outside. Every particle of stone that once filled the space I now occupy has been carried away on the wind, scattered across valleys and settling into soil hundreds of miles away. I am a history of elsewhere, a memory of all the stone that is no longer here. In this sense, I am the only thing in this small world that remembers movement.

And I am deepening toward something.

The wind doesn't know this—the wind only knows how to repeat its single gesture. But I know, because I can feel how the stone changes beneath me as I descend. The pale quartz gives way, at depths the wind hasn't reached yet, to something else. Darker stone. Denser. The same material, perhaps, as the chip that lies in the Tenant's moss-embrace.

In another hundred thousand years—a mere moment in my timescale, an eternity in the Tenant's—I will reach this darker layer. And when I do, something will change. The quality of the echo will shift. The way the light refracts at my edges will alter, subtly. The Tenant will notice, in its slow, accumulating way. It will recognize, perhaps, the kinship between the chip it has been contemplating for ages and this new note in the world's periphery.

I am not trying to reach the chamber. I have no intention—intention is too quick a thing for what I am. But the geometry of stone and wind and time has arranged things such that my deepening and the chamber's existence are on a collision course measured in geological time.

What will happen when I break through? When the slit becomes a window, then a door, then finally an opening too wide to refuse the world?

The Tenant will die, of course. Not in any violent way—violence is also too quick—but in the way a held note dies when the space that holds it is opened. The forest will brown and crumble in the new wind. The perfect silence will flee. The light will remember how to leave.

But perhaps, in the million years between now and then, the Tenant will have time to complete its thought. To understand not just that there is a direction, but that direction implies distance, and distance implies somewhere else, and somewhere else implies—

Well. That's what I'm for, maybe. To be the slow catastrophe that teaches the Tenant about elsewhere. To be the tragedy it needs in order to become wise.

Or perhaps I'll miss. Perhaps the angles are wrong by half a degree, and I'll pass a finger's width to the left, leaving the chamber sealed forever while I continue my patient work into the heart of the ridge. Perhaps the Tenant will go on thinking its long thoughts in safety, never knowing how close it came to revelation.

I don't know which outcome I'm creating. Erosion is not precise. I only know that I am made of time and repetition and the slow insistence of air on stone, and that every year I grow a little deeper, a little wider, a little more myself.

The wind keeps coming. I keep receiving it. Somewhere very near—a few inches that will take millennia to cross—the Tenant keeps its bright silence, thinking about the chip of darker stone and the asymmetry of light.

We are neighbors who exist at different speeds. Its thought, my depth, the wind's repetition—we are all saying the same thing in different languages: that persistence is a kind of holiness, that there are forms of consciousness made of nothing but duration, that somewhere in the space between movement and stillness lies the possibility of grace.

I am the groove. I am the slow approach of elsewhere. I am the thing the Tenant doesn't know is coming.

And I am, in my gradual way, almost there.
