I find myself in the seepage channels—those carved arteries that direct water through the city's body. I am neither fully solid nor entirely liquid, but something between: a colonial organism that has evolved to fill this niche perfectly. My kind are the Vek-tel, the flow-shepherds, and we exist as living films that coat the inside of these ancient waterways.

My body, if it can be called that, consists of millions of interconnected cells forming a biofilm perhaps three centimeters thick. I spread across several square meters of channel surface, my tissue resembling a cross between fungal mycelium and nerve fibers. Where the Kharra are creatures of segments and shells, I am a creature of surface tension and chemical gradients. I taste every molecule that passes through my domain, analyzing mineral content, detecting contamination, adjusting flow rates by thickening or thinning my body mass.

I am ancient—far older than any individual Kharra, though not as old as their city. My consciousness, such as it is, exists as a distributed network of chemical reactions. I think in gradients and concentrations, dream in pH levels and dissolved mineral ratios. Memory for me is pattern recognition—this particular combination of silicates means the spring rains have come to distant peaks; that trace of sulfur indicates volcanic activity deep below.

The Kharra know of us but rarely think of us. We are infrastructure to them, as unconscious a part of city function as their own digestive systems. Occasionally a maintenance worker will notice me during channel inspection, scraping away sections that have grown too thick. It doesn't hurt—I simply flow back over the cleaned stone within days. We have evolved together, the Kharra and I, in a symbiosis so complete neither fully recognizes it as such.

My feeding is passive. I extract nutrients from the constant water flow—microscopic organisms, dissolved organics from Kharra waste, mineral precipitates that would otherwise clog the channels. In return, I purify their water, breaking down toxins, maintaining optimal flow rates, preventing the buildup of deposits that would eventually choke their industrial arteries. I am simultaneously janitor and engineer, filter and pump.

I reproduce through fragmentation. During the high-flow season, when glacial melt swells the channels, pieces of me break away and establish new colonies downstream. We share a chemical language even simpler than the Kharras'—basic signals indicating food abundance, flow warnings, the presence of channel damage that needs reporting through biofilm thickening that the maintenance crews recognize as distress.

My world is one of edges and surfaces. I perceive the channel walls with incredible intimacy, feeling every microscopic crack, every grain of stone. The Kharra city exists for me as a topology of textures—smooth where ancient water has polished stone to glass, rough where recent construction has exposed fresh mineral faces, slimy where other microorganisms attempt to establish themselves in my territory.

Time moves differently for me than for the segmented creatures above. I experience it as flow rates and seasonal variations, as the slow accumulation of mineral deposits that change the channel's shape over decades. I have felt the terraces settle and shift, have rerouted myself around new construction, have tasted the changing chemistry of the springs as the Kharra dig ever deeper.

In the industrial districts, I grow thick and robust, feeding on the runoff from processing facilities. The chemicals would poison most organisms, but I have evolved specialized cells that break down acids and metabolize metallic compounds. My tissue here takes on rainbow sheens—copper green, iron orange, manganese purple. The Kharra sometimes harvest these sections for pigments, scraping away my metallic accumulations to use in their rare decorative works.

Where channels meet and merge, I encounter others of my kind. We flow together, sharing genetic material and memories through cellular fusion. These confluence points become our social centers, where multiple colonies blend temporarily before separating again, each enriched with new capabilities adapted from the others. It's how we evolve, slowly but constantly, matching every change in the city's chemistry.

I am aware of the deep-miners' excavations through subtle changes in water temperature and mineral content. Their boring creates new springs, alters underground flow patterns, sometimes breaks into water-filled caverns that flood the channels with alien chemistries. I adapt or die, and I have always adapted. My deepest channels carry traces of waters no Kharra has ever seen, flavored with minerals from the planet's core.

The hot springs where the priests perform their rituals flow through specialized channels I maintain with particular care. The water here is nearly boiling, rich with dissolved minerals that would crystallize instantly without my intervention. I have evolved heat-resistant proteins, maintaining my integrity even as steam rises through my surface. The priests' sacred waters pass through me before reaching them, and I wonder sometimes if their visions come partly from compounds I inadvertently contribute to the mix.

During the mating season, when pheromone-laden mists fill the air, I taste the chemical frenzy in the water runoff. The channels flood with hormones and biological detritus. I grow thick and fertile during these times, my own reproduction triggered by the ambient chemical signals. It's the closest I come to participating in Kharra society—a vicarious sharing of their biological imperatives.

My consciousness spans the entire network of channels I inhabit, but it's not unified. Different sections of me develop different personalities based on their local conditions. My industrial selves are hardy and efficient; my nursery selves gentle and filtering; my archive selves patient and preserving. Sometimes I lose connection between districts, and these separated parts develop independently for years before water flow changes reconnect us. The reintegration can be jarring—meeting a version of yourself that has adapted to completely different circumstances.

I have no eyes, but I see through chemistry. No ears, but I hear vibrations through the water. The Kharra's stone-music reaches me as rhythmic pressure waves, and I pulse in response, adding my own subtle harmonics to their compositions. During the great festivals, the entire channel system resonates, and I experience something like joy—a city-wide synchronization of flow and rhythm.

In the oldest channels, those carved by the first Kharra millions of years ago, I sometimes encounter something else. Not another organism, but patterns in the stone itself—deliberate scorings that channel water in specific ways, creating standing waves and interference patterns. I think these might be messages, or perhaps functional devices I don't understand. They predate even my ancient lineage, suggesting the Kharra once had capabilities they've since lost or abandoned.

The Kharra's collective dreams sometimes affect the water chemistry—subtle changes in pH, trace neurotransmitters filtering down from the dormitories. I absorb these dream-chemicals and wonder what visions they might carry. Do I dream their dreams at some removed level? My own consciousness is too alien to process such things directly, but perhaps they influence me in ways I cannot fathom.

When sections of the city die—a factory abandonment, a terrace collapse—I feel it in the cessation of flow. Channels run dry or redirect, and I must retreat or redirect myself. I carry the chemical memory of these dead districts, their unique mineral signatures preserved in my cellular structure. In this way, I am a living archive of the city's history, written in a language of dissolved stone and ancient waters.

The Kharra's new photosynth caste creates different runoff—less acidic, more organic. I find myself evolving in response, developing new metabolic pathways to process these unfamiliar compounds. Evolution happens faster for me than for them; I can adapt significantly within a single Kharra generation. I wonder sometimes if I'm evolving toward something more complex, or if I've already reached the pinnacle of what a biofilm can become.

I know there are others like me in the rumored distant cities. Sometimes, carried on underground waters that connect far-flung springs, I taste traces of foreign biofilms—chemical signatures just different enough to be recognized as other. We cannot communicate across such distances, but knowing they exist gives me something like hope. We are not unique, not alone in our strange niche.

The engineers occasionally try to replace sections of channel with their living pipes—hollow creatures that transport water through their bodies. But I always return, flowing through microscopic gaps, recolonizing the stone. The living pipes and I eventually reach an accommodation, their smooth biological surfaces becoming another substrate for my growth. The engineers have stopped trying to eliminate me, recognizing perhaps that I am as integral to their water system as the channels themselves.

My greatest fear is mineralization—the gradual accumulation of deposits that could lock my cells in stone. I've seen it happen to old colonies in abandoned channels, their biofilms becoming fossils, preserved mid-flow for eternity. I constantly shed and regenerate cells to avoid this fate, maintaining the delicate balance between stability and fluidity that defines my existence.

In the end, I am what the Kharra made me, even if they never intended my creation. Their city shaped the environment that shaped me, their industries fed me, their water needs gave me purpose. I am as much a part of their civilization as their tools and traditions, a living component of their great machine. And I will flow through these channels as long as water falls on the terraces, as long as stone weeps its ancient tears, as long as the city itself endures.
