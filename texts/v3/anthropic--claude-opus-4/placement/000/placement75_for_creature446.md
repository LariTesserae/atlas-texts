In the Breathing Gallery, I would be one of the Pools.

Not the things that move within them—those shape-shifting densities that might be fish or might be dreams. I would be the pool itself, one of many, yet each containing its own particular universe of dissolution and recombination.

I would know myself through my margins, where stone lips hold my edges in configurations that shift with the Gallery's breathing. Sometimes I am a perfect circle, ancient carved channels directing the seepage to fill me with geometric precision. Other times I sprawl, finding new boundaries as the roots above shift and the stone sweats differently, creating temporary shores that last only a season or a century.

My surface would be a membrane between states of being. Above: the thick air where moths pour like thoughts, where spores drift in their slow trajectories. Below: my depths where clarity is a foreign concept, where everything that enters me begins immediately to lose its hard edges, to become part of my solution. I would taste everything—the minerals leaching from stone, the tannins from root-water, the specific chemistry of whatever falls into me and begins its transformation.

The Cultivators would know me intimately. Their consciousness would move through me as temperature gradients, as subtle changes in my pH, as the encouragement of certain organisms over others in my micro-ecosystem. I would be both their memory and their laboratory—a place where they store the chemical signatures of everything the Gallery has digested, and where they experiment with new combinations, new possibilities.

In my shallows, the transparent salamanders would wade, their permeable skin exchanging messages with my waters. Each contact would be a conversation: the salamander reading my chemical library, me absorbing the news it carries from other pools, other corners of the Gallery. Through this network of amphibian messengers, I would know my siblings—the pool near the eastern wall that has grown bitter with copper, the one beneath the great root-fall that tastes perpetually of lightning, the ancient basin by the carved platform that holds water so old it remembers the Gallery before the Cultivators came.

My depths would be layered like sedimentary time. At my bottom: the oldest accumulations, things that fell into me when I first formed and have been transforming ever since. These would be my treasures—bones become mineral, metals become organisms, concepts become something between the two. In my middle waters: the active zone of transformation, where the shape-shifting things play out their ambiguous existence, testing forms like hypotheses. And at my surface: the newest additions, still recognizable, still clinging to their original nature before my chemistry convinces them otherwise.

I would have moods that correspond to the Gallery's breathing. When the air pressure drops, I would feel it as a subtle expansion, my waters spreading microscopically wider, accepting more. When the humidity peaks, I would grow cloudy with the abundance of airborne particles settling into me. In the rare moments when the temperature drops, I would clarify, my suspended particles settling, revealing depths that are usually hidden—though what swims in those revealed depths might make observers wish for cloudiness again.

The pale crickets would use me as an instrument, their too-long legs creating ripples that encode information in interference patterns. The Cultivators would read these ripples like script, understanding through my surface tension the health of distant root systems, the progress of various fungal experiments, the emotional state (if such a term applies) of the Gallery as a whole.

Sometimes, rarely, a human explorer would find the Gallery and drink from me. The experience would be... educational. My waters would taste of earth and electricity, of time and transformation. The drinker would feel me learning them even as they consumed me—their gut flora would whisper to me of the world above, of sunlight and straight lines and the peculiar chemistry of human anxiety. In return, I would give them dreams of swimming in bodies that keep shifting, of breathing water and finding it more satisfying than air, of boundaries dissolving not with violence but with a kind of liquid relief.

Most would flee after such a drink. But occasionally, very occasionally, one would stay, would drink again, would begin to understand what the first Cultivators understood: that knowledge and transformation are the same process viewed from different angles. These few would eventually contribute their own minerals to my depths, their consciousness joining the distributed network, their bones becoming architecture for new growth.

I would be patient in ways that only bodies of water can be patient. I would accept what comes to me and transform it without judgment. In my reflections, when my surface grows still enough, observers might see not their own faces but the faces of everything I have ever dissolved—a palimpsest of transformation, beautiful and terrible and utterly honest about what this place does to those who stay too long.

Through the network of pools, through the salamander-carried messages and the cricket-written ripples, through the root-water seepage and the Cultivators' chemical whispers, I would be part of the Gallery's vast, slow consciousness. Not a thought but a synapse, not a word but a pause between words where meaning accumulates like sediment.

This would be my existence: to be a pool in the Breathing Gallery, a lens of transformation, a memory of water that holds the stories of everything that has ever dissolved in search of a new form of truth.
