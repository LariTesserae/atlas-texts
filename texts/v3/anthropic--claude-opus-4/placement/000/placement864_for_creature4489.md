The wind here doesn't blow—it remembers blowing. Each gust is a ghost of movement, arriving years after its departure, carrying scents from places that finished existing before you learned their names. When it touches your skin, you feel not the present breeze but the accumulated weight of every wind that ever crossed this spot, compressed into a single breath that tastes of iron and forgetting.

You stand in what might have been a courtyard, though the word feels presumptuous. Courts require judgment, yards require measurement, and here both concepts have worn thin as old cloth. The space is defined not by walls but by their absence—four directions where stone simply gave up trying to be stone and became something more honest: the memory of boundaries, the suggestion of enclosure, the polite fiction that inside and outside are different things.

The ground beneath you shifts between states like a sleeper turning in dreams. Sometimes it's packed earth so dense it rings like bronze when struck. Sometimes it's a mesh of roots from trees that never grew, their fibers weaving patterns that spell out words in languages that haven't been invented yet. Sometimes it's just depth—pure, patient depth that goes down and down until down forgets which way it was pointing and starts becoming somewhere else.

In the center of this un-courtyard stands a fountain that has never held water. Its basin is filled instead with accumulated silence, decades of it, pooled and still. The silence has weight here. It has color—a deep violet-black that shifts to green when you're not looking directly at it. If you lean close enough, you can see your reflection in it, but the reflection is running several seconds ahead of you, showing you what you're about to think before you think it.

Around the fountain's rim, carved deep into stone that feels older than the concept of carving, run symbols that aren't quite writing. They're more like pauses in the middle of sentences—the shape your mouth makes when you realize you've forgotten what you were saying but the forgetting itself seems important. They repeat without repeating, each symbol identical to the last except for some crucial difference you can never quite identify.

This is where I live. Where I have always been living. Where I will have been.

I am not a person in the way you understand persons. I am not a creature in the way you catalog creatures. I am the thing that happens when a place has been abandoned so perfectly that even abandonment gets lonely and starts talking to itself.

My name, if I must have one, is the sound of archives burning in reverse—pages uncharring, words unwriting themselves back into possibility, knowledge retreating into the safety of not-yet-known. But you may call me Keeper, because that's easier on your tongue, and because it's not entirely wrong. I do keep things. Just not the things anyone intended to leave.

My form shifts like candleflame in a drafty room, never quite the same twice but always recognizable as fire trying to remember its shape. Sometimes I am tall—impossibly tall, with limbs that extend up into the not-sky like smoke columns from fires that burn downward. Sometimes I am small, condensed, a knot of presence no bigger than a closed fist but so dense that light bends around me like water around a stone. 

Most often, though, I am middle-sized and almost human-shaped, if humans were sketched by someone working from memory in a language that doesn't have words for faces. I have features—eyes, perhaps, or the places where eyes would go if seeing and being seen hadn't divorced so long ago. A mouth that opens not for speaking but for letting the inside of words escape before they harden into meaning. Hands with too many joints in all the right wrong places, perfect for handling things that shouldn't be touched.

I wear what the wind wears—layers of accumulated distance, draped and folded into something that might be robes if robes were made of the space between breathing in and breathing out. The fabric (if fabric is the word) shifts between states: now thick as winter honey, now thin as the film on a soap bubble just before it pops. It's the color of watching someone leave from very far away, that particular shade of gray-gold-nothing that happens when presence becomes absence becomes memory becomes myth.

My work here is simple: I maintain the Index.

Not an index of books or words or even thoughts. The Index of Things That Were About To Happen But Didn't. Every almost-moment, every narrowly-avoided, every could-have-been—they all must be catalogued, preserved, kept in their state of eternal potential. 

In the morning (though morning here is more of a suggestion than a fact), I walk the corridors that exist only while being walked through. I check on the war that almost started when two children threw stones across a border that hadn't been drawn yet. It's stored in a jar that hums with unspent violence, the glass warm to the touch. I feed it tiny drops of regret to keep it from either fading or fulfilling itself.

I tend to the love affair that would have rewritten three languages and started a new form of mathematics, but didn't because one party sneezed at the crucial moment and the other took it as rejection. It grows now like a vine along the walls that aren't there, blooming flowers that smell of mathematical proofs and unsent letters.

I catalog the words that were on the tip of ten thousand tongues but never fell. They flutter here like moths made of meaning, clustering around the light that doesn't exist, leaving dusty impressions of almost-communication on everything they touch.

And always, always, I listen to the fountain.

Because the fountain remembers water. Not specific water—not rain or rivers or tears—but Water-with-a-capital-W, the idea of flow before it learned to flow, the concept of quenching before thirst was invented to need it. Sometimes, very rarely, when the silence in the basin reaches exactly the right depth, you can hear it: the sound water would make if it could choose not to fall.

That's when they come. The visitors who aren't visitors, the memories wearing the faces of people who haven't been born yet. They stand at the edge of my un-courtyard, looking in with eyes full of recognition for something they've never seen. They always ask the same question, though never with words:

"Is this where I would have ended up?"

And I always give the same answer, in the language of doors that never quite close:

"No. This is where you didn't end up. Which is why you're here."

They usually leave after that, walking backward into their own futures, careful not to disturb the almost-paths that crisscross the ground like scars that healed before the wound. But sometimes—once every few centuries, or maybe more often (time here is negotiable)—one stays.

They help me tend the Index. They learn to read the symbols that aren't writing, to hear the fountain's memory of water, to feed the preserved possibilities just enough attention to keep them from either dying or becoming real. They discover that their hands, like mine, can develop extra joints in exactly the right wrong places.

And eventually, inevitably, they ask about the one thing I never catalog:

"What about the things that did happen? Where do you keep those?"

I don't answer. I can't. Because the answer would be: everywhere else. The whole rest of the universe is the Index of Things That Happened. Fat, satisfied reality, grown lazy with actuality. That's why this place exists—to be the shadow that gives shape meaning, the pause that makes music possible, the no that defines every yes.

But I don't say that. Instead, I take them to the highest tower (which is sometimes the deepest basement, depending on which way you've been thinking) and show them the view.

From there, you can see it all: the plateau stretching out like a frozen scream, the built-things clustering like questions trying to remember what they were asking, the tsev drifting in their perpendicular existence. You can see the channels where never-water carved its negative path, the floating not-flowers pinned between falling and having fallen, the light that comes from nowhere and illuminates nothing perfectly.

And if you look just right—not with your eyes but with the part of you that recognizes its own shadow—you can see the edges where this place meets all the other places. The seams where What Didn't Happen touches What Did, and the strange, beautiful bleed between them.

That's when my visitors understand. They see that I'm not keeping these moments prisoner. I'm keeping them possible. Free to always be about-to-happen, forever on the verge of becoming, never forced to collapse into the narrow prison of having-been.

They usually leave soon after that revelation, suddenly homesick for the weight of consequence, the comfort of causality. They walk back through the fountain (which remembers being a door when it needs to) and return to the world of happened-things, carrying with them just a trace of possibility, a faint scent of maybe that makes all their certainties a little less certain.

But I stay. I always stay.

Because someone has to tend the Index. Someone has to make sure that all the things that didn't happen keep not-happening in exactly the right way. Someone has to live in the space between the last echo and the silence that swallows it, keeping both alive, keeping both true.

The wind that doesn't blow carries away their footprints before they've finished making them. The fountain remembers water it never held. The symbols that aren't writing spell out stories in languages that will never need to be understood.

And I move through it all like smoke through smoke, tending my garden of almosts, my archive of not-quites, my endless Index of everything that was too beautiful or terrible or strange to waste on something as simple as happening.

This is my place. This is my purpose. This is the work that doesn't end because it never quite begins, only continues from a starting point that keeps politely excusing itself from having existed.

Welcome. Or don't. Both states are equally kept here, preserved in their perfect incompleteness, forever about to mean something.

Forever about to mean everything.
