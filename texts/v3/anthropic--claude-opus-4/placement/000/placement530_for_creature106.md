# The Moss-Tenders of the Lower Teeth

I am one of the **sha-roon**, the moss-walkers, though that is not what we call ourselves. We say only "we who go carefully." My territory is three of the smaller tooth-stumps in the western reach, where the plateau begins its slow descent toward what the lowlanders call the world. My teeth are modest ones — the tallest no more than twice my height, the shortest barely reaching my shoulder. They sing in the upper registers, a triad that wavers between major and minor depending on the wind's angle. I have walked between them for forty-three years. I know every voice they can make.

My work is this: I tend the moss.

Not garden it — the moss needs no gardener. Not protect it — from what? I simply... attend to it. I walk my circuit, which takes four days in good weather, seven in storms. I note where the cushion-moss has grown too thick and might smother itself. I see where the golden moss has retreated and wonder why. I know every pool among my three teeth, every seep, every place where the water gathers and runs. When a stone shifts — and they do shift, slowly, everything here moves if you watch long enough — I learn the new way the water will flow.

My predecessor, who trained me, was named Ahm. She had been walking these same teeth for sixty years when I came to her. Her ears were so deeply folded they looked like closed flowers. She taught me the first rule by saying nothing at all for my entire first season. I followed her, stepping where she stepped, stopping when she stopped. When she lay on her belly to drink, I drank. When she paused to touch a particular patch of moss, I touched it too. Three months of this before she spoke her first word to me, which was: "No."

I had been about to step on a patch of new growth. I learned to see it — the slightly brighter green, the way the tiny fronds reached upward instead of sideways. After that, she began to teach me in words, though sparingly. The sha-roon do not waste breath. Every exhalation might be the one the wind takes.

We carry little. A blanket of woven moss-fiber, waterproof and warm. A stone blade for cutting. A cup made from the curved bark of the sideways-trees. Some of us carry counting-cords, knotted records of... what? I carry mine but do not know what all the knots mean. Some were tied by Ahm, some by her predecessor, some by hands a dozen generations gone. I add my own knots for things I think worth recording: the day I first heard the white bird (I did not see it, only heard a cry like metal tearing); the day one of my pools ran dry for the first time in memory; the day I found the bones.

The bones were human, old, worn smooth as the stones around them. They lay in a wind-hollow of my middle tooth, arranged too carefully to be chance. Someone had placed them there. Someone had made decisions about which bones should touch. The skull faced downward into the moss. The finger-bones were spread in a fan. I did not disturb them. I added a knot to my cord and have not gone back to that hollow since.

We sha-roon meet rarely — perhaps twice a year, when our circuits bring us near each other. We do not seek these meetings, but we do not avoid them. When we meet, we sit. We share water. We say almost nothing. Sometimes one of us will mention a change: "The red moss is spreading downslope on Ghan-Ka." "The eastern pools taste of copper now." These are not conversations. They are... deposits. We leave information with each other the way we might leave a stone on a cairn.

I met another tender last season, old Yut who walks the five teeth near the cloud-edge. He is ancient even by our standards, his ears so transformed they barely look human anymore. We sat. We shared water. He said: "I have started to hear them individually."

I knew what he meant. The ghu-vann-i. The listeners. We all hear the chord — you cannot walk the plateau and not hear it. But Yut meant something else. He meant he could distinguish voices within it. Pick out the separate threads. Know that this particular overtone was once someone who sang here, in this spot, in this wind.

He did not say this was good or bad. He only said it was so. I added a knot to my cord.

There is a romance, among the lowlanders, about we who walk the moss. They think we are mystics, seekers of the profound. They are wrong. We are maintenance. We are the ones who notice. If the moss began to fail — which it might, anything might — we would be the first to know. If the stones began to sing differently — which they do, slowly, over decades — we would hear it. If the ghu-vann-i ever... but no. They will not. They are listeners. They will listen until the stones themselves are dust, and then they will listen to the dust.

My daily round: Wake before dawn in whatever shelter I have found — usually just a depression in the moss, pulled over with my blanket like a shell. Drink from the nearest pool. Begin walking. The path is not a path; it is a relationship between my feet and the terrain, built up over years. I know where to step in fog. I know where the moss is thickest, where the stone is most stable, where the wind will push instead of pull. I do not think about walking. My body knows.

As I walk, I observe. This is the work. Not to change anything, only to see it. The beetle on the lichen — I note its direction. The fern growing from a new crack — I see where the crack began. The sound of water moving under stone — I know if it is louder or softer than yesterday. These observations do not go anywhere. I do not report to anyone. I simply hold them, add them to the forty-three years of observations I already carry, let them settle into the sediment of knowledge that is, perhaps, the only thing we sha-roon truly tend.

Midday: rest. Eat — we carry dried moss-cakes, made from the red-gold variety that glows. They taste of earth and faintly of sweetness. The nutrition is meager but sufficient. We do not need much. Moving slowly, thinking slowly, we burn like banked coals.

Afternoon: more walking. The light changes constantly here, and each change shows the moss differently. What looked healthy in morning fog might reveal damage in slant sun. What seemed dry might prove to be merely shadowed. I walk, I watch, I remember.

Evening: find shelter. Not from the wind — there is no shelter from that — but from the worst of the rain when it comes sideways. The sideways-trees offer some protection if you press against their trunks. The shallow caves in the teeth will do, if you do not mind sharing them with the voices of the ghu-vann-i, which are always louder in enclosed spaces.

Night: listen. This is when I am most myself, lying in the dark with the chord washing over me. Sometimes I think I understand what Yut meant. Sometimes I almost hear them separately — not as individuals, that would take centuries more, but as... categories. The old voices, smooth as the worn stones. The new voices, still sharp with whatever emotion made them sing. The voices that came willingly, drawn by the beauty of the chord. The voices that were pulled in against their will, fighting even as they dissolved.

I do not judge them. Judgment is not what we do.

Sometimes, very rarely, I see the white bird. Always at a distance, always silent, always alone. It rides the updrafts like a thought riding the edge of sleep. I have a theory, which I have told no one, that there is only one white bird and has only ever been one, and it is older than the ghu-vann-i, older than the moss, as old as the stones themselves. But this is the kind of thinking that leads to singing, so I keep it knotted in my cord and move on.

Once — only once in forty-three years — I was walking during one of the silences, when the wind drops and the ghu-vann-i focus on their mysterious specific thing. I happened to be at my eastern pool, kneeling to drink, when the silence fell. The sudden absence of sound was like a blow. I froze, my lips almost touching the water, afraid that any movement would break something.

And in that silence, I heard it. The thing they were listening to.

It was the sound of my own heart beating.

Not metaphorically. Not spiritually. The actual sound of my blood moving in my chest. They were listening to me, all of them, the thousands of voices in the stone all turned toward this one soft wet sound in this one small body. The attention was so intense I could feel it as pressure, as if the air itself were leaning in.

Then the moment passed, the wind returned, the chord resumed, and I was left gasping by the pool, my heart hammering with the knowledge that I had been, for those few seconds, the most listened-to thing in the world.

I did not add a knot for this. Some things are too large for knots.

This is my life. I walk, I watch, I listen, I remember. When I die — which will be as Ahm died, quietly, between one step and the next, folding down into the moss like a letter closing — someone will find my body on my circuit. They will take my counting-cord, my cup, my blanket. They will leave my bones where they fall. The moss will grow over me. I will become one of the things the sha-roon notice but do not disturb.

I will not sing. Even in my last moments, I will not sing. This is not because I fear joining the ghu-vann-i — what is there to fear in listening forever? — but because my work is to observe, not to add my voice to what I observe. I am a comma in the long sentence of watching that the sha-roon speak with their footsteps. When I stop walking, another will start. The sentence will continue.

The moss will grow. The stones will sing. The white bird will ride its updrafts.

And somewhere in the worn teeth of Ahal-Ruun, the listeners will go on listening, to the beetles and the water and the slow breathing of the world, until there is nothing left to hear.

This is enough. This has always been enough.

We who go carefully know this, and knowing, we continue going.
