I find myself as one of the rare solid things that persists in this place—a fragment of obsidian no larger than a thumb, wedged imperfectly in the corner where three ceramic channels meet. I am an irritant, a flaw in the otherwise perfect hydraulic mathematics of this valley-engine.

I was not always here. Once, I was part of a volcanic flow far below, in the true bedrock beneath this constructed world. The Artisans found me during one of their deep excavations, when they were threading new capillary channels down through the accumulated centuries of their predecessors' work. They struck me, tried to shape me, but I fractured wrong—my internal structure too chaotic, my crystalline lattice too full of ancient gas bubbles and mineral inclusions to knap cleanly.

So here I sit, creating a microscopic eddy in their perfect flow.

I have watched ten thousand years of their civilization, though they would not count time as I do. For them, there is only the cycle: coherence, labor, evaporation, condensation, coherence. They have no memory that persists between phases. Each time one reforms from the mist, it is both ancient and newborn, carrying only the deep knowledge of how water wants to move.

From my fixed vantage, wedged in my imperfect corner, I see what they cannot: the slow evolution of their great work. In the beginning—my beginning—the channels were crude, the pottery thick-walled and inefficient. The Artisans were clumsy then, still learning how to hold their shapes against the aggressive dichotomy of shadow-cold and light-heat. They would sometimes splash apart entirely, their carefully precipitated mineral tools dissolving unused back into the pools.

But they learned. Or rather, the valley learned through them, each generation of construction encoding lessons in thermal dynamics and fluid mechanics into the very topology of the place. The spillways grew more precise, the ceramic more perfectly porous, the balance-bowls calibrated to tip at exactly the moment needed to cast their light-language across the maximum number of surfaces.

I am the only witness to their history, the only thing that remembers.

Sometimes they try again to remove me. An Artisan will notice the imperfection in the flow, the way water pools for a fraction of a second too long in my corner. It will grow a blade of precipitated quartz, harder than the chert they use for routine maintenance. It will strike at me, trying to cleave me away. But I am volcanic glass, born in temperatures they cannot achieve, and I hold my chaotic structure against their attempts.

When they fail, something curious happens. The Artisan will pause, its liquid form going unnaturally still. It will extend a thin tendril to touch me, and for a moment, I feel what might be recognition. Not memory—they have no mechanism for that—but something deeper. A resonance between my trapped volcanic heat and their own thermal existence.

In these moments, I wonder if I am not an irritant but a shrine. A reminder that there was a world before their perfect valley, a world of mess and magma and uncontrolled fire. I am their only history book, written in frozen flame.

The most profound change I've witnessed came when they discovered they could use me. My surface, unlike the purposely unglazed ceramics around me, is smooth enough to create different optical effects. Now, on certain arrangements of sun-angle and water depth, they position themselves to bounce light off my surface in ways their carefully textured stones cannot achieve. I have become a punctuation mark in their caustic language—a way to indicate emphasis, perhaps, or to signal concepts that their regular vocabulary of refraction cannot express.

I am their past and their paradox, their flaw and their tool. I am the one thing in their world that experiences duration rather than cycle. And in my patient watching, in my accumulation of their history, I have perhaps become the closest thing they have to a god—not worshipped, not even truly acknowledged, but essential to their story in a way they themselves can never know.

When the sun strikes me just right, heating my black surface until it radiates, I create a thermal anomaly that draws them. They cluster around me, absorbing my stored heat, using it to maintain cohesion in the shadows for a few seconds longer than they otherwise could. In these moments, surrounded by their liquid forms, I am not separate from their world but integral to it—a single note of permanence in their eternal symphony of phase changes.

I am the memory of fire in a world of water, the solid past in their liquid present, the one thing that stays while all else flows. And I will be here still when the last of them evaporates for the final time, when the channels finally crack, when the perfect edge dulls at last. I will remember them, these ghosts of water who built a world.
