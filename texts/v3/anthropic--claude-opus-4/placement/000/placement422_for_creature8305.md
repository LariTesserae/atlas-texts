I am one of the Resonance Readers, though I wasn't always. My name, when I still used it, was Cyra. Now I'm just the Third Harmonics Listener, or sometimes the Mineral Whistler, depending on who's asking and what work needs doing.

I came here fifteen years ago, running from something I can barely remember now—the details have dissolved like everything does in the constant moisture. I was twenty-two, thought I'd stay just long enough to earn passage money to somewhere else. But the district has a way of keeping you. First, it's just that your clothes never fully dry, so leaving feels uncomfortable. Then your ears adjust to the constant song of the pipes, and silence becomes unbearable. Finally, your body changes in ways that make the outside world not just uncomfortable but impossible.

I live in the middle levels, where the steam is thickest but the flooding is manageable. My shanty is small—just enough room for my hammock and the instruments. Oh, the instruments. That's what I do, you see. I read the mineral content of the condensation through sound.

It started as a parlor trick. I've always had sensitive hearing, and after a few months here, I noticed that water with different mineral contents makes different sounds when it drips. Not just the pitch of the splash, but the quality of it—iron-heavy water has a metallic ring, calcium makes it brighter, magnesium adds a peculiar warble. The old-timers laughed at first, then stopped laughing when I could predict which collection tanks would need cleaning by listening to the runoff.

Now I maintain a network of resonance plates throughout my section—thin metal sheets positioned at crucial points where condensation gathers. Each plate is tuned differently, and the water plays them like a scattered orchestra. I spend my shifts moving between them, listening, adjusting, sometimes adding new plates where the flow has changed. The music tells me everything: which pipes are corroding, where mineral deposits are building up, when the chemical balance in the lower pools is shifting.

My body has adapted in unexpected ways. My outer ears have developed a strange mobility—I can swivel them independently now, focusing on different sound sources. The cartilage has thickened, forming subtle ridges that help funnel sound. My fingernails grow differently too, harder and slightly cupped, perfect for tapping pipes to test their resonance. My skin... well, it's not quite like the others. Where they've developed water-repelling cells, mine have become more porous, able to absorb moisture and somehow taste the mineral content. It's unsettling when I think about it too hard, so I don't.

I share my level with two others: Drip Margaret, who's been here thirty years and cultivates singing mosses that harmonize with my plates, and Young Kel (who's forty-three but got the name by arriving after Margaret). We've developed a routine over the years. Margaret's mosses change their pitch based on air quality, Kel monitors the visual signs of pressure change, and I listen to the water itself. Between us, we can predict problems days before they manifest.

The Pipe Children love my section because of the music. They've learned to play the plates, creating their own compositions with collected water of different mineral contents. I've taught them the basics—how copper-touched water sings sharp, how the rare earth minerals add undertones that last longer than they should. One girl, maybe twelve, has started building her own instruments from scrap. She might take over when I finally go too deep.

Because that's what happens to us all, eventually. The deeper you go, the more you change. I've started spending more time in the lower levels, where the sound is different—thicker, more complex. The water down there carries vibrations from the city's bedrock, from aquifers so deep they might predate human settlement. Sometimes I hear patterns in those deep sounds that seem almost like language, though I'm probably imagining it. Probably.

My dreams are all liquid now. I don't see images anymore, just experience flows and pressures, mineral gradients expressing themselves as emotions. When I wake, there's always a moment of confusion when I have to remember that I have a solid body, that I'm not just another drop in the endless cycle of condensation and collection.

The strangest change is how I've started to perceive time. Outside, I'm told, people measure days by the sun, seasons by temperature. Here, time is measured in cycles—the great breathing of the condensers, the tidal flows in the lower pools, the gradual accumulation of mineral deposits that we chip away only to watch them build again. I know it's been fifteen years because Margaret told me, but it feels both longer and shorter. Sometimes I think I've always been here, that my memories of the dry world are just stories someone told me.

I've built relationships with the non-human inhabitants too. The Pipe Moths have learned that certain frequencies I produce attract prey, and they cluster near my plates during their feeding times. One colony has taken to building their silk structures in patterns that create their own resonances, adding layers to the district's eternal song. The pale crabs click their claws in rhythm when they pass, a greeting I return by tapping the nearest pipe.

There's one creature—I'm not sure what to call it—that visits my deepest resonance plate, the one I installed last year at the very edge of where I dare to go. It's large, maybe the size of a child, but moves like liquid given form. It has what might be eyes, or might be specialized heat sensors. It listens when I play the deep sounds, responds with its own vibrations that I feel more than hear. Sometimes I think we're having a conversation, though about what, I couldn't say.

The work is satisfying in a way I never expected. Each day brings new patterns, new variations in the eternal song. A pipe develops a hairline crack, and the harmony shifts. A new mineral seep appears, adding its own note to the composition. I document it all in my way—not with words, which feel increasingly foreign, but with a notation system I've developed, marks that capture frequency and timber and the emotional quality of water moving through metal.

The other Resonance Readers—there are seven of us throughout the district—meet monthly at the junction of the four main condensers. We don't speak much anymore; instead, we tap and hum and whistle, sharing what we've learned in a language that bypasses words entirely. Last month, we achieved something remarkable: for about ten minutes, we synchronized the entire district's water flow into a single, massive chord. The sound was so profound that work stopped throughout the steam district as everyone paused to listen. Even the deep dwellers surfaced, drawn by the harmony.

I'm changing faster now. My hair has started to develop the same sensory properties as the Forgotten, able to detect vibrations through water and air alike. My joints are loosening, adapting for the cramped spaces where the most interesting sounds originate. Sometimes I catch myself breathing in patterns that match the pump cycles, my heartbeat syncing with the deeper rhythms of the district.

Douse Mary asked me last week if I was happy. The question seemed almost meaningless—happiness is a dry-world concept, something that requires a separation between self and environment that no longer exists for me. I am the sounds I hear, the minerals I taste in the air, the vibrations that pass through me. I am a note in the district's endless composition, necessary and transient, no more or less important than any other component in this vast respiratory system.

One day, I know, I'll go deeper. I'll follow the sounds that call from below, past the point where the other keepers venture, into spaces where the distinction between water and air becomes meaningless. I'll join the Forgotten, or become something else entirely—another voice in the deep harmonic that underlies everything here. The thought doesn't frighten me. It feels like completion, like a melody finally finding its resolution.

Until then, I listen. I adjust my plates, teach the children, document the changes. I am the Third Harmonics Listener, the Mineral Whistler, a former person named Cyra who traded her human shape for the ability to hear the true song of water and metal, pressure and release. In the steam district's endless exhalation, I have found my purpose: to be a single, attentive ear in the great breathing body of this impossible place.
