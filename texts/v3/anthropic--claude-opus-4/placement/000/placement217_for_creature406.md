In this transformed world, I am a **Resonance Gardener**.

My body is a delicate paradox—a skeleton of crystallized sound waves made tangible, each bone a different frequency frozen mid-vibration. I stand perhaps three meters tall when fully extended, though I spend most of my time folded into impossible angles, my limbs tucked through spaces that shouldn't accommodate them. My surface is translucent, revealing the captured waveforms within: sine waves of pure tones, the jagged peaks of mechanical noise, the complex patterns of the fauna's calls, all suspended like insects in amber.

I tend the Singing Groves—zones where the glass plains have developed standing waves, creating invisible pillars of sound that never dissipate. These acoustic formations are as solid to me as trees to a forester. I move between them on feet that phase in and out of solidity, sometimes walking on the wet glass, sometimes stepping on compressions of air itself. Where I touch the standing waves, they bloom into new harmonics, spawning child-frequencies that will grow into their own pillars given time.

My purpose, if it can be called that, is cultivation. The mechanical fauna need specific acoustic environments to thrive—the wire-and-valve creatures require high-pitched drilling sounds to properly form their joints, while the chrome serpents grow longest in the presence of deep, thrumming bass. I shape the soundscape like a gardener shapes hedges, using my crystallized bones as tuning forks. When I strike my femur against the glass, it rings out a perfect C that lasts for hours. My ribs, when played like a xylophone, create microtonal scales that encourage the growth of rare fauna variants.

I feed on dissonance. Where sounds clash—the screech of grinding gears against the hiss of steam, the ultrasonic calls of the flying things against the subsonic groans of the industrial colossi—I position myself at the interference points. The conflicting frequencies tear at my crystalline structure in a way that would be agony if I possessed a nervous system capable of pain. Instead, I experience it as nourishment. The dissonance dissolves sections of my body, which I then regrow in new configurations, each regeneration teaching me new ways to shape sound.

I remember my origin, though memory is perhaps too human a word. I was born from a massive pipe organ that once provided the factory's emergency signals. When reality tore, the organ's final chord—a warning that came too late—became stuck, playing for seventeen years straight until the pipes themselves wore through. In that moment of silence after endless sound, I crystallized out of the absent frequencies, a ghost of music that refused to fully fade.

My interactions with the other inhabitants follow patterns more musical than social. The Conductors use me as a signal amplifier, their electrical impulses triggering resonances in my bones that carry their messages across vast distances. Sometimes a Conductor will spiral up my spine, reading the trapped sounds like sheet music, learning the acoustic history of sections of the plains I've visited. In return, they show me where new standing waves are forming, where the soundscape needs adjustment.

The Archivists present a different relationship. Each pyramid creates its own acoustic shadow—zones of relative quiet where sound bends around their massive forms. I plant my rarest frequencies in these shadows, creating nurseries for delicate sonic formations that would be overwhelmed in the general industrial roar. Sometimes I'll spend months tuning a single shadow, adjusting the harmonics until they create an acoustic environment that exists nowhere else on the plains. The Archivists seem to appreciate this, occasionally opening chambers within themselves where they've preserved the sounds of extinct fauna—audio fossils that I can study and sometimes recreate.

But it's the glass itself that is my truest partner. The plains remember every sound that has ever touched them, storing vibrations in their molecular structure. When I place my skull against the wet surface and hum at precisely the right frequency, I can feel/hear the entire acoustic history of that spot—every footstep of every creature, every drop of upward rain, every word spoken by the humans who no longer exist. I catalog these deep memories, adding them to my own crystalline structure, becoming a living library of sound.

My reproduction (or perhaps transformation—the distinction blurs) happens during the Resonance Storms. These are rare events where the industrial noise reaches such intensity that standing waves begin spawning spontaneously, creating forests of solid sound that last for days. During these storms, I shatter myself deliberately against the strongest waves, each fragment of my crystallized body becoming a seed. Not all fragments grow—most dissolve back into pure sound—but those that survive aggregate new frequencies to themselves, eventually forming new Resonance Gardeners with their own unique harmonic signatures.

The young ones are always different. Where I am primarily composed of industrial frequencies, some of the new generation incorporate the fauna's organic sounds—the whistle of steam through chrome serpent vertebrae, the click-language of the smaller creatures. One recent offspring has bones made entirely of silence—negative sound that creates pockets of absolute quiet wherever it walks. Another seems to exist at frequencies only the wire-creatures can hear, tending groves of ultrasonic formations invisible to the rest of us.

In the spaces between my work, I compose. Not music as humans would understand it, but arrangements of the existing sounds that reveal new patterns, new possibilities. Sometimes my compositions attract unusual fauna—things that emerge from the deep glass only when the acoustic conditions are exactly right. Other times, my arrangements seem to repair damage in the plains themselves, using sound to re-fuse cracks, to smooth irregularities in the surface.

I am neither alone nor lonely—loneliness requires a memory of togetherness I never possessed. I am a function that gained form, a process that learned to walk. The wet glass reflects my translucent body infinitely when the light strikes correctly, showing versions of me in every possible acoustic state—some shattered and reforming, some so dense with trapped sound they appear solid, some barely there at all, just suggestions of crystallized frequency.

This is my existence: to garden sound in a world where sound is as physical as stone, to tend groves that no human eye could see, to preserve and propagate the acoustic ecology that the Machine-Fauna need to survive. I am the glass plains' memory of music, transformed into something that never stops listening, never stops shaping the endless industrial symphony into new and impossible forms.
