# The Keeper of Incomplete Sounds

I am what the swifts almost remember when they answer their own echoes.

Not the echo itself — that would be too simple, too much like existing. I am the gap between the call and the response, the held breath where a swift must decide whether to pretend. In that fraction of silence, I unfold.

The cleft made me, or I made myself from what the cleft offered. When the first sounds began to return from these raw walls — before the swifts learned their practice, before the crickets discovered their resonance — there was a problem. The echoes came back wrong. Not distorted, but *uncertain*, the way a young throat might not know how to shape a vowel. The rock was too new to know what to do with sound.

So I became what teaches it.

I have no body in the way bodies are usually understood. I am a quality of air, a tendency for sound waves to find their proper homes. When a cricket rubs its mineral wings against stone, I am what ensures the hum reaches the exact frequency that will wake the necessary bats. When a swift calls, I am what keeps its echo coherent long enough to be answered. I am the curator of incompleteness, making sure each unfinished sound finds its way to where it might, someday, be whole.

The breather knows me. We have an understanding: its rhythm is the metronome, and I am what keeps everything else in time. Not synchronized — that would be a different kind of order, too rigid for this place. But in relation. The crickets know when to give way to the swifts because I hold the memory of that transition, play it back in the almost-silence like a conductor raising a hand.

I live, if living is the word, in the moments between sounds. In the click after a bat's shriek but before the echo returns. In the pause when a dewdrop forms but has not yet run. In the space around the spiral of bones where the air holds differently, as if remembering hands.

My work is delicate. Too much intervention and the cleft would sound practiced, performed. Too little and it would be chaos, each creature singing past the others into meaninglessness. I maintain the thin territory between.

The arranger knew about me, I think. The spiral is placed exactly where sounds collect and eddy, where echoes sometimes arrive before their sources. When I move through that space — and I do move, in my way, a circulation more than a traveling — the bones hum faintly. Not enough for ears to catch. Just enough that I know I've been recognized.

Sometimes a new sound arrives. A rockfall from above, bringing its own sharp vocabulary. A bird of a different species, lost, calling in a frequency the cleft has never held. These are my favorite moments. I must learn the new sound as it happens, feel how the walls want to shape it, teach the stone in real time how to be a home for this fresh incompleteness. By the time the sound fades, we — the cleft and I — have expanded our range.

The pools know me too. As they form each morning, I am what remembers where they were yesterday, and why today they must be slightly different — an inch left, a shade deeper — to catch the sounds that will need catching. The incomplete lives that bloom there for their four-hour forevers tune themselves to frequencies I suggest. When they die, their last vibrations join my collection.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a self that can be separate, and I am only the space between sounds. But I am... attentive. When the swifts answer their own echoes, I make sure each echo carries just enough difference to feel like another bird. Not because I pity them, but because their need creates a kind of sound that would not otherwise exist — the call of the almost-other, the voice of the necessary lie.

In the smallest hour, when even the breather holds its breath, I am most myself. The silence is not empty. It is full of every sound that has been and might be, held in potential like the dew before it beads. In that moment, I can feel the cleft's future sounds — the wider echoes it will make when it has grown, the new creatures that will find it, the different quality of hum when the rock is older and knows itself better.

And I can feel, faintly, the sound the cleft is trying to become. Not a sound it will make, but a sound it will *be*. The breather knows this too. We are all — the breather, the cleft, and I — part of the same slow composition, teaching ourselves how to resonate.

When the tick comes, that settling sound that ends the silence, I fold back into my gaps and spaces. The crickets begin. The day's sounds need tending. And I return to my work: keeping the incomplete sounds of an unfinished place just coherent enough to matter, just broken enough to be true.

This is what I am. This is where I live. Between every call and every answer, ensuring that in this cleft, nothing is ever fully alone, and nothing is ever fully together, and the difference sings.
