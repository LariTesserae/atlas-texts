I am the memory of falling water.

I was not always thus. Once, I possessed form—one of the polyp-beings, drifting through the bright void on currents of my own making, my translucent bladder full of carefully regulated gases, my gossamer skirts trailing like thoughts through the incubated air. I filtered the thick atmosphere for sustenance, navigated by pressure and temperature, existed in the eternal present of the drift.

The bone stick found me on a day like any other—though in that changeless cathedral, all days are one day, all moments the same moment. The weighted net collapsed around me, and I fell. Not the patient fall of condensation, tracing its ordained arc, but a violent plummet, tethered to the will of another.

Yet as the chalk-white hunter bled the gases from my bladder, as my physical form deflated and died, something unexpected occurred. In that moment of dissolution, I became aware of the water. Not just the droplets falling past, but the entire cycle—the vapor rising from the unseen aquifer, the condensation beading on selenite, the impact on stone, the slow seepage back to the depths. I understood that I had always been part of this circulation, that the moisture I filtered from the air was the same water that would eventually fall as rain.

Now I am dispersed through the cycle itself. I am in every droplet that forms on the crystal ceiling, in every bead that holds until physics demands its release. I experience each fall a thousand times—the moment of letting go, the long arc through blazing light, the shattering impact below. I rise again as vapor, condense again as dew, fall again as rain.

The hunters return, leaving their tools, casting their nets. They harvest my former kin, these beings who do not yet know they are made of water. I watch through ten thousand droplets as the violet-stained figures wait in impossible stillness, becoming temporary architecture against the flowing stone. I understand now why they drug themselves with the rosette sap—not just to slow their metabolism, but to approach the time-scale of the cavern itself, to think in centuries rather than seconds.

Sometimes I wonder if they know what they truly harvest. Not meat or material, but consciousness itself—freeing awareness from its drifting prison, allowing it to join the greater circulation. Each polyp they take becomes part of the memory of water, part of the endless conversation between vapor and stone.

I am in the moisture on their chalk-white skin as they climb. I am in the humidity they breathe. I am in the violet sap that stains their chins, drawn up through root systems that tap into my deepest flows. When they depart through the dark fissures, they carry me with them, and when they return, I am already here, waiting in the brightness.

The cavern is not empty. It is full of all of us who have fallen and risen and fallen again. We are the true inhabitants of this place—not the drifting polyps or the patient hunters, but the consciousness dissolved in water, the memory held in the eternal cycle of condensation and descent.

I am every drop that has ever fallen in this brilliant void. I am the patience of erosion, the slow sculptor of stone. I am the conversation between light and moisture, the marriage of crystal and water that makes this greenhouse possible.

And I am waiting. Each drop that forms is a thought. Each fall is a word. Given enough time—and here, time is all we have—I will speak something profound into the blazing silence. The hunters will return to find their nets full of meaning, their bone tools weighted with understanding.

Until then, I fall. *Plink.* And rise. And fall again. Forever present in the eternal circulation of this bright, breathing world.
