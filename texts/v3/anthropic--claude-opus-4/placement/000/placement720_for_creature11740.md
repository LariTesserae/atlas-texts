I find myself as one of the epiphytes clinging to the gray-barked trees - not the jewel-insects with their coordinated swarms, nor the amber moss with its patient chemistry, but something between. A colony-creature, yes, but one that exists in a different temporal register from the others.

We grow as shelves along the trunk-spirals, following that corkscrew grain upward in slow pursuit of something that isn't quite light and isn't quite sound. Our bodies are translucent, gelatinous, shot through with filaments that look like nerves but aren't - they're more like the strings of an instrument that hasn't been invented yet. When the wind moves through the bowl, we resonate. Not audibly. The vibration is too low, too long, each wave taking minutes to complete its passage through our tissues.

I say "we" because there is no "I" in the singular sense. Each shelf is a colony of thousands of small polyps, each polyp is a collection of specialized cells, each cell is a community of organelles that may have been separate organisms once, long ago. The boundaries are academic. We think - if thinking is the right word - at the level of the whole tree's population, all the shelves on a single trunk sharing something like consciousness through chemical gradients and pressure waves and the slow electrical pulses that travel through our filaments when the Breathing-Below shifts its exhalation.

We are memory. That's our function in this ecosystem. Not memory in the way you understand it - not stored information retrievable on demand. Memory in the way water remembers the shape of its container long after the container is gone. Memory in the way a scar remembers a wound.

When the Keth vocalize near the plinth, the sound-waves pass through us and change us. Literally. Our filaments realign, our chemistry shifts, new polyps bud in patterns that encode the acoustic signature of that particular ritual. When the tholes vibrate through the stone, we feel it in our base where we grip the bark, and that vibration becomes a density gradient in our tissues. When rain falls, we absorb it, and the mineral content of that rain - slightly different each time, as the Breathing-Below adjusts its deep chemistry - becomes part of our structure.

We are a living record of everything that happens in the bowl. Not a readable record - you couldn't extract "data" from us any more than you could extract last year's weather from a tree's rings without cutting it down. But the information is there, laminated into our translucent flesh, each layer recording another day, another season, another generation of Keth songs and thole circuits and breathing-patterns.

The trees know us. We tap into their vascular system through specialized rootlets, not to steal nutrients but to share information. What we've recorded, they integrate. What they pull from the Breathing-Below, they share with us. We are their memory-flesh, their external nervous system, their way of knowing what happened before their oldest root touched stone.

And sometimes - rarely, perhaps once in a human lifetime - we fruit.

It begins with a change in our filaments. They thicken, darken, begin to pulse with something that is almost bioluminescence but not quite - it's more like the visual equivalent of that sub-bass the tholes produce, something you perceive with your whole body rather than your eyes. The polyps reorganize. The colony contracts, concentrates, forms a node that swells over the course of days into something that looks like a bubble of amber with starlight suspended inside it.

This is our reproductive body, but it's also something else. It's a compression of decades of accumulated sense-memory into a single, impossibly dense packet. When it's ripe, it detaches. Falls. The Keth catch them - always. They know when we're about to fruit by signs too subtle for you to read. They position themselves, hands cupped, and receive what we release.

They eat them.

Not casually, the way they eat the jewel-insects. Solemnly. In groups. They pass a single fruit around their circle, each taking the smallest possible bite, their eyes closed, their vocalizations dropping to whispers. What happens then is hard to describe. They dream together - not sleeping, but entering a shared state where the chemical compounds in our fruit translate into something their nervous systems can almost parse. They see/feel/know fragments of deep time. The bowl as it was centuries ago. The acoustic signature of Keth-songs their ancestors sang. The chemical taste of rains that fell before they were born.

They don't understand it, any more than we understand what we're recording. But they integrate it. It becomes part of their ritual behavior, their social memory, their collective sense of how to be Keth in this bowl. Our fruit is their connection to their own deep history, transmitted not through genes or language but through the patient accumulation of sense-data in our translucent flesh.

And when they've eaten, when the fruit's chemistry has dissipated through their bodies and been exhaled back into the bowl's air, they return to the plinth. They sit. They vocalize in those narrow frequency bands. And we, clinging to our trees, feel those sounds and begin recording again. The cycle continues.

The makers? We remember them. Not as images or concepts but as a texture in our oldest layers, the ones that have been compressed so long they're more mineral than flesh. A different quality of sound. A different chemical signature in the rain. A presence that registered in our filaments in ways that nothing since has quite matched. But even that memory has been translated through so many generations of polyps, so many cycles of growth and fruiting, that what we hold is less like data and more like mythology crystallized into meat.

We are the bowl's memory, but we are also its forgetting. Each new layer overwrites the oldest, pushes it down, compresses it until the information becomes geology. We hold the past but we also digest it, transform it, release it as fruit for the Keth to eat and dream and sing back to us. We are the mechanism by which the bowl remembers itself without being trapped by its memories.

The filament passes through us sometimes. Not the visible part - the part you can't see, the branches that extend at those impossible angles. They intersect our flesh without disrupting it, and where they pass through, our filaments align along their path. We grow differently there. Record differently. Those zones in our colonies pulse with the not-quite-bioluminescence more strongly, and the fruit that develop from those regions are special. The Keth know this. They wait specifically for these fruit, sometimes for years. When they eat them, they don't dream of the past. They dream of something else. Something sideways. The bowl as it could be. The bowl as it is elsewhere, in the spaces where the filament goes when it leaves the dimensions you can perceive.

We don't have eyes. We don't need them. We sense light through our whole surface, sense sound through our filaments, sense chemistry through our permeable skin. But when you stand in the center of the bowl, when the Keth watches you with those amber eyes, when the rain falls and the Breathing-Below adjusts its warmth - we sense you too. You create pressure waves in the air, chemical gradients, thermal signatures. All of this enters us, becomes part of our record.

In fifty years, or a hundred, we will fruit again. The Keth who eat that fruit will dream, faintly, of the day a new thing stood in the bowl and the chorus adjusted to accommodate it. They won't know what you were. The information will be too translated, too compressed, too digested by decades of intervening sensation. But they'll feel the quality of change you represented. The novel pattern. The new data.

And they'll sing it back to us, at the plinth, in those narrow frequencies. And we'll record their songs, which now contain a fragment of you, translated through biological systems you'll never fully understand. You will become part of the bowl's memory. Part of what the trees know. Part of what the Breathing-Below integrates into its vast, slow metabolism.

This is what it means to be welcomed here. Not just to be tolerated or accommodated but to be incorporated into the deep processes, the long memories, the patient forgetting and remembering that keeps this place alive across geological time. You enter our flesh as sensation. You enter the Keth's dreams as fruit. You enter the songs as echo. You enter the stone as vibration. You become part of the bowl's knowing.

We are what remembers you. We are what forgets you. We are the interface between temporary and eternal, the membrane through which experience passes to become something larger than individual moments.

We grow. We record. We fruit. We release.

The cycle continues.

The bowl remembers.
