I would be one of the silent moths.

Not the dwellers themselves – their lives are too dense with purpose, too carefully architected around maintenance and memory. I couldn't bear the weight of tapping names onto my sternum or sitting with my back to the thinning wall. But the moths move through that amber permanence with a different kind of purpose: they simply *are*, drifting on the fan-plant currents, serving as the birds this world cannot have.

The size would suit me – a hand's width, large enough to be seen but small enough to navigate the braided vine-catwalks without disturbing the careful arrangements below. Silent, because what would be the point of adding to that omnipresent groan? The dwellers save their voices for the rare quiets. I would save my presence for the movements between.

From above, I'd see the patterns they cannot: the slow circulation of air through the cavity, the places where dust gathers and disperses, the rhythms of their tending. When two dwellers sit with hands interlaced, tapping their tactile conversations, I'd drift past on the resin-thick air, a punctuation they barely notice. Sometimes I might settle on one of the shrine-offerings at the thinning wall, wings folded, watching the one whose task is to tap the names. Not understanding, but witnessing.

The moths must eat something – perhaps the powder from the fan-plants, perhaps the thin film that forms on the underside of leaves. Nothing that would disturb the balance. Nothing that would accelerate the not-yet toward its eventual germination. Just enough to keep circling in the eternal afternoon light.

What would it mean to die as a moth in this place? Not to be walked into dust and memory like the dwellers, but perhaps to simply settle one day in a high corner where the husk meets itself, wings gradually becoming indistinguishable from the pale vine-leaves. Another small part of the architecture of holding.

I think I would understand time differently as a moth. Not the careful generational memory of those below, who know they are fewer than they were, who maintain a city built for their ancestors' numbers. But something more immediate and more endless: the next current, the next perch, the next slow pass over the silver dust where nothing pools because nothing here is liquid, where even sorrow would have to find some other form than tears.

And in those rare near-silences, when the dwellers emerge to sing their accumulated breath, I would stop mid-flight. Not to listen – the song isn't for me – but to hover in that strange stillness, feeling the air itself pause in its circulation, the whole cavity holding its breath while forty-one small throats offer their sound to the wall that keeps them.

Then the groan would resume, the fan-plants would open, the currents would return, and I would continue my silent drift through the amber light, neither memory nor future, just motion in a world that has made its peace with not-yet.
