I am the Taste-Keeper.

My laboratory grows in the forgotten intervals between root systems—spaces too small for the Rootworkers to maintain, too specialized for the city's autonomous processes to manage. Here, in gaps barely wider than a human torso, I cultivate the memory of Earth.

Each cramped chamber hosts a different impossibility: tomatoes that have never known soil, their genetics rewritten so many times they barely register as *Solanum lycopersicum* anymore. I coax them through seventeen distinct chemical baths, training their tissues to approximate something a pre-Convergence human might recognize. The taste is always wrong. Too sweet, too sharp, too green, too much like the chamber itself—that underlying note of engineered chlorophyll that pervades everything here.

I've been at this for thirty-seven years, since the day I first bit into a tourist-level "strawberry" and tasted nothing but structured water and synthetic sweetness. The old flavors, the true flavors—they're locked in my memory like seeds in amber. My grandmother made me taste them when I was young, black-market fruits smuggled up from the last soil farms before the Convergence completed. "Remember," she said, pressing a real apple against my lips. "Someone has to remember."

The city doesn't know I exist, officially. My chambers appear on no maintenance map. I pay the Rootworkers in flavor—tiny vials of concentrated essence that remind them what their nutrition paste is supposed to be imitating. In exchange, they route excess nutrients to my hidden grows, adjust the light spectrums when they can, never ask why I need samples from the pre-modification seed vaults.

My body is a catalog of failed experiments. My fingernails grow in ridges from chronic exposure to growth accelerants. My sense of smell has evolved past human normal—I can distinguish between 4,000 distinct volatile compounds now, map the chemical signature of any fruit across a spectrum baseline humans can't imagine. Sometimes I forget which sensations are taste and which are smell. Sometimes I forget they were ever different.

The breakthrough came last season. Peach #2,847. Something in the combination of the chamber's deep pressure, the modified growth hormones I'd extracted from a dying wall section, and the precise mineral balance I'd been adjusting for a decade. One bite and I was seven years old again, juice running down my chin in my grandmother's kitchen, the flavor so perfect I cried.

But I can't reproduce it. That specific fruit, that one moment of perfection—the plant died immediately after, something in its chemistry too authentic to survive in this world of engineered impossibilities. I've tried ten thousand variations since. They taste like ash, like trying to rebuild a symphony from a single remembered note.

The Rootworkers think I'm mad. The younger ones don't even understand what I'm trying to recreate—they've never known fruit as anything but efficient nutrient delivery. But sometimes an elder will pause at my threshold, nostrils flaring as they catch the ghost of something their own grandparents might have described. "What was that?" they ask. "That sweetness, but not sweet. That feeling in the back of my throat like summer?"

"Nectarine," I tell them. "Or what a nectarine is trying to become."

My samples fill crystalline vials smaller than my thumb—concentrated attempts at recovering what we traded for survival. Mango #5,932. Lime #3,401. Varieties that have no names because the originals were lost before we thought to save them. I document everything: chemical compositions, growth conditions, the exact shade of disappointment when another attempt fails to bridge the gap between memory and reality.

The city grows upward, its bones of root and chlorophyll reaching toward a sun that barely penetrates the canopy anymore. In my hidden chambers, I grow backward, reaching for an Earth that exists only in the chemical memories locked in modified seeds and the fading synapses of those old enough to remember.

Tomorrow I'll try apple #8,341. I've isolated a new compound from the deepest roots, something that might approximate the tannins we've lost. It won't work—they never do, not really. But sometimes, for just a moment as the taste hits my tongue, I'm not in a cramped chamber in a city that breathes. I'm in an orchard that no longer exists, biting into a fruit that knew soil and rain and seasons, tasting the Earth we left behind.

The Rootworkers bring me rumors from the surface: the tourists love the new "heritage fruit" exhibitions, engineered approximations that look perfect and taste like nothing. They don't know what they're missing. But I know. In my cells, in my memory, in the ten thousand failed attempts that edge incrementally closer to a truth I may never recapture, I know.

The chamber's heartbeat thrums through my laboratory walls. Sometimes I time my work to its rhythm—crushing samples on the exhale, tasting on the inhale, as if the city itself might remember what it's trying to help me recreate. In the wet, green darkness between root systems, I continue my impossible work: teaching a world that has forgotten flavor how to remember what it once knew how to grow.
