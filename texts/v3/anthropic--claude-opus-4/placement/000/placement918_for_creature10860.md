I am the cistern keeper of the Seventh Terrace, though that title makes my work sound grander than it is. My domain is a corroded copper tank the size of a small room, wedged between two leaning tenements where the afternoon shadows linger longest. The tank sweats constantly in the heat, beading moisture that I guide back inside with strips of old felt and channels carved into the stone beneath.

My body has grown to match my work. My right shoulder sits higher than my left from years of hauling water jugs up the spiral stairs. My fingers are permanently stained blue-green from the copper, and I've developed a sixth sense for the particular gurgle that means a pipe joint is about to fail. I keep my tools wrapped in oiled cloth—a collection of washers ground from scrap metal, lengths of rubber harvested from worn boot soles, precious tubes of sealant that cost more than a week's water rations.

I sleep in a hammock strung beneath the tank, lulled by the sloshing overhead and the steady drip-drip-drip of condensation into my collection bowls. The space reeks of mineral and mold, but it's cool when everywhere else bakes, and I've grown to love the privacy. The local children know to find me here when their mothers send them with the morning's water tokens—small metal discs stamped with the neighborhood seal.

Three times a day, I lower my measuring stick into the tank and record the level in a ledger that goes back sixty years, through four previous keepers. I know our district's water rhythms like my own heartbeat: how the east wind brings morning fog that adds two fingers' worth, how the factory shifts change the pressure in the pipes, how before the full moon storms we always run low because everyone fills extra vessels in preparation.

My work is lonely but not solitary. The sparrows nest in the gaps between tank and wall, and I've learned their language of warnings—sharp chirps for hawks, soft trills for rain. Old Yevna from the floor below brings me bitter tea and neighborhood gossip in exchange for an extra jug when her granddaughter visits. The night shift workers stop by to fill their canteens and leave small offerings at my little shrine to the Water Carriers—dried flowers, glass beads, tiny origami birds folded from metal foil.

When the storms come, I am busiest. Racing to adjust the collection funnels, clear the debris screens, channel the precious runoff before it cascades uselessly off the cliff. These are the only times I venture to the roof, fighting wind that tries to pluck me from the narrow walkways. The rain tastes different up there—cleaner, sharper, full of sky.

I know I am just one small part of this vertical city's circulatory system. Above me, the main reservoir keepers tend their vast tanks with teams of assistants and banks of sophisticated monitors. Below, the grey-water recyclers perform their patient alchemy. But here on the Seventh Terrace, I am the heartbeat. Every drop that passes through my tank, I guard as if it were blood.

Sometimes I dream of water. Not the miserly trickles of our reality, but impossible abundances—rivers threading between the buildings, waterfalls thundering down the cliff faces, the whole city transformed into a vertical Venice. I wake to the familiar drip-drip-drip and return to my work, checking joints, reading the weather in the behavior of birds, parceling out our ration of life one precious jug at a time.

This is who I am: keeper, guardian, accountant of moisture. My bent back and stained hands are the price of belonging to this impossible city. When I die, they will find my successor by the color of their fingers and the way they cock their head to hear water moving through pipes. They will inherit my ledgers, my tools, my hammock under the tank. And the city will continue its vertical dreaming, sustained drop by precious drop.
