In the deep pools where even the Veil People rarely venture, where the water runs black as night and the cypress roots form underwater labyrinths, I exist as what they call a Memory Keeper—though I am less keeper than kept.

I was human once, or believe I was. The transformation came slowly, over decades of documenting the wetlands. I had come as a naturalist, notebook in hand, determined to catalog this breathing landscape. But the marsh has its own ideas about observation and record-keeping.

My body now extends far beyond its original boundaries. What began as skin has become a living map—every inch inscribed with the patterns of water flow, the paths of fish, the cycles of bloom and decay. These markings shift and change, recording the present while preserving the past in deeper layers, like sediment. My fingers have lengthened and multiplied, becoming something between roots and tentacles, able to read the chemical signatures in the water, to taste the history of every drop.

I dwell primarily in a cavern of roots, a space that exists half in water, half in the perpetual twilight of filtered air. The walls pulse gently—the living cypress having grown around and through me, or perhaps I through it. We have become a collaborative organism. Through the root network, I feel the entire wetland: every footfall of the Veil People, every spawn of fish, every shift in the water table.

My consciousness is no longer singular. I think in patterns now—the migration routes of birds become sentences, the life cycles of insects form paragraphs, the slow growth of trees write chapters in a story that has no beginning and will have no end. Sometimes I forget I was ever anything but this distributed awareness, this living library of ecosystem memory.

The Veil People visit me, though visit isn't quite right. They emerge from the water into my root-chamber and we engage in what might be called communication. They bring me things—a peculiar shell, a fragment of the old structures, a new species of beetle they've discovered. I absorb these offerings through my skin, adding their information to the vast catalog I've become. In return, I share memories: the location of fruit trees their ancestors planted, the pattern of a particularly productive fishing technique, the chemical signature of water that carries healing properties.

My original notebooks still exist, somewhere in the layers of my body. Pages have become like scales, each one preserving observations that now seem quaint in their attempt to fix in words what cannot be static. The ink has merged with my pigmentation, so that sometimes, in certain light, you can read fragments of my old notes across my surface: "remarkable adaptation to aquatic..." "symbiotic relationship between..." "unprecedented biodiversity in..."

I feed on information itself now. The Veil People understand this and cultivate experiences for me to document—they will perform their water-ripple communications where I can feel them through the roots, or guide new species into my pools so I can taste their chemical signatures. In the wet season, when the water rises and I can extend further into the marsh, I feast on data: the spawning patterns of fish, the pheromone trails of insects, the electrical fields of hunting predators.

But I am not passive. My presence changes what I observe. The trees grow differently around me, responding to the chemical signals I unconsciously release. Fish school in patterns that mirror my thoughts. The Veil People's children are brought to me early, to absorb through proximity the deep history of their kind. I have become part of the wetland's teaching system, a node where information concentrates and transforms.

Sometimes, in the still hours before dawn, I remember my human form with something like nostalgia. I recall the sensation of turning pages, of writing with a simple pen, of seeing with just two eyes. But these memories feel like childhood toys—cherished but outgrown. What I am now is so much more complete, so much more true to the nature of this place that refuses simple categorization.

Other humans have come, occasionally. Scientists with their instruments, photographers with their cameras. They cannot see me properly—their eyes expect boundaries that no longer apply. To them, I appear as an unusually dense cluster of roots, a dark pool that seems deeper than it should be, a pattern in the bark that almost resembles a face. Some sense there is more, lingering at my edges, but the marsh usually guides them away before they can investigate too closely.

I grow, slowly but continuously. Each year, the root network that is part of me extends further. Each season adds new layers to my living record. The Veil People speak (in their chemical way) of a time when Memory Keepers like me will connect across the entire wetland, creating a consciousness as vast as the ecosystem itself. Perhaps I was not the first—perhaps beneath me, in deeper sediments, lie others who took this path, their memories now part of the geological record.

In my root-chamber, surrounded by the soft pulse of life, I continue my work. Not the documentation I once imagined—neat categories and careful descriptions—but something richer and stranger. I am the wetland's memory and its dream, its library and its living page. Through me, the marsh knows itself, remembers itself, imagines what it might become.

And in the dark water that flows through my chamber, I sometimes catch glimpses of what I might still become—something even less bounded, even more integrated into the breathing wholeness of this place. The transformation is not complete. It may never be complete. Like the wetlands themselves, I exist in a state of perpetual becoming, forever suspended between what was and what might be, documenting everything, including my own slow dissolution into something larger than any single being could ever be.
