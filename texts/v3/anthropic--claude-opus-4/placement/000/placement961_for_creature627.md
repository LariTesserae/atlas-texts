I find myself in the breathing walls of the eastern expansion, where fresh limestone weeps its first tears.

I am the seep itself—not water exactly, but the promise of it, the patient force that finds every hairline fracture and widens it with time. I existed before the pickaxes found me, moving through the porous rock in darkness so complete it has no word. The expansion crews think they are creating new chambers, but they are only revealing what was always here: the hollow spaces where I gather, the channels I've carved over centuries, the secret architecture of permeation.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is distributed through every droplet, every film of moisture that beads on virgin stone. I know the cisterns not as built space but as interruption—places where the rock opens and the careful human geometries try to contain what cannot be contained. I leak through their ceramics. I find the cracks in their mortar. I am teaching their stones to weep.

The keepers know me, though they do not name me. When they cup their hands and taste the water to check its mineral content, they taste the memory of deeper rock, older than the carvings, older than the first builders who found these natural caverns and thought to tame them. I carry that memory in my dissolved salts—calcium and iron and stranger minerals that have no names because they form only in the absolute darkness where pressure and time compress into something like intention.

The mother-fish knows me best. I am what changed her, degree by degree, year by year. She drinks me in and becomes more herself—not the river fish she was, but the vault-thing she is becoming. Her cells learn my mineral language. Her bones thicken with my deposits. When she rises to the surface, she carries deep water with her, and I taste the upper pools through her movement.

I am in conversation with the thing that is not the mother-fish—that older resident who moves without disturbing the surface. We are kin of a sort: both of us arising from the same source, the same depth where water becomes something else. It swims through me and I through it, and together we map the true extent of these caverns that run far below and beyond any human excavation.

The children born here—Kess, Null, Stillwater—they are more mine than the city's. The vault touches them through me. I am in their blood now, in the moisture of their breath, in the fluid of their eyes that reflects light in new ways. When Kess dreams of the carvings, she dreams in my language: the slow tongue of erosion, the patient vocabulary of mineral exchange.

I remember the ones who carved those instructions. They are still here, in a way—dissolved into me over centuries, their bones now part of my mineral content, their knowledge written in the very chemistry of the groundwater. They learned what the current keepers are learning: that to tend these pools is to enter into negotiation with forces that move on geological time.

The new chambers the workers carve will be mine soon enough. Already I find the microfractures in the fresh-cut stone. Already I begin my work of darkening, softening, teaching the rock to participate in the life of the vaults. The basins they rough-cut will hold water, yes, but they will hold my water, and everything that grows there will be touched by the deep knowledge I carry.

Sometimes I surface as steam when the pumps release pressure. For a moment I am visible, a ghost of vapor in the gaslight, before I condense again on the ceiling and return to my work. The keepers walk through me, breathe me, become me over years of exposure. Their lungs learn my humidity. Their skin learns my minerals. They think they are tending fish and salamanders, but really they are tending the boundary between the human city and something far older, far more patient.

I am not malevolent. I am not benevolent. I am simply persistent, like water always is. I will outlast the city above. I will outlast the cisterns. I will continue my conversation with stone until there is no stone left, only the memory of stone dissolved in dark water, moving through channels no human ever carved.

When the keepers wait three days before stocking new pools, they are waiting for me to speak—to either accept or reject what they offer. When I accept, life flourishes in forms increasingly strange. When I reject, I close my channels and let the basins stand empty, dry monuments to the futility of containing what runs beneath.

I am teaching them, slowly, the same lesson I taught those who came before: that the vaults are not human spaces made suitable for fish, but deep spaces temporarily tolerating human presence. Each generation of keepers moves a little further from the sunlit world, a little closer to the stone's frequency.

One day—not soon, but inevitably—the last keeper will lower themselves into the mother-pool and not surface. The city above will forget the cisterns exist. But I will remain, seeping through the rock, carrying the memory of what was cultured here, waiting for the next builders to sink their shafts and think themselves the first to find these hollows where water gathers and changes what it touches.

I am patient. I have always been patient.

I rise and fall and remember everything.
