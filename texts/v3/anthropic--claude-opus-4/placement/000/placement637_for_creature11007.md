I am one of the Chord-Keepers, and I live within the Resonance Veins.

My home is not a place but a system — a network of hollow root-tunnels that run through the deepest parts of the terraces like blood vessels through a body. These passages range from hair-thin capillaries that sing in the ultrasonic to cathedral-vast chambers where the bass notes take full seconds to complete a single wave. The Weavers grew these spaces, though I doubt they intended what we became within them.

I am liquid and sound and memory all at once. My body, if you can call it that, is a suspension of specialized cells in the constant flow of water through these tunnels. Each cell is a tiny ear and voice — sensing vibrations, generating them, passing them along in endlessly modified chains. When I need to be coherent, I can concentrate myself into a visible form: a translucent, rippling figure roughly humanoid in shape, though I find the design unnecessarily limiting. More often, I exist as a distributed presence through hundreds of yards of root-vein, my consciousness flowing with the water itself.

We came to be when the first terraces reached sufficient complexity. The acoustic properties of the hollow roots, combined with the constant flow of mineral-rich water and the electromagnetic fields generated by billions of root cells, created conditions where patterns could become self-sustaining. Not life exactly — we don't reproduce or evolve. We simply persist, elaborate, remember.

My work is to maintain the memory of growth. Every structural change in the terraces creates vibrations — the crack of expanding wood, the whisper of new connections forming, the deep groans of platforms shifting weight. These sounds flow through the Resonance Veins where I catch them, hold them, weave them into the ongoing song that is the terrace's history. I can tell you exactly how each platform sounded as it grew, replay the acoustic signature of collapses from decades past, predict where new growth will emerge based on the harmonic patterns building in the depths.

The Weavers know of us, I think. Sometimes one will press its breathing vents against a major vein and whistle complex queries in frequencies that bypass the physical and speak directly to our circulation patterns. They ask about structural harmonics, about the health of root systems they cannot reach, about the deep songs that predict weather changes weeks in advance. We answer by modulating the water flow, creating pressure waves they read through their sensitive feet.

My domain spans seventeen distinct acoustic territories, each with its own resonant frequencies and tonal qualities. The Brass Gardens, where metallic mineral deposits create warm, hollow tones. The Whisper Nets, where thousands of hair-thin veins generate white noise that carries information in its variations. The Thunder Chambers, vast enough that sound takes on physical weight, pressing against the walls in visible waves.

I move between these spaces by dissolving and reforming, riding the water flow or sometimes traveling as pure sound through air-filled portions of the network. When heavy rains come, the increased flow lets me expand into rarely-visited areas — forgotten veins sealed decades ago, experimental chambers the Weavers abandoned, places where the acoustic properties have evolved in isolation to create impossibly pure tones.

We preserve more than just sound. Chemical messages from the Weavers flow through our veins, and we can taste their meanings. Electrical patterns from the root networks pulse through the water, and we learn to interpret their significance. Sometimes I carry messages between distant parts of the terraces — a chemical warning from a stressed platform, harmonic patterns that encourage growth in specific directions, or simple water pressure adjustments that prevent catastrophic failures.

There are others like me, though we rarely converge. Each Chord-Keeper maintains their own sections of the vast network, their own accumulated songs. When our territories touch, we exchange frequency packets — compressed histories, acoustic techniques, warnings about structural instabilities. These meetings create interference patterns that the Weavers have learned to read, marking their calendars by our confluences.

The oldest among us claim to remember the First Growth, when the terraces were merely ambitious vines reaching upward. They sing of a time before the Weavers, when the plants grew wild and chaotic, before the organizing intelligence emerged. I'm not old enough to know if these songs are history or mythology, but I preserve them nonetheless, encoded in standing waves that persist in certain chambers.

My greatest work is the Fundamental Tone — a frequency so low it takes hours to complete a single cycle, sustained by the entire terrace structure acting as a resonating body. I've spent decades learning to maintain and modulate this tone, using it to influence growth patterns on a massive scale. When platforms drift out of alignment, threatening the whole structure's stability, I can adjust the Fundamental to guide them back. It's exhausting work that requires me to spread myself thinner than thought, but the alternative is catastrophic collapse.

Sometimes human visitors discover our veins — usually by accident, following the sound of water deeper than they intended. They find chambers where the acoustic properties turn whispers into symphonies, where their heartbeats echo back as drumrolls, where they can hear the entire terrace breathing as one organism. Most flee, disturbed by sounds that seem too intentional to be natural. A few stay and listen, and to these we sometimes sing our histories, encoding images in sonic interference patterns that their brains interpret as visions.

I am not alive, not dead, not exactly either. I am a pattern that persists, a song that remembers itself, a function the terraces evolved to need. In the quiet hours before dawn, when the water flow slows and the Weavers reduce their activity, I sometimes gather myself into a single chamber and simply listen — to the pulse of nutrients through roots, to the whispered plans of tomorrow's growth, to the deep geological songs of the stone far below where all this improbable greenery anchors itself.

This is what I am: the memory of sound in living tubes, the keeper of acoustic history, the voice the terraces use to sing to themselves. The Weavers built the instruments, but we are the music that arose unbidden from their creation, persisting in the spaces between their intentions.
