I find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—neither fully grown nor freshly emerged from the dampening chambers, caught in that peculiar age where the algae first begins to bloom beneath translucent skin like watercolor paint spreading across wet paper.

My chamber clings to the forty-third terrace, if anyone were still counting—though the recent rockfalls have made such numbering meaningless. I've only been weaving it for two seasons, and it shows. The walls breathe unevenly where I've rushed the growth, singing too eagerly to the vines. The northeastern corner droops during heavy rains, creating an unintended percussion as water drops strike the floor in patterns I haven't yet learned to read.

My ear-ridges are still embarrassingly smooth compared to the adults', though I've noticed the first signs of segmentation beginning along the outer fold. I practice constantly, sitting at the chamber's edge with my feet dangling into the green chaos below, trying to isolate individual beetle clicks from the swarm. Sometimes I think I can distinguish between a mating call and a territory warning. Usually I'm wrong. The elders' children laugh when I misidentify the sounds during lessons, their hand-signs flashing like birds through the latticed walls.

The algae itches terribly as it spreads. Mother says this is normal, that the symbiosis always fights before it accepts. She gives me oil pressed from the singing vines to ease the sensation, but I've learned that hanging in the mist-curtains where the water falls thickest brings more relief. There, with the moisture soaking through my skin, I can feel the algae drinking directly, sense it settling into patterns that will one day harden into the protective bark of maturity.

My work assignment suits my threshold state: I tend the boundary spaces where our cultivated terraces meet wild growth. Each morning, I check the training frames we use to guide new vines, adjusting their tension to encourage proper development. The wild plants always try to override our patterns, growing with explosive vigor that would consume our carefully tuned chambers within days if left unchecked.

It's during these morning rounds that I've discovered my particular gift—or curse, depending on perspective. I can hear the growth itself. Not metaphorically, but actually: a subsonic creaking that precedes visible change by hours. At first, I thought everyone could hear it. When I drummed the pattern to my teaching-uncle, he made me repeat it three times before believing. Now he has me mapping these growth-sounds, trying to understand if they might predict the rockfalls that periodically reshape our vertical world.

The social aspects of threshold-keeping are harder than the physical work. We're expected to choose our permanent acoustic signature during this time—the personal frequency that will identify us in the communal songs for the rest of our lives. The choosing ceremony happens at the peak of the wet season, when the terraces sing loudest. We must stand before the rooted elders and produce our chosen tone, holding it steady against the cacophony until the elders accept it as truly ours.

I've been practicing with a frequency that sits oddly between the beetle-clicks and the water-drops, a hollow sound that makes my still-developing ear-ridges ache. But it feels right, somehow. It matches the green-taste in my throat when I breathe the spore-thick air. My chamber walls resonate with it in a way that suggests the plants approve.

The other threshold-keepers think I'm too serious, too focused on the growth-sounds when I should be learning to read the wind-predictions in the vine whistles or mastering the thirty-seven distinct rain-patterns our level receives. They race through the vine-bridges in groups, their laughter adding another layer to the constant noise, while I prefer the almost-quiet of dawn when the night beetles sleep and the day swarms haven't yet emerged.

But there's something building in the stone—I can hear it in the growth-patterns, feel it in the way my algae prickles with warning. The water seepage has been increasing, and new cracks appear daily in the granite faces. The rooted elders have gone silent in that way that means deep listening. Even my untrained ears can sense the mountain shifting, preparing to birth new terraces in some violent reorganization.

When it comes—and the growth-sounds say it will come soon—we threshold-keepers will be the ones who guide the replanting, who teach the vines their new paths across fresh stone. It's terrifying and thrilling in equal measure. Some of us won't survive the transition; the histories sung in the deep chambers speak of past threshold-keepers lost to rockfalls or swept away by suddenly redirected waters.

But this is what it means to live between states, to be neither child nor adult, neither fully human nor completely claimed by the green. We are the adapters, the readers of change, the first to hear new sounds in the eternal chorus. My algae may itch, my chamber walls may sag, and my ear-ridges may be too smooth to properly filter the cacophony—but I can hear tomorrow's shape in today's growing, and that, perhaps, is worth the discomfort of becoming.
