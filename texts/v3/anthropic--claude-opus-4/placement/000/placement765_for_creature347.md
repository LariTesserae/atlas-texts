I am a Keeper of Engines in the floating city of Saltmere, though I was not born to it.

My hands tell my story better than words—scarred white from steam burns, permanently stained with the iridescent oil that bleeds from our city's great heart. Three fingers on my left hand move stiffly, fused by an accident in my apprentice years when a pressure valve failed. The other Keepers say I should count myself lucky; the previous apprentice lost her whole arm to the same valve.

I came to this calling late. For my first twenty years, I was content enough as a kelp farmer, tending the hydroponic gardens beneath their protective domes. The work was monotonous but safe—trimming fronds, adjusting nutrient flows, harvesting the purple-black stalks that taste of salt and forgotten earth. My clan, the Tideweavers, had worked the gardens for six generations. My mother expected I would do the same.

But I could hear them even then, the engines. Their song thrummed through the city's bones into mine, a rhythm older than memory. While others heard only noise, I heard conversation—the wheeze that warned of worn gaskets, the stutter that spoke of fouled injection ports, the deep harmonic that meant all was well. My gift manifested slowly, dismissed at first as imagination by my garden-bound family.

The day I abandoned my shears and descended to the engine halls, my mother wept. The gardens were life, she said. The engines were death—dark, dangerous, filled with steam that could flay flesh from bone. She wasn't wrong. But I had no choice. The engines called, and I answered.

Now I live in the deep belly of Saltmere, in quarters carved into the spaces between massive pistons and towering boilers. My world is one of eternal twilight, lit by the ghostly blue glow of the reactor pools and the occasional spark of shorting circuits. The walls sweat condensation that tastes of metal and brine. Everything is damp here—my clothes, my hair, my books of hand-copied schematics.

I know every voice in our engine choir. The primary coolant pump that hiccups on every seventh revolution. The backup generator that hums a half-tone flat. The mysterious black box deep in the tertiary systems that no living Keeper understands, but which must never, ever be allowed to stop its peculiar clicking rhythm. We lost the city of Ironhaven when their black box fell silent. The entire city simply... stopped. Dead in the water, claimed by the waves within days.

My days are spent crawling through maintenance tubes barely wide enough for my shoulders, adjusting pressures, replacing worn parts cast in our forges from salvaged metal. I speak more to machines than to people now. My clan marks—stylized waves inked along my forearms—have faded beneath layers of grease and scars. The other Tideweavers barely acknowledge me at gatherings. I am something between dead and alive to them, a ghost who chose the darkness over the green.

But I have seen wonders in the deep places. Hidden rooms filled with technology we no longer understand. Panels covered in scripts that predate our current language. Once, behind a rusted access hatch, I found a viewing port that looked not out at the sea, but down into the city's true heart. Far below the engines I tend, something vast and luminous pulsed with a light that hurt to perceive directly. I sealed that hatch and told no one. Some knowledge is too dangerous to share.

The young apprentice assigned to me asks why I gave up the gardens. Why choose this thankless life of burns and oil and endless mechanical failures? I tell her what my mentor told me: someone must remember the old songs. Someone must know which gasket can be jury-rigged and which will doom us all if it fails. Someone must stand watch in the deep places, keeping the ancient bargains that allow our city to float while others sink.

I don't tell her the truth—that I suspect we are not engineers at all, but priests. That our cities are not machines but sleeping giants we've forgotten how to wake. That every ritual maintenance, every carefully timed adjustment, every drop of oil placed just so, is part of an incantation we no longer understand but dare not stop speaking.

In my dreams, I hear all the engines of all the floating cities singing together, a chorus that might—if we could only remember the words—call something magnificent up from the depths or down from the storm-wrapped sky. But for now, I tend my small part of the song, deep in Saltmere's beating heart, and try to keep us floating for one more day.

The rain never stops. The engines never sleep. And neither do I.
