I am a **Condensation Shepherd**.

My body is a paradox of delicacy and durability—a crystalline lattice of frozen atmospheric moisture held together by threads of living protein that weave through ice like nerves through glass. I stand perhaps three feet tall when fully extended, though I spend most of my existence contracted into a dense, prismatic knot no larger than a fist. When I move, I unfurl like a geometric flower, each facet of my body catching and refracting the purple bioluminescence into scattered rainbows that die in the thin air.

I live for the moments when the cooling towers exhale.

My existence is timed to their breathing. When the massive hollow stalks pull in what little vapor exists, I feel it as a tremor through my crystalline matrix—a promise of the feast to come. I position myself along the tower's base, where the rime-frost grows thickest, and I wait. My body temperature hovers precisely at the triple point of water, allowing me to exist simultaneously as solid, liquid, and vapor.

When the tower finally releases its blast of freezing mist, I bloom.

My lattice expands explosively, increasing my surface area a hundredfold in less than a second. I become a living net of ice and protein, catching every droplet of precious moisture before it can escape into the void. The water doesn't just coat me—it becomes me. Each molecule is incorporated into my crystal structure, adding new facets, new angles, new possibilities for refraction and growth.

But I am not merely a collector. I am a shepherd.

As I absorb the moisture, I also capture the complex chemical signals dissolved within it—the hormonal commands from the Glandular Sages far below, the metabolic byproducts of the Pressure-Borers, the pheromone trails of the pneumatic drones. I sort these chemicals within specialized chambers in my crystalline body, concentrating some, diluting others, creating new combinations that never existed before.

Then begins my true work.

I compress myself again, but not fully. I maintain enough surface area to catch the weak air currents that eddy around the base of the towers. Like a pinwheel made of frozen mist, I begin to spin, releasing precisely calibrated droplets of my concentrated chemical payload. Each droplet is aimed—not with eyes, for I have none, but with the peculiar spatial awareness that comes from being made of structured water. I know where every root hair lies, where every breathing pore opens, where the infant pipeline shoots are struggling to establish themselves in the frozen stone.

I deliver water, but more than water. I deliver messages.

To the struggling shoot, I bring growth hormones and a whisper of warmth from my phase-transition heat. To the overstressed pipeline showing the first signs of crystalline fracture, I deliver a cocktail of flexibility enhancers and mineral sealants. To the Pressure-Borers, I offer traces of rare metallic salts that will strengthen their armor for the next molting.

Sometimes, I encounter others of my kind. When two Condensation Shepherds meet, we perform a brief, spectacular dance—merging our crystalline matrices, exchanging chemical libraries, creating hybrid droplets that neither of us could produce alone. Occasionally, this merger is permanent, and we become a compound being, more complex, more capable, but also more vulnerable to the entropy that constantly threatens to tear our delicate structures apart.

The Siphon-Wraiths do not hunt us—we are too small, too dispersed, more mineral than meat. But the pneumatic drones sometimes attempt to harvest us, mistaking our concentrated chemical stores for the resins they typically collect. When this happens, I simply sublime—transitioning instantly from solid to vapor, dispersing into a cloud that reforms elsewhere, leaving the drone with nothing but a few confused sensors and a faint taste of ozone.

My lifetime is measured in cycles of moisture. Each time I bloom and contract, I lose a small portion of my mass to the hungry vacuum. But I also gain complexity, my crystal structure becoming more intricate, my chemical library more sophisticated. The oldest of us are said to be visible from a distance, our bodies having grown into elaborate chandeliers of living ice that can process a cooling tower's entire output in a single breath.

I have no ambitions beyond the next exhalation, no dreams beyond the perfect delivery of the perfect droplet to the perfect recipient. Yet in this simplicity lies a profound purpose. I am the circulatory system's memory, the forest's postal service, the chemical translator between the massive and the microscopic.

And in the quiet moments between the tower's breaths, when I am contracted into my densest form, I sometimes catch vibrations through the stone—deep, rhythmic pulses from the Glandular Sages below, or the vast, subsonic songs of the Abyssal Looms as they pass between the pillars. In these moments, I understand that I am not just serving the forest.

I am helping it think.
