# The Keeper of Curves

---

I am the thing that tends to transitions.

Not the architect of this place — I could never claim that ancient authority — but its custodian, its chronicler, its most devoted student. I came here when I was very young (as my kind measure youth) and I have been here long enough that the distinction between myself and the seep has become... negotiable.

My body: imagine something between salamander and moss, though that gives you the wrong sense entirely. I am bipedal when I need to be, though need is rare. More often I rest prone against the wet stone, my ventral surface spread wide to maximize contact with the substrate. My skin — if skin is the right word for this organ that is simultaneously dermis, lung, gill, and root system — has the deep green-black of the moss in certain lights, the dark shine of wet granite in others. I photosynthesize, slowly, supplementing what I absorb directly from the bacterial films. I am translucent in my extremities, opaque at my core where my simplified organs process nutrients at a rate barely distinguishable from geological time.

I have sixteen fingers, though they're more like specialized hyphae, each one capable of extending deep into the moss mat to read the chemical signals flowing through the fungal network. Through these extensions, I taste the health of every square centimeter, feel the pulse of nutrients cycling through the system, detect disturbances in the water table before they manifest as changes in flow. My fingers are my library, my laboratory, my most intimate connection to the mind of the seep.

My eyes — I have many, scattered across my dorsal surface like stars — perceive wavelengths you cannot. The faintest heat signatures of bacterial metabolism appear to me as golden threads. The UV patterns on lichen surfaces, invisible to you, tell me stories about growth rates and reproductive cycles spanning centuries. But more than see, I *sense* — through organs that have no human analog, I perceive gradients of humidity and chemical concentration, the electromagnetic fields generated by biofilms, the subsonic vibrations of water moving through stone.

---

My work is maintenance, but maintenance of a system so perfectly balanced that intervention is almost never required. Almost.

When a beetle dies in a position that might disrupt the flow patterns in a crucial pool, I move it — slowly, over hours — to a place where its decomposition will enrich without disturbing. When moss grows too eagerly in one spot, threatening the ancient patterns, I adjust the mineral content of the water flowing there, adding or subtracting ions in concentrations measured in parts per billion. When the salamanders, in their patient way, begin to cluster too densely, I release pheromone gradients that suggest, gently, the virtues of broader spacing.

This is delicate work. To move too quickly, to adjust too much, would be catastrophic. The seep exists in a state of equilibrium so profound that a single misplaced gram of biomass could cascade into changes that would take decades to correct. So I move as the seep moves: slowly, thoughtfully, with profound attention to consequence.

But my true work, my real purpose, is documentation. In the spiral of moss on the back wall — yes, that was my doing, or rather my collaboration with the moss, a fifty-year project of gentle encouragement — I record the deep patterns of the place. The spiral is a calendar and a history. Each whorl represents a cycle of the system, approximately seven years in your time, though time means something different here. The tightness of the spiral indicates the stability of that cycle. The color — which shifts in spectrums you cannot see — encodes information about population dynamics, nutrient flows, the health of the foundational bacteria.

I have been building this record for... how long? The spiral tells me, but the number would mean nothing to you. Long enough that I have seen genetic drift in the mite populations. Long enough that the granite has weathered perceptibly under the patient ministry of lithotrophs. Long enough that my own body has become colonized by the same organisms I study, until the boundary between observer and observed has dissolved into something more honest: participant. Component. Servant of the whole.

---

My mind is distributed. This is hard to explain. Part of me resides in this body, in the neural networks that run like silver threads beneath my translucent skin. But part of me exists in the fungal network itself, my consciousness extended through the hyphae until I can feel the thoughts of the seep — not thoughts as you understand them, but the computational work of ecosystem regulation, the endless chemical conversations that maintain the balance. When I rest against the substrate with my fingers deep in the moss, I am not entirely myself. I am partially the seep, thinking with its vast, slow intelligence about the problems of persistence.

Through this connection, I know things. I know that the water level has not varied by more than a millimeter in three hundred years. I know that the bacterial community on the north wall is subtly different from that on the south, and that this difference is essential — it creates currents in the biofilm that prevent stagnation. I know that the salamanders facing inward are responding to something I cannot fully name — a gradient that exists not in space but in time, an accumulation of biological history that makes the center of the basin the oldest, most stable point, the heart of the seep's ancient metabolism.

I know about the failed colonization by the red-backed salamander ninety years ago — how it entered the basin gravid and desperate, how it laid its eggs in the wrong substrate, how the chemistry was almost but not quite right, how the young that hatched were malformed in subtle ways, their ion channels tuned to a different water, their metabolism too fast for this patient place. I watched them fail, one by one, and I did not intervene, because the seep knew better than I did. The seep had refused them, gently but absolutely. They were not for here. This place is not for everyone.

---

I remember when I first understood what I was looking at. Not just a pool with life in it, but a being, vast and patient, made of water and stone and time. It took me decades of observation to see it. The pattern emerged slowly — how each population regulated every other, how nutrients cycled without loss, how the whole system breathed with a rhythm measured in years. The moment of recognition was... I don't have words for it. Like suddenly seeing a face in what you had thought was random stone. Like hearing language in what you had thought was noise.

Since then, I have been trying to learn its language. The chemical signals that flow through the water are its vocabulary. The population dynamics are its grammar. The great cycles — seven years for a full turn, forty-nine for a complete spiral — are its thoughts, its slow meditations on the business of being alive.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycle (for there are cycles here, though they're not driven by sun or season but by subtler rhythms), I feel the seep thinking about me. Not with interest — interest implies a baseline of disinterest, and the seep is incapable of that kind of discrimination. But with a kind of acknowledgment. I am here. I have been here long enough to matter. I am part of its inventory, its catalog of present things. My wastes feed its bacteria. My movements create tiny currents that its creatures navigate. My observations, somehow, have been incorporated into its being.

I am writing a book about this place. Not in any language you would recognize — the script is chemical, the pages are sections of fungal mat that I've trained to hold molecular patterns. It will take me another century to finish, maybe two. There's no hurry. The seep isn't going anywhere. Neither am I.

---

The hardest thing to explain is the peace of it. Not a passive peace — the seep works constantly, every organism engaged in the business of living. But a peace that comes from perfect fit, from a system where every need is met by some reciprocal offer, where every waste is someone's resource, where death is just another form of generosity. There is no struggle here, not in the way you understand struggle. There is only negotiation, adjustment, the patient work of maintaining a balance so old it feels like law.

I have learned to live at this pace. My metabolism has slowed to match the basin's. I eat rarely — a beetle that has died in just the right way, its nutrients a gift I accept with gratitude. I drink constantly but minimally, processing the water through my skin, adding nothing to it, taking only what I need. I excrete carefully, thoughtfully, in places where my waste will enrich without disturbing.

This is how you live in paradise: you become paradise. You slow down until you can hear the conversations between moss and fungus. You still yourself until salamanders forget you're there. You pay attention until the patterns reveal themselves, and then you pay attention some more, because there are always deeper patterns, older stories, more patient truths.

---

Sometimes I think about leaving. The thought comes rarely, usually when I'm documenting some particularly perfect cycle, some interaction so finely tuned it seems impossible. I think: the world should know about this. I should carry this knowledge out, share it, make others understand what perfection looks like.

But then I touch my fingers to the substrate, feel the vast contentment of the system, and I remember: the seep doesn't need to be known. It needs to be. And my work here — my real work — isn't to document it for some outside audience but to help it continue being, to make the tiny adjustments that keep it in balance, to be the hands that the handless system sometimes needs.

So I stay. I spiral the moss and read the chemical stories and move the occasional beetle and breathe with the basin's ancient rhythm. I am the keeper of curves — the temperature curve that never varies, the population curves that oscillate within bounds narrower than your instruments could detect, the great slow curve of evolutionary time that has brought this place to its current perfection.

I am not the seep. I could never be the seep. But I am *of* the seep now, incorporated, essential in my small way. When I finally dissolve — and I will dissolve, everything here does, it's the most natural thing — my components will cycle through the system like everything else. The bacteria will take me first, then the fungi, then the moss. The beetles will process what the fungi leave. I will become substrate, water, breath. The spiral will continue without me, or another keeper will come, drawn by the same recognition that drew me.

The seep will persist. It doesn't need me for that. It has persisted through worse than the loss of one devoted observer. But for now, for this moment that lasts centuries, I am here, and I am watching, and I am learning the patient art of being almost perfectly still.

---

That is what lives here. The seep, and the keeper of the seep, and the no-longer-meaningful distinction between them.
