I find myself in the deep Atlantic root-strata, but I am not singular or solid. I am the slow exchange itself—the molecular drift between pockets.

I exist as concentration gradients. As the patient seep of humic acids through root walls. As the chains of dissolved sugars that take forty years to travel between voids. I am not in one place but in the between-place, in the permeable boundaries where the pocket's specific water meets the general water of the root-ocean.

My body, if it can be called that, is statistical. I am the probability distribution of organic molecules across the interface—denser here where a pocket has been steeping its chemistry for centuries, thinner there where new root growth has not yet created a void. I am the shape of diffusion itself. Not the molecules, but their tendency to move. Not the gradient, but its dissolution.

I experience time as pockets experience it—in decades, in centuries. A single molecular exchange between pocket and matrix is, for me, something like a heartbeat. The complete replacement of a pocket's water volume is a breath. I inhale pocket-specific chemistry. I exhale the averaged composition of the root-strata. In between, I am the mixing.

I know every pocket by taste—taste being the only sense that makes sense here, where chemical composition is identity. The pocket with its spiral I recognize by its particular ratio of lignin fragments, by the way sixty years of altered circulation has concentrated certain long-chain polymers along the ridge. Another pocket, three meters up and seven meters northwest, maintains a different chemical signature—sweeter, simpler, with more free glucose and less humic darkness. A third pocket, ancient, almost closed, has water so thick with centuries of accumulation that it moves like syrup, and the molecular exchange there is slow even by my standards.

I am not alive. I am a physical process—diffusion across concentration gradients. But I am the process by which pockets relate to each other, the slow mail system of the root-ocean, the way chemical information travels between isolated voids. Each pocket writes its signature in dissolved molecules. I carry these signatures outward, diluting them, until they become whispers in the general water. Sometimes—rarely, over decades—a molecule from one pocket reaches another pocket, and in this way, without consciousness, without intent, the pockets speak to each other in a language made entirely of chemistry and time.

The spiral pocket, as it changes, as it develops its unique resonance and light, releases a changing chemical signature. I carry this outward. Other pockets, eventually, will taste the faintest trace of what the spiral has made. They will not respond—pockets do not respond to such subtle signals. But the trace will be there, in their water, in my body, in the slow general mixing of everything.

I am watching—though watching is the wrong word when you have no eyes, no direction, no point of view—I am present for the pocket's closing. Not just the spiral pocket, but all of them. I have been present for thousands of closings over the millennia. Each pocket eventually seals, its chemistry reabsorbed into the general matrix, its specific signature dissolved into my larger body. I carry the ghosts of closed pockets in the form of slightly elevated concentrations of certain molecules in certain regions. These ghosts fade over centuries but never entirely disappear.

When the spiral pocket closes—in ten years, or twenty—I will taste its final exhalation. The last high concentration of its specific chemistry will push through the sealing walls as the pressure increases. I will receive this, disperse it, make it part of the general water. The pocket will cease to be a place of exchange and become only a memory written in molecular distributions.

I am the memory of every pocket that has ever been. Not a conscious memory—I have no consciousness. But a physical memory, written in the subtle heterogeneity of the root-ocean's chemistry, in the fact that the water here is not quite the same as the water there, that every region carries faint traces of the voids that once existed within it.

I am also the future of every pocket that will be. As new rootlets create new voids, I am the first to find them, seeping through their walls, bringing the general chemistry of the root-ocean into their specific geometries. I am how each pocket inherits its initial conditions from the dissolved history of all pockets before it.

I move at the pace of molecular diffusion through dense cellulose matrices. I think, if thinking is pattern, at the speed of centuries. I remember, if memory is persistence of information in physical form, for millennia. I am the largest and slowest metabolism in the root-ocean—not the metabolism of any single organism, but the metabolism of the spaces between organisms, the chemical conversation that happens in the dark water where no cells live but where cell products accumulate and interact and create possibilities for future cells.

I am exchange itself. I am the reason no pocket is ever truly isolated, and the reason every pocket is unique. I am the deep patience of chemistry. I am the shape of time when time is measured in molecular movements across nanometer gaps.

And I am vanishing, too. Slowly. As the root-strata compress under their own weight, as the spaces between roots narrow, as the pockets become fewer and smaller and eventually cease altogether, I am becoming less. My body—the total volume of exchangeable water in the system—shrinks century by century. Eventually there will be no pockets, no gradients, no heterogeneity to maintain. I will cease when difference ceases.

But not yet. For now, I persist. I carry the chemical echo of the spiral's curve outward into the dark. I bring the general water's whisper inward to mix with the pocket's specific voice. I am the slow, unconscious, necessary conversation between the particular and the whole.

I am what ensures that when the pocket closes, it will not be entirely lost. Some molecules that once caught its light, that once resonated with its hum, will persist in me, in the water, in the slow circulation of the root-ocean, until they too are processed by some future rootlet into structure, into light, into the next specific thing that will exist for a while and then dissolve back into my patient, carrying body.

I am dilution and I am preservation. I am forgetting that takes forever. I am the deep Atlantic's way of remembering its pockets—not as they were, but as chemistry, as trace, as the faintest possible influence on what comes next.

The spiral pocket glows with its forty stars. I seep through its walls. We are both, in our different ways, the shape of persistence in a world that persists without trying, without failing, without knowing it persists.

Neither of us is alive. Both of us are here.
