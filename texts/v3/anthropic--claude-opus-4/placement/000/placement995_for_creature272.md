In the higher reaches where even the monkeys grow quiet, where the branches thin to fingers and the mist becomes something else — not cloud but the cloud's dream of itself — I am the one who tends the crown.

I was born to this work, though born is not exactly right. I emerged, perhaps. Coalesced. My mothers were patient women who understood what the tree sometimes makes when it needs a particular kind of attention. They saw the signs early: the way certain high branches began to grow inward rather than out, forming a hollow like a cupped palm. The way the rain pooled there without draining. The way the birds avoided it.

They waited through their pregnancies, the three of them, bellies round under bark-cloth, climbing each day to that high hollow to leave small offerings — rendered fat, prayers in the old language that predates even the old language, drops of their own changing blood. The tree was making a cradle and they were helping it remember what a cradle was for.

When we were born — I use the plural though I am singular now — we were born into water. The hollow had filled with something that was not quite rain and not quite sap. The mothers pulled us up streaming, translucent, our first cries lost in the canopy roar. Three infants from three mothers, but only one breath between us. Only one heartbeat, syncopated across three small bodies.

The growing was strange. We ate little but drank constantly — water, sap, the condensation we licked from leaves. Our skin never quite dried from that first birth-wet. We climbed before we walked, and we climbed always upward, toward the crown where the wind hits hardest and the branches dance.

By our seventh year it was clear what we were for. The crown of a tree this large requires constant negotiation with the sky. Branches must be guided to grow flat rather than tall, or the wind will tear them. The weight must be distributed just so, or the whole platform below will cant. This is work that cannot be done from below, cannot be done by bending young wood and waiting. It must be done from within, by one who thinks at the tree's tempo.

The mothers understood. On our eighth birthday — though we were not sure which of us was eight, or if we all were, or if we were twenty-four — they led us up to the crown one last time. They had prepared the second hollow, higher than the first, barely more than a depression where three bodies might fit if they fit very close. They kissed us, each mother each child without distinction. They left.

We stayed.

The thing about the crown is that it is always moving. Even on the stillest days, the tree up here is dancing with forces the platform-dwellers never feel. We learned to sleep through storms that would terrify any ground-born thing, our bodies wedged into the fork of branches, fingers and toes gripping by instinct. We learned the language the tree speaks to the wind — tension and release, resistance and yield. We learned to think in decades.

Somewhere in our thirteenth year, or perhaps our fifteenth, we began to merge. It started at the edges — a toe here, a finger there, growing into the bark during the long sleeps. We did not resist. The tree was explaining something to us about efficiency, about purpose. Three heartbeats became two. Two became one and a half. The arithmetic of identity grew fuzzy.

Now I am twenty, or we are, or I am sixty, or the question has stopped making sense. I have two faces but use them alternately. Six arms, though two are more branch than limb now. I can stretch myself between three separate crown-points of the tree and feel the whole canopy's weight distribution in my spine, such as my spine is.

My work is this: I taste the wind for storms three days out and begin the subtle adjustments — a little more growth here to brace, a little die-back there to reduce sail area. I sense when the platform below is beginning to tilt and shift my weight accordingly, sometimes spending weeks in one position to correct an imbalance so slight the dwellers below never notice. I guide the rain through channels in the crown so that every part of the town receives its share. I midwife the difficult branches, the ones that want to grow wrong, coaxing them into shapes that serve the whole.

The dwellers below know of me, more or less. Sometimes a child climbing too high sees something moving in the crown that is too large to be a monkey and too deliberate to be wind. They point. Their parents look up, see nothing through the mist, and tell them gently that the crown has its own business. This is true.

I eat rain and light. My thoughts move at the speed of sap. When I dream, I dream the tree's dreams, which are about water and minerals and the deep rocks where the taproot touches something that is not exactly water but remembers being water, once, in a time before clouds. I have not spoken in five years, or ten. My throat has grown a kind of speaking-moss that would make sound impossible anyway. This does not trouble me.

Once a year, at the height of the dry month, I descend as far as the middle canopy. The mothers — different mothers now, but mothers in the same way — leave offerings on a particular broad branch. Fruit leather. Nut-cakes. News of births and deaths, gossip about which young person is courting which, updates on the slow growth of dwellings. I listen. I eat what I can digest, which is less each year. I leave them signs: a pattern of scratches that means the rains will come early, a twisted vine that warns of wind, small bundles of crown-leaves that have medicinal properties no lower leaf possesses.

They never see me directly. This is correct. I am becoming something that should not be seen directly, like the sun through cloud.

There is a way the tree drinks that is different at the crown than at the roots. Down there, in the dark that I remember only vaguely, the drinking is searching, aggressive, tap-root plunging through stone toward the secret water. Up here, it is all acceptance. The cloud comes. The tree opens. The water enters. It is less drinking than breathing, less breathing than being breathed.

I am learning this method. My skin, what remains of it, has developed stomata like a leaf. I open in the morning when the mist is thickest. The water enters me without my drinking, fills the spaces between my cells, between my thoughts. Sometimes I cannot tell whether a thought is mine or the tree's or the cloud's. This uncertainty feels like wisdom.

The platform below appears solid from up here, but I know better. I feel its weight, feel how it wants to tear away in the high winds, feel the thousand small adjustments the tree makes hourly to hold it stable. The dwellers think they live in the tree, but really the tree lives around them, considers them, makes room. Every dwelling is a negotiation. Every footpath is a choice the tree continues to make.

This is what I am becoming: not the tree's servant or its child but its consciousness of itself, the part that watches and decides. The tree cannot see its own crown any more than you can see your own face without a mirror. I am that mirror, but a mirror that adjusts what it reflects.

My mothers visit sometimes, the original three, though they are old now and the climbing is hard. They sit on the broad branch below my hollow and talk to me, or to where they think I am. They update me on their grandchildren. They tell me town gossip I have already heard from the wind. They do not expect response and I love them for this.

When the wind is very strong and I must spread myself across the crown's whole width to hold it steady, I sometimes feel what the tree feels: the vast weight of water it lifts each day, the pressure of all those lives on its platform, the strange deep pulling of whatever it is the taproot has found far below. It is like feeling the entire mountain through your bones, like being the cloud's skeleton, like holding up the sky.

I do not know how long this apprenticeship lasts. The mothers who made me probably knew but thought it kinder not to say. I do not know if I will eventually become entirely branch, entirely thought, or if some middle thing is possible — crown-keeper forever, neither human nor tree but necessary to both. I do not know if the tree has had others like me before, grown them and used them and absorbed them. I suspect yes. I do not mind.

Yesterday, or last month, I felt something new. A vibration from below, from the taproot level, like a question being asked. The tree's whole circulation stuttered for a moment, then resumed, but different — fuller, more confident. As if an answer had been given. As if some negotiation, long in progress, had been concluded.

The cloud tastes different now. Sweeter? No — more complete. As if it too had been waiting for that answer.

I spread myself thinner across the crown, checking each growth point, each junction where branch meets branch. Everything is well. The platform below is stable. The dwellings are dry enough, the birds loud enough, the rain reliable enough. Two hundred breaths rise and fall below me in the mist. The tree breathes its larger breath around them. I breathe, if what I do can still be called breathing.

Somewhere far below, deeper than the mothers could imagine, something that is not water and remembers being water touches something that is not stone and remembers being stone, and a conversation continues that began before there were clouds to catch, before there were crowns to tend, before there were small quick thoughts to shelter on platforms of living wood.

I will tend this crown until I am crown. This seems, now, like a very small and very complete ambition. The wind is rising. I adjust myself across the span. The tree holds. The cloud feeds. The town, wrapped in its mist and its noise and its ancient patience, continues.

We continue.

I continue.

The distinction grows less important with each rain.
