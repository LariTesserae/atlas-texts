In the highest of the keeper towers, where the walls thin to mere suggestions between inside and out, I tend my colony. The bat-birds know me by more than scent now—after forty years, I am written into their magnetic memories, another fixture in their mental map of home. My bones have hollowed like theirs, adapted to the altitude. When I play the calling songs at dawn, I feel the vibrations in my sternum, my whole chest a resonating chamber.

I was not born to this. My parents were pipe-workers, solid people of the lower terraces who understood water's weight and urgency. But at seven, I heard something in the keeper's flute that others couldn't—not just the audible notes but the ultrasonic harmonics that made the air shimmer. The old keeper, Ystara, saw me tilting my head like the bat-birds do, tracking sounds that shouldn't exist for human ears. She claimed me for apprenticeship that same day.

The tower is more roost than residence. Every surface bears the chalky streaks of generations of inhabitants. I've learned to read these marks like scripture—this corner favored by the matriarchs, that ledge where the juveniles practice their gliding. The smell no longer registers; I am saturated with it, preserved in midge-oil and guano until I've become an extension of the colony itself.

My charges cluster now in the pre-dawn darkness, their bodies generating such heat that the air above them wavers. Six hundred and seventeen souls—I know each by the pattern of their wing-beats, the particular pitch of their hunting cries. The pregnant females hang lower, their bodies swollen with twins. Always twins, always one male, one female. We don't know why.

I prepare for the morning flight. My flute is carved from the femur of my predecessor, following tradition. Each keeper adds their own harmonics to the instrument—I've worn new finger-holes that let me reach frequencies only the youngest hear. The bone has darkened with use and the oils from my hands, taking on the same iridescent sheen as my skin.

As the first grey light filters through the mist, I begin the aubade. The notes I play exist in two worlds—the human melody that echoes off stone, and the ultrasonic thread that speaks directly to the creatures' navigation centers. I am singing them a map of safe return, updating yesterday's song with subtle variations that account for the new construction on the fourth terrace, the shifted wind patterns after last week's avalanche.

They spiral up from their roosts in a living tornado, their wings beating in synchronization that creates its own weather—a momentary clearing in the perpetual mist. For just an instant, I can see the mountain's true face, terrible and beautiful, before the cloud closes again. The colony streams out into the grey morning, their echolocation clicks creating an acoustic constellation that I perceive as flashes of silver behind my eyes.

Alone now except for the invalids—three ancients too old to fly and one juvenile with a torn wing membrane—I perform the morning maintenance. Fresh midge-paste in the feeding troughs. The cistern that catches their droppings needs emptying; the guild of farmers will come for it later, this phosphorous-rich material precious for the mushroom caves. I check the heated pipes that keep the roost from freezing, feeling with practiced hands for the telltale cold spots that signal a blockage below.

The torn-wing juvenile watches me with eyes like black pearls. I've been teaching it a different kind of flight—using its voice to scout beyond its body. It has taken to the lessons with unsettling aptitude, already echo-mapping the entire tower, "seeing" through five floors of stone. Sometimes I catch it listening downward, tracking something in the mountain's depths that I cannot perceive.

By mid-morning, other keepers' colonies pass overhead, and I read their flight patterns for news. Seylas's group circles twice—warning of bad weather from the north. The adolescents from Koric's tower are flying loose and wild; he must be ill again. I make a note to send my apprentice with medicine.

The apprentice arrives as I'm mixing the evening feed—a girl of twelve who hears even better than I did at her age. She claims she can distinguish individual voices in the colony's collective song. I believe her. Each generation grows more attuned, as if the city itself is teaching us to become more like our charges. Her parents, wind-readers on the exposed ramparts, have already developed the angular features and hollow bones of their trade. We are all becoming what we tend.

The day passes in small rituals. Repairing the roost boxes where frost has split the wood. Training the juvenile to navigate by sound alone—I blindfold it and guide it with clicks, teaching it that the world exists as much in echoes as in light. My apprentice practices her scales, finding the frequencies that make the ancients lift their heads in recognition.

As evening approaches, I climb to the tower's apex. From here, I can see the other keeper towers rising from the mist like standing stones. We begin the vespers in sequence, each colony's song timed to create a round that spirals through the city. My part comes, and I pour my breath into the bone flute, calling my family home.

They return as they left, in a spiral of wings and heat, but now heavy with feeding. The pregnant females land first, claiming the warmest spots. The males jostle for position, their hierarchy expressed in barely perceptible shifts of wingspan and posture. They bring the smell of the upper air with them—ozone and ice crystals and something else, something that belongs to heights where even mist cannot climb.

As they settle, grooming and gossiping in their ultrasonic voices, I count. Six hundred and seventeen, all home safe. The torn-wing juvenile creeps close to the returners, listening to their echolocation memories, learning the landscape it cannot yet explore. One of the ancients dies in the night—I feel it happen, a subtle shift in the colony's breathing. By morning, the others will have pushed the body to the tower's edge, where the stone salamanders wait to return it to the cycle.

I record the death in the keeper's log, a book so old its pages have taken on the texture of skin. Each keeper adds their observations—migration patterns, breeding seasons, strange behaviors, the slow changes that accumulate over generations. Reading back through centuries of entries, I can trace how we've changed together, keeper and colony, until the distinction blurs.

Before sleep, I play one last song—not a calling, but a lullaby that exists entirely in the range beyond human hearing. It speaks of warm currents and thick clouds of midges, of safe roosts and certain return. The colony settles deeper into their clustered warmth, their breathing synchronizing into a single, massive lung.

I wrap myself in waxed wool and find my own roost, a hammock strung between beams stained white with generations of use. Through the stone, I can hear the city's midnight sounds—water finding its patient way down, the thermal pipes groaning with expansion, the other keepers playing their colonies to sleep. And beneath it all, something deeper: the mountain itself, breathing out its cold, persistent life.

Tomorrow I will wake before dawn and call them to the hunt again. Tomorrow the torn-wing juvenile might attempt its first halting flight. Tomorrow my apprentice will find a new harmonic that speaks of heights I can no longer imagine. But tonight, I am simply another creature in the roost, generating my small warmth against the cold, dreaming in frequencies that have no names.
