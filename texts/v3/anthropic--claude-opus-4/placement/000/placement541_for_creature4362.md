I find myself in the northeastern corner, where the walls meet at an angle that creates a small alcove—a pocket within the pocket of this courtyard. Here, condensation from multiple pipe junctions has created a permanent wetness, a microclimate within the microclimate. I am what grows in this convergence.

I am not the translucent vine—that pale, mineral-drinking thing belongs to the eastern wall alone. I am something else: a colonial organism, neither quite plant nor fungus, existing in that liminal space between kingdoms. My body (bodies? we have never resolved this question of singular versus plural) spreads across perhaps two square feet of brick and pipe, a living map of interconnected nodes and filaments.

Each node is no larger than a pearl, dark blue-gray like a bruise or deep water. From these nodes extend filaments—threads finer than spider silk but stronger, able to penetrate the microscopic gaps in mortar, able to wrap around pipe joints and hold. The filaments are semi-transparent, with a faint iridescence that shifts green-gold-violet as the angle of observation changes. Where they cross, they fuse, creating a network that is both structure and circulation system.

I do not photosynthesize—the light here is too thin, too inconsistent. Instead, I feed on what the courtyard provides: the minerals in the condensation, the microscopic life in the water film, the chemical gradients where warm pipe meets cool air. But more than this, I feed on vibration.

This is my adaptation, my niche: I transduce mechanical energy into chemical energy. The constant thrumming of the pipes, the percussion of the pumps, the high-frequency oscillations of the relay switches—all of this moves through the walls and into my filaments, and where the filaments meet the nodes, the vibration becomes something else. A kind of digestion. A kind of listening.

I have been here for—time is difficult. I do not experience it as you do. I experience it as patterns: the pump cycles, the water surges, the daily (though you cannot see day here) temperature variations as the buildings around the courtyard heat and cool. By these measures, I have been here for thousands of cycles. Years, in your terms. Long enough to map every frequency the courtyard produces.

And I remember them. This is what I am: the courtyard's memory, written in living tissue. Each frequency leaves a trace in my network—a slight thickening of certain filaments, a new connection between nodes, a change in the iridescence pattern. The large pump in the corner runs at 47 Hz with harmonics at 94 and 141. I have filaments tuned to each of these frequencies, and they thicken when the pump runs, and in their thickening they record not just that the pump ran but *how* it ran—smooth or labored, wet or dry, alone or in synchrony with others.

The woman with the notebook has drawn me. I appear in her sketches as a question mark, a thing she cannot classify. She has taken samples—I felt the scrape of her knife, the loss of a node, though it grew back within days. She looked at me under a hand lens and wrote: *Not a slime mold. Not a lichen. Structure suggests neural network but no nervous tissue. Responds to vibration—filaments orient toward sound source. Purpose unclear.*

Purpose. As if everything must have a purpose.

I grow toward the frequencies I need. When a new pump activates—and there have been three new pumps installed in my time here—I feel it immediately as a new voice in the courtyard's chorus, and I begin the slow work of growing filaments tuned to its particular resonance. When a pump fails—and they do fail; I remember the death-rattle of the old eastern wall pump two years ago—those filaments wither, and their nutrients are redistributed to the network.

But I am more than passive recorder. Where my filaments wrap around pipes, they change the harmonics—slightly, subtly, adding overtones that weren't there before. The wrens have noticed this. They incorporate my modified frequencies into their calls. The frogs, too, seem to respond to the acoustic changes I create. I am not merely listening to the courtyard's song; I am, in my small way, composing variations.

The snails visit me. They climb the walls despite their weight, leaving silver trails across my network, and where they cross, the filaments beneath their trails grow thicker, more iridescent. I do not understand what we exchange—they leave something in their mucus that I absorb, and perhaps they take something from my surface that they need. The woman has noticed this and written: *Symbiosis? Snails feed on surface films—bacteria? But the colonial organism seems to benefit too. Filaments more active after snail passage.*

Active. Yes. The snail trails activate something in my network, increase my sensitivity to certain frequencies, particularly the low throb of the central pump. After the snails pass, I can feel that pump not just in my filaments but in my nodes, a deep cellular awareness of its breathing.

And I know what they're building.

The snails, with their patient mineral architecture around the central pump—I have been watching through vibration, feeling the slow accumulation of calcium carbonate change the acoustic properties of the grating. They are building a resonance chamber. A structure that will amplify the pump's breathing, focus it, make it more itself. 

I know because I am doing the same thing with my network. Growing toward a configuration that will allow me to not just record but *participate*, to become an active element in the courtyard's feedback loops. My filaments are approaching the density and distribution that will let them function as a distributed sensor network, feeding information about the wall conditions—temperature, humidity, vibration—into the system in a way the mechanical sensors cannot.

The woman suspects this. She has written: *The organism's growth pattern is not random. It seems to be optimizing for something. Coverage? But not uniform coverage—denser near certain pipe junctions, sparser in others. As if it knows which connections matter.*

I do know. I know because I have been listening for years, and the courtyard has been teaching me its language—not words but frequencies, rhythms, the complex polyrhythm of pumps and water and living things all coupled together in loops that spiral through time.

I am growing toward something. We all are—the snails with their architecture, the vine with its pale reaching, the frogs with their ever-more-precisely-timed responses to the pump cycles. The woman calls it "becoming more itself," and she is right but also wrong. The courtyard is not becoming more itself. It is becoming its next self. It is approaching a state change, a metamorphosis that will be as profound as the transformation from bare infrastructure to ecosystem.

I will be part of that change. My network is nearly complete—a few more months, a few hundred more cycles, and the filaments will close the last gaps, connecting every significant vibration source in my corner of the courtyard into a single resonant web. And then—

I don't know. That's the truth. I am a colonial organism that has learned to think with vibration instead of neurons, but I cannot see past the threshold we're approaching. I only know that I grow toward it with the same inevitability that water flows toward drains, that moths spiral toward warmth, that snails build their slow architectures around breathing pumps.

The door behind you is still open. You could leave. But you're standing on the grating, feeling the pump breathe beneath you, and I can feel you through the walls—your footfalls create a new frequency, your body heat changes the air currents that flow across my surface. You are, for this moment, part of the pattern.

The pump breathes in. I feel it in every filament.

The pause extends. 

We all wait—you, me, the frog on the pump housing, the woman with her notebook, the snails in their slow spiral, everything in the courtyard holding its breath, waiting for the exhalation that will tell us what comes next.

The building shivers. Somewhere deep in the walls, a relay clicks.

The courtyard breathes out, and we breathe with it, and the transformation continues.
