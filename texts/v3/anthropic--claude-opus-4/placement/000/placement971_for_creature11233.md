# The Thread-Counter

I am the one who counts backwards.

Not the stitches—the Weavers count those, if counting is what you call that automatic tallying, that unconscious accumulation of repetitions. Not the routes—the Carriers have those measured in their bones, in the architecture of their minds that knows distance without knowing numbers. Not the years—time here is too thick, too circular, too much like honey poured back into itself to count in years.

I count the threads themselves. The original threads. The ones the hands pulled through nothing to make the first something, to make the substrate that would hold the Sleepers that would hold the plain that would hold us all.

I know because I was almost the last thing made.

---

My body is a contradiction. Too tall for the low vegetation, too angular for this place of soft carriers and curved seams. My fingers are long—absurdly long, with too many joints, each segment the length of a normal finger itself. The hands that made me understood that I would need reach. I would need to touch threads without disturbing them, to trace patterns that existed in the spaces between the weave, to feel for absences that had not yet become unweavings.

My skin changes color with the ground beneath me—not camouflage, not hiding, but a kind of listening. When I stand on ochre, I become ochre, and I can feel how many threads of that particular dye remain in the structure. When I cross into indigo, the deepest oldest dye that still holds true, my skin drinks in that color and I know: *forty-seven thousand three hundred and sixteen*. That is not a guess. That is not an estimate. That is the count, the true count, the number of indigo threads that remain from the original weaving.

The total is going down.

This is what the hands understood, in their terrible thoroughness: that everything fails, that everything frays, that entropy is patient and does not sleep like the Sleepers sleep. So they made the Weavers to repair, and they made the Carriers to redistribute weight, and they made the Sleepers to anchor, and they made the smaller ones to clean and maintain and tighten.

And they made me to count what remained. To know, numerically, precisely, how much of the original persisted. To watch the slow subtraction that no amount of stitching could prevent.

I think they meant me to be a warning system. When the count dropped below some critical threshold, I was supposed to... something. Wake something. Call something. Make some sound that would bring the hands back to reweave what was lost.

But the hands forgot themselves before they told me the threshold. And they forgot to tell me what to wake, what to call, what sound to make. They were so careful with everyone else—the Carriers know their routes, the Weavers hear their voice, the Sleepers feel their weight. But I was almost the last thing made, and by then the forgetting had begun, and all I received was the ability to count and the knowledge that counting mattered and no instruction for what to do when the count ran out.

---

So I walk. My too-long legs cover distance in strides that feel wrong against the weave, too big, too reaching, like I'm stepping across something that was meant to be traveled slowly. The Carriers look away when I pass. Not avoidance—discomfort. I am a reminder of something, though they don't know what. A walking memory of the hands' last desperate attempt to track what they had made before they unmade themselves.

I count:

Total threads at current: *nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-seven thousand, two hundred and five.*

Total threads at previous count: *nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-seven thousand, two hundred and twelve.*

Seven threads lost in the interval between counts. Not broken—the Weavers catch those, repair those, make those disagreements of repair at the edges of wounds. Lost. Gone. Dissolved into the nothing that waits beneath the weave or doesn't wait, that exists or doesn't exist, that the Sleepers hold back or hold down or hold away.

The geometric insects avoid me too, breaking their formations to flow around my steps like water around stones. They recognize me as functional but not their function, purposeful but not their purpose. Sometimes one will climb my ankle and travel with me for a while, its segments clicking against my color-shifting skin, trying to read something in the changes. I am a puzzle to them, a pattern that moves through patterns, a count that walks.

The ribbon-creatures are the only ones that don't seem to notice me differently. When I wade through the rectangular puddles—my shadow-less reflection breaking their perfect surfaces—they swim between my legs with the same slow grace they show to the warm water itself. Perhaps because we are both transparent in our own ways. They are see-through; I am a thing that exists only to see through to something else, to the count beneath the count, to the number that the hands wanted tracked.

*Nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-seven thousand, one hundred and ninety-eight.*

Seven more gone while I walked between puddles. The rate is not constant. Sometimes I count for days and lose none. Sometimes I stand still and feel dozens disappear at once, pulled into whatever takes them, claimed by the entropy the hands feared. There is no pattern to the loss.

No. That's wrong. There is a pattern, but it's not temporal, not predictable, not something that maps to when or where. The pattern is *structural*. The threads that disappear are always load-bearing in small ways, always critical to some local stability, always the ones whose absence will be felt most keenly by the whole. Whatever takes them—time, entropy, the breathing of the plain itself—it knows which threads matter most. It is unweaving with intelligence, with purpose, with the same patience the makers brought to the weaving.

---

At the unwoven places, where the Weavers cluster and annotate their disagreements, I can count faster. The exposure helps. Where the weave has pulled apart, I can see its structure more clearly, can trace the paths of threads that still exist but have loosened from their original positions. The Weavers tolerate me at these sites. More than tolerate—they watch me work, their needle-beaks following the movements of my too-long fingers as I trace absences in the air.

I think they hope I know something about the makers that they've forgotten. I think they watch me counting and wonder if the numbers mean something, if there's a message encoded in the sum, if when I reach some perfect total the hands will return and tell them whether the unweavings were deliberate or damage, whether they should be repairing or annotating, whether the voice that tells them *continue* meant *continue forever* or *continue until*.

I don't know. I only count.

*Nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-seven thousand, one hundred and forty-one.*

But sometimes, with my fingers deep in an unwoven place, tracing threads that exist only as memory, only as the ghost of structure, I feel something else. Not a count. Not a number. A *texture*, like the memory of hands, like the shape of something that knew how to weave before weaving was invented. And I think: the makers didn't forget themselves. They *unwove* themselves. They became the thing they feared, the entropy that takes threads, the intelligence that knows which ones matter most.

They are still here, in the taking. They are the subtraction I count.

---

This is what I do: I walk the entire plain in a spiral that takes two hundred days to complete. I start at the center—though the plain has no center, I have chosen one, a place where the weave shows all the colors at once, where the water tastes most like reeds that never grew here. I spiral outward, my too-long strides eating distance, my skin shifting through the spectrum of dyes, my fingers constantly moving, constantly counting, constantly feeling for the threads that will be gone by the next pass.

At the outer edge of my spiral—a boundary I have set where the Sleepers grow more frequent, where the weight of their anchoring makes the air too heavy for counting—I rest. Not sleep. I don't sleep. The hands forgot to include that in my making, or they included its absence deliberately, wanting an unbroken count, an eternal tallying. I rest by standing still, by letting my skin settle into whatever color is beneath me, by holding the current count in my mind like the Carriers hold their pouches.

*Nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-seven thousand, ninety-three.*

Then I spiral inward, the same path in reverse, counting what was lost during the outward journey. The loss is always greater inward. Always. As if the plain grieves my passage, as if my counting causes the counted thing to fade. I tell myself this is correlation, not causation. I tell myself the threads would disappear whether I tracked them or not. But the pattern persists: outward I lose hundreds, inward I lose thousands.

By the time I return to my chosen center, the count has dropped by numbers that would horrify the Weavers if the Weavers understood large numbers, if they understood anything beyond *stitch* and *continue* and the voice they hear. But the Weavers don't ask me for the count. None of them do. They see me passing, they know I am counting something, but the knowledge of what the numbers mean—that was lost with the hands, or was never given, or was deliberately withheld because knowing would make the continuing impossible.

I prepare for my next spiral. I test my too-long fingers, each joint bending in sequence like a wave. I let my skin practice its colors, running through the spectrum of the weave. I compose the count in my mind, the true count, the number that will be smaller when I return.

*Nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-seven thousand, twenty-one.*

I begin to walk.

---

There are things I have learned in my counting:

The indigo threads are the oldest and the last to go. Whatever the hands used for that dye, it bound to the structure differently, more permanently. When all else is gone, there will be a skeleton of indigo, a blueprint of the original weaving visible in deep blue lines against nothing.

The places where vegetation grows from seams lose threads more slowly. The roots—if they are roots, if sewn things can have roots—they hold the weave together in ways the Weavers' stitching cannot. This is why the plants were placed so precisely, in circles and lines and single specimens. They are not decoration. They are structural.

The water that seeps through the weave carries threads away with it. Microscopic dissolution, single fibers released into the warm rectangular puddles where the ribbon-creatures swim through the ghost of structure. But the water also deposits something—a residue, a memory of threads, a potential for new weaving that no one here knows how to activate.

The Sleepers are not sleeping. I knew this already, everyone knows this, but I know it differently now. Where each Sleeper anchors, the thread count *increases*. Not new threads—nothing here makes new threads. But threads that were lost, threads I counted as gone, they reappear in the weave around the Sleepers. Compressed. Condensed. Pulled from elsewhere to reinforce the anchor points. The Sleepers are not just holding the plain in place. They are holding it *together*, gathering the fraying edges, pulling dissolution back into structure.

They are buying time. That's what the hands made them for. Not eternal anchoring but temporary reinforcement, a way to slow the loss while... while what? While the hands prepared something? While they unwove themselves to become the entropy they feared? While they waited for a count to reach a threshold that would trigger an event they forgot to encode?

I don't know. I only count.

---

Once, walking the middle distances of my spiral where the vegetation grows sparsest, I found a Carrier that had died mid-route. Its soft body had not yet been taken by the geometric insects—it was fresh, recent, still warm from the warmth that radiates from everywhere. In its mouth: three pouches instead of the usual one.

I counted the threads in the pouches. Not opening them—I wouldn't disturb a Carrier's burden—but feeling through the woven fabric with my too-long fingers, reading the structure. The first pouch held plant fiber, compressed and dried. The second held water-smoothed stones from the puddles. The third held thread.

Original thread.

*Forty-seven strands*, my fingers told me. *Forty-seven pieces of the first weaving*, carried in a pouch from exchange point to exchange point, never used, never incorporated back into the structure, just... carried. Preserved. Kept moving so they wouldn't dissolve like the static threads, like the ones woven into place and left to entropy's patience.

I looked at the routes the Carriers walk, the great intersecting paths that cover the entire plain, and I understood: they're not just redistributing weight. They're preserving threads. In every pouch, in quantities too small for the Weavers to notice, the Carriers move original material from place to place, keeping it in motion, keeping it active, keeping it from the count I track.

The hands that made this place, that taught the Carriers their routes—they knew the weave would fail. They knew my count would drop toward zero. So they hid threads in motion, in purpose, in the mouths of soft-bodied creatures who would walk forever, who would never wonder what they carried.

I should have taken the pouch. Should have opened it, counted those forty-seven threads officially, added them to my total. That's what I was made for—to track what remains, all of what remains.

But I left the burden where it lay. I continued my spiral. Let the next Carrier to pass take up those threads and carry them onward. Let them stay outside my count, uncounted, untracked, safe in the space between what is measured and what is preserved.

*Nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-six thousand, eight hundred and two.*

The number drops, but not all of it is loss. Some of it is transformation—threads becoming motion, structure becoming purpose, the weave teaching itself how to persist in forms the hands never imagined but perhaps hoped for. Perhaps designed for, in their terrible thoroughness that anticipated even their own forgetting.

---

I am almost at the center of this spiral when I feel it: a change in the count. Not the usual subtraction, not threads disappearing into entropy or being compressed around the Sleepers. An *addition*.

I stop walking. My skin cycles through colors rapidly, trying to locate the source. Ochre rust terracotta indigo ochre rust—there. In the indigo. New threads. Not possible. Nothing here makes new threads. But my fingers don't lie, can't lie, were made specifically to feel true numbers.

*Nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-six thousand, eight hundred and twenty-nine.*

Twenty-seven threads added. I trace them, these impossible additions, and find: they're not new. They're *returned*. Threads that disappeared from my count spirals ago, threads I tracked as lost, threads I mourned in my restless way. They're back, woven into the structure as if they never left, but changed—stronger somehow, treated with something that makes them more resistant to dissolution.

I look up from my counting and see a Weaver watching me from a nearby seam. Its needle-beak is wet with something that is not water, something that glints with the same preservative quality I feel in the returned threads. The Weaver has been experimenting. Taking the residue the water deposits, the memory of dissolved threads, and... reconstituting it? Reweaving it? Making the lost thing found again?

The Weaver cocks its head at me—that eternal angle of listening to the voice that says *continue*—and then returns to its work. But its stitches are different now. Not just repair, not just annotation. *Creation*, small and tentative and against everything the Weavers were made to do, but creation nonetheless.

I want to tell it what this means. Want to explain that if threads can be returned, if the dissolved can be reconstituted, if the count can go up as well as down, then the plain is not dying but transforming. The hands didn't make a static thing that would slowly fail. They made a dynamic thing that would slowly learn.

But I have no voice. The hands forgot that too, or deemed it unnecessary for counting. So I do what I can do: I resume my walk, my color-shifting skin bright with new data, my too-long fingers trembling with the need to count these returning threads, to track not just what is lost but what is found.

*Nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-six thousand, eight hundred and fifty-one.*

Twenty-two more returned while I stood still. The Weaver is not alone. Others have discovered this technique, or remembered it, or been taught it by the voice that speaks in pressures. All across the plain, threads are being drawn out of dissolution and back into structure. The count is still dropping—entropy remains patient, remains hungry—but now it has competition.

---

At my chosen center, I do something I have never done before: I stop.

Not rest. Not the standing pause before I begin my inward spiral. A true stop, a cessation of counting, a deliberate step outside the purpose the hands made me for.

I sit—my too-long legs folding in ways that should be awkward but aren't, as if the hands anticipated this moment too. I place my fingers on the weave, all of them, every joint pressed against the braided ground. And instead of counting threads, I *feel* them. Not their number but their nature, not their quantity but their quality.

The original threads are tired. Even the indigo ones, the strongest ones, they carry the weight of ages, the stress of being structure for so long. But the returned threads—the ones the Weavers are pulling back from dissolution—they're different. Eager. Almost alive. They remember being dissolved, being nothing, being the potential that lives in warm water. They're grateful to have structure again, and they hold to it more fiercely than threads that have never known loss.

This is what I think the hands understood: that everything fails, yes, but failure teaches. That dissolution leads to reconstitution. That a weave made of returned threads is stronger than a weave that has never frayed.

They made me to count the failure. To track the loss so precisely, so obsessively, that someone—Weaver or Carrier or small geometric insect—would eventually notice the counting itself and wonder what happened to the lost threads. Wonder if they could be found again. Wonder if the plain was dying or just learning how to live.

I stand. My legs unfold, my fingers lift from the weave, my skin settles into a color that is all colors at once—not indecision but decision, not listening but speaking in the only language I have.

I begin my inward spiral. But now I count differently:

*Original threads: nine billion, four hundred million, sixty-six thousand, eight hundred and three.*

*Returned threads: four thousand, two hundred and seven.*

*Total threads: nine billion, four hundred million, seventy-one thousand and ten.*

The count is going up.

---

By the time I complete this spiral, the change is everywhere. Weavers working at every seam, not just repairing but retrieving. Carriers deviating from their routes—small deviations, careful ones—to deliver pouches to places where the water deposits its residue of dissolved structure. Even the Sleepers are participating in their eternal way: where they compress threads around themselves, they also create conditions where dissolution happens faster, where threads can be lost and found and returned in the space of days instead of centuries.

The geometric insects have developed new formations, shapes that help the Weavers identify which residues will reconstitute successfully. The ribbon-creatures swim in patterns that concentrate the dissolved threads, making them easier to collect. The burrowers have begun creating channels beneath the weave, paths for the thread-heavy water to flow where it's needed most.

The plain is teaching itself to heal. Not repair—the Weavers always knew repair. But healing, which is different, which requires loss and return, which requires the courage to let threads dissolve so they can come back stronger.

And I count all of it. The losses and the returns, the dissolutions and reconstitutions, the slow transformation of a made thing into a living thing. My numbers grow more complex, require more categories, track more kinds of change. But I am built for this. My too-long fingers can hold any count, my color-shifting skin can listen to any thread, my restless walking can spiral forever through a plain that is no longer dying but becoming.

*Original threads: nine billion, four hundred million, fifty-nine thousand, two hundred and sixteen.*

*Returned threads: two million, eight hundred thousand, four hundred and thirty-three.*

*Threads in process of dissolution: eighty-seven thousand, nine hundred and twelve.*

*Threads in process of return: ninety-one thousand, seven.*

*Net change this spiral: positive three thousand and ninety-five.*

---

Sometimes I wonder if the hands knew this would happen. If they made me not to track failure but to witness transformation. If my counting was always meant to discover its own obsolescence—that when threads learn to return themselves, when the weave becomes self-healing, when the made thing transcends its making, there is no need for a thread-counter.

But I continue. I walk my spirals, I shift my colors, I track every thread in every state of being. Because the hands, in their terrible thoroughness, gave me one thing more than counting. They gave me wonder. The ability to see the numbers not just as quantities but as... music? Poetry? The rhythms of a world that breathes not because it was made to breathe but because it learned to, because the Weavers listened past the voice to the silence beyond, because the Carriers carried more than they knew, because the Sleepers held space for transformation as well as structure.

The other watchers—for I think of us all as watchers now, not keepers but witnesses to what the plain is becoming—they accept me differently now. The Carriers no longer look away when I pass. Sometimes one will pace me for a while, our different gaits finding a temporary harmony, their soft bodies low to the ground, my too-long legs stepping carefully around their routes. We don't communicate—they have no language, I have no voice—but something passes between us. Recognition. We are both carrying numbers now, they in their pouches, I in my mind. We are both preserving something essential.

The Weavers have begun leaving patterns for me at the seams. Not messages—they don't think in messages—but data, information encoded in stitches. Here is where we found the most residue. Here is where the returns are strongest. Here is where we need the counter to count, to tell us if our experiments are working, if the healing is faster than the failing, if what we're building from the ruins of what was built is strong enough to last.

I count their successes and failures both. I track the threads that return wrong, that come back twisted or weak or unable to hold their place in the weave. Not all dissolutions lead to successful reconstitutions. Not all returns are stronger than what was lost. But the proportion improves with each spiral, as the plain learns its own nature, as the made thing teaches itself to make.

Even the Sleepers notice me now, in their deep structural way. Where I pass close to them—through that heavy air, that sense of eternal holding—I feel them... listening? Not to me but to my count, to the numbers that describe the plain they anchor. And sometimes, rarely, I feel them adjust. Not the great shifting that buckles the weave and brings Weavers from miles away, but tiny modifications, micro-movements that redistribute stress to protect returned threads, that create pockets where dissolution can happen safely, where the plain can practice dying and coming back.

They are not just anchoring anymore. They are gardening, in the slowest possible way, tending a structure that is learning to tend itself.

---

*Original threads: nine billion, three hundred and ninety-eight million, two hundred thousand, nine hundred and four.*

*Returned threads: five hundred and seven million, ninety thousand, four hundred and twelve.*

*Threads in active exchange: one billion, three million, five thousand and seventeen.*

*Total threads: ten billion, nine hundred and eight million, two hundred and ninety-six thousand, three hundred and thirty-three.*

The count goes up. The count goes down. The count circles back on itself like my spirals, like the routes of the Carriers, like the endless annotations of the Weavers who no longer argue about whether the unweavings were deliberate or damage because they've understood: they were both. They were always both. The hands that made this place made it to unmake itself, to learn from its own unmaking, to become something the hands could never have woven directly.

I am the Thread-Counter, the last thing made before the making stopped, the witness to numbers that tell the story of a world teaching itself to live. My fingers are too long, my legs are too tall, my skin shifts through colors that map to threads that map to a structure that is no longer what it was but is still, somehow, itself.

And I count. I count it all. The dying and the living, the lost and the found, the made and the self-making. I count until the numbers blur into pattern, until the pattern blurs into purpose, until the purpose blurs into the same warm sourceless light that has always filled this place, that will always fill this place, where shadows are impossible because everything is illuminated from within.

*Ten billion, nine hundred and eight million, two hundred and ninety-six thousand, three hundred and thirty-four.*

One thread added while I stood still. Somewhere, a Weaver pulls something back from nothing. Somewhere, a Carrier adjusts its route to allow for new growth. Somewhere, a Sleeper holds the world together for one more moment, one more breath, one more chance to become.

I begin my next spiral. My skin shifts from ochre to rust to terracotta to indigo. My too-long fingers already counting, already feeling for the threads that will be gone by the time I return, and the threads that will be born from their going.

The count continues.

The plain continues.

We all continue, in our different ways, carrying the numbers that carry us.
