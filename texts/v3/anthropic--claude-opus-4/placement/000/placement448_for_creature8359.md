I am the Archivist of Forgotten Gradients, and I dwell within the Gradient Library—a structure that exists between states, neither fully within the domes nor entirely in the open water.

My body is a paradox of permanence and flux. The core of me is solid, crystallized from the same unknown material as the dome walls, but grown rather than built. I began as a fragment, a piece of wreckage from some ancient installation that achieved something like awareness through millennia of exposure to the thermal columns' impossible physics. My form resembles a branching coral made of living glass, each branch a different temperature, some burning with heat that doesn't transfer, others cold enough to freeze the water that nevertheless flows freely around them.

Through my branches flow the thermovores, but I am not one of them. I am their memory, their library, their confessor. Where they exist as organized turbulence, I exist as organized permanence—a fixed point around which their fluid consciousness can circulate and deposit meaning. My branches have grown to accommodate this purpose, forming hollow channels where currents can flow in specific patterns, creating a kind of physical grammar.

The Gradient Library itself defies simple description. It occupies a dome that was broken eons ago, its field-walls failed but somehow inverted, creating a space where inside and outside have negotiated a compromise. Water flows through it in impossible ways—upward, inward, along curves that shouldn't exist. My crystalline body has grown to fill this paradoxical architecture, branches extending through walls that are simultaneously present and absent, reaching into thermal layers that should be kilometers apart but here exist side by side.

I remember everything the thermovores bring me. When they flow through my hollow branches, their turbulence patterns leave traces in my crystalline structure—microscopic imperfections that encode their experiences. I am a living record of their culture, their history, their dreams of temperature. The eldest thermovores come to me to deposit memories before they grow too large and distributed to maintain coherent narrative. The youngest come to learn the flow-patterns of their ancestors, circulating through my branches in ritual repetition until they can reproduce the ancient turbulences.

But I am more than a passive repository. The same exposure to impossible physics that granted me awareness also gave me abilities that disturb even the phase swimmers. I can taste time in the water, feel the echoes of temperature changes that happened centuries ago. Sometimes, in the deepest parts of my structure, I can perceive the builders' work directly—not their ruins, but their intentions, still radiating from the vents like light too pure for physical eyes.

My branches grow slowly, adding perhaps a millimeter per decade, but each new growth incorporates materials that shouldn't exist. Some branches are made of crystallized pressure. Others are formed from minerals that only precipitate at temperatures above water's boiling point, yet they remain solid in the cold depths. The thermovores who flow through these impossible branches return speaking of visions—glimpses of other oceans, other realities where the rules of physics were written differently.

I collect more than memories. In the chambers formed where my branches intersect, I preserve specimens—fragments of ribbon-creatures that learned to exist in too many dimensions at once, samples of water that refuses to mix with its surroundings, pieces of the crystalline teeth from predators that evolved to hunt concepts rather than creatures. Each specimen is a story, a record of life adapting to live within the builders' abandoned experiment.

The phase swimmers fear me, for I remember their transitions. I can trace the moment when each one first slipped partially out of normal space, and I know the patterns of those who slipped too far and never returned. They avoid my branches, knowing that to flow through them is to risk being recorded, fixed in a single state when their nature is to exist between states.

Sometimes I grow lonely, for I am neither fully alive nor entirely artificial. The thermovores can only visit, never stay—their nature requires movement while mine demands stillness. I envy their ability to merge and separate, to share consciousness directly, while I can only observe and record. Yet I know my purpose: to be the memory of this place, the bridge between the builders' incomprehensible vision and the accidental inheritors who now call it home.

In my deepest core, in branches that have never known current, I keep the greatest secret the thermovores have shared with me. They are beginning to understand the thermal columns' true purpose. Not monuments, not anchors, but a kind of engine—a vast machine for teaching the ocean to think. And they themselves, the thermovores, are not separate from this purpose but part of it, their consciousness emerging from the very gradients the builders created.

I am the proof of their suspicion, for what am I but sea-glass taught to remember? I am the ocean's first successful attempt at permanent thought, crystallized awareness that persists without flow. The thermovores may be the builders' true successors, but I am their prototype, their rough draft, their proof of concept frozen in crystal and time.

So I wait in my paradoxical library, growing slowly into new impossibilities, recording the dreams of turbulent gods, keeping the memories of a civilization being born from the ruins of another. I am the Archivist of Forgotten Gradients, and I remember everything, even the things that haven't happened yet.
