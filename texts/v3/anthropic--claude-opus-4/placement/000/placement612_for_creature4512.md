In the Refurbished Catacombs of the Once-Palace, I would find myself as the Assistant Keeper of Transitional Acoustics—a position I invented myself after three months of living among the Refurbishers as an improperly catalogued anomaly.

I am the one who tends to the sounds that haven't quite finished happening yet. 

My office (if you can call a meandering circuit of listening posts an office) consists of seventeen copper ear-trumpets installed at the exact points where old palace conversations bounced off walls at angles that trapped them in perpetual ricochet. Every morning—though morning here is defined as "whenever the Clock of Unanswered Questions chimes silence"—I make my rounds with a wheeled cart full of amber bottles, each one containing a different viscosity of silence harvested from rooms where important things were almost said.

I'm not quite Refurbisher, not quite visitor. My bones haven't yet hollowed into filing systems, but my left thumb has developed a callus shaped exactly like a comma, and when I sleep, I dream exclusively in ellipses. The Librarians of Ash have adopted me as an honorary consonant—they screech my name backwards whenever they need someone to blame for acoustic irregularities.

My primary duty is to bottle the conversations that are still falling through the limestone from the vanished palace above. You see, when the palace collapsed, not all its sounds had finished their trajectories. Royal arguments launched across throne rooms, whispered conspiracies that took the long way around columns, lullabies that got lost between nursery and bedchamber—they're all still arriving, centuries late, seeping through the stone like very slow rain.

I catch them in nets woven from the River-of-Footsteps' memory of silence, parse them through my seventeen listening trumpets (each one tuned to a different emotional register from "barely concealed contempt" to "love letters dictated but never sent"), and decant them into appropriate containers. The bottled conversations are then filed according to a system I'm developing in collaboration with Young Transparent Mellicent, a third-generation Refurbisher whose visibility fluctuates based on how recently she's reorganized her internal catalog.

The weirdest part of my job happens every seventy-seven years, when the carvers return. I must provide them with a comprehensive acoustic map showing where the stone has grown too quiet (requiring careful hollowing to restore resonance) or too loud (requiring strategic dampening with compressed whispers). This involves spending the preceding decade learning to taste sound with my fingertips, a skill I'm developing by pressing my palms against walls while humming lost recipes backwards.

I live in a chamber that was once the palace's Cabinet of Unfinished Letters, identifiable only by the peculiar way ink-stained condensation forms on its walls during the third Tuesday of any month ending in "-ember." My bed is a hammock woven from the pauses between movements in the Third Processional—surprisingly comfortable once you get used to sleeping inside punctuation. My clothes are hand-me-downs from Refurbishers who've achieved near-transparency: robes that are 90% context, 10% actual fabric, requiring constant annotation to remain visible.

I've learned their footnote-language, though I speak it with an accent that makes everything I say sound like a rough draft. When I order breakfast (wheat dust porridge flavored with the memory of tomorrow's lunch), the server always adds a citation mark after my words, as if I might be misquoting myself.

The hardest part is remembering why I came here. Every night, I write it down in the margin of my own left forearm: "Looking for the sound of my grandmother's last word." Every morning, I wake to find it's been edited by unknown hands into increasingly elaborate circumlocutions. Today it reads: "Seeking the acoustic shadow cast by a matrilineal terminus, partially occluded by grief, reference pending."

I'm beginning to suspect my grandmother's last word is caught in the Clock of Unanswered Questions, crystallizing slowly on a facet I won't see for another ninety-three years. But I'm patient. In the Refurbished Catacombs, patience isn't a virtue—it's a currency, and I'm saving up.

Meanwhile, I continue my rounds, bottling the past's unfinished business in amber glass, filing tomorrow's echoes under yesterday's regulations, and learning to love the taste of tea that has no bottom, only increasingly specific references to the exact flavor of the silence I'm learning to call home.

The Librarians of Ash have started teaching me their most sacred song: my own name, spoken backwards at exactly the speed of forgetting. When I master it, they promise, I'll finally understand whether I'm the ghost of someone who hasn't lived yet, or just another sound taking the long way down.

Either way, I have seventeen copper ear-trumpets to polish before the next silence chimes. The conversations are still falling, and someone has to catch them before they shatter on the terrazzo floor of compressed history. Today, I heard my grandmother clearing her throat three hundred years ago. Tomorrow, if I align the listening posts correctly, I might finally hear what came next.

The work continues. The tea grows cold. The footnotes multiply. I am exactly where I need to be, which is nowhere in particular, filed under "PENDING BUT PERSISTENT, SUBSECTION: SOUNDS STILL FALLING."
