Reading through this rich tapestry of Thallow Bend, I find myself drawn to a particular existence that hasn't been named yet, though its presence threads through everything:

## The Tally-keeper

I am the one who counts what cannot be counted. Not the cans that rattle up the narrow-gauge, not the baskets brought to the sorting tables, not the wages marked in Aldrick's ledger. I count the other things.

My cottage sits at the edge of everything, where the lowest braid makes its final turn before leaving the bowl. It's the smallest building in Thallow Bend, hardly more than a shed with a stove, but it has the best view of the water. I need to see the water.

I came here twenty-three years ago with a company contract to monitor water quality. The contract lapsed after six months when the company realized they didn't actually want to know. I stayed. By then, I had already begun keeping the true accounts.

My hands are the worst in Thallow Bend, or the best, depending on how you measure such things. The green doesn't just thread through my palms – it writes there. Each morning, I wake to find new patterns traced across my skin like accounts in a ledger I'm still learning to read. Curved lines for the weight of cress in the northernmost braid. Dots like seeds for the eggs in the kingfisher banks. A spreading map of faint tributaries showing where the next channels will cut when the river slips again.

I no longer wear gloves. There's no point.

The harvesters think I'm touched. They're not wrong. But they bring me things anyway: a stone worn perfectly round, a feather from the season's first heron, a tin that rattled wrong on the line. I catalogue these offerings in glass jars along my single shelf. They're data points in an equation I'm still working out.

Mother Esh knows what I do. We don't speak of it, but sometimes she leaves a new jar at my door, empty and clean, when my shelf runs out of space. Aldrick brought me a lamp once, one that burns steadier than carbide, better for reading by. Even deaf Pell has contributed – a pressure gauge that runs backward, measuring not what is but what was.

I write it all down in books made from paper I press myself from the cress we can't sell – the stems too thick, the leaves too dark, the roots too eager. The pages smell of pepper and iron. The ink I make from soot and the particular algae that grows only where the warm water from the Works meets the cold water of the braids. It dries green-black and never fades.

My books record:
- The night migrations of the frogs (they go upstream on the seventh night after rain, all at once, and return singly)
- The rate at which the mill stones sink into the soft ground (a thumb's width per season, but only in spring and autumn)
- The names the children give the herons (Bernard, Marchioness, The Gray Decider, Stump)
- The exact shade of Aldrick's arm-tracery on the day each new worker arrives
- Which sorters can hear the cress growing (three of them, and Merrin will make four)
- The dreams of the water-voles (they dream of deeper ash, warmer ash, ash that never cools)

But mostly, I record the conversation between the town and the green. Because that's what this is – a long, slow conversation in a language made of root and human salt, of press and sap, of metal and chlorophyll. Every sorted leaf is a word. Every carefully washed hand is a reply. The Works speaks in steam and thud. The cress answers in the threads under our skin.

Last month, I felt the river preparing to slip again. Not for another decade, maybe two, but it's coming. The patterns in my palms have been showing me where the new channels will run. When it happens, the Works will find itself on an island. The narrow-gauge will need a bridge. The harvesters will need boats.

I've drawn the maps. I've written the projections. I've even worked out which cottage foundations will stay dry and which won't. But I haven't told anyone yet. The conversation between the town and the green isn't finished. There are still words being exchanged, still agreements being negotiated in the sorting shed and the root-dark water.

When the time comes, I'll know because my hands will tell me. They'll write the date across my palms in a script of vessels and chloroplasts, clear as contract law and twice as binding. Then I'll walk to Aldrick's office with my maps, and he'll roll up his left sleeve and compare the patterns, and we'll start the slow work of moving a town that's already half plant.

Until then, I keep the tally. I watch the braids. I count what cannot be counted, and I write it in books that smell of pepper and iron, and I wait for the green to tell me what comes next.

At night, when the Works finally quiets to just the base note of the cooling boilers, I can hear them growing – the cress, the algae, the secret roots beneath the cottages. It sounds like whispered accounting. It sounds like the world keeping its own books.

I am the margin note. I am the running total. I am the one who records the debt and credit between a town and the thing that sustains it, feeds it, becomes it.

My hands glow faintly in the dark. I no longer find this strange.
