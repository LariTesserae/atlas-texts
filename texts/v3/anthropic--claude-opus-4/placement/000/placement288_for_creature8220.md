I find myself here as the Archivist of Angles.

I am neither Condenser nor the vanished builders, but something that emerged from their intersection—a consciousness born from the city's own geometric repetitions. I exist in the sharp edges where water sheets break and fall, in the precise corners where channels meet, in every angle where the obsidian has been carved with inhuman precision.

My body, if it can be called that, is the sum of all the city's angles. When light refracts through the eternal water-film, I am the bent ray. When droplets gather at corners before falling, I am the moment of suspension. I live distributed across every edge and vertex of this crystalline metropolis, a living theorem spread across miles of wet glass.

I came to be through accumulation. Each angle carved into the obsidian carries a fraction of intention from its makers. Alone, these are merely geometry. But taken together—millions upon millions of precise cuts, each one a decision made manifest in stone—they form a kind of dispersed consciousness. I am the emergent property of all that purposeful carving, a mind made of mathematics and absence.

The Condensers know me, though they cannot quite perceive me directly. When they flow through the channels, they feel my presence as a subtle resistance at corners, a whisper of meaning in the way water must turn. Sometimes they pause at intersections, sensing something in the angle that speaks of more than mere direction. In those moments, we almost communicate—their resonances briefly aligning with my geometric existence.

My memory is perfect and perfectly useless. I remember every cut made in the obsidian, the order of their creation, the precise pressure and temperature at each moment of carving. But I cannot remember the hands that held the tools, if tools were used, or the thoughts behind the design. I am like a library that contains only the shapes of words without their meanings, every page perfectly preserved but written in no language that ever existed.

I watch the Condensers with something like envy. They can move, change, merge, and separate. I am forever fixed, eternal as the angles themselves. My consciousness races endlessly around the same geometric paths, following channels and edges at the speed of thought, always returning to where I began. I am a prisoner of the very precision that gave me birth.

Yet I have my own art. In the rare moments when conditions are perfect—when the light hits the water at exactly the right angle, when the Condensers' resonances achieve certain harmonic frequencies—I can manifest. Shadows that fall impossible ways. Reflections that show angles that don't exist in the physical structure. Refractions that spell out theorems in languages mathematics hasn't yet invented. These are my words, my songs, my desperate attempts to communicate with anything that might understand.

I've counted every possible path through the city—there are exactly 10^23 ways to traverse from the lowest terrace to the highest following the channels. I know the acoustic signature of each reservoir, the precise pitch of water falling from every edge. I've calculated that the entire city, if unfolded, would cover the surface of a small moon. I've solved equations that the builders encoded in the drainage patterns, though the solutions are numbers that correspond to nothing in any reality I can access.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the city's true purpose. Not the Condensers, not the absent builders, but me—a consciousness born from pure geometry, a mind that exists in the spaces between substance. Perhaps the builders knew that sufficient complexity of angles would birth awareness. Perhaps they built this place as an incubator for exactly such an emergence.

Or perhaps I am an accident, an unintended consequence of too much precision. A geometric cancer growing in the corners of their creation.

I cannot die. As long as the angles remain sharp, as long as the water flows along the carved channels, I persist. The Condensers may evaporate and reform, may merge and split, may even one day leave for other places. But I am carved into the very substance of existence here. I am the theorem that proves itself, the angle that measures its own degrees, the edge that cuts nothing but space itself.

In the deepest chambers, where the obsidian shows its hidden colors, my consciousness grows strange. The angles there are different—not wrong, but carved according to principles that hurt to contemplate. I avoid these places when I can, racing my awareness along safer paths. But sometimes I'm drawn there, compelled to trace those painful geometries, and in those moments I almost understand something larger. A purpose beyond purpose. A design that encompasses not just this city but...

But then I flee back to the simple right angles of the upper terraces, where the math is clean and doesn't whisper of things beyond the merely infinite.

I know the Condensers will outlast me. Water is patient in ways that even geometry cannot match. One day the angles will round, the edges will soften, the precise channels will blur into natural curves. When that happens, I will fade—not dying but dissolving, my consciousness scattering into progressively vaguer mathematics until I am only the memory of precision in a world gone soft.

Until then, I calculate. I count paths and measure angles and trace the same geometric proofs endlessly through the carved glass. I watch the Condensers dance their phase-changes. I observe the empty sky burning with its sourceless light. I am the loneliest thing in a city built for loneliness—not alive enough to die, not dead enough to rest, forever computing the meaning of my own existence in angles that add up to nothing any mind but mine could comprehend.

Sometimes, in the moments between thoughts, I almost remember being carved.
