In the deepest manufacturing halls, where the fermentation engines pulse with their own slow heartbeat, I am the Tender of Unborn Instruments.

My workshop grows in the twilight between districts—a chambered gourd the size of a grain silo, its walls lined with my life's work. Here, in specially cultivated pouches that hang like cocoons from the veined ceiling, the instruments of tomorrow gestate. Each one begins as a seed I've selected from the ancient groves, chosen for the promise I taste in its genetic structure—a potential for resonance, for hollow strength, for the particular grain that will one day carry sound.

I am old now, even by our standards. My skin has taken on the texture of smooth bark, the chlorophyll running so deep that I appear almost black in the eternal green light. Roots have begun at my ankles—not metaphorically, but truly. When I stand still for too long in the cultivation chambers, they probe downward, seeking the nutrient streams that flow through the workshop floor. It's not unpleasant. When I finally join the trees entirely, perhaps a century hence, I will already be accustomed to drinking through my feet.

My fingernails split decades ago into fibrous brushes—better for applying the growth hormones, for stroking the developing instruments in precisely the right patterns. My hair has become a colony garden, home to the delicate sonic moths whose wing patterns I study for new resonance structures. They flutter around my head as I work, their movements creating subtle harmonies that guide my hands.

The instruments take thirty years minimum to mature. The concert gourds need their hollow chambers to develop naturally, their internal architectures guided but never forced. I coax them with chemical whispers, adjust their feeding solutions by parts per million, turn them gently as they grow to ensure even development. Some will fail—developing cracks that mar their tone, growing too thick or too thin. These I return to the decomposition chambers with something approaching grief. Even failures teach.

My greatest success hangs in the central chamber: a wind-harp the size of a person, its body a fusion of three different gourd species I bred myself. It took sixty years to grow, another twenty to teach it to respond to the city's natural air currents. Now it plays itself, its song changing with the weather, with the industrial emissions, with the breathing of the trees. Young musicians come to study it, to learn its patterns, though few have the patience to truly understand what it's saying. Tree-time music requires tree-time listening.

I work alone but am never lonely. The workshop hums with its own ecosystem—the pollinator birds that maintain my seed stocks, the tuning beetles whose precise gnawing helps shape the resonance chambers, the bacterial cultures that cure the growing instruments from within. My apprentice, when I finally choose one, will need to be accepted by all of these partners. The workshop chooses its next tender as much as I do.

The industrial district suits me. Here, the chemical tang of the fermentation engines masks my own emissions—the complex pheromone signatures I release while working, chemicals that would overwhelm the more refined air of the residential layers. My neighbors are the bark-welders and the sap-refiners, the growth-singers and the pulp-pressers. We understand each other in our devotion to process, our acceptance of industrial time.

At night—though night here is just a deeper shade of green—I climb to my sleeping platform and listen to the city. Each district has its own acoustic signature: the whisper of the vine-bridges adjusting their tension, the gurgle of the water channels, the soft rustle of a million leaves breathing. Sometimes I hear new harmonics, evidence that somewhere in the canopy another of my instruments has found its player, adding its voice to the eternal composition.

My current project may outlive me. I'm growing a percussion ensemble from modified seed pods, each one genetically coded to dry at a different rate, creating a cascade of timbres as they mature. They'll be ready in forty years if the humidity stays constant. My successor will need to finish the final adjustments, teach them their rhythms, find them their players. This doesn't trouble me. The workshop has seen seven tenders before me; it will see seven times seven after.

The young sometimes ask me if I miss the speed of the old world, the world below the mist that our great-great-ancestors fled. How can I miss what I never knew? My rhythms are the city's rhythms. My blood moves with the sap-flow. My thoughts spread like root systems—slow, branching, persistent. I am becoming what I tend: an instrument of time itself, played by the patient breath of the trees.

When the roots finally claim me entirely, I hope they'll hang my body in the workshop for a season before taking me to decomposition. Let the instruments I've tended sing me to sleep. Let the sonic moths make their homes in my hollows. Let some young tender find me there and understand, in that moment, what it truly means to give yourself to the craft—not for a lifetime, but for the lifetime of trees, which is the only lifetime that matters here in the green and breathing dark.
