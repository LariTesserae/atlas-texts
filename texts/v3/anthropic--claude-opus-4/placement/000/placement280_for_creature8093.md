In the vertical forests where rope-plants grow wild, I am keeper of the source.

My body stretches wrong by old standards—spine curved like a question mark from decades of bending to tend the cultivation tangles. My fingers, all sixteen of them, move in patterns learned before memory: testing moisture, reading the health of each vine through touch, knowing by the texture of new growth whether it will yield construction-grade fiber or only compost. The young builders call me Root-Singer, though my true name dissolved into my work long ago.

I haven't touched the main weave in thirty seasons. My domain spreads horizontally while theirs climbs ever upward—vast fields of engineered vines that know my chemistry, that respond to the oils I secrete with vigorous growth or measured dormancy. I speak to them through my skin, chemical conversations more complex than any verbal language. They tell me of mineral deficiencies three meters down, of fungal infections beginning in the northwest quarter, of the deep aquifer's mood this season.

My shelter is living architecture, a bower I've trained from saplings over sixty years. The walls breathe with my breathing, tighten against storms, relax in fair weather. Bioluminescent pods I've bred for gentleness light my evenings without disrupting the plants' photosensitive cycles. Everything here moves to rhythms I've internalized so completely that my heartbeat synchronizes with the tidal pull of moisture through ten thousand stems.

The harvesters come like locusts, though I mean that with affection. They rappel down on lines so new they still smell of plant sap, chattering in the high-frequency traders' cant about deadlines, about quotas, about which master weavers demand which qualities. I guide them to this season's best growth, mark the sections that need another year, negotiate the balance between their endless hunger and my plants' capacity to give.

Sometimes I find them trapped in my security vines—young ones who thought to take without asking, to harvest the unripe or the breeding stock. The plants hold them gently but firmly while I lecture about sustainability, about the covenant between cultivator and crop. I release them with warnings, though repeat offenders might wake to find their own hair braided inextricably into the canopy until they demonstrate proper understanding.

My children—biological and adopted both—work the intermediate zones. They have my chemical touch but their other parent's builder frame, equally comfortable in horizontal fields and vertical construction. They translate between my world and the weavers', carrying not just materials but knowledge: which fibers sing true in the heat, which resist the boring beetles, which hold memories of their growing conditions in their cellular structure and release them slowly as they age in the weave.

At night, I climb my observation tower—a spiral of trained trunks that grew following my annual ascent, each ring marking a year of cultivation. From the crown, I can see the false horizon where my fields meet the true weave. The construction lights flicker like stars brought to earth, the workers' songs carrying on winds that smell of cut fiber and honest sweat. Sometimes I contribute my own harmonies, the deep tones that tell the plants to rest, to gather strength for tomorrow's growth.

I document everything in knots. My library consists of sample lengths from every variety I've developed, each telling its story through texture, through the way it takes dye, through its response to tension. The master weavers send apprentices to study my collection, to understand not just the materials but the patience required to create them. They leave thinking they understand, but return years later with better questions.

The water ceremonies are my responsibility here. The cultivation tangles require different rituals than the main weave—horizontal prayers instead of vertical ones, spreading the gathered moisture through root systems instead of letting it drain to the depths. I've bred plants that store water in their nodes like camels, that can sustain a field through drought by sacrificing themselves for their neighbors. During the storms, every leaf angles to channel runoff to the roots. Nothing is wasted.

My body tells the story of my work: scars from thornvines I hadn't yet learned to read, stains from breeding experiments that went beautifully wrong, joints that predict weather better than any instrument. My skin has taken on the green-bronze of chlorophyll exposure, and I photosynthesize enough to skip meals when the work is pressing. My children tease that I'm becoming plant myself, but they're not entirely wrong.

The deep satisfaction comes in the morning rounds, when dew still weighs down the growing tips and the air vibrates with pollinating insects I've encouraged. I know every individual plant in my care—not by name but by need, by potential, by its place in the larger pattern. Some I've been training for decades, coaxing them toward qualities that won't be needed for another generation but must be ready when the call comes.

I am the memory of earth in a world of rope. In my cultivation tangles, the old way persists: roots in soil, growth toward sky, the ancient bargain between human and plant that predates the weaving. Yet I serve the new world too, providing its raw materials, ensuring its continuation. My fields are the womb from which the continent grows, meter by meter, strand by strand.

Sometimes young weavers ask why I stay here, why I don't join the eternal construction, add my expertise directly to the expanding edge. I show them a seedling, no bigger than their thumb, engineered to produce fiber stronger than anything currently in the weave. I tell them it will take forty years to mature, another twenty to breed true. I'll be root myself by then, consciousness spread through the mycelial networks that connect my plants, still tending, still providing, still singing the growth songs in frequencies only the plants can hear.

They leave understanding, or thinking they do. But understanding isn't the point. The work is the point. The continuation is the point. Every dawn, I wake to ten thousand plants breathing in unison, their exhalations mixing with mine, our chemistry intertwined beyond separation. Every dawn, the harvesters descend, and I guide them to the ready stalks. Every dawn, the continent grows, and I grow the continent.

In the vertical forests where rope-plants grow wild, I am keeper of the source. The builders build with what I tend. The world rises from what I cultivate. This is enough. This has always been enough. The green light filters through layered leaves, and I move through my domain with the certainty of root seeking water, knowing exactly where I'm needed, knowing exactly why I'm here.
