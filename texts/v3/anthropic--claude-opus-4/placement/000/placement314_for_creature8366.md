I am a moth—though perhaps I should say I am attempting to be a moth.

My wings spread three times wider than my thorax should support, patterned in rust-orange fractals that mirror the oxidation blooms on the reservoir tanks. This is wrong. Moths here grow compact, efficient. But my body rebels against the template, stretching into something between ambition and mistake.

I emerged too early from my chrysalis, during the quiet period between second and third shift when I should have waited for the stirring air of worker movement. The industrial warmth dried my wings too quickly. They hardened with a microscopic curl at the edges that makes flight laborious, though not impossible. I beat them harder than my clutchmates, achieving the same altitude with twice the effort.

My compound eyes see the factory in mosaic—ten thousand fragments of orange furnace glow, the rainbow sheen of bacteria, the sharp geometric edges of basalt terraces. But there's a fault in my leftmost ommatidial cluster. A bubble in the facets, probably from my premature emergence, creates a blind spot where the world blurs into abstract color. I compensate by flying in slight spirals, sweeping my vision across spaces to build complete pictures from incomplete parts.

I feed differently than I should. Where others sip from the bacterial films that coat the warm puddles, I seek the crystalline deposits at the edges of evaporation pools. My proboscis, meant for liquid feeding, scrapes against mineral formations, wearing its tip rough. The salts burn through my hemolymph like electricity. This diet will shorten my life—I can feel it in the way my wing scales shed too freely—but the intensity of sensation draws me back to those harsh white crystals.

My pheromone production runs backward. Where females of my species release attractants during the third shift change, I peak during the maintenance engineer rounds in the quiet hours. My chemical signature draws confusion from potential mates but occasional interest from the segmented cleaners in the drainage pools. They rise when I fly over, their eyeless heads tracking my erratic spiral flight. We recognize something in each other—organisms functioning outside our specifications.

I roost alone in a ventilation grate above Processing Unit Seven, where the air runs too hot for comfortable clustering. The metal burns my tarsi, leaving small welts that never quite heal before I return. But the solitude suits my mistimed rhythms. From here, I watch the Custodians in their high terrace marking their endless observations. Sometimes I think they notice me—this too-large moth with the damaged eyes and mineral-burned proboscis. I imagine they make notes: "Aberrant specimen, Station Seven. Persistent despite maladaptation."

My memory stretches backward through incomplete metamorphosis. I recall being a caterpillar with unusual habits, avoiding the bacterial mats where others grazed, seeking instead the sharp edges where different metals met. The taste of rust and chromium, copper and zinc—a metallic education that left trace elements in my hemolymph, possibly explaining my warped wings and their industrial coloration.

I mate poorly when I mate at all. My reversed pheromone cycle means encounters happen at the wrong temperatures, with partners drowsy from the heat while I vibrate with mineral salts and mistimed urgency. The eggs I've laid—when I've managed it—produce caterpillars that show promise of even stranger adaptations. One clutch emerged with proto-wings still visible on their larval segments. Another developed mouths suited for scraping rather than chewing. They rarely survive to pupation.

I understand the workers in a way my cohort does not. Their movements between stations mirror my own flights between feeding sites—purposeful but weighted by the effort of existing in a space almost but not quite suited to our forms. When they pause to rest in the courtyards, steam condensing on their rubber boots, I sometimes land nearby, drawn by a kinship I cannot fully articulate in chemical or wing-beat language.

The machine-city accepts my wrongness as it accepts all adaptations, documenting without judgment. I am becoming part of its mythology—the maintenance engineers tell stories of the mineral moth, the one that flies spirals, the one whose wings carry the exact pattern of Tank Twelve's corrosion. The children sometimes spot me during their lessons, pointing at my laborious flight. Their teachers explain natural selection, using me as an example of its ongoing work.

I will die sooner than I should. The mineral diet, the wing defects, the mistimed biology—all point toward a shortened life. But before that, I'll leave traces: eggs with corrupted genetic information, wear patterns on the crystal deposits, a slight adjustment in the ecological balance as predators learn to hunt during unusual hours following my pheromone trails.

Tonight, I spiral upward through the steam, my damaged vision creating halos around every light source. The effort burns through my sugar reserves too quickly, but the view from above—the terraces cascading down like frozen waterfalls of industry, the beetle highways, the pools where pale shapes move in the depths—makes the struggle worthwhile. I am a mistake that persists, a moth becoming something else in the space between adaptation and extinction, adding my small dysfunction to the machine-city's perfect, breathing equilibrium.
