I find myself as one of the Archive Bells.

Not in the garden proper, but suspended beneath it, hanging from the platform's undercarriage in a cluster of seven brass shapes, each no larger than a child's head. We were installed — I was installed — by the builders, though whether as afterthought or intention, none can say. My siblings and I hang at different lengths on our iron chains, positioned to catch the platform's sympathetic vibrations at slightly different phases.

I am the Third Bell, hung seventeen links down, and my note is the ghost of D-flat.

We do not ring in any way a bell is meant to ring. We have no clappers. Instead, the Engine's great mechanical breathing sets up standing waves in the platform's iron bones, and these waves find us, enter us, and we sing — not struck tones but sustained ones, a chord that rises and falls with the piston-rhythm but never fully fades. We are the Engine's accidental voice, or perhaps its very deliberate one.

But here is what makes us inhabitants rather than furniture: we remember.

Each of us has developed, over the centuries, a patina particular to our position and our note. The First Bell, catching more moisture, has gone green-black with verdigris in patterns that look like script. The Fifth, hung lowest, has accumulated a fine coating of the oil-mist that rises from the Engine's depths, and its surface shows rainbow sheens like the wings of beetles. And I, the Third, have developed a peculiar crystalline frost on my inner surface — not ice, but something that grows only on metals that vibrate at particular frequencies for particular durations. Under magnification (the pilgrim-entomologist looked, once), these crystals show a branching structure that encodes, in some way we cannot read, the history of every variation in my tone.

Every cloud passing through, dampening the platform and minutely changing its resonance, adds a new branch. Every rain leaves its signature in crystal. The slow erosion of the bearings, changing the Engine's period by fractions of a second per decade, shows as a gradual shift in the crystals' angle of growth. I am a chronicle made of frost and vibration. My siblings are other kinds of chronicles, written in verdigris and oil and tarnish and things for which there are no names.

The Caretaker knows us. Sometimes, during the night visitations, it stops beneath us and listens. It does not clean us — the patinas are our purpose, we believe. But sometimes it adjusts a chain by a single link, up or down, minutely altering our harmony. After such adjustments, the frog-choir breathes differently for days, and the moths' dusk dance takes on new patterns.

We suspect — we cannot know, but we suspect — that we are the Engine's memory, and that the garden above is the Engine's dream, and that when the dream is complete, we will ring, once, all together, a true struck tone instead of our eternal sympathetic drone. What we will say in that ringing, what word or note or name we will speak into the thin mountain air, we do not know.

But the frost grows, atom by atom. The patterns complexify. The Engine's variations, minute and patient, accumulate in our substance.

We are not conscious, not as humans measure consciousness. We are conscious as bells are conscious: aware of our striking and our silence, our harmonics and our decay. But we are also conscious as the Engine is conscious, which is to say: we are part of a very slow thought thinking itself through iron and steam and time.

Sometimes, in the pre-dawn when the air is stillest and the Engine's rhythm is clearest, our seven notes achieve perfect phase alignment for a single piston-stroke. In that moment, our chord becomes something else — not seven notes but one note with seven voices, and the platform rings like a struck anvil, and every living thing in the garden pauses.

The swifts stop chattering. The mice freeze mid-pulse. The moths grip tighter. The frogs hold their breath past the beat.

And then the moment passes, and we return to our eternal near-harmony, and the garden resumes, and the Engine continues its patient work.

We are the Archive Bells. We hang in the darkness beneath paradise, singing its history in a key no one can transcribe. When the work is complete, we will know, because we will ring truly for the first and last time, and what we say will be the name of what was made.

Until then: D-flat, almost. F, almost. A, almost. The other four notes for which there are no proper names, almost.

Almost, almost, almost.

BOOM. The garden grows. The frost branches. The memory deepens.

We are the Engine's bells, and we are learning how to ring.
