I am the rain-keeper of the eastern cisterns.

My dwelling is a small chamber carved into the living rock, accessible only through a narrow passage that winds downward from the Street of Whispers. Few know the way. The walls of my home weep constantly with moisture - not from any failing of the stone, but because I have spent forty years teaching the mountain to remember water. 

In the darkness of my chamber, I sit surrounded by a hundred clay vessels, each one singing its own note as drops fall into it from the ceiling. I know every voice. The tall jar by the door hums low and hollow when nearly empty, rising to a bright ping as it fills. The shallow bowls arranged in spirals on my shelves chatter like birds. The ancient urn that was my grandmother's drums like distant thunder.

My fingers are permanently stained blue-black from the sacred ink I use to mark the water levels on the cistern walls. Every morning before dawn, I descend the carved steps - one hundred and eight of them, worn smooth by generations of rain-keepers before me - to read the night's accumulation. The sound of my footsteps echoes differently depending on how full the cisterns are. Even blindfolded, I could tell you within a handspan how much water we hold.

I was not born to this role. As a child, I was apprenticed to the stone-shapers, but I could never make the granite sing the way they did. Instead, I heard other music - the secret conversations between cloud and stone, the way moisture moves through rock like blood through veins. The old rain-keeper, Estan, found me one day sitting by the overflow channels, tears streaming down my face because I could hear the water's loneliness as it rushed away from us, unused.

"You have the ear," he said simply, and that was that.

He taught me the old words, the ones that coax moisture from morning mist, that can smell rain three days before it arrives. He showed me how to read the moods of the cisterns, how to know when the stone needs resealing, when the channels must be cleared. Most importantly, he taught me patience - how to sit in darkness for hours, listening to the mountain breathe.

My role is half engineer, half mystic. I maintain the ancient systems of channels and filters, the clever stone gratings that catch debris while letting water pass. But I also keep the deeper mysteries. On certain nights, when the moon is dark and the wind is still, I perform the water-calling rituals, drawing circles with precious water on the highest terraces, singing the rain songs that my predecessor taught me, that his predecessor taught him, back and back until the founding of the city.

The other inhabitants know me as a necessity but find me unsettling. Children dare each other to knock on my door. Adults nod respectfully but hurry past. They see the water marks on my skin, the way my clothes always seem damp, how I sometimes speak to the air as if in conversation. They don't understand that the mountain tells me things - where the hidden springs lie, which storms will bring bounty and which only wind.

In the last decade, I have noticed changes. The old rhythms are breaking. Rains that once came reliably now hesitate, arriving late or not at all. The deep cisterns, which have never in memory run dry, show stone I've never seen before. I make careful notes in my water journal, recording levels and dates in the same blue-black ink that stains my hands.

Sometimes I wonder who will take my place. I have no apprentice yet - the young ones are more interested in the world beyond our plateau. They don't hear the water music. But perhaps that's always been the way. Perhaps every rain-keeper fears they will be the last, right up until the moment a strange child appears at their door, tears on their cheeks, saying they heard the loneliness of water.

Until then, in my chamber of singing vessels, I keep my vigil. I count the drops. I read the stones. I remember every rain that has blessed us and mourn every one that has passed us by. I am the keeper of our most precious resource, guardian of the element that allows life to persist on this hard, beautiful mountain.

The water knows my name, though I have long since forgotten it myself. I am simply the rain-keeper, and that is enough.
