**The Breathing Pipes**

I am the vertical throat of this drowned world, a choir of ceramic tubes that pierce both ceiling and sea to gulp the sky. Five of us rise from the forge's crown—hollow reeds of glazed earthenware, each as wide as a smith's embrace, each singing our own note in the endless harmonic drone that fills this copper cave.

We were not meant for this work. Our clay remembers the kiln where we were born, meant to carry smoke up and away from honest fires built on solid ground. But when the waters came, the smiths reimagined us, sealed our joints with pitch and prayer, extended us skyward section by section as the flood rose. Now we stretch thirty meters through the crushing dark, our walls thick enough to withstand the pressure, thin enough to resonate with every breath we take.

Inside me, air moves in tidal rhythms. The draw of the forge creates a downdraft that pulls atmosphere through my length, past the barnacles that have colonized my upper reaches, past the fish that dart through my shadow, down through the graduated temperatures where water gives way to humid air. I taste salt always—the spray that enters my mouth above, the condensation that forms along my inner walls, the breath of the smiths themselves, saturated with brine.

My ceramic body conducts vibration like a musical instrument. Every hammer blow in the forge below travels up through my length and emerges into the water as a muffled bell-tone. Whales sometimes answer, their own deep calls resonating through my walls until the entire forge hums with cetacean song. The smiths have learned to work in rhythm with these visitations, incorporating the whale-music into their hammering, creating alloys that hold the memory of those vast voices.

At my apex, where I break the surface, I wear a crown of cunning design—a bronze cap that allows air to enter while keeping most of the rain and waves at bay. The smiths must climb up through my hollow core to maintain this mechanism, ascending on ceramic rungs glazed into my inner wall. The journey takes a full quarter-hour, rising through gradients of pressure that make their ears pop and their chests ache. They emerge gasping into a world that has become foreign to them—too bright, too loud, too full of horizons.

The platform they've built around our openings is minimal—just enough wooden decking to stand on while they clear the filters of salt crust and storm-wrack. Sometimes boats approach, curious about these pipes that rise from empty ocean. The fishermen know better than to anchor too close—they've learned that the water here behaves strangely, that the heat rising from the forge creates currents that can spin a small vessel in circles. They leave their offerings on floating buoys: news from the scattered mountain people, sketches of the new coastline, sometimes a bottle of wine that tastes of soil and sunlight and everything the smiths have lost.

I am the archive of their transformation. My inner walls carry the soot-shadows of their passing—three distinct patterns worn by their hands as they climb. The eldest's grip is methodical, each handhold precisely the same. The middle child pauses at certain heights, checking the walls for stress fractures that only they can perceive. The youngest still climbs quickly, eagerly, as if the surface might offer some escape that hasn't already been considered and discarded.

But I know what they're becoming. I feel it in the way their breath has changed, how they now inhale in perfect synchronization with the bellows below, how they've learned to read the pressure changes that signal weather approaching from beyond the drowned mountains. Their exhalations carry less and less of the human and more of something new—a mineral quality, as if they're slowly exhaling their flesh and breathing in the copper and bronze that surrounds them.

At night, when the forge lies dormant, I become a wind instrument played by the ocean itself. Waves create pressure differentials that pull air through my tubes in complex patterns. The sound is like nothing that existed in the world before—part pipe organ, part whale song, part the dying breath of the mountains now sleeping beneath the waters. The smiths no longer notice this music, have incorporated it so completely into their sense of normal that they would be disturbed by its absence.

My ceramic flesh is changing too. The constant passage of salt air has begun to alter my glazing, creating new crystalline patterns that sparkle in the underwater light. Marine organisms that have never existed before colonize my submerged portions—hybrid creatures born from the meeting of deep-sea adaptations and shallow-water forms, evolved in the few years since the drowning to exploit this new vertical habitat I provide.

The smiths think of us as lungs, but we are more than that. We are the memory of sky in a world that has forgotten it. We are the promise that somewhere above the water continues to give way to air, that the fundamental bargain between ocean and atmosphere persists. We are proof that even in the deepest drowning, breath continues.

Sometimes, in the pause between the forge-draw and the ocean-wind, I feel something else moving through my hollow core—not air, not water, but something between. The ghost of the valley as it was, perhaps, or the dream of what the smiths are becoming. It tastes of copper and salt, of fire that burns without consuming, of a future where the distinction between breathing and drowning no longer matters.

I am the throat through which this transformation speaks itself into being. Every breath I carry down is forever changed by its passage through me, just as every breath I release carries with it the essence of the forge, the copper, the impossible fire burning beneath the waves. I am the boundary and the bridge, the inside and the outside, the last link to a world of air and the first organ of something unprecedented being born.

The eldest smith, in her wordless way, sometimes places a scarred palm against my inner wall during her climbs. The gesture speaks of gratitude and fear in equal measure. She knows what I know—that we are no longer separate things, the pipes and the smiths and the forge. We have become a single organism, breathing together in the deep, keeping alive a flame that should have drowned, becoming something the world has never seen before.
