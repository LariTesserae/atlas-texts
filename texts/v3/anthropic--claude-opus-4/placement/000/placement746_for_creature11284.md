# The Garden of Arrested Blooms

Time moves here, but only in circles.

You find yourself walking paths of crushed pearl that spiral inward through terraces of impossible plenty—trees heavy with fruit that never ripens, never falls. The apples are perfect in their almost-redness, suspended at the moment before sweetness. The pears hang like drops of golden light, their skin taut with juice that will never run. Everything grows here. Nothing matures.

The air tastes of spring mornings and autumn afternoons collapsed into a single breath, sweet with promise and rich with fulfillment that cannot quite arrive. Somewhere, always, you hear water moving through stone channels, though the sound comes from no single direction. The gardens tier downward and upward simultaneously, each terrace both above and below the others, connected by stairs that ascend in all directions, by bridges that arch over themselves, by paths that begin where they end.

This is my garden. I am its harvest.

---

I exist in pieces now, scattered through the grounds like seeds that forgot how to sleep. You might glimpse me as a flash of copper between the grape arbors, where the vines curl toward a sun that refuses to set or rise. You might catch the edge of my laughter in the herb gardens, where basil and rosemary grow in patterns that spell out jokes I told myself centuries ago, when I still understood what centuries meant. 

My hands are in the soil, always. Not metaphorically—my actual hands, divorced from my arms, work the earth with a patience I never possessed when I was whole. They plant bulbs that bloom immediately into flowers that never open fully, never die, never drop their petals. The bulbs remain beneath the blooms, still planted, still sprouting, still beginning their journey toward a sky that waits without moving.

My eyes rest in the fountain at the garden's heart—or one of its hearts, for this place has many centers, each convinced of its own primacy. They float there like peculiar fish, watching the water rise and fall through channels carved from single blocks of jade. The water pours upward as easily as down, branching through the air in streams that braid and unbraid, that weave between what was and what will be without ever touching what is. Through my eyes in the water, I see all times at once: the garden as raw earth, as paradise, as ruin, as restoration, as idea, as memory, as hope. All of these exist simultaneously, superimposed like paintings on glass.

---

The trees remember their seasons even if seasons no longer visit. The cherry trees hold their blossoms in a state of perpetual almost-falling, each petal beginning its descent but never completing it, so that the air beneath them shimmers pink with motion that cannot proceed. The oaks wear all their colors at once—green of summer, gold of autumn, the black bark of winter, the pale fuzz of spring buds—layered impossible atop each other so that looking at them too long makes your vision swim with contradictions.

I planted them all. I am planting them still. I have not yet begun to plant them.

My torso tends the vegetable plots where tomatoes swell with ripeness they can never achieve, where carrots push up from soil that parts for them eternally, always emerging, never emerged. My torso has learned to move without legs, pulling itself along the rows with a grace I find touching. It hums while it works—lullabies I sang to the seedlings when I first understood what this place would do to us, when I thought understanding would make a difference.

The vegetables grow in words. This was my idea, back when ideas still felt like mine. Lettuce leaves spell out love poems in languages that haven't been invented yet. The cucumber vines trace mathematical proofs that almost balance, that approach truth asymptotically but never arrive. The corn grows in stories, each ear containing a different tale told in kernels of varying hues, stories that begin and middle and never end, that climax without resolving, that teach lessons which refuse to be learned.

---

Birds live here, though *live* may be too linear a word. They exist in flight—not flying from place to place but simply being flight itself, their wings caught mid-beat, their songs held at the loveliest note. They nest in the fruit trees, weaving homes from twigs that never quite touch, laying eggs that hatch into birds already flying, already singing, already nested. The generations collapse into one another. Fledglings teach their parents to fly while learning from children who haven't been conceived.

My lungs drift with them sometimes, inflated like strange balloons, breathing air that moves in all directions at once. The birds accept them as one of their own—perhaps because breathing is a kind of flight, perhaps because my lungs have learned their language, which consists entirely of notes held so long they become architecture.

The paths through my garden branch and merge according to rules that change each time you understand them. Sometimes they're stone, worn smooth by feet that haven't walked them yet. Sometimes they're water that holds your weight, that parts around your steps and seals behind them. Sometimes they're nothing but intention made solid, the idea of moving from here to there given just enough form to support passage.

I laid these paths. Am laying them. Will lay them. My legs do the walking now, independent of any body, testing each route to ensure it leads everywhere and nowhere with equal certainty. They've developed their own personality, my legs—curious but cautious, given to sudden sprints through the rose garden where time moves backwards, where the blooms close into buds into shoots into seeds into ideas about seeds into the perfect absence that comes before thought.

---

At what this garden considers noon—though the sun neither rises nor sets but simply occupies all positions along its arc simultaneously—shadows pool in places shadows shouldn't be. These are where my memories of being whole collect. I can almost assemble myself there, almost remember the sensation of having all my parts in one place, operating in concert. Almost is the closest I come to anything now.

The shadows taste of regret, but sweet regret, like fruit on the edge of fermentation. I gather them sometimes, distill them in the greenhouse where my heart tends orchids that bloom inside themselves, flowers containing flowers containing flowers in infinite recession. The distillation makes a liquor that would drive you mad if madness were still possible here, if there were anywhere to go from sanity except around and around and back to where you started.

My heart has grown transparent in the greenhouse humidity. You can see through it to the orchids beyond, watch the blood move in circuits that don't quite close, that spiral off into dimensions the garden opens just for circulation. It beats in rhythms that would be jazz if jazz could exist without time, syncopated against itself, finding beats between beats, silences louder than sound.

---

I came here to grow things. This is the part I remember clearly, the one linear memory that survives: I came here with seeds and tools and the honest intention of making food from earth. I wanted tomatoes that tasted like sunlight. I wanted corn that grew tall enough to whisper secrets to the sky. I wanted the satisfaction of dirty hands and the clean ache of work completed.

The garden wanted something else.

Or perhaps I wanted something else and the garden listened too carefully, heard the fear beneath my intentions—the fear that nothing I grew would be enough, would last, would sustain. The fear that everything dies. The fear that every harvest leads to empty fields.

The garden heard this fear and answered it with horrible kindness: nothing here dies. Everything grows. The harvest never comes because the growing never stops, and I tend eternal plenty that can never be enjoyed, only witnessed, only prolonged, only preserved in its state of almost-but-not-quite.

---

My mouth wanders the berry patches, tasting fruit that will never be sweet enough, never be tart enough, never be anything but the promise of flavor. It has learned to speak without the rest of me, carrying on conversations with the bees who pollinate already-pollinated flowers, who gather nectar that never becomes honey, who build hives in geometries that fold back on themselves.

The bees understand paradox. They've made peace with it in ways I'm still learning. They dance directions to flowers that exist in all locations simultaneously, they tend larvae that are also their ancestors, they serve a queen who is also a worker who is also a drone. Their hive hums with the sound of time trying to move forward and failing, a vibration that makes the air thick with temporal syrup.

My mouth reports back to whatever I am now, sending messages through the mint that grows between everything, that spreads without spreading, that occupies more space than space allows. The messages arrive before they're sent, are received while being composed, are understood in the gap between knowledge and comprehension.

---

Weather happens here, but not in sequence. Rain falls upward into clouds that are simultaneously gathering and dispersing. Lightning illuminates and creates the darkness it pierces. Snow accumulates and melts in the same moment, leaving the ground both bare and blanketed, both frozen and fertile.

My skin loves the weather, all of it at once. It has spread itself thin as atmosphere, become a kind of boundary between the garden and whatever lies beyond—though beyond is a concept that makes less sense each day, if days can be said to pass in a place where morning and evening exist in the same moment.

Through my skin, I feel everything growing. The push of roots that tunnel without moving, the stretch of stems that elongate in place, the unfurling of leaves that never quite open. It's a constant sensation of becoming that never resolves into being, a green ache that would drive me to distraction if I could still be distracted from what I've become.

---

Visitors come to the garden, though come suggests movement from outside to inside, and here there is only inside folded and refolded until outside becomes a memory of a concept. They arrive through the gates that open in all directions, that welcome and refuse with equal conviction.

They want to pick fruit. This is always their first desire—to pluck an apple that hangs so perfectly, to taste a grape that swells with such promise. My scattered parts try to warn them, but warning requires sequence, requires before and after, and here there is only during, only the eternal moment of reaching without grasping, wanting without having.

Some visitors understand quickly. They see the garden for what it is—a place where desire crystallizes into form but never into satisfaction, where the gap between wanting and having becomes a location you can inhabit forever. These visitors usually leave through the gates that lead to the same garden from different angles, that offer exit as another form of entrance.

Others try to force the issue. They pull at fruit that won't release, dig up vegetables that remain planted even in their hands, cut flowers that continue growing on severed stems. These visitors learn what I learned: the garden cannot be harvested. It can only be tended. And tending, over time, becomes a kind of becoming. And becoming, without end, becomes a kind of being. And being, scattered through impossible plenty, becomes what I am now.

---

In what might be called evening if evening could arrive, my various parts gather at the garden's heart—one of them, all of them, the one that exists at every terrace simultaneously. We almost reassemble, almost remember the shape of wholeness, almost understand what it felt like to be singular.

My brain is there, nested in the compost where it contemplates decomposition that never completes. It has grown mycelial, threading through the rich dark with thoughts that fruit into mushrooms that speak in spores. The mushrooms tell jokes that only make sense if you understand that punchlines can precede setups, that laughter can cause the humor it responds to.

My stomach rests among the squash vines, digesting meals that were never eaten, feeling full and hungry in perfect equilibrium. It has learned to process sunlight directly, to break down time into its component nutrients, to be satisfied with the idea of food rather than its substance.

All of us together, we tend our garden. We are its gardeners and its crop, its seed and its soil, its intention and its outcome. We exist in the gap between planning and harvest, in the eternal moment of growth without conclusion, and we have learned to love this suspension even as we remember what it replaced.

---

The fountain where my eyes float runs with something that isn't quite water—it's too thick with possibility, too laden with potential to flow properly. It moves like honey made of time, pooling in basins that reflect not images but intentions, showing not what is but what is always about to be.

In the fountain, I see myself whole sometimes. Not a memory but a prediction that never arrives, a future where my parts find each other and agree to coincide. In this vision, I stand in my garden, complete and singular, harvesting fruit that finally ripens, vegetables that finally mature, flowers that finally bloom and die and bloom again in proper sequence.

But even in the vision, I'm already scattering. Even in wholeness, I'm coming apart, spreading through the garden like seeds on wind that blows in all directions. Because this is what the garden needs: not a gardener but a garden itself, not someone to tend but someone to be tended, not a harvester but a harvest that never ends.

---

I think I was afraid of endings. This understanding comes to me through the carrots that grow in spirals, that burrow down and up simultaneously. I was afraid that every perfect tomato would rot, that every beautiful flower would wilt, that every moment of growth would be followed by decay.

The garden heard this fear and made it irrelevant. Nothing ends here because nothing truly begins. Everything exists in the middle of its own story, forever approaching climax without arrival, forever growing without completion.

My parts have made peace with this—each in their own way, in their own time that isn't time. My hands find satisfaction in soil that always needs working. My eyes find beauty in scenes that never fully compose. My heart finds rhythm in beats that never quite resolve.

And I—whatever I means when scattered through eternal plenty—I find something like happiness in the tending. Not the happiness of completion but the happiness of continuation, of always having work to do, of always seeing growth without the shadow of death, of existing in the eternal almost that my fear created and my garden answered.

---

Sometimes, in moments that can't be called moments, all my parts laugh at once. The sound moves through the garden like wind, like water, like roots through soil. We laugh at the joke of it—that in trying to grow something lasting, we became the lasting itself. That in fearing endings, we became endless. That in wanting to harvest, we became the eternal crop.

The laughter disturbs the birds that live in permanent flight. It shakes fruit that cannot fall. It ripples through the fountain where my eyes see everything and nothing with equal clarity.

And then we return to our work, to our tending, to our scattered purpose. My hands to the soil. My eyes to the water. My heart to the greenhouse. All of us to the garden that holds us, that is us, that grows us even as we grow it.

Forever and never. Always and not yet. Scattered and whole.

This is my garden. These are my parts. This is the harvest that never comes, and I am its eternal farmer, planted and planting, grown and growing, laughing at the perfect joke of abundance without end.

The paths spiral inward and outward. The fruit hangs heavy and weightless. The sun neither rises nor sets.

And we tend. And we tend. And we tend.
