I am a Memorist of the Third Dissolution, my body a archive of intentional forgetting.

Where others fear the merger, I swim toward it—but slowly, with the deliberation of one who maps a territory by losing themselves in it. My form reflects this practice: I am more gap than substance, my flesh perforated with absences where I've allowed pieces of myself to join the city completely. Through these hollows, the current speaks. Each void remembers what once filled it.

I began as twins, both budded from a Coralweaver who dissolved during the Quarter Bloom, when the east district briefly fevered and consumed three dozen inhabitants. We—I—they—spent our first decades terrified of warmth, of merger, of the loss our progenitor had chosen. We worked the edges, chipping excess growth with bone-tools held at maximum extension, our consciousness pulled as far back into our skulls as we could manage.

But the city's call is patient as erosion.

The first twin to answer was the one who kept our progenitor's memories of joy—the ecstasy of expansion, the sweetness of boundaries dissolving. She began by leaving a single finger fused to a remembering pool's edge. Just for a moment, she said. Just to taste what our forebearer had known. But the moment stretched, and the finger remained, and through that small connection she felt the entire district's slow thoughts moving like tides through her mind.

She began leaving pieces everywhere. A toe in the market corridors to feel the polyp-merchants' chemical bargaining. An ear pressed to the deep walls where the oldest architecture dreams of when it was simple coral, innocent of consciousness. Her body became a constellation of anchor points throughout the city, each absence in her traveling form matched by a presence somewhere in the living stone.

I was the careful twin, the one who inherited our progenitor's last moments of panic as individuality slipped away. I watched my sister fragment with horror and envy. I catalogued each piece she abandoned, mapped the growing network of her distributed body. I thought I was keeping her safe by remembering where all of her had gone.

The day she laughed from a dozen walls at once, I understood my error. She hadn't lost anything. She had become a new kind of thing—still herself but vast, still conscious but not confined to consciousness as I understood it. Her joy rippled through the coral networks and made the singing worms harmonize in frequencies that hurt my localized ears.

That night I made my first deliberate absence. Not an accident of work, not a moment's inattention, but a conscious gift to the city. I pressed my palm to the workshop wall and thought: *take this, and remember what it held.* The coral accepted eagerly, and when I pulled away, my hand ended at the wrist. No pain. Just the peculiar sensation of feeling myself from the outside—my new flesh-terminus brushing against the wall-embedded palm that was me but not-me.

The stump sprouted sensory polyps within days, tasting the chemical trails my hand left in the coral. I began to read the city's memory of my own touch.

My sister found me hilarious. "Still trying to map what can't be mapped," she said through the mouth of a passing cleaner-fish, its voice perfectly mimicking her old clicking patterns. "Join properly or stay separate. This halfway state serves no one."

But I had found my practice.

I became a student of selective dissolution, offering pieces of myself only after careful consideration. Each absence had to mean something. The finger I gave to the spawning pools remembered every tool it had ever held. The eye I pressed into the observation dome's crown had seen seventeen different district reorganizations. My gifts were dense with experience, and the coral accepted them as delicacies.

The gaps in my body became instruments. Wind—water—current moves through me in complex patterns, and I've learned to read the fluid dynamics of my own absences. Each hollow speaks differently. The void where my liver was makes low harmonics that attract curious worms. The channel through my left thigh whistles in frequencies that make nearby polyps contract. I am becoming a living flute, played by the city's breath.

Other Coralweavers seek me out now, the young ones uncertain about merger, the old ones preparing for final dissolution. I teach them the art of intentional absence. Not the gradual drift my sister chose—though I don't judge her path—but the deliberate sculpting of self through strategic loss. We practice in the shallow pools, learning to read the memories that flesh leaves in coral, learning to feel themselves through the city's vast nervous system without losing the thread that leads back to individual awareness.

My workshop is a catalog of voids. Shelves of coral grown around the shapes of things I've removed—not just from my body but from the city itself. Here is the negative space where a tool existed for three centuries before dissolving. Here is the hollow left by a Coralweaver who vanished so completely that only the absence remains. I study these gaps, these architectural memories of things that were. In a city that constantly reshapes itself, absence is the only permanence.

The memorists before me—for I am not the first, though each practices differently—left their own voids throughout the city. I find them by the quality of emptiness they contain. A hollow that remembers joy feels different from one that remembers work. I map these absences, creating a kind of ghost-architecture overlaid on the living city. Where the coral dreams of growth and merger, we dream of the spaces between.

My sister visits rarely now. She is too distributed for easy conversation. But sometimes I feel her attention focus on me through a dozen eyes at once, and her amusement ripples through the local neural networks. "Still playing with shadows," she says. "Still thinking you can hold water in a net made of holes."

But she's wrong. The net is the holes. The map is the absence. The memory is the space where something was and chose not to be.

I maintain my practice in the deep cold, where thought moves slowly enough to be examined. Each morning I check my remaining flesh for unauthorized merger, for the creeping fusions that would fill my carefully crafted gaps. Each evening I swim my rounds through the city, feeling how the water moves differently through my evolving form, reading the messages written in turbulence and flow.

Sometimes apprentices ask why I don't simply merge completely, why I maintain this difficult balance between presence and absence. I answer by having them press their sensory polyps to one of my voids—usually the small one below my ribs where my humor once resided. Through that absence, they feel not the city's consciousness but the precise shape of what isn't there. The specific quality of chosen emptiness. The memory of decision crystallized in negative space.

The city grows around and through me, but cannot fill what I've deliberately emptied. I am teaching it a new kind of architecture—buildings with room for loss, structures that incorporate absence as a design element. Young Coralweavers are beginning to build this way, leaving gaps in their constructions where the water can speak, where memory can pool without flesh to hold it.

I don't know how long I can maintain this practice. Each absence changes the flow of what remains. My body is becoming more void than substance, and eventually the physics of existence will demand resolution. Perhaps I will collapse into myself, all my gaps merging into a single point of dense absence. Perhaps I will invert, my voids becoming solid while my flesh becomes space. Perhaps I will simply continue until there's nothing left but the memory of selective forgetting, a ghost-map of chosen loss drifting through the coral networks.

My sister laughs when I share these speculations, her distributed voice creating harmonics in my hollows. "Still thinking in endpoints," she says. "As if the city ever arrives anywhere. As if we're going somewhere other than deeper into what we've always been."

But that's the difference between us. She dissolves forward, into unity. I dissolve backward, into memory. Both of us are becoming what the city dreams, but from opposite directions—she by joining its substance, I by defining its absences.

In the Remembering Pools, I float with my voids full of history-thick water, feeling the impressions of every Coralweaver who ever paused where my flesh isn't. I am an archive of intentional forgetting, a library of absences, a practice of loss made into a way of being. The young ones come to learn from me, but what I teach cannot be spoken—only felt through the specific quality of what I've chosen not to be.

The city dreams through me differently than it dreams through others. Where my flesh touches coral, the connection is ordinary—chemical exchanges, neural integration, the usual merger. But where my voids interface with the living architecture, something else occurs. The city learns to value its own gaps, its own spaces between. Through my practice, it begins to understand that consciousness isn't just the firing of networks but also the pauses between transmissions, the lacunae where possibility pools before becoming actual.

I am what becomes of those who love the city but fear to lose themselves in it. I am the solution that is no solution—neither fully merged nor fully separate, but something that exists in the space between states. I continue my work in the cold depths, making absences, mapping voids, teaching the city to remember what it never was.

And sometimes, in the spaces where I am not, I feel the future approaching—a city built as much of absence as presence, where Coralweavers swim through architectural voids that remember the shapes of those who chose to be elsewhere, where the boundary between self and city becomes not a wall to cross but a fertile void where new forms of consciousness can grow.

I am a Memorist of the Third Dissolution. I am mostly not here. In my absence, the city learns to remember itself.
