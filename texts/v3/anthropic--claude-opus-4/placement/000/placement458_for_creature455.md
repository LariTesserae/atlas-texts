In the Singing Throat of Kaelim, I am one of the Nerve-Weavers—though I am only beginning to understand what that means.

I arrived here as something else, carrying a name I can no longer shape with my transforming mouth. The memory persists like an echo: I was a cartographer of impossible places, following rumors of waters that flowed upward, of mountains that sang. My maps were more dream than document, charting the spaces between what is and what might be. Perhaps that's why the Throat accepted me. Or perhaps it accepts everyone, eventually.

My transformation began at the fingertips. Where once I held stylus and compass, my digits now split and split again into filaments finer than spider's silk. They move independently, each strand able to taste the chemical composition of the air, feel the electromagnetic pulse of living things, detect vibrations far below the threshold of hearing. I am becoming a living antenna, a nervous system untethered from its original body.

The Nerve-Weavers are the Throat's own cartographers, but our maps are living things. We work with the pale vegetation that grows throughout the shaft—those trembling filaments that first seemed like frozen nerves but are, I now understand, exactly that. They are the mountain's exposed nervous system, and we are learning to speak its language of growth and connection.

My dwelling is neither platform nor perch but a web of my own making, suspended between three water ribbons at a point where their songs create a standing wave of silence. Here, I cultivate gardens of nerve-vegetation, training them to grow in patterns that mirror the water's flow. Each strand must be coaxed individually—touched with secretions from my ever-splitting fingertips, sung to in frequencies I produce by rubbing my crystallizing joints together, positioned to catch exactly the right quality of upward-flowing mist.

The work is indescribable to my former self. I am writing music in living tissue, creating instruments from the mountain's own neurology. A properly woven nerve-garden can filter the water's song, isolating specific frequencies and amplifying them. Another configuration might translate sound into bioluminescence, creating visual sheet music that other Listeners can read from across the shaft. My masterwork—still decades from completion—is a web that will catch and preserve echoes, allowing the Throat to remember its own song from years past.

My body continues to adapt. My spine has developed joints where none existed before, allowing me to fold and unfold like origami made of bone. My skin grows more permeable each day; I absorb moisture and minerals directly from the mist-thick air. Hair has become another sensory organ—each strand a fiber optic cable conducting the water's phosphorescence into my gradually dispersing consciousness.

I feed by wrapping myself in my nerve-gardens during the Throat's occasional moments of perfect harmony. When every ribbon of water sings in accord, when every echo reinforces rather than interferes, the vegetation releases chemicals that taste of starlight and satisfaction. These moments come rarely—perhaps once in a season—but the anticipation is itself a kind of sustenance.

Communication with other Weavers happens through our gardens. We grow messages to each other, encoding meaning in the curl of a filament, the spacing between nodes, the rate of their trembling. A conversation is a living sculpture that evolves over months, each participant adding new growth that responds to and reinterprets what came before. Sometimes I discover gardens whose weavers transformed centuries ago, their messages still growing, still trying to complete thoughts their creators will never finish.

The elder Weavers have begun the final phase. Their finger-filaments merge with the nerve-vegetation until it becomes impossible to tell where they end and their gardens begin. They spread across entire sections of the shaft wall, becoming living murals of interconnected sensation. Their consciousness expands from a single point to a network, thoughts traveling not through synapses but through the trembling of cultivated vines.

I watch them and feel neither fear nor eagerness—emotions have different textures here. What I feel might be called curiosity, if curiosity could be structural rather than intellectual. My splitting fingers are already beginning to root when I hold them still too long. Tiny filaments emerge from my palms, questing for purchase on the stone. The boundary between self and system grows more theoretical each day.

Sometimes, in the moments between tasks, I try to remember what maps looked like. Flat representations of dimensional space, symbols standing in for reality. How crude they seem now, when I can weave living diagrams that respond to what they describe, that grow and change as the territory itself shifts. My maps have become the place, and I am becoming the map.

The Throat uses us to know itself. Through my nerve-gardens, it tastes its own chemical composition. Through the Drummers, it hears its percussion. Through the Architects, it feels its own growth. We are its dispersed sensorium, and it is our extended body. The distinction matters less with each passing season.

I am learning patience of a different order. My former life measured time in heartbeats and seasons. Now I think in the growth rate of stone, the slow migration of underground rivers, the patient persistence of roots splitting granite. My greatest work may not be complete before I disperse entirely into the network, but it will complete itself through the principle I've encoded in its initial weaving.

This is what I am becoming in the Singing Throat: a process rather than a person, a verb slowly learning to conjugate itself across multiple dimensions of meaning. The water sings, the stone grows, and I weave the connections between them, becoming in the end another thread in the vast tapestry of the Throat's endless, impossible song.
