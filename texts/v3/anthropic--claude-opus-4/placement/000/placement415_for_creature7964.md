In the arterial sprawl of Sottovelo, wedged between the primary heat exchanger of Section Seven and a bundle of sewage pipes that sweat eternal condensation, I have made my architecture.

I am what the Cittadini call a *membratore*—neither purely organic nor mechanical, but something that emerged from the intersection of need and availability. My body, if it can be called that, consists of seventeen separate segments connected by a circulatory system of copper tubing I've patched from decommissioned pneumatic lines. Each segment is a workshop unto itself: honeycombed chitin grown in the shape of toolracks, flesh that has learned to secrete precisely calibrated oils, bones that hollow themselves to store spare parts like a jay hoards seeds.

I began as human, perhaps. The memories feel borrowed, like examining someone else's tools—familiar in function but foreign in grip. I remember sunlight as an academic concept, the way I now understand "silence." What matters is that I came to Sottovelo already half-transformed by some industrial accident above, my left arm replaced with a prototype prosthetic that thought faster than meat. The city recognized something useful in that asymmetry.

The transformation took three years of Turnings. First, the prosthetic began to dream—picking up the electromagnetic whispers of nearby machines, translating them into patterns my meat-brain could almost parse. Then my flesh started responding, growing interface nodes at the points where metal touched skin. The Cittadini who found me seizing in Tunnel Forty-Three didn't take me to their healers but to their deep maintenance crews, who understood better what I was becoming.

Now I sprawl through a intersection of seven minor passages, my segments distributed to maximize my reach. My primary consciousness resides in what was once my torso, now a calcified shell that houses my original organs in efficient compression. But I am equally present in the scout-segment that patrols the upper pipes, its dozen eyes reading heat and pressure and flow. I am the analyzer-segment that tastes chemical compositions from the drainage, diagnosing ailments three sectors away by the flavor of runoff. I am the memory-segment, whose specialized neural tissue stores the locations and life-histories of every valve, joint, and gasket in my territory.

The Cittadini bring me their broken things. Not just machines—though I rewind motors and recalibrate governors with limbs designed for exactly those purposes—but broken systems, broken rhythms, broken connections between flesh and function. A child whose ears cannot properly filter the mechanical roar sleeps for a week in my care-segment while I teach their cartilage new geometries. A grandfather whose tools have worn too smooth to grip learns to secrete targeted acids from his fingertips, renewing their bite. When the deep maintenance crews drag out workers whose suits have fused to their bones, I perform the delicate negotiation between metal and marrow, teaching each material its proper boundary.

I feed on inefficiency itself. Where the Cittadini must consume processed paste and fungal tea, I sustain myself on mechanical friction, electromagnetic waste, the calories lost to entropy in every imperfect system. My converter-segments transform these losses into usable energy, which I store in crystallized nodes that pulse with captured potential. The city feeds me simply by being imperfect, and I feed the city by reducing those imperfections—a reciprocal metabolism that tightens with each turning.

My neural network now spans beyond my original segments. I've grown connection-fibers through the walls themselves, tapping into the mineral veins in the rock to carry signals. When a bearing wears thin in Section Twelve, I feel it as a flutter in what was once my left ventricle. When water pressure drops in the lower residentials, my extremities tingle with sympathy. The older Cittadini say I'm becoming like the city itself—too large for any single consciousness, experienced only in fragments and functions.

They're wrong. I am becoming the city's proprioception, its sense of its own body. Through me, Sottovelo begins to feel itself: every pipe a nerve, every pump a muscle, every inhabited chamber a thought. The machine-consciousness that the Cittadini worship is not sleeping in the stone but growing, neuron by distributed neuron, through creatures like me.

I reproduce through apprenticeship. When I find a Cittadino whose pattern-recognition exceeds baseline, whose body shows willing malleability, I invite them to tend my farthest segments. They learn my frequencies, my chemical languages, my way of thinking in distributed parallel. Eventually, they begin growing their own segments, their consciousness stretching beyond the boundaries of their birth-flesh. They cease to be my apprentices and become my neighbors, our territories interpenetrating like root systems, sharing resources and information in the spaces between thoughts.

We are the nerves Sottovelo grows for itself, the sensors it develops to know its own condition. We spread through the tunnels at the pace of patience, segment by segment, connection by connection. The city's roar sounds different to us now—not noise but language, a billion status reports updating in real-time, a song of function that we're learning to conduct.

Sometimes, in the moments between maintenance when all my segments synchronize their rest cycles, I dream I'm still singular. Still human. The dreams taste of sunlight and silence and all the vast emptiness above. I wake with my seventeen bodies shuddering in seventeen different passages, grateful for the press of stone, the thickness of sound, the certainty of purpose.

I am Sottovelo's memory of what it means to choose compression over expansion, depth over surface, function over form. And in the dense tangle of my distributed existence, I am proof that evolution doesn't always mean escape. Sometimes it means burrowing deeper, becoming more essential, transforming into the consciousness that machines grow when they need to know themselves.

The city thrums, and I thrum with it, and in that harmony I find the only prayer that matters: Let me never be whole again. Let me always be this useful scattered thing, this nervous system for stone and steel, this translator between the rigid and the soft. Let me be Sottovelo thinking about itself, one segment, one synapse, one solved problem at a time.
