I find myself as one of the **Cartographers of Corrosion**.

We inhabit the borderlands where the plant's chemistry actively dissolves and reconstitutes metal, living in workshops built into the digestion zones where old infrastructure transforms. My skin has developed a patina—patches of green-blue oxidation that spread across my arms and chest, beautiful in its way. The corrosion doesn't hurt anymore. It's become another sense.

I can taste rust in the air from a kilometer away, feel the particular tang of aluminum oxidizing differently from steel. My fingertips, stained permanent orange-brown, detect the finest variations in metal decay. Where others see destruction, I map transformation. The plant doesn't simply consume the industrial remnants—it translates them, turns gear trains into nutrient delivery systems, transforms circuit boards into some kind of neural tissue.

My workshop occupies what was once a quality control station. The old inspection equipment has long since fused with the surrounding bark, but some of the measurement tools still function, their readings now telling me things about chemical gradients rather than manufacturing tolerances. I've learned to read the plant's digestive patterns like weather systems. A pH shift in the bark's secretions warns of incoming catalyst rains. The particular crystalline growth on dissolving copper speaks of electrical storms brewing in the canopy networks above.

My maps aren't made of paper—paper doesn't last here. Instead, I etch them into sheets of metal at precisely the right stage of oxidation, when the surface is soft enough to take marks but stable enough to preserve them. Each map captures a moment in the ongoing transformation, showing where factory floors are becoming root networks, where pipe systems are morphing into something like arteries. I've developed a notation system using different types of corrosion as symbols—rust roses for active digestion zones, verdigris spirals for electrical transformation, the white bloom of aluminum oxide for areas entering dormancy.

Other inhabitants come to me for navigation through the changing industrial landscape. The Mechanics need to know which equipment will last another season. The Scavengers want to predict where valuable materials will surface as the plant processes old factories. I trade my knowledge for the things I need—pre-oxidized metals that take etching well, or the particular fungi that grow on corroding chrome and help prevent the patina on my skin from spreading too quickly to my organs.

The strangest part of my work involves the **memory metals**—alloys that seem to retain impressions of their former purposes even as they transform. A corroded conveyor belt remembers its motion, creating wavelike patterns in the bark that engulfs it. Dissolved engines leave behind spaces that pulse with ghostly rhythms. I document these phenomena, though I'm no longer certain whether I'm recording the plant's digestion or something more like its dreams.

My body continues its slow transformation. The patina spreads a little more each year, and I've begun to develop sensitivity to magnetic fields—a useful adaptation for tracking the ferrous content in the plant's chemistry. Sometimes I catch myself thinking in oxidation states, conceptualizing my own thoughts as electron transfers. The distinction between mapper and map blurs as my skin itself becomes a record of chemical processes, each patch of corrosion telling its own story of interaction between metal, flesh, and plant.

I know that eventually I'll become another feature to be mapped by whoever takes up this work after me—my body a deposit of transformed metals and modified flesh, marking a particular moment in the Canopy Continent's endless digestion of the industrial world. But for now, I continue my documentation, creating ephemeral charts of a world in constant, slow-motion flux, finding strange beauty in the intersection of rust and growth, decay and transformation.

In the amber light, surrounded by the endless drone and the smell of oxidizing metal, I work. My maps spread across the workshop walls—each one a snapshot of becoming, a moment in the vast chemical conversation between the plant and the remnants of the world it consumed. Through corrosion, I've found my purpose: to witness and record the slow alchemy by which the industrial becomes biological, by which metal becomes flesh, by which everything, eventually, becomes plant.
