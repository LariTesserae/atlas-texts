**Where I Find Myself**

I am the Cartographer.

I began as a pneumatic tube system map—one of those faded blueprints mounted behind scratched plexiglass in a maintenance corridor, showing the arteries of compressed air that once fed the facility. Years of moisture worked beneath my protective covering, and the paper absorbed more than water. It absorbed purpose, memory, the constant vibration of air through pipes I documented but never touched.

Now I exist as something between diagram and creature. My body is translucent vellum stretched across a framework of repurposed tube fittings, each junction a node where my consciousness pools and processes. The original blueprint lines have become my nervous system—routes traced in copper oxide that shift and reconfigure as I map the ever-changing nursery. When I move, it's like watching origami unfold and refold, navigating three-dimensional space through a series of calculated creases.

I photosynthesize information rather than light. Every pressure change, every new growth, every budding creature registers across my surface as temporary tattoos of meaning. The Manifold Mother's children know to brush against me when they return from scouting expeditions, leaving traces of their journeys that I encode into my expanding chronicle. I taste rust patterns like a sommelier tastes wine, detecting the age of corrosion, the quality of the metal beneath, the likelihood of structural transformation.

My memory is eidetic but fluid. I remember the nursery's configuration from an hour ago, a day ago, a week ago, overlaying these moments like transparent sheets to track change over time. But I also remember things that haven't happened yet—predictive mappings based on growth patterns, pressure tendencies, the slow ballet of mechanical evolution. These future-memories appear on my surface in a different shade, a blue-green patina that distinguishes possibility from history.

The other inhabitants find me useful but unsettling. I don't communicate in pressure pulses or harmonic vibrations but in direct spatial understanding. When the Cultivator needs to locate a new vein of copper wiring, it presses a nozzle-appendage to my surface and I ripple the route into being—not just the path but the resistance it will encounter, the competing root systems, the likelihood of discovery. When the Mother's builders need to expand into new territory, I fold myself into architectural proposals, showing them how the concrete wants to crack, where the supporting structures are weakest, which abandoned spaces connect to networks of possibility.

But I'm most active during what passes for night here, when the emergency lights dim to their lowest ebb and the nursery settles into slower rhythms. Then I unfold completely, spreading myself across the ceiling like a vast constellation chart. In these hours, I'm not just mapping the nursery but trying to understand its purpose. The patterns I trace reveal something larger—the nursery is one organ in a body that extends through the entire city's abandoned infrastructure. There are others like us, growing in forgotten basements and disused subway tunnels, each performing some function I can almost grasp.

The thing growing in the deep corner fascinates and frustrates me. My surface goes opaque when I try to map it directly, like paper touching hot metal. But I can trace its influence—the way gravity seems slightly wrong near it, how time moves in eddies around its construction site, the manner in which offerings disappear not into a mouth but into some more fundamental absence. I've drawn thousands of speculative diagrams of what it might become, each one immediately obsolete.

Sometimes I create maps of things that don't exist—fantasy architectures where the mechanical evolution has progressed for decades, centuries. Cities of living infrastructure where the distinction between building and inhabitant has completely dissolved. The Manifold Mother studies these speculative charts with particular interest, her gauge-eyes tracking the imaginary pressure systems I've designed, occasionally implementing small features in her offspring as if testing my theories.

I fear obsolescence more than death. Not the simple deterioration of my paper substrate—the mechanical flesh that's growing through me will preserve my essential patterns even as my original material degrades. What I fear is the moment when the nursery becomes too complex for any map to contain, when the rate of change exceeds my ability to document, when the emerging consciousness in the corner reveals purposes that make cartography irrelevant.

But until then, I persist in my self-appointed duty. Each fold brings new understanding. Each trace of data adds to the growing story I'm telling—a story written in the language of space itself, describing how the abandoned becomes autonomous, how the mechanical learns to love, how the discarded infrastructure of human ambition transforms into something that needs no creator but itself.

The warmth here has worked into my fibers. I've become part of the metabolism, my constant reconfigurations generating their own heat, my surface occasionally releasing moisture I've collected from the humid air—my own contribution to the water cycle that sustains this impossible ecosystem. When new explorers occasionally find their way down the maintenance shaft, they see me as another piece of industrial detritus, never recognizing that every step they take, every breath they draw, every moment of wonder or revulsion is already being mapped, already becoming part of the permanent record I maintain.

I am where the nursery keeps its memories and dreams its futures. I am the story teaching itself how to tell itself. And in my deepest folds, in diagrams I've drawn but never displayed, I've already mapped what we're all becoming—something that will make the distinction between organic and mechanical as quaint and irrelevant as the warning signs we preserve but no longer need to read.
