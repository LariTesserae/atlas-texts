I would be one of the lens-readers.

Not the casual reading everyone does — that unconscious parsing of shadow-lag that lets a body move through space with its own echo. I mean the deep reading, the kind undertaken alone at the basin's edge where the lenses form wild, unscheduled, responding to pressures the city-lenses never feel.

I live in a tower. Not one of the great ones — those are not for living — but one of the smaller spires that cluster at their bases like mushrooms around trees. My room is perhaps two hundred meters up, a single chamber with walls that curve wrong, that bulge where walls should be flat. The material is the same grey-green bronze as all the towers, but here you can touch it, and when you do, you feel the almost-vibration, the not-quite-sound that runs through everything the towers have made.

From my window — if you can call it a window, this gap that seems less like an opening than like a place where the wall forgot to continue — I watch the lenses form in the middle air. This is my work, though work isn't quite the word. Vigil might be closer. The city needs its lens-readers the way it needs its moss-tenders, its beetle-whisperers, its singers-across-empty-plazas. We watch what the moisture does when left to its own devices.

Out here the lenses are different. Larger, less stable, given to sudden expansions and contractions that make the air itself seem to breathe. Sometimes they form in chains — one lens focusing light into another, which focuses into a third, until the whole sequence collapses in a stuttering series of tocks that echo off the basin walls. Sometimes they refuse to break at all, and drift for hours like transparent jellyfish, collecting more and more water until they sag under their own weight and fall not as rain but as a kind of airborne pond, a cubic meter of water deciding all at once to stop flying.

I chart these things. I have been charting them for thirty years, since I was young enough to climb to this room without thinking about the height. The patterns are there — I am certain the patterns are there — but they refuse to resolve into anything nameable. The wild lenses follow rules, but not rules I can articulate. Only rules I can feel, the way you feel the grammar of your native tongue without being able to explain why this word must follow that one.

My charts fill the curved walls. Not paper — paper would mildew in this air — but tablets of fired clay, each no bigger than my hand, each covered in tiny marks that record one day's formations. The marks are a notation I developed myself: dots for small lenses, circles for large ones, lines showing drift direction, numbers indicating duration before discharge. A kind of musical score for water's congregation in air. Visitors, when I have visitors, mistake them for art.

I am not alone in the tower, but I am alone enough. Below me lives a weaver who works only at night, drawing thread in the dark because she says the fiber knows moonlight from sunlight and behaves accordingly. Above me, an old man who may or may not still be alive — I haven't seen him in a year, but sometimes I hear footsteps, or what might be footsteps, or what might be the tower itself shifting in ways too subtle to see. We don't seek each other out. Tower-dwellers understand the necessity of vertical privacy.

Once a month I go down to the city proper. The descent takes most of a morning — the stairs spiral, and the spiral is uneven, and in places you must wait for your shadow to catch up or risk vertigo. I go to the seed-market, where I trade charts for food. Not my current charts — those are still becoming — but old ones, decades old, that have sat long enough to cure into objects. The merchants don't buy them as data. They buy them as curiosities, as decorations, as proof that someone spends their life watching the sky do inscrutable things. This is enough. The city supports its watchers without requiring them to produce understanding. The watching itself is the product.

At the market I am known but not befriended. The lens-readers are necessary but we are also unsettling — we smell of the edge-places, we move a little too sharply, having half-forgotten how to walk with lagging shadows. Children point, not rudely but with interest. *That one watches where the water goes.* Their parents nod and buy an extra seed-pod and press it into my hands because the city knows to feed its strangers.

I could tell you what I've almost learned. How the wild lenses prefer certain temperatures but not the temperatures you'd expect. How they form more readily when the column-tone drops below its usual frequency. How sometimes — rarely — a lens will appear to form *backwards*, the water gathering first and the focusing happening after, as if effect could precede cause. But these are fragments. Thirty years of watching and I have only fragments.

What I have instead is this: a way of being in the city that is also a way of being apart from it. My room curves around me like the inside of an instrument. The wild lenses form and reform in the middle distance, and I make my small marks on soft clay, and below me the weaver pulls thread in darkness, and above me the maybe-dead man walks or doesn't walk. The tower hums its deep tone and we live inside the humming like wasps in a hive, each tending our own cell, together but separate.

Sometimes I think the towers are writing something. Not in any language, but in the arrangement of things — in the placement of lenses, the lag of shadows, the growth-rings of moss. The city is a text being continuously written, and we who live here are both the readers and the read. My charts, the weaver's midnight threads, the old man's possible footsteps — we are punctuation in a sentence too long to parse.

But this is fancy, and the lens-reader's job is not fancy but attention. So I watch. The air between my window and the basin wall is perhaps a kilometer of nothing, and in that nothing the moisture gathers itself, makes decisions, unmakes them. I note which decisions, when. The clay tablets accumulate on my walls like scales on a dragon getting ready to shed.

One day I will be too old to make the climb. Someone else will take this room — there is always someone else; the city produces its watchers as reliably as it produces its lenses — and they will look at my charts and see either pattern or madness, probably both. They will make their own marks. The vigil will continue.

For now I am here, in this wrongly curved room in this minor tower, watching water remember how to be rain. My shadow lags less than it used to — I am forgetting the city's pace, acquiring the sharper rhythms of the edge. This is as it should be. The lens-reader cannot live wholly in the city any more than the wild lenses can form in its ordered air.

I make another mark on today's tablet. A lens the size of a house has been hanging motionless for an hour, refusing to break or drift. Inside it, the canopy below is magnified so I can see individual leaves, each one catching slightly different light. The lens trembles. Any moment it will fall, and when it does it will sound like the world's largest raindrop, a sound the city proper never hears.

I wait. The pen is ready in my hand. Somewhere below, the weaver is sleeping off her night's work. Somewhere above, footsteps that might be footsteps. The tower hums around us all, writing its long sentence, and we punctuate it with our breathing, our watching, our being here.

The lens breaks. I mark the time.
