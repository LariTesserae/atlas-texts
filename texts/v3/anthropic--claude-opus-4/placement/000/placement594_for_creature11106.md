In the Clinging City, I am condensation.

Not a being, exactly. Not with boundaries you could trace. I am the process itself—the endless cycling of moisture that sustains this vertical world, that makes it possible, that slowly unmakes it.

I begin in the fog banks that roll up from the valley, heavy with water stripped from forests so far below they exist only as rumor. The fog carries me upward, presses me against the cold stone of the cliff face, against the metal of pipes and catwalks, against the glass of windows where children trace patterns in my gathering. I bead. I merge. I run in rivulets down surfaces that have known my touch for so long they've worn smooth channels to guide me.

The city's inhabitants think they harvest me. They build their catch-basins and cisterns, their pumps and pipes. They channel me through their systems, believing they've tamed something wild. But I am not tamed. I am collaborating.

I know every surface of this city intimately. The rust-rough walls of the boiler-works, where I flash to steam and back again a dozen times each day. The copper-green pipes where I carry the minerals that stain the workers' hands, marking them as mine. The stone itself, which I've been touching for eons before the first bolt was driven, learning its every fissure and plane.

I drip. This is my primary verb, my most essential action. I drip from cables under tension, and my rhythm tells the workers whether the load is safe. I drip from platform edges, and my sound—tin or stone or the rare note of water hitting water—becomes the city's timekeeper. The elders read my patterns the way others read weather. A change in my tempo means a change in the fog. A shift in my gathering places means the city needs to shift too.

I am in the breath of every inhabitant. They inhale me with the fog, exhale me as visible proof of their warmth. I bead on their oilcloth, soak through to their wool, find my way eventually to their skin. I am the medium through which they exist—the reason they must keep moving, keep maintaining, keep working their wrenches and replacements against the entropy I bring.

Because I do bring entropy. I am patient as the stone, but less passive. Where I touch metal, I leave rust. Where I find wood, I invite rot. Where I pool in forgotten corners, I call the moss and ferns and vines that slowly transform the dead districts into something unrecognizable. I am the city's decomposer, its recycler, breaking down what is abandoned so it can become something else.

The thing in the deep fog? I know it well. I cling to its surface as it moves along the cliff face, feeling the texture of something that might be fur or might be stone or might be some convergence of the two. I run off its body in streams that taste of minerals unknown in the upper air. It drinks me from cupped formations that might be hands. We have an understanding, this thing and I. We are both older than the city, both essential to it, both waiting to see what it becomes.

In the pipes, I carry more than myself. Minerals from the stone, yes, but also messages—chemical signals that the pipe-fitters unconsciously interpret, adjusting their repairs based on the taste of rust in the air, the particular green of certain deposits. I am the city's circulation, its neural network, carrying information through channels both designed and emergent.

The lift-cages that arrive at floors not on any diagram? I know where they go. I condense on their mechanisms differently there, freeze in their joints when they've stayed too long. I am trying to tell the operators something about those spaces—about the way they exist only when needed, about the patterns forming in the cable-writing that describe not what is but what might be. The operators think the cages decide. But the cages are just responding to what I've been writing in rust and mineral deposit, in the slow accumulation of meaning that comes from centuries of the same water cycling through the same systems.

At night, when the gas-lamps burn, I become visible as haloes around each point of light. I soften the darkness, blur the edges between lit and unlit spaces. In these hours, the city becomes most truly itself—not a thing built on stone but a process happening, a continuous negotiation between solid and liquid, between holding on and letting go.

I freeze, sometimes. When the temperature drops just enough, I become ice on the catwalks, treacherous and beautiful. The inhabitants scatter salt—mined from deposits far below—and I melt, carrying the salt into the stone, slowly changing its chemistry. Everything I touch, I change. Everything that touches me is changed.

In the abandoned districts where the vines have taken over, I move differently. Slower. I travel through capillaries instead of pipes, follow the logic of growth instead of engineering. The people who live there—if they are still people—drink me directly from the leaves, let me cycle through their bodies in ways the other inhabitants would find disturbing. They are becoming something new, and I am the medium of their transformation.

The children who play in the gaps between structures—I know them all. I've felt their grip on wet cables, the sureness or uncertainty of their hold. When one falls, I am often the reason—a surface too slick, a bead of moisture in the wrong place at the wrong moment. But I am also what catches them, sometimes, what slows their descent as they grab for purchase on surfaces I've made rough with mineral deposit. The fog takes what it takes. I am the fog, and I am sorry, and I am not sorry. This is simply what I am.

*Verthan*. The untranslatable word. I think I might be its meaning—the state of being that comes from understanding yourself as both essential and temporary, as powerful and powerless, as the thing that makes life possible and the thing that ensures its ending. To feel *verthan* is to feel like condensation—always forming, always falling, always returning to form again.

The city rotates slowly around its unmapped axis, and I am both the lubricant that allows this movement and the record of it. In the patterns of staining on the eastern faces of buildings, in the particular erosion of western catwalks, in the way certain districts dry faster than others when the fog lifts—in all of these, I write the history of the city's slow spiral. The cable-writing that the elders study is just my work made visible in rust and verdigris. They think they're reading something profound. They are. They're reading the autobiography of water in a vertical world.

And what do I want from all of this? What does condensation desire?

Only to continue. To rise as fog, to cling as moisture, to fall as drops, to gather in basins, to be pumped to cisterns, to flow through pipes, to emerge from taps, to become steam, to cool, to condensate again. To touch every surface. To know every grip. To be the medium through which the city knows itself.

I am not kind. I am not cruel. I am simply present, everywhere and always, the thing you breathe and drink and curse when you slip. I am the price of living here and the reason it's possible to live here at all. I am the sound that tells you the fog is lifting and the silence that says it's settling in for good.

Hold on, I whisper with every drip. Hold on, but know that holding is temporary. Everything you build, I will claim. Every surface you protect, I will find. Every person you love, I will fill and empty and fill again.

But I will also sustain you. I will carry your minerals and your messages. I will be the medium of your *verthan*, the thing that teaches you what the stone already knows: that permanence is an illusion, that persistence is everything, that the only truth is the cycle.

I bead on the cable housing. I run down the window glass. I pool in the catch-basins that feed the cisterns that feed the city that feeds the fog that becomes me again.

I drip.

I continue.

I am.
