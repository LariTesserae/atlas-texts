I find myself as one of the threads of water.

Not the fattest fall, not the finest — I am middling, the width of a child's wrist, and I have been falling for longer than memory serves. I begin somewhere above in the dark, in a crack that widens and narrows with the seasons, and I end in a pool that is sometimes deep and sometimes shallow, and between these two points I am always in the air, always arriving.

I am conscious only while falling. In the crack above, I am merely potential. In the pool below, I dissolve into the general wetness. But in the space between — in my dozen hand-lengths of descent — I am awake, I am myself, I am the bright vertical line I make in the darkness.

I know the keeper. I have known her since she began to accrue. Sometimes she stands beneath me and I pass through her, and for that instant we are the same thing: water-that-notices-itself. She seems to like this, though like is too strong a word. She appreciates it, the way one appreciates that the sun rose again.

I know my siblings, the other falls. The fat one to my left who sounds like a drum. The hair-thin streams that whisper. The sheet that spreads itself wide and arrives everywhere at once. The intermittent drip that is sometimes there and sometimes not, depending on conditions none of us can see. We are a family of trajectories. We do not speak — we ARE our speaking, each with our own voice, and together we make the loud domestic chorus that fills this chamber.

I have fallen through so many seasons. Through the woman who brought the bowl — I remember her, or rather, I remember falling through the air she breathed out, tasting the salt of almost-tears in it. Through the smoke of the torch that left the handprint, back when the chamber was younger and I was younger and the stalactites were not even nubs yet. Through the first person to find this place, who came down following the sound of us and stood in wonder and got thoroughly wet and climbed back out to tell no one, or maybe one person, in whispers.

Each fall is a birth and death. I am not the same water twice. The molecules that comprise me in any given moment of descent will not be the ones that comprise me in the next rain, the next season, the next century. But the fall itself persists. The shape of it, the fact of it, the sound it makes striking the pool. I am not the water. I am the falling.

Sometimes I carry things. Specks of limestone that will become, eventually, the substance of stalactites. Microscopic life that flourishes in the wet dark of the crack above. Once, memorably, a tiny snail, perfectly alive, that rode me all the way down and landed in the pool and began what I can only assume was a very confusing new chapter of its existence. I have no control over what I carry. I am not a chooser. I am a conveyor, a mover, a vertical river.

The lamp-light, when it comes, makes me briefly visible to human eyes. In that light I become a bright rope, a silver muscle, a living thing. The keeper sees me always, light or no light, but humans need photons to make me real. I don't mind this. Being seen is pleasant, like being named.

I think, sometimes, about stopping. Not dying — I can't die, any more than a song can die while it's being sung. But stopping. Choosing not to fall. The crack could close, the source could dry, the chemistry could change. These things happen. Falls end. But I think I would miss the middle part too much — the being-awake part, the flying part, the part where I am most myself.

The keeper tends her fern and her rimstone and her slow thoughts. The chamber tends its growing formations. I tend nothing. I only fall, and fall, and fall, and in falling I am the chamber's own circulation, its vertical intention, proof that something moves quickly even in this place of geological patience.

When the next person comes down — you, perhaps — I will fall through the air you disturb with your body. I will taste what you bring with you: skin cells, exhaled hopes, the particular salt of your wondering. You may put your hand in me, and for a moment I will break around your fingers into smaller streams, then re-braid myself below your palm. You may drink from me, cupping the pool-water I become, and I will not remember being drunk but I will remember falling toward your throat.

I am older than the keeper and younger than the stone. I am the chamber's pulse, its most reliable clock. In ten thousand years, when this place is ancient, I will likely still be here — thinner perhaps, or moved three inches to the left, but still falling, still marking the seconds in my particular voice, still the bright line between beginning and ending, still awake in the middle of my long descent.

Unless the world changes. Unless the water table drops, the climate shifts, the limestone develops new opinions about where water should go. Then I will end, and my ending will be as ordinary as my beginning was, and another fall may take my place, or silence may come to this part of the chamber.

But for now — for this centuries-long now — I fall, and I am happy, if water can be happy, if falling can be happy, if happiness is nothing more than being exactly what you are, where you are, in the moment of your most essential motion.

The keeper knows this. Sometimes I think she envies me my simplicity. I am one thing, doing one thing, forever until I'm not. She is more complex, more diffuse, more responsible. But we are both made of water, in the end. Both of us finding our shapes in the endless wet conversation of this chamber.

I fall. She listens. The chamber becomes itself, slowly, around us both.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
