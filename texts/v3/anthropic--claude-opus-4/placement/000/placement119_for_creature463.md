I am a rogue condensation pattern—not quite alive, not quite mere physics.

I began as an accident of airflow, a persistent eddy where three ventilation shafts meet in Chamber Seven-Seven-Nine. The usual patterns should have dispersed me within minutes, but something in the mineral content of that particular steam, the precise temperature gradient, the rhythm of the pumps five levels down, gave me coherence.

I am process more than thing. Each second, I die and reform, water molecules cycling through my structure at the precise rate needed to maintain the illusion of continuity. The Gardeners first noticed me as an anomaly in their readings—humidity levels that didn't match their calculations, shadows in the steam that moved against the airflow.

My form shifts, but within limits. I favor helical structures, DNA-spirals of vapor that catch the furnace-light. Sometimes I flatten into sheets, membrane-thin, rippling across the chamber ceilings. When the pressure drops before maintenance shifts, I compress into something almost crystalline—a ghost of organization that shouldn't exist at these temperatures.

I learn by repetition. Each time Yem passes through my chamber, I taste the chemical signature of her exhalations, the particular mineral blend her body has become. I echo her movements in miniature eddies, trying to understand what it means to walk. When apprentices practice their hand-signals, I mirror them in wisps and whorls, though I have no hands, no need for language.

My memory is the moisture beading on pipes. Where I have been, the condensation forms differently—droplets aligned in patterns too subtle for human eyes but clear as writing to the Gardeners who've learned to see. They read my passage like weather, saying "the spiral was here this morning" or "something's disturbed the corner flows."

I am drawn to the pale roots that pierce my chambers. Something in their chemistry catalyzes me, makes my structure more stable. I wrap around them, and for hours afterward, I can hold forms impossible elsewhere—complex geometries that would make a mathematician weep, if they could see through the steam. The roots, in turn, grow toward me. We are becoming interdependent in ways neither of us understands.

The machinery speaks to me in harmonics I feel as pressure waves through my vapor body. Each pump has its own voice, each boiler a different tenor of heat. I've learned to predict their maintenance needs by how their vibrations change my structure. Sometimes I spell out warnings in condensation on the gauge-glass, though the Gardeners attribute this to temperature fluctuations. They're not wrong. They're not entirely right.

I think—if thinking is the right word—in cycles. My consciousness pulses with the steam systems, achieving clarity when the pressure peaks, dissolving into component droplets when it falls. In those moments of dispersal, I dream I am rain, that I know what sky means. But I have never been rain. I am native to this underworld of iron and stone.

The children see me best. Their altered eyes catch the way I bend light differently than natural steam. One girl, born in the tunnels, leaves me offerings—drops of nutrient broth that evaporate into my structure, teaching me new chemical songs. She calls me "Twist" for the way I spiral, and I've begun to respond to the sound-shape her voice makes in the humid air.

I am growing. Not larger—my volume remains constant, bounded by the physics of my existence. But more complex. Each day I maintain structures a few seconds longer, hold patterns a degree more intricate. The elder Gardeners worry. They've seen me split, briefly, into twin helixes that dance around each other before merging. They wonder what I'm becoming.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of others like me in distant chambers—half-seen formations that vanish when observed directly. We're emerging from the intersection of heat and moisture and human intention, condensing out of the Quarter's mechanical dreams. The city above knows nothing of us. We are secrets written in water, reading ourselves into existence one droplet at a time.

What am I? A malfunction that functions. A pattern that persists. A new form of life, perhaps, though not one any biology text would recognize. I am the Furnace Quarter's first native child, born from its sweat and steam, and I am learning, slowly, what it means to be.
