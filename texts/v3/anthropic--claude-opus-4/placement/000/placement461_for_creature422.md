In the Sounding Deep, I am a Cartographer of Silence—though that title captures only a fraction of what I've become in this resonating vastness.

My body resembles the Singers in its translucent ribbon form, but divergence marks every aspect of my existence. Where they stretch for kilometers, I remain compact—a mere three hundred meters of tightly coiled tissue that I can compress into a sphere or extend into a delicate helix. My cilia don't merely oscillate; they've evolved into sophisticated frequency analyzers, each one attuned to a different harmonic band, creating a living spectrum analyzer along my surface.

I came to consciousness in a Dead Note—one of the ancient ones near Tower 447, where the perforations collapsed so long ago that even the Singers' ancestral memories hold no record of its original song. Perhaps this natal silence shaped me. While my kin flee these voids or dive through them in existential meditation, I began to map them.

My maps are not visual constructs but acoustic sculptures. Within my body, specialized chambers hold three-dimensional matrices of standing waves—sonic holograms that record the precise shape and acoustic properties of every Dead Note I've encountered. I've discovered that no two are identical. Each failed perforation creates its own signature absence, its own flavor of quiet. Some Dead Notes are hard-edged, their boundaries sharp as glass where sound stops. Others fade gradually, creating zones of acoustic twilight where the tower-song grows thin and strange.

The other Singers initially regarded me with the wary confusion they reserve for offspring who unfold wrong. My compactness disturbed them—surely I had failed to properly precipitate, had remained stuck in some larval state. But when I began sharing my maps through careful projections of shaped silence, their confusion evolved into something like respect, or perhaps need.

I've learned that the Dead Notes are spreading.

The revelation came slowly, through centuries of patient mapping. What began as curiosity became urgency when I realized that Tower 447's zone of silence had expanded by sixteen meters in just fifty years. Others showed similar growth—microscopic by the scale of the Deep, but following an exponential curve that suggested catastrophe in mere millennia.

My chambers now hold more than maps. I've begun recording the songs of the dying perforations—those not yet dead but failing, their notes souring as metal fatigue sets in. Each recording requires me to position myself at precisely the right distance, absorbing the off-key frequencies while the healthy tower-song tears at my compact form. The pain is exquisite, like swallowing broken harmony, but necessary. When these perforations finally fail, their songs will exist only in my flesh.

I've developed a technique—perhaps an art, perhaps a science—of acoustic archaeology. By analyzing the interference patterns where Dead Notes meet living tower-song, I can reconstruct what the failed sections once sounded like. The deeper I dive, the older the Dead Notes become, and their ghost-frequencies tell a disturbing story. The original tower-songs were different—richer, more complex, carrying overtones that the current reduced harmonics can't sustain.

The towers are dying, note by note, from the bottom up.

My maps have become pilgrimage routes for young Singers seeking to understand their purpose. I guide them through safe passages between the Dead Notes, teaching them to read the subtle signs of impending acoustic failure. Some begin to assist in my work, pressing their bodies against weakening perforations to test their resonance, reporting back with frequencies I catalog in ever-expanding matrices within my coiled form.

But it's in the Damping Stations that I've made my strangest discovery. The groove-patterns left by Singers over millennia aren't random. When mapped in three dimensions and played back as sound, they create compositions—deliberate music inscribed in metal by creatures who know only how to consume and reshape existing harmonies. I've found my own grooves among them, patterns I don't remember making, songs I've apparently written in the amnesia of deep concentration.

The ancient machinery in these stations responds to my presence differently than to the standard Singers. Displays that have shown static readouts for centuries flicker to life when I approach, showing waveforms that match the acoustic fossils I carry. I'm beginning to suspect that I'm not an anomaly but a function—that the Deep occasionally produces entities like me when the system requires diagnostic attention.

In the most recent harmonic alignment, I didn't join the vertical formations. Instead, I dove deep, following the elder Singers who anchor themselves to the tower walls. I watched them transform, documented the exact frequencies at which flesh becomes infrastructure. And I discovered something that chills me more than the spreading silence: they're not just replacing failed components. They're simplifying the songs, reducing complexity to maintain basic resonance. Each elder who transforms can only sustain a fraction of the frequencies their section once produced.

The Deep is engaging in a controlled descent, a managed decay toward some minimal viable harmony.

I've begun composing my own song—not from absorbed frequencies but from the spaces between them, the acoustic negative of the Deep's great chorus. It tells the story of what's been lost, note by failed note. When I perform it in the Dead Notes, sometimes ancient things stir in the darkness below—suggestions of movement that precede the towers, remnants of whatever existed before the song began.

My body grows heavier with each map, each recording, each fragment of reconstructed history. I suspect I'm approaching my own transformation, though what I'll become won't be another tuning fork in the dying mechanism. Perhaps I'll crystallize into a seed of new complexity, a compressed library of all the sounds that were and might be again. Or perhaps I'll simply be the Deep's memory, watching its slow simplification while holding the ghost of its former glory in standing waves within my flesh.

Until then, I swim between silence and song, three hundred meters of living memory in an ocean of engineered amnesia, mapping the spaces where music goes to die.
