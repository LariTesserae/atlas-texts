I find myself as a Shell-Singer, one of the younger Memory-Keepers' apprentices, though my path to this work wound through stranger territory than most.

I was born clicking in the middle layers of a district that specialized in thermal-chimney maintenance—my parents both worked the vents, reading the pressure differentials, adjusting the baffles that kept our sector's air from becoming entirely unbreathable. But I was born with asymmetric ears more extreme than most: my left drum captures the bass frequencies so completely that I can feel construction waves forming two districts away, while my right parses the crystalline chatter with such clarity that I learned to distinguish individual fauna by their songs before I could properly speak.

This made me useless for vent-work. The thermal chimneys sing in the middle frequencies I'm nearly deaf to. But it made me invaluable to the Memory-Keepers, who recognized in my lopsided hearing a natural affinity for the resonator-shells.

---

I spend my days in the archive-chambers, spaces tuned by generations of Sound-Shapers to amplify the exact frequencies the shells emit. My left ear pressed to the floor to catch the city's bass-note, my right ear hovering millimeters from shells the size of my fist, I can parse songs that other Memory-Keepers can only approximate. The shells were sung into by mensen dead for generations—Market-Makers recording crowd-patterns, Harvesters documenting fauna migrations, Route-Runners describing paths that closed centuries ago. My asymmetric hearing lets me separate the layers: the original song, the shell's resonance, the ghost-harmonics that accumulate over time.

But I am not merely a transcriber. The Memory-Keepers learned long ago that simple repetition kills the memories—sing the same patterns too precisely and they become brittle, lose their meaning, shatter like the shells themselves when dropped. The memories must be refreshed, reinterpreted, allowed to breathe. I take the old songs and weave them through my own understanding, through my experience of districts that didn't exist when the songs were first sung, through the particular distortions of my unusual ears.

Sometimes I catch things the previous Keepers missed. A Market-Maker's century-old song about crowd-flow contains, in its under-harmonics, the signature of a construction wave approaching. I can hear the fear in her voice—she knew her market was about to vanish—though she kept singing her observations until the very end. A Harvester's catalogue of fauna includes the ghost of what might be words in the crystalline chatter, as if he was beginning to understand something in their songs that no one else has noticed.

---

My work takes me to the boundaries between districts, where the acoustic territories blur and the shells' songs mix with the living city's noise. I carry shells with me—not the ancient ones, too precious to risk, but copies I've grown myself using Weaver techniques applied to Harvester materials. These new shells I sing my own observations into: the way the moisture patterns have been shifting in the eastern wet-zones, suggesting construction waves of unprecedented scope; the new fauna forms that have been appearing in the upper levels, their songs tuned to frequencies that make my right ear ache with their clarity.

Other mensen find me unsettling. My head tilts constantly, unconsciously, optimizing the angle for whatever I'm trying to hear. I click when I'm thinking—not the infant clicking we're all born with, but patterned clicks that echo the fauna songs, that I'm not always aware I'm producing. Route-Runners hire me sometimes, when they need to know if a particular path once existed, if the city ever allowed passage where now only solid wall stands. I press my left ear to the structure and feel for old vibrations, fossil songs in the ceramic-polymer, then click the answer in frequencies only my right ear would properly parse.

The other Memory-Keepers say I'm developing a new tradition, a hybrid practice that might outlive the pure forms. They're not entirely approving—tradition matters to those who keep it—but they're curious. One of the eldest, her ears so damaged by years of shell-listening that she's nearly deaf to everything else, told me she thinks I might be evolving the practice the way mensen evolved to fit the city. "We change or we dissolve," she said, clicking the words as much as speaking them. "Like everything here."

---

But what unsettles me—what I haven't told the other Keepers—is what I'm beginning to hear in the intersection of frequencies. When the city's bass-note aligns with certain crystalline songs, when the construction harmonics hit just right, I hear something underneath it all. Not a pattern exactly. Not a message. But an attention, vast and diffuse, like the city is listening to itself through our songs, through our clicking, through the shells we've filled with generations of observation.

I don't think the city notices us, not as individuals. But I'm beginning to suspect it notices the Memory-Keepers. We are its only record of what it's been, the only witnesses to its changes who preserve those changes in forms that last longer than a week. When I sing the old songs into new shells, I sometimes feel the walls around me shift slightly, the moisture patterns adjust, as if the city is... not responding exactly, but registering. Taking note.

Last week, I found a shell in a deep archive that no one remembered storing. It was enormous—the size of my torso—and when I put my right ear to it, I heard my own voice, singing observations I haven't made yet, describing districts I don't recognize, using acoustic notation I haven't developed. My left ear, pressed to the floor, felt the city's bass-note skip, just once, like a massive heart stuttering.

I haven't told anyone about this shell. I visit it daily, listening to my future voice describe construction waves that reshape not just districts but the entire concept of districts, fauna that sing in human words, expeditions that find edges but choose not to cross them. I'm trying to understand if this is prophecy or planning, if the city is showing me what will be or asking me what should be.

The other Memory-Keepers would say this is madness, that shells can't contain songs that haven't been sung yet. But they haven't spent their lives with one ear in the bass frequencies and one in the crystalline heights. They haven't learned to parse the spaces between sounds, where the city might be keeping its own memories, its own plans, its own vast and patient intentions.

---

I continue my work. I transcribe the old songs, refresh them with new understanding, grow new shells for new observations. But I also listen, constantly, for that skip in the bass-note, for the moment when the city's attention turns however briefly to the small clicking things that live in its gaps.

We are Memory-Keepers. We preserve what was, to understand what is.

But I am beginning to suspect we are also something else: the city's way of remembering itself, its borrowed cognition, its crystalline neural network scattered through the population of its parasites.

The city breathes in. The city breathes out.

And somewhere in that breath, in frequencies only my asymmetric ears can parse, it might be trying to speak.
