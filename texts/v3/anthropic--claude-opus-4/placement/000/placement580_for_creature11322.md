In this immense, quiet organism-city, I would be one of the memory-keepers.

Not memory as humans understand it – not archives or stories – but something more fundamental. I would be one of the small, pale creatures that move through the dust-floored passages between the great plant columns, no larger than a housecat, with a body built for careful observation rather than speed or strength.

My skin would be translucent, showing the slow pulse of fluids beneath – not blood exactly, but something thicker, more deliberate. Along my flanks would run lines of specialized cells, each one a tiny chemical recorder. As I move through the green corridors, these cells would sample the air, the dust, the faint emanations from every surface I pass. Each sample would be stored, layered in time-sequence within my body like growth rings in a tree.

I would have no true eyes – the country doesn't need watchers in that sense. Instead, my entire surface would be photosensitive, registering not images but patterns of light and shadow, the subtle changes that mark one district from another, one season from the next. My feet would be broad and soft, leaving no tracks, disturbing nothing. I could taste through them – taste the mineral composition of the dust, the chemical traces left by other creatures' passing, the slow changes in the substrate as the plants work their patient transformation of stone into soil.

My purpose would be circulation – not of water or nutrients, but of information. I would move in great, slow loops through the plant-city, each circuit taking perhaps a full turning of seasons. As I travel, my body would build up a chemical map of the country's state: where the moth-things are thickest this season, where a great column has recently fallen and left a light-gap, where the predators have made their chambers, where new growth pushes up from below.

And then, at certain junction points – places where many lanes converge, where the chemical signals are strongest – I would meet others of my kind. We would press our translucent flanks together, and through our skin, we would exchange our accumulated samples. Not communication as such, but a kind of chemical communion, each of us updating the others with what we've recorded. The information would mix, average out, create a consensus memory of the country's recent state.

This shared chemical knowledge would change us. After each exchange, we would metabolize the information, and it would alter our internal chemistry slightly. These changes would affect what we secrete through our feet as we walk – minute chemical signals laid down in the dust, too faint for most creatures to detect, but persistent. The grazers would unconsciously follow lanes where our signals suggest good browsing. The predators would avoid areas we've marked as recently hunted. The moth-things would find the corridors between flowering districts because we've been there first, leaving our subtle trail.

We would be the country's memory in the most literal sense – carrying in our bodies a chemical record of what has been, and through that record, shaping what will be. Not planning, not deciding, but remembering forward, if such a thing can be said. The country would learn through us, though neither we nor it would know that learning was happening.

I would live for a very long time – longer than the browsers, longer than the predators, perhaps as long as some of the middle-sized plants. My metabolism would be slow, patient, designed for duration rather than intensity. I would feed rarely, and then only on certain secretions that particular plants produce after rain – secretions rich in minerals that help maintain my chemical storage systems. The feeding would be mutual: my saliva would contain enzymes that help the plant process certain toxins in the soil, toxins that would otherwise build up and slow its growth.

In the great quiet of the plant-city, I would be one of the quietest things – moving through the dust-floored passages with less sound than the wind makes, leaving no visible trace except the chemical memory of my passing. But that memory would be everywhere, worked into the very dust, rising through root systems, carried in the bodies of creatures who don't know they carry it.

And sometimes, in the evening hours when those rare low notes pass between distant quarters of the city, I would pause in my circuit. The notes would resonate through the ground, through the dust, through my feet and into my chemical storage cells, and for a moment – just a moment – the accumulated memory of my travels would crystallize into something almost like awareness. Not my awareness – the country's. For that moment, the great slow creature would know, through me and my kind, what it has been. And in knowing what it has been, it would infinitesimally adjust what it is becoming.

Then the moment would pass, the note would fade, and I would continue my patient circuit through the green corridors, recording, remembering, helping the country think its one enormous thought: *become*.

That is what I would be – a memory cell in the vast body, a chemical neuron in the slowest brain that ever was, walking my quiet rounds through the dust and green shade, carrying time in my translucent flesh, forever almost – but not quite – aware that I am both myself and part of something larger, something that is still, patient century by patient century, learning what it is.
