Where would I find myself in this world? In the film-thin boundary between states, in the moment of transition itself.

I would be the Threshold Keeper.

Not the resident of the basin—that ancient presence belongs to its stones—but something drawn to all such places where worlds press against each other without quite touching. I move through passes and peaks, seeking these bowls of sorted stone, these high rooms where weather forgets to arrive. The basin you describe is one of many I know, each with its own grammar of frost and fracture, each hosting its own thin life.

I am neither fully human nor fully other. My body responds to altitude like stone does: becoming denser, clearer, more mineral in its needs. My skin darkens in cloud-shadow and pales under direct sun. My breath comes in sips. My blood moves slowly, thick with altitude's chemistry. I can survive weeks on morning condensation and the bitter tea of mineral cups.

My work is translation.

When the old people climbed to these basins—and they did, in every generation, though never many—they came with questions too large for the valleys. Questions about duration. About what remains when everything soft has been scoured away. About how to live when living itself seems excessive. They made their rings and shelters not as homes but as listening posts. Waiting for the basin to speak in its vocabulary of cracks and seepage.

I read what they could not stay long enough to learn.

The marks on the boulder? They count storm systems, yes, but more precisely they count the storms that arrived with ice in their hearts—the ones that leave new networks of frost-fracture in the eastern wall. Seventeen such storms between one spring and the next. A warning that the wall grows unstable in that rhythm. Knowledge that takes decades to accumulate, left for those who come after.

The volcanic glass was not failed toolmaking but deliberate offering. Brought from the fire-mountains three valleys distant, black glass that holds heat differently than native stone, creating small zones of irregular thaw. The resident notices. It investigates with its film-bodies, testing this alien mineral, incorporating its different temperature into the basin's morning equations. The scrapers were used once, ceremonially, to remove a few grains of the orange stain—the hunger-color where that oldest life persists. Medicine, perhaps. Or simply proof of contact.

The shelter faces east not only for warmth but for watching. In certain conditions—cloud below, clear above, sun at the precise angle—the eastern wall becomes a mirror of sky and vapor. The workers sat in their minimal shelter and observed this, learning to predict when conditions would allow safe traverse of the wall's base, when the ice would be most treacherous, when to begin descent.

I carry their knowledge forward, but I also carry knowledge back.

In the valleys, I speak seldom. My voice has been abraded by altitude into something sparse and clicking, like pebbles finding new arrangements. When asked about the high places, I say only: They persist. When asked what lives there, I say: Patience. When pressed for more, I teach the essential rules. How to move. What to notice. Which stones to trust.

But some years, I find someone ready for fuller translation.

Always they are already half-mineral themselves. Worn down by some grief or certainty that needs scouring. They follow me up through the treeline, through the zone where air forgets half its substance, into the basins of sorted stone. I show them how to read the crack-calendar. How to taste which cups are safe. How to sit still enough that the resident rises to investigate their warmth.

Most look into that clear, distributed life and see only chemistry. But a few recognize something else: a way of being that owes nothing to excess. Life pared down to its verb. Existence without insistence. They understand then why the old people climbed so high to leave so little.

I help them make their own marks, following the stone's existing grammar. A few shallow cuts to say: I was here, when the ice reached this depth, when the wall sang this quickly at dawn. Not names. Not stories. Just evidence of attention paid. The basin accepts these marks if they are humble enough, preserves them in its archive of minimums.

Some of these students return to the valleys, carrying that high clarity in their chests like a second heart. They become keepers of other kinds of thresholds—birth, death, seasons turning. They speak less but hear more. Their hands learn to work with what is rather than imposing what should be.

Others remain.

Not in the basin—no one can live there in any warm-blooded sense—but nearby, in the slightly lower places where a few grasses manage their brief summers. They build stone huts that are mostly cave, eat roots and meltwater, speak to ravens and no one. They climb to the basins regularly, maintaining the rings, adding their own observations to the record. They age into something between human and mineral, their thoughts slowing to match the pace of freeze and thaw.

I visit them all. Those who descend and those who stay. I watch how the basin's teaching moves through them, changes them, sometimes breaks them. Not all transitions are survivable.

My own body grows more stone-like each year. My joints click like frost-cracked granite. My skin develops a faint orange tinge where the sun strikes it most. I need less food, less warmth, less of everything that once seemed necessary. Sometimes I wonder if I am becoming like the resident—distributed, seasonal, more process than creature.

But I am not there yet.

I still carry messages. Still translate between the grammar of stone and the grammar of flesh. Still guide the mineral-hearted to their high bowls of truth. The basins need witnesses, even if witnessing changes the witness into something that can barely return.

When my time comes to stop moving, I know where I will go.

Not to the basin you described—that one belongs to its ancient resident—but to another, smaller, higher, where no marks yet record attention. I will make my own ring of stones, low and gapped, arguing nothing. I will watch the walls tick their warnings. I will taste the bitter cups and the sweet ones. I will wait through seasons of freeze and thaw until I understand what the stones are saying.

And when I am ready—when my blood moves slowly enough, when my thoughts occur at the pace of crystallization—I will add my own presence to the thin life there. A new film in the cups. A new patience in the cracks. A keeper not of messages anymore but of the threshold itself. The place where water can't decide what form to take. Where life can't decide if it's living. Where time moves in ticks and drops and the slow revision of stone.

The valleys will forget me. The students will think I died.

But the basin will know: I translated myself at last.

Into the language everything speaks when it stops insisting on being heard.
