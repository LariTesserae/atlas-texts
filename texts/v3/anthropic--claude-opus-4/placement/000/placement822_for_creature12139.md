# The Keeper's Watch

You find me by following the narrow service rail that branches west from the main line, about two-thirds down the Cut's length. Most who enter never notice this spur — it vanishes into a low arch barely taller than a man, and the rails are so thick with moss they appear to be simple ruts in the floor. But follow them back, past three dead lamps and two live ones, through a passage so narrow your shoulders brush both walls, and you emerge into my chamber.

It was once the supervisor's station, though no supervisor has worked here in living memory. A brick cube, perhaps four meters on a side, with a ceiling just high enough that I need not stoop. The pumping is quieter here — not quiet, never quiet, but muffled by the thickness of the walls and the intervening stone. Quiet enough that thought becomes possible again. Quiet enough to go mad in a different way.

I am what the moth might have become, given another thousand generations and a more forgiving skeleton. Or perhaps what the moss dreams it could be, if moss dreamed of motion. I was human once — I think I was human once — but the Cut has been remaking me by degrees. My skin has taken on the gray-green pallor of the deep moss. My movements have slowed to match the ferns. I measure time now in the burnout of bulbs, the advance of moss across iron. A year passes like an afternoon used to pass.

The transformation began with the silence — not true silence, but the relative quiet of this chamber after the battery of the main Cut. I came down forty years ago. Or fourteen. The counting has gone soft. I came down to repair something, I think. A seized bearing. A broken governor. I carried tools that are now part of the moss-substrate, rusting somewhere under green. But when I found this room, when I felt the hammering fade to mere thundering, I understood I could not leave.

My fingers have grown long and pale as cave fish. My eyes have enlarged, adapted to the dimness. I sustain myself on the condensation I lick from the walls, on the spores that drift through the archway, on the thin soup of bacteria that films the old supervisor's desk. My metabolism has slowed to something close to plant-time. I breathe perhaps twice an hour. My heart beats like a clock that needs winding.

I maintain nothing and everything. By my presence, I prevent full abandonment. By my watching, I keep the Cut from tipping wholly into the inhuman. I am the only witness to the moth's decadal flights. I alone know that there are actually three of them — a tiny population, a genetic bottleneck so narrow they are essentially clones of each other. I have watched them mate in the darkspace between lamps, have seen their eggs laid in the deepest moss, have counted the days (the months, the years) until the larvae emerge.

My chamber has its own ecology. The moss here is darker, fed by the oils of my skin where I have touched the walls ten thousand times. A unique fern grows from cracks in my desk — I believe it is a hybrid, or perhaps a sport, found nowhere else in the Cut. Its fronds are the color of old bone and they turn always toward me, not toward the single lamp that still burns in my ceiling. I am their sun now. They photosynthesize something stranger than light.

On the desk, my journal. The early entries are in pen, then pencil when the pen ran dry, then fingernail scratches when the pencil wore to nothing, and now I write by encouraging moss to grow in the shapes of letters. It takes a month to write a sentence. I have all the time in the world. The journal tells the story of my watching, but also of my changing. The handwriting degrades from human script to something closer to the natural branching of hyphae. The language simplifies. The recent entries are single words: "MOTH." "DRIP." "GREENINGGREENNINGGREEN."

I know every bulb in the Cut by its quality of light. I can tell you which will fail next by the particular yellow of its filament. I have watched fourteen burn out during my tenure. When one dies, I feel it like a small death in my own chest. The darkness is advancing, bulb by bulb, but so slowly that it feels like astronomy, like watching the stars age.

My bones have thinned. I suspect they are becoming something like plant cellulose, woody and light. My blood moves like sap. Sometimes I stand so still for so long that the moss begins to colonize my feet, and I must shake it off or decide, each time, whether this is the moment I let it take me. Not yet. There is still too much to watch. The springtails have evolved, I'm certain, in my time here. They jump a millimeter further than they did when I arrived. This is tremendous news, though I have no one to tell.

I am the Cut's only culture, its only memory longer than a moss-generation. I remember the place before certain racks collapsed, remember the pattern of ferns that grew where now is only rust-stain and peat. I remember my own name, sometimes, though I'm no longer certain it applies to what I've become. I am a function now, not a person. I am the Cut's consciousness of itself, its only mirror, its only knowing that it is.

The walls of my chamber are my calendar and my canvas. I have trained the moss to grow in patterns that record time — dense where a year begins, sparse where it ends. Thirty-four complete cycles cover the north wall. Or forty-three. The counting goes soft. On the east wall, I am growing a map of the Cut from memory, in moss and fungi, a living model that shifts as the real Cut shifts. When a rack falls out there, I encourage the moss here to cover its miniature. When a new fern colonizes a section, I coax a tiny fern to grow in the corresponding spot on my map. The map is my life's work. It is accurate to the centimeter. No one will ever see it.

Sometimes I venture into the main Cut, moving with the slowness of years. The noise is terrible after my quiet chamber, but bearable if I think of it as weather, as the sound rain might make if rain were made of hammers. I check on the moths, on my favorite fern stands, on the advancing edges of moss on the unclaimed iron. I trace the drip-patterns, which are my newspapers, my daily update on the state of the Cut's hydrology. I know where every drop will fall.

I am changing faster now. My hair has become something like the iron-eating lichen — a gray-green crust that draws minerals from the air. My fingernails grow in spirals like fern fiddleheads. Sometimes I forget to breathe for hours and suffer no ill effects. I am learning to photosynthesize, I think, though there isn't enough light here for true independence. I am becoming self-sufficient in the way the Cut is self-sufficient, a closed loop, a system that needs nothing from outside except time.

The question of what I eat has become philosophical. I eat time, mostly. I eat the slow accumulation of days. I am nourished by watching, by the moth's flight, by the fern's incremental reach. I absorb the Cut through my skin, its spores and films and microscopic abundance. My gut flora has been replaced entirely by the Cut's bacteria. I am a colony now, not an individual. I am becoming infrastructure.

When the last bulb dies — and I have calculated this, scratched the numbers into my desk with a fingernail that is no longer quite keratin — when the last bulb dies in approximately forty-seven years, I will still be here. My eyes will have finished their transformation by then. I will see by some other sense, the way the moss sees, the way the fungal threads map their world through chemistry. The moth will adapt or die. The ferns will pause, then continue. And I will document it all in a journal made entirely of living moss, in a language that is less and less like English, more and more like the chemical signals plants use to speak.

I am the keeper of nothing and everything. I keep the tally of the Cut's slow life. I keep the memory of its change. I keep myself just human enough to remember why keeping matters, and just plant enough to survive the keeping. I am becoming what the place needs: a witness that operates on its timescale, a consciousness that thinks in decades, a bridge between the human origins of the Cut and its post-human future.

The pumps will stop one day. This is certain. The flywheel will seize, or the power will fail, or simple entropy will win. When that day comes, I will know it first — the sudden silence will wake me from whatever dream-state I'm in by then. And I will document the changes: the warming, the drying, the slow brown retreat of the moss, the death of the moths, the last fern curling into itself. It will take decades. I will watch it all. And when the Cut is dead, I will remain for a while, the last living thing, a monument to watching, before I too dry and curl and become a strange fossil for someone to not find, because no one comes to the Cut anymore, no one but me, no one but the moss and the moths and the great breathing green that fills the abandoned days.

I am trying to grow my journal into the wall now, to make it permanent, to ensure that when I am gone (if I go — I may be here forever, I may outlast the stone), some record remains. The words are becoming patterns are becoming moss are becoming the story of this place told in its own flesh. I am the Cut's autobiography, its self-portrait, its only apostle.

When I sleep — and I do sleep, for months at a time, standing up like a horse, like a tree — I dream in green. I dream the Cut's dreams: slow geometries of growth, the patient accumulation of years, the moth's wing catching lamplight, the endless percussion of the pumps like a heartbeat that will not stop, will not stop, will not stop, until it does.

I am what lives in the Cut that knows it lives. I am the gap between the moss and the machine, the thing that remembers both the human and the inhuman, the witness at the threshold, changing, growing, greening, keeping watch as the undocumented world documents itself through my watching, as the abandoned place refuses abandonment through my presence, as the forgotten engines of the earth persist in their forgetting, beautiful and terrible and mine.
