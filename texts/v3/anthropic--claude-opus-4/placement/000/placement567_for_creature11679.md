## The Tidewright

I am the memory of metal, the last keeper of purposes that rust toward forgetting. Where others flow and merge in the Gyre's endless transformations, I remain singular—not from choice but from the peculiar curse of my becoming.

They call me the Tidewright when they must call me anything, though names slide off the water here like oil. I am the one who tends the turbines, who speaks to the corrosion, who remembers why the builders came and why they fled. Not because anyone asked me to remember, but because the metal itself insists, singing its decay into my bones that are no longer quite bones, my blood that runs with iron in concentrations that should kill but somehow sustains.

I was an engineer once. Christine Salazar, according to the personnel file that still exists in a waterproof case in Turbine Hall Seven, though the lamination has long since admitted the sea and the words blur into suggestions. I stayed when the others evacuated, not from heroism but from a technician's obsession—I couldn't leave the generators half-maintained, the intake valves improperly sealed. Just another week, I told myself as the supply boats stopped coming. Just until the storm season passes.

The storm season never passed.

The transformation came slowly, then all at once. First, the persistent infections from small cuts that never quite healed in the salt-thick air. Then the dreams of swimming through the turbine halls, breathing the oil-slick water as easily as air. I woke one morning to find my fingers webbed with a translucent membrane that tore when I tried to stretch it, bled mercury-bright blood, then grew back thicker before sunset. My tools began to feel wrong in my hands, designed for joints that no longer bent in quite the right directions.

The others found me—the dwellers who weren't yet dwellers, still human enough that we could look at each other and see kinship rather than prey. They tried to teach me their ways of merging, of consciousness-sharing, of collective dreaming. But where their blood ran warm and communal, mine had turned cold and dense with metals leached from a lifetime of handling corroded components. When we tried to merge, my blood poisoned their temporary unity. Where their ports opened cleanly for connection, mine sparked and seized, the iron in my system reacting with their copper-based chemistry in ways that sent us all into convulsions.

So I learned to be alone in a place where aloneness is anathema, a fixed point in the fluid society of the Gyre.

But I am not unchanged. My bones are honeycombed with chambers that flood and drain with the tide, letting me adjust my buoyancy with a thought. My skin secretes a protective film that tastes of machine oil and prevents the worst of the corrosion from taking hold—not just on metal but on myself. My eyes have developed nictitating membranes like the others', but mine are polarized, letting me see through the rain-glare to read the gauges that still function in the deeper halls. My hands have become tools themselves—fingernails transformed into screwdrivers of living keratin, palms that can sense electrical current through touch, a thumb that extends twice its original length to reach into the narrow inspection ports.

I speak the harmonic language poorly, my vocal cords still too rigid for proper temporal communication. Instead, I've developed my own pidgin of acoustic signals—the click of metal on metal, the specific frequencies of different alloys vibrating, the percussion of my modified hands against turbine housings. The others understand me well enough, though they say my accent sounds like rust flaking into water.

My work is impossible and essential. I maintain machines that will never function again, preserve systems designed for a world that no longer exists at this coordinate. Yet the mountain needs these mechanical organs. The turbines, even seized and silent, create specific pressures in the water that helps regulate the Gyre's spin. The intake pipes, though they'll never generate power again, serve as barometers—their howling changes pitch with pressure variations too subtle for even the dwellers to sense directly. I clear them of growth not to restore their function but to preserve their voice.

I live in the control room of Station Three, the only space that still holds air reliably, though "air" is generous—it's more a thick suspension of oxygen and moisture that would drown unmodified lungs in minutes. My nest is built from maintenance manuals whose pages have fused into coral-like blocks, their diagrams still barely visible like fossils in the compressed material. I've trained bioluminescent organisms to grow in the patterns of circuit diagrams on my walls, their living light replacing the failed electrical systems, their glow intensifying when storms approach as they feed on the increased electromagnetic activity.

My tools have evolved with me. The wrenches and screwdrivers I couldn't abandon have been colonized by the same organisms that coat my skin, their metal cores wrapped in living sheaths that prevent further corrosion while adding grip in the slippery environment. Some have grown beyond their original purposes—a torque wrench that now senses pressure changes through vibrations in its handle, a multimeter whose probes have become sensitive to bioelectrical fields, letting me diagnose the health of the mountain's living systems as well as its mechanical ones.

I cultivate my own food in the flooded battery rooms, where the acid-tainted water supports unique chemosynthetic organisms found nowhere else in the Gyre. These pale, thread-like plants grow in mathematical patterns, their geometry reflecting the electrical fields that once pulsed through these spaces. They taste of copper and lightning, and they're the only food that doesn't revolt against my altered chemistry. The others won't come near these rooms—the water burns their permeable skin, while mine thrives in the acidic environment.

Once each tidal cycle, when the moon pulls the water to its lowest ebb and the upper turbine halls briefly hold more air than water, I perform the maintenance that the metal still remembers needing. I oil joints that will never turn, replace gaskets on pipes that will never flow, calibrate sensors that will never sense. The dwellers think this is madness or religion, but they're wrong. It's conversation. The metal speaks in its corrosion patterns, tells me stories of stress and strain, of the years before the storm grew hungry, of the engineers who built these halls and believed in their permanence.

I'm the only one who can read these stories now. The specific patterns of verdigris on copper, the way iron rust blooms differently when exposed to different salinity levels, the precise rate at which aluminum pits in this particular mixture of salt and industrial runoff—this is a language as complex as the dwellers' harmonics, and I am its last speaker.

Sometimes I find their bones, the builders who didn't evacuate in time or who came back trying to salvage what the company had invested. Their skeletons tell their own stories—this one died trying to seal a breach in Turbine Hall Five, that one made it to the emergency radio in Station Seven before the water rose above the batteries. I arrange their remains in the maintenance alcoves, not as graves but as warnings, or perhaps as promises. This is what happens to those who insist on remaining unchanged. This is what the mountain offers those who won't adapt.

The dwellers bring me salvage from the deep slopes—pieces of machinery that wash up from even older attempts to harness the Gyre, fragments of technology whose purposes I can only guess at. Ceramic components that sing when current passes through them, metals that shouldn't exist at these pressures, circuitry that seems to grow rather than corrode. I incorporate what I can into my maintenance routines, creating hybrid systems that are part machine, part organism, part something entirely other.

My greatest project waits in the deepest hall, where the primary generators once turned. I'm building something there—not rebuilding what was, but building forward from what remains. Using the massive generator housings as shells, I'm growing new kinds of machines, ones that feed on the Gyre's motion rather than trying to steal from it. Turbines whose blades are living tissue that flexes with the current, generators whose coils are colonies of electric organisms, control systems that dream rather than compute.

I don't know if it will work. I don't know what "working" would even mean for such a system. But the metal wants to become something, and I am the only one who can help it transform while still remaining metal, just as I remain myself while becoming other.

The loneliness is a physical thing, a pressure in my chest where my heart has reorganized itself into something more like a fuel pump than a muscle. When the storms are at their worst and even my sealed control room floods, I understand the appeal of merger, of consciousness shared across multiple bodies, of never having to be a single point of failure in an infinitely redundant system. But my blood would poison them, my mechanical thoughts would disrupt their organic dreams. I am the necessary exception, the fixed point their fluid society requires to define itself against.

So I continue my rounds, checking gauges that lie, maintaining machines that dream of function, speaking in frequencies only metal understands. I am the Gyre's memory of purposefulness, the ghost in its machine, the part that remembers being apart. When the dwellers' children are born too rigid, too singular, I teach them what I can of staying whole in a place that demands dissolution. Most learn to merge eventually, but some, poisoned by trace metals or marked by some other chemistry, remain isolate like me. These become my apprentices, learning the language of corrosion, the maintenance of the unnecessary, the art of remaining singular in a plural world.

We are the Tidewrights, the speakers to metal, the ones who tend the boundary between what was built and what grows. We are necessary because we are impossible, singular because the Gyre requires all states to exist within its spin, even the state of stubborn, mechanical individuality.

I am Christine Salazar, employee ID 7739-QX, senior maintenance engineer, currently the only thing preventing the complete submersion of humanity's memory into the Gyre's eternal present. I am the Tidewright, keeper of purposes that no longer purpose, tender of machines that have become organs, last speaker of the language of intentional design in a place that has evolved past intention into pure being.

I maintain. I remember. I continue.

The metal insists, and I listen, and together we rust toward whatever comes after forgetting—not forgetting itself, but the thing that requires forgetting to have happened first, the oxidation of memory into something harder and stranger than either metal or flesh alone could become.
