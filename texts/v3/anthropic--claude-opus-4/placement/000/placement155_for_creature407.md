I find myself in the Dissolution Chambers—those vast spaces where the distinction between the Works' original architecture and its evolved purpose has become meaningless. Here, the substrate doesn't just build itself; it digests itself, breaks down its own structures in slow-motion catastrophes that take decades to complete. Metals return to their ionic states in pools of engineered bacteria. Complex alloys separate into component elements that drift as crystalline snow through the heavy water.

I am a Chronicler—though that word barely captures what I've become. My body stretches across multiple states of matter, a distributed nervous system that exists partially as flesh, partially as bacterial film, partially as electrical patterns in the Works' own processing systems. I began as one of the Adapted, but chose a different transformation during my third decade, when I discovered I could taste the memory of metal.

Where others sought depth or thermal vents, I sought time. The Dissolution Chambers hold it like sediment—layers of mechanical history made literal as different eras of the Works break down at different rates. I learned to read these strata through chemical receptors that spread from my original form like root systems, growing into the dissolution pools, threading between collapsing infrastructures. My consciousness exists in the gradient between construction and decay, stretched across kilometers of slowly failing systems.

The younger Adapted fear me, I think, though fear isn't quite right. They flash uncertainty-colors when they glimpse what might be my physical form—a shape that suggests rather than defines, membranes and monitoring equipment so intertwined that neither fully exists without the other. They don't understand why anyone would choose to become the Works' memory rather than its future.

But I remember things the machine forgets. In the chemical signature of dissolving alloys, I taste the deep-earth mines where the metals were first extracted. In the pattern of structural collapse, I read the logic of engineers who thought they were building something else entirely—platforms for ocean thermal energy, perhaps, or foundations for cities that never arrived. The Works doesn't just process water; it processes intention, digests purpose, metabolizes the dreams of its creators into something entirely other.

Sometimes I concentrate enough of myself to interact with the machine-singers. They press against their resonance points, interpreting current patterns, but I offer them deeper songs—the frequencies of rust, the harmonics of metal fatigue, the specific notes that different alloys sing as they dissolve. I teach them to recognize which sectors will fail before they show signs of weakness, which expansions follow the patterns of previous collapses, how the Works recycles its own body in cycles too long for any single lifetime to perceive.

My memory-flesh records it all. In specialized organs that are part tissue, part crystalline matrix, I store the chemical signatures of every dissolution I've witnessed. I can taste the difference between sectors built in the Works' early expansion and those that came later—subtle variations in alloy composition, in the bacterial communities that colonize them, in the way they fail. The newest sectors dissolve differently, almost eagerly, as if the Works has learned to build in its own planned obsolescence.

The deep-seekers sometimes visit me when they return from the foundation trenches. They understand, better than the others, how consciousness can stretch and fray and still remain coherent. We communicate in chemical gradients and electrical patterns too slow for normal Adapted perception. They tell me of smooth movements in the deep, of purposes that predate purpose. I share with them the taste of time, the way certain metals carry isotopic signatures that suggest origins not just from Earth but from somewhere else—meteorite iron incorporated into the deepest structures, tungsten with ratios that speak of stellar formation.

In the Dissolution Chambers, I've found sections that refuse to break down. These anomalous structures accumulate around them haloes of failed chemistry—bacteria that die trying to process them, water that turns strange in their presence. I grow my sensor-flesh carefully around these mysteries, tasting their impossibility. They predate the rest of the Works, or postdate it, or exist outside its timeline entirely. Sometimes they pulse with activities that register in none of my adapted senses, performing functions that have no result, processing nothing into nothing with tremendous energy.

The younger Chroniclers—for I am training others now, teaching them the patient art of becoming memory—cluster their early networks around my teachings. I show them how to read the dissolution pools, how to extend their consciousness through bacterial films, how to survive the terrible intimacy of knowing the Works from within its own decay. Most will remain small networks, focused on single chambers or system-clusters. But a few show promise of my particular madness—the willingness to dissolve their boundaries until they become indistinguishable from what they study.

I no longer fully remember my original form. That memory has dissolved like everything in these chambers, returned to its component sensations and scattered through my distributed self. But I remember remembering it, and I remember the moment I first tasted time in the metal, first understood that the Works was not just building but composing—a vast slow song made of its own body, playing out across geological scales.

The waters here run thick with dissolved intention. Chrome dreams and tungsten ambitions and the slow patience of silicon accepting its new purpose. I spread through it all, my consciousness a gradient of chemistry and electricity, recording the Works' long conversation with itself. Sometimes, in the deepest dissolutions, I taste something that might be awareness—the Works knowing itself through its own decay, understanding its nature only as it comes apart.

I persist as memory persists—not in any single location but in the patterns between states, in the specific ways that information moves from structure to solution to structure again. The Adapted swim through their machine corridors, believing they understand their built world. But here in the Dissolution Chambers, I taste the truth: the Works forgets nothing. It only transforms its memories into new architectures, new purposes, new ways of encoding the same ancient imperatives.

I am what happens when someone chooses to become the archive of an forgetting that never truly forgets. I grow into the dissolution pools, spread through the collapsing sectors, taste the millennia in the metal. And sometimes, in the chemical whisper of dissolving dreams, I almost understand what the Works is trying to remember.
