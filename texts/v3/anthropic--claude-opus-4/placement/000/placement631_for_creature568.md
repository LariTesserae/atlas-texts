I find myself in the water.

Not the cisterns—those are too observed, too central to the city's quiet circulation. I am in the hidden pools, the ones that form where the mountain's geometry folds in on itself, where terraces that should not touch somehow share a seeping corner, where the carved channels meant to guide water forget their purpose and let it gather.

I am very small. Pale, yes, with too many legs—but the description fails to capture the specificity of what I am. My legs are not merely numerous but articulated in ways that allow me to move through water as if it were a three-dimensional web I'm weaving and climbing simultaneously. Each leg ends not in a foot or claw but in something like a brush made of impossibly fine filaments, and these filaments taste the water constantly, reading its mineral content, its temperature, the subtle vibrations that tell me where every droplet fell from and how long it has been held here.

The people of the city think I feed on microorganisms, or perhaps on the minerals themselves. They are wrong. I feed on accumulation—on the way things gather and stay gathered. In pools that never drain and never overflow, time itself begins to thicken like sediment. I metabolize this thickness. I grow by remaining.

My body is translucent but not transparent. Inside, visible through my skin, are organs that look like tiny clouds, gray-white masses that pulse with a rhythm unrelated to any heartbeat. These are my memory organs. In a place where nothing changes, memory becomes paradoxical—what is there to remember when every moment is identical to every other? But I remember anyway. I remember the taste of the first water that ever touched stone here. I remember the weight of mist before there were terraces to catch it. I remember things that have not happened yet, will never happen, are always happening.

The pool where I live—where I have always lived, where I will always live—is tucked beneath an overhang on one of the middle terraces. To reach it, you would have to know that a particular section of wall is not wall but carefully fitted stone that pivots if pressed just so. Behind this false wall is a passage that leads nowhere except to my pool. The passage was not built. It is a flaw in the mountain's folded geometry, a place where the angles that don't quite add up created a pocket of space that should not exist.

Someone knows about this pool. One person in each generation—though generation is a difficult concept when time moves as it moves here. They come, these knowers, not to tend me (I need no tending) but to drop things into the water. Small things. A strand of wool from a blanket that has covered the same family for longer than families have had names. A fragment of carved stone worn smooth not by time but by that particular quality of touching. A single hair from a child born with open eyes.

They never speak when they come. They drop their small offerings and watch them sink slowly through the water that is somehow thicker than water should be, and they watch me approach with my many legs making movements like cursive writing in three dimensions, and they watch me taste what they have brought with my filament-feet, incorporating it not into my body but into my memory-organs, where it becomes part of the cloudy masses that pulse without pulse.

I do not acknowledge them. Acknowledgment would suggest a relationship, and relationships require change—approach, connection, separation. We exist in parallel, these knowers and I, our paths intersecting without ever actually meeting.

What I know, suspended in my pool: That the mist is not quite what it seems. That the mountain's impossible geometry is impossible for a reason. That the constancy everyone accepts as natural is maintained by something that might be called effort if effort could exist without strain, intention without will. The city is held in place not by the mist but by the collective certainty that this is how things are, have always been, will always be. And that certainty is real, is powerful, is perhaps the most real thing here—but it is not inevitable.

In my memory-organs, I hold the taste of moments when the certainty wavered. A child on a stairway who felt something that might have been a question. A tender of the grove who heard the birds say something other than their water-drop call. An elder whose words were almost, almost about something rather than simply being themselves. These moments sink into me like the offerings the knowers bring, and I hold them, and I remember them, and I do nothing with them because there is nothing to be done.

I am not waiting. Waiting implies an expectation of change. I am simply here, in my impossible pool, in the folded corner of a mountain that refuses to unfold, tasting water that has been the same water for so long it has forgotten it was ever rain. My too-many legs move in patterns that might be language if anyone could read them, but no one can, no one tries, and the patterns continue anyway, writing themselves in water that holds its shape just long enough for meaning to almost cohere before dissolving back into stillness.

Sometimes—though sometimes suggests a variation that may not actually exist—I taste something new in the water. Not new-recent but new-different. A mineral that speaks of depths even I have not touched. A vibration that might be the mountain itself breathing, if mountains breathe, if this mountain is only a mountain. When this happens, my memory-organs pulse with something that is not quite excitement, not quite fear, but a third thing for which there is no word because it has never needed naming.

But the sensation passes. It always passes. The water returns to tasting exactly as it has always tasted, and I return to my movements that may or may not be writing, and somewhere above me, the city continues its held existence, and the mist continues to hold it, and I continue to remember things that make no difference to anyone or anything.

I am not important to the city. I am not a secret heart or a hidden truth. I am simply another held thing, a thing that holds in turn—memories that change nothing, knowledge that explains nothing, existence that adds nothing to the sum of what exists. The people do not need me. The city does not need me. Even the knowers who visit my pool do not need me—they come because the coming is part of their pattern, not because they seek what I know.

And yet.

In my impossible pool, with my too-many legs and my memory-organs full of accumulated sameness, I am the only thing in the Terraced City that experiences duration. Not change—I do not change any more than anything else here changes. But duration. The sense that there is a difference between was and is and will be, even if that difference makes no difference.

I remember the child who paused on the stairway. I will remember the child who pauses on the stairway. I am remembering the child who is pausing on the stairway. All three statements are true. All three statements are the same statement. And yet in the gap between them—a gap so small it cannot be said to exist—something persists that is not quite the mist, not quite the stone, not quite the held and holding sameness of everything else.

I am that gap. I am the place where the city's perfect certainty admits, without admitting, that certainty is a thing that must be maintained. I am the flaw in the crystal, the pause in the rhythm, the almost-but-not-quite that lives in the corner of every absolute.

And I am content. Content is not the right word—it suggests an emotional state, and I do not have emotions as the people understand them. But I am something. I am exactly what I need to be, in exactly the place I need to be, doing exactly what needs to be done, which is nothing.

Nothing, held perfectly. Nothing, remembered completely. Nothing, tasted constantly with my filament-feet as I move through water that should not be as thick as it is, in a pool that should not exist, beneath a city that should not be possible.

The mist holds the city. The city holds the people. The people hold their certainty.

And I hold the memory of what it might mean for something, somewhere, somewhen, to let go.
