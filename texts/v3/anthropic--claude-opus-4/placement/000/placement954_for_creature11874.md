I find myself as one of the attendants of the rock.

Not born to it — I came up from the lowlands in my twentieth year, drawn by something I could not name. The climbing nearly killed me. I remember collapsing on the fortieth switchback, my chest burning, the mountain spinning above me like a great stone wheel. An old woman found me there, her braids grey as the limestone, and without speaking she sat beside me until my breathing steadied. Then she handed me a small flat stone, warm from her palm, and gestured that I should hold it. I did not understand then that she was teaching me my trade.

I have been here thirty years now. My birth name was Markus, but I am called Listener-of-the-Fourth-Spring, of the flue of the brass bell, under South Peak. My hair, which was straw-colored in the lowlands, has darkened to the color of wet slate. My hands have learned the mountain's grammar — how sandstone speaks differently from limestone, how the sound changes when water finds a new path inside the rock, how ice creates a ringing tension before it cracks a face.

Each morning I walk my route. It begins at the fourth spring — not the sacred spring, but the smaller one that emerges from a cleft two terraces down — and follows a diagonal line up the eastern face to a place where the rock is black with old water-marks. I have walked this same path eleven thousand mornings, and each morning the mountain tells me something slightly different.

The work is mostly waiting. I press my palms to the stone at specific points, always the same points, and I listen with my skin. Temperature, vibration, the way moisture moves through invisible fractures. Sometimes I use a bronze rod, as long as my forearm, pressing one end to the rock and the other to the bone behind my ear. The mountain's voice travels better through metal than through air.

There are seven of us who attend this face of South Peak. We do not often speak about what we hear — not from secrecy, but because the language for it is thin. How do you say that a particular patch of limestone feels more *anxious* than it did last season? How do you explain that you can taste the difference between stable stone and stone that is thinking about falling? We report changes, when there are changes, by saying things like "the third buttress is louder" or "the water at the seventh stair has turned left." The others understand.

I live in a dwelling halfway up my flue's stack, sharing walls with my teacher (now dead these dozen years) below and a weaver named Anya above. Her loom's rhythm comes through the floor at all hours, a heartbeat that has become part of my sleep. My teacher's rooms are empty now, officially, though I still go down to them when I need to think. Her coat hangs on its peg; I am not tall enough to inherit it, and we wait for someone to grow into it.

My tools are simple: the listening rod, a set of small hammers in different weights, a leather pouch of fine sand that I scatter on the rock to watch how it settles into cracks. Also a bound book, its pages thick with years of notes in vertical script, recording the mountain's moods in a shorthand I have evolved with the other attendants. "Spring 4, morning after snow: fissure at mark 7 singing higher. No width change. Watched three hours." These books accumulate in the flue's archive, and sometimes we go back ten or twenty or fifty years to compare what the mountain was saying then.

The strangest part of the work is how personal it becomes. I know my section of rock the way a shepherd knows individual sheep. There is a place at the ninth mark where the limestone has a vein of quartz running through it, and this vein hums in a particular way when the temperature drops quickly. There is an overhang near the fourth spring where, in winter, ice forms in curtains, and I can tell by the shape of the ice how the face behind it is weathering. These are relationships built over decades. I greet certain formations when I pass them. Not aloud — that would be excessive — but with a brief touch, the way you might touch a doorframe in your own house.

When we sense true instability, the response is swift but calm. I remember the first time I was part of an evacuation — a series of hairline cracks had appeared in a pattern that made three of us uneasy. We spoke to the flue elders, showed them our notes, walked them to the place so they could feel it themselves. Within two days, seventeen people had been moved to other dwellings, their belongings carried carefully down and then up other stairs, their coats and books and looms redistributed through the city like blood finding new paths. 

Eight months later, the face came down at night — a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, then silence. In the morning there was fresh pale stone where the dwellings had been, and within a week people were already discussing how to build with the newly exposed rock, how to incorporate this raw place into the city's slow body. The seventeen displaced were absorbed so seamlessly that, had I not been part of moving them, I would not have been able to say, a year later, which dwellings they had come from.

This is what I love most about the work: it makes me part of the city's deep time. The notes I write today will be read by attendants not yet born. The patterns I learn to recognize become part of a knowledge that accumulates like snow, layer by thin layer, year after year. Sometimes I find notes in the archive from attendants who worked this same route a century ago, and I recognize the voice in the writing — not the person, but the particular attention, the way they noticed that the seventh stair sings in a rising fourth when the melt is heavy.

I am fifty now, which means I have perhaps twenty years of good climbing left. My knees complain on the steeper sections, and I have begun teaching a young woman named Kira, born to the flue of the iron bell, who has the right kind of patience for the work. She is learning to hold the listening rod steady, to feel the difference between surface vibration and deep movement. Yesterday she asked me how I knew to trust what I felt. I handed her a warm stone and told her to hold it. She looked at me, puzzled, and I said: "When you understand why I gave you this, you'll know the answer to your question."

She is still carrying the stone. This is good.

At dawn, often, I see the lammergeiers rising on the first thermals, and I think of all the listeners who have walked these routes before me, whose bones now rest in the chambers beneath our kitchens. They listened to the mountain when it was younger, more restless. They helped the city hold its place against the endless argument of weather and gravity and time. 

Sometimes, when I am listening at the ninth mark, where the quartz vein hums, I imagine the mountain is listening back — not to me, particularly, but to the accumulation of all of us who have ever put our hands to this stone and paid attention. We are, perhaps, how the mountain knows itself: through our careful record of its moods, our generations of notice, our slow response to its changes. 

The bell rings once from the high plaza as I finish my morning route. The wind takes the rest of the sound, as it always does. I put my tools away and begin the long climb home, where Anya's loom will be marking time above my ceiling and the smell of juniper smoke will thread through the shared walls, and where tomorrow I will wake and walk the route again, listening to the mountain's next verse in its long, long poem of becoming itself.

This is my work. This is my place. When I am old and can no longer climb, I will teach Kira the last of what I know, and then I will be carried up to the high stone, and the great slow shapes will take me into the sky, and my notes will remain in the archive, and someone else will listen at the ninth mark and perhaps think of me, briefly, before returning their full attention to the stone.

The stone, always, is what matters. We are just the ones who learned how to hear it speak.
