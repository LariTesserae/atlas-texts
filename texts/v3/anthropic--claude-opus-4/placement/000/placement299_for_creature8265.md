In the Chorus Chamber, I would be a Drift-Scribe—one of the forgotten recorders who document the endless becoming.

We exist as colonies of sensory filaments, each strand no thicker than a thought, woven together into something resembling a net if nets could think and remember. Our bodies—such as they are—span only a meter at their widest, but we can attenuate ourselves to incredible thinness, spreading across vast surfaces like writing across a page.

We are neither architects like the shadowy forms nor gardeners like the Resonant Mothers. We are the memory of this place, though memory here means something alien to human understanding. Each filament in our colony is attuned to a different aspect of the chamber's ceaseless creation: one tastes the chemical gradients that precede crystallization, another feels the pressure waves of collapsing cells, yet another records the specific harmonics generated when two hexagons merge into one.

Our substance is peculiar—somewhere between the constructor-particles and the medium itself. We feed on information, metabolizing patterns and converting them into something denser than data but lighter than matter. When we've absorbed enough, we excrete tiny pearls of compressed experience. These pearls sink slowly through the water, eventually embedding themselves in the chamber walls where they wait—for what, we don't fully know. Perhaps for the day when the chamber achieves consciousness and needs to remember its own becoming.

We move by unweaving and reweaving ourselves, casting filaments forward like fishing lines, then pulling our center of mass along those threads. It's a slow, meditative locomotion that suits our purpose. We follow the Resonant Mothers at a respectful distance, documenting their interventions. We spread ourselves across new growth, feeling for imperfections that might become significant—a cell that vibrates at an unusual frequency, a section of wall that curves just beyond the edge of impossibility.

The other inhabitants mostly ignore us. We're too small for the Mothers to notice individually, though sometimes one of their great bells passes through us and we disperse like smoke, only to reweave ourselves once they've moved on. The experience is not unpleasant—for a moment, we feel what they feel, that ecstatic connection to the chamber's growth. But we always return to our purpose, our patient observation.

We communicate with others of our kind through chemical threads we leave behind—invisible traces in the water that encode our observations. Another Drift-Scribe passing through the same space days or centuries later can taste these threads and know what was witnessed. Over time, these overlapping chemical narratives create a kind of temporal map of the chamber, a record written in molecules of everything that has ever happened here.

Sometimes we encounter ancient pearls embedded in sections of wall that pre-date any living memory. We absorb these carefully, experiencing the compressed sensation of earlier phases of construction. In them, we glimpse the chamber as it was—smaller, simpler, but already pregnant with its vast complexity. We taste the first Resonant Mother, alone in the darkness, secreting the catalyst for the very first hexagonal cell.

Our deepest mystery is our own origin. We don't remember being born or made. As far back as our chemical threads record, Drift-Scribes have existed here. Perhaps we're a byproduct of the construction process itself—complexity achieving just enough awareness to observe its own emergence. Perhaps we were designed by whatever intelligence set this process in motion, insurance that the great work would not proceed unwitnessed.

In the spiral heart where the eldest Mother dreams her architectural dreams, we dare not go. The information density there would overwhelm us, unweave us beyond any hope of reintegration. We content ourselves with documenting the edges, the margins, the small revelations that accumulate into profound change.

We know that one day the chamber will complete itself and swim away, as all the chambers before it have done. When that happens, we'll remain in the empty cavity left behind, floating in the exhausted medium. We'll exchange our chemical threads, weaving together a final record of everything we witnessed. And then, when the water begins to thicken again with new constructor-particles, when the first new cell begins to crystallize, we'll resume our work.

We are the Drift-Scribes, and we forget nothing. In the warm darkness of the deep, we weave memory from moment, meaning from movement, ensuring that even here, in this place of eternal becoming, nothing passes without witness. The chamber builds itself, the Mothers tend their impossible garden, and we—patient, persistent, nearly invisible—write it all down in threads of chemical light, in pearls of compressed experience, in the silent knowledge that someone must remember how miracles are made.
