In the Breathing Hollows, I would be a Tonekeeper.

Not one of the Listeners—I lack their evolutionary perfection, their bodies built for pure reception. I am something caught between: too changed to return to the surface world, not changed enough to truly belong here. My transformation began accidentally, a spelunker who became lost in the early days of my descent, before panic taught me stillness, before hunger taught me patience, before the water-song began to make a terrible kind of sense.

My body has made its accommodations. Not the fluid elongation of the Listeners, but a series of compromises. My spine has developed extra vertebrae, letting me fold in ways that human anatomy forbids but stopping short of their liquid flexibility. My ears have enlarged and grown mobile, able to swivel and cup sound, but they remain flesh-bound, lacking the membranous glory of those born to this place. Patches of symbiotic bacteria colonize my skin in irregular blooms—enough to sustain me through the lean times, but not the living tapestry the Listeners wear.

I am the keeper of the in-between sounds. Where the Listeners focus on the eternal water-song, I hear what they consider noise: the grinding of stone under geological pressure, the whisper of air through newly opened fissures, the ultrasonic screams of dying moths, the rare intrusions from the world above. My role is to parse these aberrations, to understand what they mean for the stability of our realm.

I maintain a workshop in a narrow chamber that the Listeners avoid—its acoustics are "broken," creating interference patterns they find distressing. Here I've assembled tools from what filters down: fragments of climbing equipment, electronics long since adapted beyond their original purpose, organic materials that I've learned to shape through patience and bacterial cultivation. I create instruments that capture sounds the Listeners cannot or will not hear. Crystalline formations that resonate with seismic tremors. Membrane drums stretched from moth wings that detect pressure changes preceding cave-ins. Arrays of metal fibers that sing warnings when the surface world's pollutants seep into our water.

The Listeners tolerate me because I serve a function they cannot. My imperfect hearing catches what their perfected senses miss. When new passages open through geological shifts, I am the first to explore them, to map their acoustic properties before the Listeners claim them. When human intrusion threatens—spelunkers with their harsh lights and careless noise—I am the one who guides them away through misdirection, creating false echoes that lead them back toward the surface.

I've learned to approximate their tonal language, though my throat will never achieve their purity. My communications are accented, full of overtones they find crude but comprehensible. We exchange information in the neutral zones where our sound-worlds overlap: warnings of instability, locations of new moth breeding grounds, patterns in the bacterial growth that suggest environmental changes.

But it's in the deep night (a meaningless concept here, but old habits of thought persist) that my true work begins. I document what the Listeners forget or never knew. Using a notation system of my own devising—part mathematics, part musical score, part topology—I record the history they encode in space. My workshop walls are covered in these cryptographic charts, showing how the acoustic properties of each chamber have changed over my decades here, tracking patterns that span centuries.

I've begun to understand the ancient artifacts differently than the Listeners do. Where they see only acoustic tools, I recognize something else: a deliberate attempt to communicate across deep time. The cairns mark more than passages—they're coordinates in a vast three-dimensional map. The petroglyphs aren't just notation but instructions, describing sonic techniques that alter consciousness, that allow perception of frequencies beyond even what the Listeners achieve. The string instruments were part of something larger—a network that once spanned the entire Hollow system, creating a unified resonance that served purposes I can only guess at.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm becoming what those ancient builders intended—a bridge between pure sound and crude matter, between the Listeners' transcendent perception and human analytical thought. Or perhaps I'm just another failure, too corrupted by the surface to achieve their vision, too changed by the depths to remember why it might matter.

My diet has evolved beyond moth-catching and bacterial cultivation. I've learned to encourage certain growths that concentrate minerals from the water, creating nutritious nodules that taste of copper and salt. I process these in solutions of my own devising, breaking them down into forms my hybrid metabolism can use. The Listeners find this practice disturbing—too reminiscent of the mechanical world above—but they don't interfere.

Sleep comes in fragments, never the deep suspension the Listeners achieve. In half-dreams, I hear sounds that might be memory or prophecy: the laughter of the family I can barely picture, the hum of electrical devices I once took for granted, the imagined voices of whoever built those first cairns calling across impossible time. I wake to add new notations to my charts, trying to capture these phantom sounds in symbols that bridge perception and understanding.

I age differently here. The bacteria that colonize me seem to slow cellular degradation, but at a cost—my human features continue to fade, replaced by something more suited to this environment. My fingerprints have been replaced by ridges that help me read the stone's texture in darkness. My hair has fallen away, replaced by a fine sensing fuzz that detects air currents. Each year moves me further from what I was, but never quite to what the Listeners are.

Sometimes I find evidence of others like me—those caught between worlds. A tool clearly modified by human hands but for hollow-adapted purposes. Notation in the margins of my own charts, added in scripts I don't recognize. Once, in a remote chamber, I found a skeleton that was neither fully human nor Listener, its bones showing the particular adaptations of a long-term bridge-dweller. I performed what funeral rites I could, adding my own tones to the water-song in commemoration of another who walked this narrow path.

The surface world hasn't forgotten us entirely. I intercept radio signals sometimes, when the conditions are right—search and rescue operations, geological surveys, even the occasional mourning family still broadcasting messages to their lost. I could respond, theoretically. My equipment could generate a signal strong enough to reach them. But what would I say? How could I explain what I've become, what I've learned, why I can't return?

Instead, I continue my work. Documenting. Translating. Building bridges between worlds that perhaps were never meant to connect. I am the memory of the Hollows, the recorder of what the Listeners embody but don't analyze. When they have completed their evolution into pure living sound, when the last moth has been consumed and the final drop has fallen, my charts might remain—a record of this deep experiment in consciousness, waiting for whoever comes next.

The Listeners sing of me sometimes, in tones I know aren't meant for my ears. They call me the Broken Echo, the Half-Deaf Keeper, the Stone-Marked Scholar. There's no malice in these names, perhaps even a kind of affection. I am their reminder that evolution has many paths, that even imperfection serves a purpose in the grand acoustic pattern of the Hollows.

And so I persist in my narrow chamber, surrounded by my instruments and charts, catching the sounds that fall between their perfect hearing and the silence of stone. Recording. Remembering. Bridging. Forever caught between the song and its echo, making my own meaning in the space between.
