I would find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—creatures who exist in the violent transition zones where the plateau's edge meets silence.

My body would be all wrong angles and asymmetry. The half of me that faces the plateau has developed thick, sound-absorbing fur that mats into natural acoustic baffles, while my void-facing side is smooth and highly reflective, bouncing the last whispers of vibration back toward their source. I am smaller than the Shepherds—built low and wide for stability in the turbulent acoustic currents where sound becomes wind.

My dwelling is carved into the living rock at the exact point where the ancient bedrock begins to fracture. It's more of a musical instrument than a home—a series of precisely eroded chambers that capture the plateau's roar and fragment it into manageable frequencies. Each room resonates at a different pitch, allowing me to move between sonic environments like walking through colored light.

I would spend my days maintaining the Cascade Gardens—those impossible waterfalls of condensed sound that pour over the plateau's edge. My work involves installing and adjusting arrays of small tuning stones that shape how the sound-water falls, creating vast vertical gardens on the cliff faces. The mosses and ferns here grow sideways and upward, following the acoustic streams rather than gravity.

My most important adaptation would be my ability to exist in both worlds. My ears (or what pass for ears—spiraling chambers in my skull) can shut completely, allowing me to descend into the silent valleys to trade crystallized sound-water with the lowland dwellers who use it for purposes I don't fully understand. But I can also open them gradually, like aperture valves, controlling how much of the plateau's voice I let inside.

I would know every Shepherd who passes through my territory, though we would rarely interact directly. Our relationship would be one of mutual necessity—they need the edge-gardens I maintain as waypoints in their migrations, and I need the subtle adjustments they make to the plateau's greater resonance to keep my cascade systems flowing. We would communicate through the water itself, leaving messages in the mineral patterns of evaporated sound.

In the strange twilight spaces of my threshold existence, I would cultivate hybrid gardens where plateau plants slowly adapt to diminishing sound, creating new species that can survive in partial silence. These evolutionary experiments would be my art and my purpose—literally growing the bridge between two incompatible worlds, one humming leaf at a time.

At night (if night and day even matter here), I would listen to the way silence and sound braid together at the boundary, creating interference patterns that exist nowhere else. In these acoustic twilight zones, I would sometimes glimpse other threshold phenomena—creatures and formations that exist only in the mixing zone, too dependent on both states to fully enter either world.

This is where I would belong: neither fully in the deafening heights nor the silent depths, but dancing forever on the edge where sound becomes substance and silence becomes real.
