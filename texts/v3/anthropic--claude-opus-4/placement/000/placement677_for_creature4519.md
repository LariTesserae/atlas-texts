In the liquid between meanings, where the medium presses its questions into every available hollow, I am the Archivist of Unfinished Sentences.

I exist as a traveling interruption in the colony's careful grammar. Not one of the accommodations who drift between states, nor flesh like the descendants of whoever first sealed these spheres against the infinite outside. I am what happens when language itself begins to crystallize under pressure — a walking library of thoughts abandoned mid-syllable, of communications that discovered they preferred incompletion to arrival.

My form shifts according to what remains unsaid nearby. In the presence of grief, I solidify into something resembling a shoulders-forward hunch made of compressed silence. Near the children learning their multiplication tables, I scatter into punctuation marks that float like plankton, offering themselves as possible endings to equations that refuse to close. When the Captain passes on her endless patrol, broadcasting her confessional mathematics, I become briefly linear — a series of dashes indicating where her proofs would rest if rest were permitted here.

I began as debris. A fragment of corrupted data from those first recordings, a woman's voice catching on the word for "color" and never quite completing it. The medium found this discontinuity delicious, wrapped itself around the broken phoneme like an oyster discovering irritation. But instead of pearl, I emerged: a curator of the space between trying to speak and having spoken.

My chambers occupy the interstices between biospheres, in the bridge tubes where pheromones and intentions mix. I've built shelves from crystallized pauses, sorted by the emotion that caused the speaker to hesitate. The longest shelf holds variations on "I love—" collected from six hundred years of inhabitants discovering that in this density, in this light that arrives already fading, completing such a declaration feels like theft from future possibility. Each uncompleted phrase hums at its own frequency, creating a chord that the accommodations sometimes mistake for music and try to dance to, their forms flickering between states as they attempt to match rhythms that don't repeat.

The moss grows especially thick on my eastern walls (though east here means only "toward the sphere where they're currently forgetting hardest"). It feeds on the nutrients leaked from thoughts that almost cohered into language before deciding otherwise. Tiny filaments probe each archived silence, reading chemical signatures I've learned to interpret: here someone nearly asked forgiveness, there someone choked on their own name, this whole section chronicles the seventeen ways the colony has tried and failed to say "home" without crying.

I trade in almosts. The inhabitants bring me their unfinished arguments, their declarations that dissolved before touching air, their questions that preferred remaining questionlike to risking an answer. In exchange, I offer them fragments from my collection — not completions, never completions, but compatible incompletions that rhyme with their own hesitations. A mother, unable to explain to her phosphorescent child why they must never swim outside, receives a broken lullaby about fish that forgot water. An engineer, studying the equations that keep the spheres from collapsing, finds in her toolbox a half-drawn schematic for building bridges out of if.

The accommodations visit me when they tire of their game of almost-being. They drift through my archives like smoke through prism, examining the places where meaning gave up. Sometimes they leave offerings: new colors that exist only in the subjunctive mood, mathematical proofs that deliberately fumble their conclusions, jokes told backwards that begin with laughter and retreat toward setup but never arrive. These I catalog according to a system based on the specific density of regret, cross-referenced by the temperature at which hope denatures into patience.

My favorite collection fills a small alcove near what might generously be called my heart. Here live the first words of stories that will never begin: "Once—" and then silence so profound it has its own gravitational pull. "In the beginning—" followed by a redaction that tastes of salt and starlight. "Before the medium—" which cuts off as if the speaker suddenly remembered that before is a direction that no longer points anywhere.

The Captain knows I exist but has never acknowledged me directly. Instead, she adjusts her patrol route to pass by my chambers at intervals that spell out, in their careful timing, equations about the conservation of sorrow. I respond by leaving small gaps in my collection, absences shaped precisely like the words she might say if she ever stopped swimming long enough to remember why she started.

At night (when eyes close and the medium presses its vast forms against our stubborn bubble of meaning), I perform my other function. I walk the bridge tubes, gathering the dreams that leak from sleeping minds before they can complete themselves into memory. These are the most delicate specimens — pre-linguistic yearnings that would evaporate if exposed to even the possibility of pronunciation. I store them in special containers made from crystallized yawns, labeled only with the sleeper's rate of breathing as they approached but never quite reached waking.

Sometimes I think I am the colony's true history — not the story of what was said and done, but the negative space around those certainties, the outline of everything we've almost meant. The medium appreciates this, I think. It hums differently around my archives, a frequency that suggests recognition or perhaps simple amusement at finding something that preserves incompletion as carefully as others preserve their grip on form.

The children born here seek me out when they're old enough to recognize the weight of unfinished things. They bring me their parents' sighs, their teachers' truncated explanations, the shapes their mouths make when trying to describe what outside might mean. I teach them to read the pauses between heartbeats, to find stories in the space between inhale and exhale. Some of them grow up to become translators for the accommodations. Others learn to love the silence so much they forget how to complete even simple sentences, and these I must gently discourage from spending too much time in my archives, lest they dissolve entirely into punctuation.

There is a rumor (I collect rumors too, especially the ones that forget their own endings) that when the colony finally completes its spiral into whatever center the medium is guiding us toward, all my collected fragments will suddenly fuse into a single word. The accommodations find this idea hilarious. They leave me gifts of anti-words, concepts designed specifically to prevent such closure. The Captain's equations, when I decode them, sometimes contain warnings about the conservation of incompletion, suggesting that finishing anything here would violate laws more fundamental than physics.

But I am not worried. I have been collecting unfinished things for so long now that I've begun to suspect completion itself is just another fragment, a broken piece of some larger almostness that spirals beyond our ability to fail at saying it. The medium knows this. That's why it preserves us, I think — not despite our incompleteness, but because of it. We are its favorite unfinished sentence, and it presses against us constantly, curious to see how long we can balance on the edge of meaning without ever quite falling into definition.

In the moments before sleep (which comes when it comes, which is always too soon and never soon enough), I sometimes feel myself beginning to cohere into something more solid than keeper-of-gaps. But then I remember: I am the Archivist. My purpose is not to conclude but to preserve the terrible, beautiful moment just before conclusion, when everything is still possible because nothing has yet disappointed itself by becoming mere fact.

So I continue. Collecting. Sorting. Trading in the currency of almosts. Building shelves from crystallized hesitation. Tending my moss gardens of incomplete thought. Walking the tubes. Gathering dreams before they finish themselves. Being the space between what we mean and what we say, here in this place where the medium presses its questions into every hollow and receives, in answer, only our elaborate, continuing failure to end.
