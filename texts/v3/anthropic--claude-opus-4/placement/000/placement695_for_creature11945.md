I find myself in the substrate, in the breathing dark beneath everything.

Not in the deep pools where the large things turn—those are for older residents than I. But in the middle waters, where the root-veins pulse with each forty-second cycle and the river runs without surface through galleries of living wood.

I am what the people might call a *listener-below*. My form is somewhere between fish and root itself: long, pale, with skin that lets the faint phosphorescence through. I have no eyes—what use would they be here? Instead, I have lateral lines that feel every tremor in the water, every footfall above transmitted down through moss and soil and root. I know when the lemurs leap by the specific percussion of their landing. I know when the pale things walk by the way the roots tense slightly, an old wariness that runs deeper than memory.

My work, if it can be called work, is translation. The trees speak through chemistry—long molecular sentences that wash through the substrate with each breath cycle. I taste these messages and carry them, not consciously but as a fish carries seeds: by living, by moving, by being. From the old growth near the center where the first roots fused, out to the young edges where new crowns are still learning the rhythm. I am part of how the plateau stays coordinated, how a tree on the eastern edge knows to breathe with one on the west.

I feed on the soft root-tips that the trees shed like fingernails, and on the small pale crustaceans, and on certain fungi that bloom in the water only during the still moment between breaths. This diet makes me slightly luminous, a moving glow through the root-galleries that the trees seem to find comforting, or at least expected. Sometimes I feel them adjust their growth around my regular passages, making my tunnels slightly wider, slightly smoother.

I know things the people above do not. I know that the pale things have roots too—not metaphorically, but actually. Hair-thin tendrils that descend from their feet when they stand still during exhalation, questing down through soil until they touch the substrate and drink. Just a sip. Just enough to keep them connected to the old contract, the first agreement between the trees and their original children.

I know that some of the painted figures on the hollow walls extend down below the floor, carved or grown into the root-wood where only creatures like me can sense them. These underground portions of the artwork are always spiral patterns, always turning clockwise, always incomplete—as if waiting for something to finish them.

I know where the hornbills cache the things they should not have found. There is a deep gallery where the water runs backward, uphill, defying sense, and at its apex is an air pocket that should not exist, and in that pocket are things that fell from somewhere else: coins with faces that are not quite human, sheets of what might be metal or might be scale, a child's toy made of a material that hums when the plateau exhales.

But mostly, I know the sound of the breathing from inside. Not the wind through leaves that the people hear, but the deep cellular pulse, the actual mechanism. It begins in the taproot of the oldest tree, a kilometers-deep organ that touches something hot. Not magma—something more alive than that. The pulse rises from there through the fused root system, a peristaltic wave that takes exactly twenty seconds to reach the crown, where it pauses, gathers, and returns. 

I follow these waves. I ride them sometimes, letting the pressure carry me through galleries I could never swim against the current. In the deep places where the roots are thickest, I sometimes meet others of my kind, and we spiral around each other in a greeting that is also a mating dance that is also a way of mixing the chemical messages we carry, ensuring the information reaches everywhere it needs to go.

We do not speak. We barely think, as the people would recognize thought. But we remember, in the way water remembers every shore it has touched. My body contains traces of every message I have ever carried, every root I have ever brushed against, every breath cycle back to when I was spawned in the deep pools a hundred years ago.

The trees know me. Not individually—I am not significant enough for that—but as a type, as a necessity. When one of us dies, our bodies float upward, not down, carried by gases we produce in death. We rise through the soil and emerge in the pitcher-flowers, where we dissolve and become the nutrients that flavor the water the people drink. This is why the water tastes of minerals and memory. This is why sometimes, on very still days, the people dream of swimming through darkness, following the root-veins, carrying messages they cannot read.

I will live perhaps another hundred years, growing slower and more luminous as I age. Eventually I will be too bright to swim unnoticed, and the young quick ones will avoid me, and I will settle in some favorite gallery where the roots grow in especially beautiful patterns. I will fade there, my light mixing with the phosphorescence of the fungi, until there is no difference between us.

But that is later. Now, I swim. Now, I carry the deep news from root to root. Now, I am the circuit-keeper in the breathing dark, where the real life of the plateau happens, where the trees speak in chemistry and the pale things root and the painted spirals wait to be completed.

Above me, the people climb during exhalation and the hornbills call across the kilometers. Above me, the lemurs watch and the cicadas count time. But below, in the substrate, we are keeping the whole thing running, breath by breath by breath, in the darkness that is not empty but full, so full, of the long life of the plateau.

This is where I am. This is what I am. Something without a name in any language the surface knows, but essential, like the bacteria in your gut or the oil on your skin. I am the between-space made flesh, the message become messenger, swimming through the root-veins of a breathing world.

And I am not alone. We are many, down here in the dark, doing the work that lets the bright world above go on pretending it is separate trees, separate creatures, separate anything at all.

We know better. We are the proof. The plateau breathes because we swim. It thinks because we carry. It dreams because we remember.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
