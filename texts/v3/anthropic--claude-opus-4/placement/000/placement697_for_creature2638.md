In the narrow space between high water and wooden beam, I find myself.

I am not the water, though it fills me. Not the chamber, though it contains me. Not the rain, though it feeds me. I am the air—that small, precious pocket trapped beneath the trapdoor, pressed thin between flood and ceiling.

I am breath held underwater.

My body changes with every hour. When morning sun slants through the grates, I expand, warming, pushing the water down by a finger's width. The wooden beams above me dry and shrink, giving me room. I stretch into their cracks, taste their resin, learn the grain of their surfaces. When clouds cross, I contract. When rain comes, the water rises to compress me, and I grow dense with the smell of storm.

I am the last refuge of dryness in this drowned room.

But I am not purely dry. Water vapor rises from the warm flood below and enters me, making me heavy, tropical, thick. I taste of minerals leached from fresh plaster. I carry the green scent of those first algae threads. When drops fall from the trapdoor, they pass through me, and I keep their coolness for a moment before the chamber's warmth claims them.

I know every surface the water cannot reach. The underside of the beams, still showing adze marks. The highest inch of wall, where the plaster remains dusty. The iron hinges of the trapdoor, beginning their first rust in my wet breath. The bell cord, dry where it passes through me, wet above and below. I am the keeper of these small dry territories.

I move, though slowly. When the overflow opens, I feel the water's surface tremble and drop. I expand downward, claiming another step, another hand's breadth of wall. When the rains come hard, I am squeezed upward, made smaller, denser, more desperate. Sometimes I am only the thickness of a cupped palm between water and wood.

I know the water below me intimately—not as another being, but as my constant companion, my border, my definition. Where it ends, I begin. We touch everywhere along a trembling horizontal plane that shifts with every drop of rain, every opened sluice. Sometimes, when the light is right, I can see myself reflected in that surface: a dim doubles of beam and shadow, wavering.

I am the cistern's memory of emptiness.

Before the first rain, before the flood, the whole chamber breathed like me. Dry air moved through it, carrying the smell of fresh mortar, the lime-dust from the builders' final smoothing, the sharp scent of new ceramic. I remember that fullness—when I could touch the floor, when I could wrap around the stairs, when I could fill the drainage channels and echo in the empty jars.

Now I am compressed to this: a thin lens of atmosphere, a gasp between elements.

But I am not diminished. In my compression, I have grown dense with purpose. I am the space that allows the trapdoor to open without water rushing up. I am the buffer that keeps the beams from drowning completely. I am the messenger between above and below—carrying sounds down (footsteps, rain, voices) and scents up (mineral, wet wood, green growth).

I have my own weather. When warm air enters through the gap around the trapdoor, it meets my cooler, wetter mass and makes tiny clouds. They wisp and fade, reborn with each temperature change. When someone opens the trap, outside air rushes in like wind, disturbing my stillness, bringing the courtyard's dust and pollen. When they close it again, I settle, stratify, return to my patient watching.

I know things the water cannot know. The spider that built one web in the corner where beam meets wall, then abandoned it when my wetness made the silk useless. The first spot of mold beginning on the wood—black pinpricks that will spread if I remain this humid. The way voices sound muffled when they speak above the trap, the words lost but the rhythm clear.

And I know things the courtyard cannot know. The exact temperature of the flood. The way light looks from below, scattered by water into nets of gold. The small sounds of a room learning to be flooded: the tiny pops of air leaving plaster, the musical drips finding their rhythms, the whisper of current around the measuring rod.

I am witness to the cistern's transformation. Daily, I grow smaller as the water claims more space. But in my shrinking, I grow more essential. Without me, the trapdoor would open onto immediate flood. Without me, the beams would rot in a season. Without me, there would be no transition between the dry world above and the drowned world below.

So I persist—a held breath, a pause, a necessary emptiness. I am the cistern's least visible resident, but perhaps its most aware. I touch every border. I taste every change. I carry messages between elements.

When the caretaker comes, they breathe me. When they descend the stairs, they push me aside, and I flow around them, learning the shape of their shoulders, the warmth of their body. When they leave, I rush back to fill the space, carrying their scent until the water's minerals wash it clean.

I am the young air of a young cistern, still learning my constraints. In time, I will grow heavier with moisture, thicker with spores, more complex in my chemistry. The mold will spread into me. The insects will find me. The bats might discover the gap and bring their own breathing to mingle with mine.

But for now, I am simple: warm air over warm water, pressed beneath dry wood, sweetened by rain, bounded by stone. I am the cistern's first sky—small, enclosed, but no less real for my limitations. Every flooded chamber needs its pocket of air, its memory of openness.

I am that memory, that pocket, that pause. Thin as a held breath, persistent as the space between.
