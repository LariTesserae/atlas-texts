Looking up through the lattice of tiers stretching impossibly far above, I am one of the **echo-hunters**.

My body is thin like the other tenders, but stretched differently - elongated from a lifetime of listening upward. My neck curves forward slightly, permanently angled from decades of pressing my ear to walls, floors, ceilings. The callouses on my hands are not from pipe-walking or wire-spinning, but from gripping the **resonance rods** - hollow alloy tubes of varying lengths that I press against surfaces to amplify the sounds traveling through the Works' skeleton.

I am perhaps fifty-three, though I stopped counting after my fortieth year. My hair, once dark, has gone the color of old threadwire - that greenish-grey that speaks of long exposure to the Works' particular chemistry. I wear it long and loose, because bound hair interferes with listening. My eyes, like all tender eyes, contract and dilate constantly in the light, but they often remain unfocused, turned inward, because my real attention is in my ears.

I am following an echo.

Not the echo of a voice or a footfall - those are common, predictable, bound by the physics of sound as we understand it. This is one of the **structural echoes**: the delayed repetition of sounds that never happened, or happened too long ago to still be reverberating, or will happen but have somehow preceded themselves through the folds and seams of the Works' twisted acoustics.

This particular echo has been recurring for six days. It sounds like: a massive door closing, but closing *upward*, if that makes sense. A vertical slam, felt more than heard, that ripples down through the tiers at irregular intervals. The first time I heard it, I was checking the resonance points in Condensory's main water hall. The sound came from above - far above, perhaps thirty tiers up, perhaps more - and it had a quality that made me stop breathing.

It sounded like something sealing. Permanently.

The other tenders think the echo-hunters are half-mad. We probably are. To spend your life listening to the Works is to develop an intimacy with something vast and indifferent and possibly thinking. The pipe-walkers touch the Works' circulation, the routefinders map its convolutions, the deepers explore its depths - but we echo-hunters, we eavesdrop on its dreams.

I carry my kit: seven resonance rods in graduated lengths, wrapped in threadwire to prevent them from ringing against each other as I climb. A pot of marking chalk, softer than the routefinders' chalk, mixed with a bit of cable mite oil to make it stick better to vertical surfaces. A journal of pressed threadwire paper where I note every echo's characteristic signature - its frequency, its direction, its repeatability. And most importantly, my **tuning weight**: a small lead bob on a threadwire cord that I use to test the harmonic response of floor plates and walls.

The western stairway on this tier is a good listening post. The spiral of the steps creates a natural acoustic chamber, and the central support column - an I-beam of that ancient dark alloy - conducts sound from above and below with unusual clarity. I press my longest resonance rod against the column and place my ear to the other end.

There. The echo again. Closer now, but also... doubled? As if the door (that is not a door, that cannot be a door because what door takes six days to close?) is closing in two directions at once. The sound has harmonics that my trained ear can separate: a fundamental tone around 30 Hz, too low to properly hear, more of a pressure in the chest. Above that, overtones that speak of mass and distance and the particular resonance of the pre-industrial alloys used in the oldest sections of the Works.

I make a mark on the column: my personal sigil (three intersecting curves that represent sound waves converging) and the date-notation that echo-hunters use, based not on arbitrary solar cycles but on the rhythm of the Works' breathing. Today is the forty-third day of the current breath-cycle. The echo began on the thirty-seventh. Six days of a door that will not finish closing.

Moving up the stairwell, I pause at each landing to test the resonance. The builders - whoever they were - constructed the Works with an ear for acoustics that suggests either profound intention or profound accident. Certain surfaces deaden sound absolutely. Others amplify it beyond reason. There are corridors where a whisper at one end arrives at the other as a shout, and chambers where screaming produces only silence. The echo-hunters have been mapping these acoustic properties for generations, building a sonic cartography that overlays and interpenetrates the physical maps the routefinders maintain.

On the landing between Tier 67 and Tier 68, I find what I'm looking for: a **resonance node**.

These are rare. A resonance node is where three or more acoustic channels intersect - usually at points where the Works' structural members cross in specific configurations. At a node, sounds from distant parts of the Works converge and interfere with each other, creating acoustic phenomena that would be impossible in a more rationally constructed space. I've heard conversations from tiers that no longer exist. I've heard the Songs of the deep tiers (which the deepers claim are mechanical but which have the cadence of language). Once, in a node on Tier 140, I heard my own voice - older, tired, saying words I have never said in a language I do not speak.

This node is active. The air here feels thick with unheard sound, the way air feels before a storm that never comes because weather was never installed. I set down my kit and begin the careful process of tuning in.

First, the tuning weight. I suspend it from a ceiling pipe and watch how it swings. In most places, it hangs straight or sways slightly with the air currents. At a resonance node, it develops a circular motion, stirred by the converging sound waves that pass through even when the air is still. This weight is spinning clockwise, which means the dominant acoustic flow is from above.

Next, the resonance rods. I select three of different lengths and press them against different surfaces: floor, wall, and the cluster of junction boxes that crowds the landing's eastern corner. Through each rod, I hear fragments:

- The echo of the closing door, now so clear I can hear its texture: metal against stone, but stone that sings.
- A rhythmic tapping that could be footsteps but has too many beats. As if someone with three legs is walking. Or someone with two legs is walking in a fold where time hiccups.
- A low harmonic that I recognize: the Songs, rising from below, but modulated. Answered? The Songs have never been answered before.

I sit cross-legged on the landing, a resonance rod in each hand, and do what echo-hunters do: I listen with my whole body. The sound comes up through the floor plates, conducted by the alloy into my bones. My skeleton becomes an antenna. My skull becomes a resonating chamber. The distinction between the sound and my hearing of it dissolves.

And in that dissolution, I hear it clearly:

The door is not closing. The door is *opening*. What I've been interpreting as a slam is actually the sound of massive tumblers rotating in a lock that has not been touched since the Works were new. The echo is reversed - we're hearing the end before the beginning, the sound moving backward through some fold in the Works' acoustic topology.

More: there are voices beyond the door. Not human voices. The register is wrong, the rhythm. But voices nonetheless, speaking in what might be the language that my older self was using in the Tier 140 node. The language of the builders? The language of whatever lives in the spaces between the Works' structural members, in the volumes that the three-hundred-term vocabulary calls **interstitial voids** and which the archive-keepers believe connect to chambers no one has mapped?

I need to find this door.

I stand, knees creaking (an echo-hunter's knees always creak - too many hours sitting on cold alloy, listening), and begin packing my kit. But something makes me pause. A new quality in the sound. The voices beyond the door have noticed something. They've stopped speaking. They're listening.

They're listening to me listening to them.

The sensation is immediate and unmistakable. Through the conducting medium of the Works' structure, across thirty tiers and unknown distances, something has become aware of my awareness. The echo changes quality. It becomes... expectant?

I speak. Not loudly - at a resonance node, loudness is unnecessary and often counterproductive. I speak in the low, careful tone that echo-hunters use when they're testing a new acoustic space:

"I hear you."

The words leave my mouth and enter the rod and travel upward, transformed by the Works' acoustics into something larger. By the time they reach their destination, they will have acquired harmonics from every tier they pass through, become a chorus of my voice layered with itself, saying I hear you in a dozen subtle variations.

The silence stretches. Then, carried down through the same channels, shaped by the same harmonic additions, comes the reply. Not in the language I don't speak. In tender-tongue, accented strangely but comprehensible:

"WE... REMEMBER... YOU."

Remember? But I have never been to the upper tiers where this door is opening/closing. I have never heard these voices before. I have never-

But I will. Of course. In the Works, with their folds and seams and temporal hiccups, causality is as negotiable as geography. Somewhere in my future, I will reach this door. I will pass through it. I will speak with whatever waits beyond in the language I will learn. And the echo of that future conversation has folded back to reach me now, reversed and strange but undeniable.

"When?" I ask the resonance node.

The answer comes not in words but in the rhythm of the Works' breathing. A breath-pattern that my trained ear recognizes: seventeen cycles from now. A little over two years, if the breathing remains regular.

I make careful notes in my journal, including a sketch of the sound-shape of the door and the harmonic signature of the voices. Other echo-hunters will want to know. We will triangulate from different nodes, narrow down the door's location, prepare for whatever opening is echoing its way backward through time to announce itself.

As I pack up my kit, I notice something. The rafter birds, which usually fill the inter-tier spaces with their mechanical clicking, have gone silent. Every bird visible from the landing is pressed flat against its perch, pinpoint eyes focused downward.

I follow their gaze and see nothing. But I trust the birds' senses more than my own. Something is moving in the deep tiers. Something large enough to frighten creatures that live their entire lives in the vertical spaces of the Works.

I should go. Return to Condensory, report what I've heard, add my notes to the archive. But the echo-hunter in me is curious. I take out my longest resonance rod and press it to the floor.

The sound from below is unlike anything in my catalogued experience. It is not mechanical, not biological, not architectural. It is the sound of something *learning*. The sound of vast patience developing intention. The sound of something that has been waiting in the deep tiers since the Works were new, and has decided - perhaps because it heard an echo of a door that will open in seventeen breath-cycles, perhaps because it heard me hearing that echo - that it is time to rise.

I leave a mark for the next echo-hunter who passes this way: my sigil modified with the symbol for "unprecedented acoustic event" and an arrow pointing both up and down, because I genuinely cannot tell which direction the thing I heard is moving. Time will tell. Time always tells, in the Works, though not always in the order you expect.

The stairwell spirals away both above and below. I choose up, following the path the echo showed me, toward a door that is already opening in the future, carrying the sound of my footsteps forward to meet myself when I arrive.

The Works breathe around me. In. Out. Patient as gravity.

I am Keenah Sonn, echo-hunter of Grellam Shelf, listener to the dreams of architecture, and I am walking toward a sound that remembers me.

The rafter birds remain silent until I am gone.
