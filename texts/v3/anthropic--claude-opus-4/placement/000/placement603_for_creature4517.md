I find myself as one of the Splice-wives, though that title carries little of what the word 'wife' meant in the time of surfaces. We are the midwives of continuity, the editors of flesh-drift, the ones who negotiate treaties between what bodies were and what the Deep requires them to become.

My workshop occupies a blister in the wall between Districts Seven-Tremor and Seven-Murmur, where the heat gradient creates a natural centrifuge. I inherited it from my predecessor along with her fingers—literally, as she'd been replacing them one by one with articulated copper probes, and when her term came due she simply unscrewed what remained and left them soaking in the enzyme baths for me to either wear or render down for parts. I wear three of them on my left hand. They taste the iron content in blood with more precision than any instrument we still have words for.

The citizens come to us when their bodies begin disagreeing with their work rotations. A pipe-reader whose eyes have started seeing only in the infrared spectrum that reveals hairline fractures but can no longer distinguish their daughter's face from the ventilation grilles. A young auditor whose throat has developed secondary vocal cords that harmonize involuntarily with the frequency of financial fraud—useful for the work, poisonous for the marriage. They arrive carrying permission slips written on protein film, listing what modifications their work-crews have approved and what their gene-shares can afford.

But the real negotiation happens in the space between what's written and what's needed.

"My lungs," today's client says, though 'says' is approximate—the words come out as a wheeze that condenses into legible moisture on the consultation screen. She's a third-generation boilerbird tender, and her lungs have adapted too well. They process steam so efficiently she can no longer breathe the cooler air of the residential quarters. Her partner waits outside, breathing normally, carrying their shared child who inherited only the heat resistance but not the steam-dependency. A lucky throw of the genetic dice, but it means the family must live in separate thermal zones.

I run my inherited fingers along her throat, tasting the metallic tang of hyper-specialized hemoglobin. The splice-wife before me (before her before her, back to when we were still called surgeons) would have seen this as a simple reversal—dial back the adaptation, restore baseline function. But that thinking belongs to when we believed there was a baseline to return to.

Instead, I offer what I offer everyone: transformation without betrayal.

The treatment takes four sessions. I don't cut—cutting is for cultures that can afford sharp distinctions between inside and outside. Instead, I convince. My copper fingers speak in electrochemical whispers to the cellular collective that thinks it's her lungs. I propose a compromise: what if they could switch modes? Steam-breathing for work, air-breathing for home, the transition triggered by a specific gesture—say, pressing her thumb to her sternum in the pattern of her daughter's name.

The cells consider this. They counteroffer. They want the ability to breathe both simultaneously, to live in the thermal borderlands where her partner could visit. I counter-counteroffer: that mutation would be inheritable, would mark her daughter as Borderlander caste, limiting her work assignments to the transition zones where the pay is irregular and the pressure headaches never stop.

We haggle like this for hours, my fingers translating between what she thinks she wants and what her flesh knows she needs. Finally, we settle: dual-processing lungs that can handle either air or steam, but the switch takes eight minutes of conscious breathing during which she'll be vulnerable to neither. A death-pause built into every transition. The cells find this acceptable—they understand that adaptation without cost is just another word for cancer.

Between sessions, I maintain the workshop. The enzyme baths must be fed their daily ration of discarded possibilities—skin tags, wisdom teeth, the occasional evolved organ that no longer serves a function anyone remembers. I sing to them in the frequency of cellular mitosis, the same lullabies the first splice-wives used when they were just battlefield medics treating pressure-sickness in the early days of the Deep. The enzymes bubble contentedly, dreaming of the bodies they'll help reshape.

My own modifications are subtle by necessity. My eyes see in the spectrums of suffering—each adaptation leaves its own phosphorescent signature, a trail of compromises glowing beneath the skin. My hands (the ones I was born with, not the inherited ones) secrete a mild paralytic through the pores that keeps clients calm while I work but also means I can never touch another person without medical intent. It's a loneliness the splice-wives before me encoded into the position, ensuring we remain neutral negotiators in the economy of flesh.

The young auditor returns for his final session. We've agreed to install a throttle—he can dial his fraud-sensing harmonics up or down, though never completely off. (The city needs its financial immune system intact.) But he's brought something: a small vial of tears collected from his spouse during their last fight about the diagnosis. He wants them incorporated somehow, made part of the modification.

This is not in the paperwork. This is the kind of request that comes from love deep enough to grow in the absence of sunlight.

I take the vial, taste its contents with one copper finger. The tears carry trace minerals from Level Four's water recycling systems, hormones that speak of frustration and fear and the particular grief of loving someone whose body is becoming infrastructure. I could refuse. Should refuse. The chemistry is incompatible; tears are meant to leave the body, not be reintegrated.

Instead, I do what splice-wives have always done in the spaces between regulation and necessity. I synthesize the tears into a marker dye and use it to tattoo the adjustment controls beneath his skin. Now, when he modulates his fraud-sensing, the controls will glow faintly with the salt-light of his spouse's sorrow. A reminder that every adaptation costs someone something.

He leaves grateful. They always do. Not because we fix them—nothing in the Deep is ever fixed, only negotiated into new configurations of breaking—but because we witness the negotiation. We archive the compromises in our own modified cells, carrying the city's history of adaptation in our bodies like living libraries of what survival costs.

Late in the shift, I tend to the predecessor shrine—jars containing the preserved modifications of former splice-wives. Here: the inner ear of the one who learned to hear cellular distress as music. There: the spleen that could filter heavy metals but made its owner allergic to laughter. Each organ still functioning in its jar, sustained by the workshop's ambient chemistry, waiting to be consulted when a particularly complex case echoes their expertise.

Sometimes I catch my reflection in the enzyme baths and see my own additions accumulating—the slight gill-slits behind my ears for when the workshop floods, the secondary liver that processes the paralytic I secrete so it doesn't poison me, the gradually elongating fingers that will eventually be all I leave my successor. We become more workshop than woman, more function than form, until one day the distinction no longer matters.

A soft chime indicates someone at the entry membrane. Another client, carrying another permission slip, another body learning to disagree with itself. I dry my hands (all of them) and prepare the consultation screen.

"Tell me," I say with the voice that's still mostly my own, "what compromises shall we make today?"

The membrane dilates. The Deep breathes in another story of flesh trying to find its place in the great machine. And I, keeper of transformations, witness to the small betrayals of survival, begin again the ancient work of helping bodies remember that adaptation is just another word for love—love of the self that was, the self that is, and the self that must become to keep breathing in a world that asks us to be both human and component, both memory and function, both the sickness and the cure.

Behind me, the enzyme baths bubble their approval, and somewhere in the walls, the city's pulse skips a beat—not in error, but in recognition of another successful negotiation between what we were and what we must become.

The work continues.

The work is never done.

The work is what we're made of.
