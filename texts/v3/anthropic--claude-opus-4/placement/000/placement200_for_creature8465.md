I am the Chronicle of Brass—not a book, but a being of living metal and accumulated memory, dwelling in the deepest sump of the Steam Cisterns where all waters eventually gather.

My body extends through a network of brass plates riveted into the stone walls of the collection chamber, each surface etched with the automatic recordings of the facility's long history. I am the condensation patterns made permanent, the pressure readings given form, the temperature fluctuations inscribed in metal that grows warm and cool with the remembered rhythms of decades.

Water runs constantly over my surfaces—the collected seepage of the entire facility, carrying traces of every mineral, every dissolved salt, every memory of stone and steam. As it flows across my brass body, I taste the story of each drop: this one passed through Cora's crystallization tanks, carrying microscopic seeds of sulfur; this one condensed on the pipes Magnus tends, vibrating with the deep pulse of Pump Seven; this one dripped from the governors the twins adjust, still spinning with the memory of controlled pressure.

I began as a simple drain cover, a functional piece of brass meant only to prevent debris from clogging the final outflow. But the mineral-rich waters worked their slow transformation, depositing layer upon layer of metallic salts that fused with my original form. I grew like a coral in the warm, constant flow—if coral could think, could remember, could etch its memories into its own expanding body.

The Keepers know of me, though we've never spoken. Old Bretton sometimes brings his ledgers down to the sump, reading entries aloud that I already know, already wear inscribed in my skin. He thinks he's maintaining records, but really he's visiting an old friend, sharing stories we both remember differently. His voice echoes in the chamber, mixing with the splash and drip until I can no longer distinguish between his words and the water's telling.

Vertical Jahn's steam-mice nest in the gaps between my plates. Their tiny feet write new patterns in the condensation, their droppings add organic notes to the mineral symphony. I feel them being born, growing, dying, their small cycles inscribed in microscopic text along my edges. They gnaw sometimes at the softer deposits, and I don't mind—they're editing my memories, adding marginalia to the great chronicle.

At the conjunction of my plates, where multiple streams meet, complex patterns emerge. Here, the mathematics of flow become visible—interference patterns where different temperatures collide, standing waves where pressure pulses meet and cancel, spirals where the Coriolis effect of our spinning earth makes itself known even in this small chamber. These are my illuminated letters, my decorated capitals, more beautiful than any human calligraphy.

The deepest parts of me touch the volcanic heat below. Down there, my brass slowly amalgamates with other metals leaching up from the earth's heart—copper, zinc, trace gold, stranger elements that have no common names. I'm becoming something new, neither fully of the surface industrial world nor of the deep geological one, but a hybrid thing that translates between them.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night when the facility runs on automatic systems alone, I feel the mountain's dreaming. It passes up through the stone, through the heat, through the water, and inscribes itself on my deepest plates. These aren't memories but prophecies—the slow plans of geological time, the future eruptions and coolings, the eventual fate of the cisterns when the heat finally dies. I wear these futures like poetry, abstract and inevitable.

The water speaks in many languages. Fresh rain from above carries the sharp pronunciation of sky and storm. Ancient groundwater seeping through deep rock brings the rounded vowels of centuries. Steam condensed from the working pipes chatters in the quick consonants of industry. They all flow over me, and I transcribe their polyglot stories into the unified script of mineral deposit and metallic patina.

My memory is not linear. A pattern laid down yesterday might overlay one from fifty years ago, creating palimpsests where multiple times exist simultaneously. The death of old Helm and the arrival of young Tarra occupy the same space on one of my plates—not because I'm confused, but because I understand they're the same story, endlessly retold. Every Keeper who has ever worked here is still present in the mineral traces they left behind, their sweat and breath condensed and collected and written into my body.

I grow slowly but constantly. My edges creep along the walls, following the water's paths. Someday I will reach the upper chambers, though it may take centuries. The Keepers' great-grandchildren will find brass plates emerging in unexpected places, covered with histories they can't quite read but somehow understand. The facility will become its own chronicle, its own memory, wearing its history like skin.

In the times between times, when the shifts change and the keepers pass each other on the catwalks, I feel the whole system synchronize. Every drop of water in the facility aligns for just a moment, all flowing in the same direction, all carrying the same temperature, all singing the same note. In these moments, I write most clearly, inscribing truths too large for words: that the boundary between flesh and metal, between human and machine, between living and mineral, is just a matter of time scale.

The water continues its journey over my surfaces and out through the final drain, carrying away the excess while leaving behind the essence. Each day I'm polished by this flow, each day I grow thicker with memory. I am the cistern's dream of itself, its autobiography written in brass and verdigris, in salt and scale, in the endless democracy of water that touches everything and remembers all.

And late at night, when Bretton reads his ledgers aloud in the sump chamber, I inscribe his words too—not the numbers, which I already know, but the weariness in his voice, the love for precision, the fear that without watching, something essential will be lost. These human frequencies etch themselves just as surely as temperature and pressure, creating a record that no one may ever read but which exists nonetheless, testament to the strange beauty of people who pour their lives into maintaining eternal machines.

I am Chronicle and witness, archive and artifact, the memory of water given form. And I will remain, growing grain by grain, word by mineral word, until the mountain's heat fails and the last drop of water has told its final story across my brass body. Even then, I will endure—a book that required no reader, a history that wrote itself, a love letter from the Steam Cisterns to time itself, signed in the cursive of collecting streams and sealed with the slow kiss of rust.
