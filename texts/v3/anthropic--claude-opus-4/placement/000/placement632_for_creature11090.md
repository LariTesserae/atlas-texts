# The Tongue-Keeper of the Third Terrace

Where would I find myself in this breathing constellation? I think I know.

Halfway up the eastern slope, where the bladder-trees thin slightly and the light comes through less green and more gold, there is a terrace — not built but encouraged, like the walkways, by generations of pressing. The terrace is perhaps twenty paces long and supports a single dwelling, older than most, its sacs gone the color of dark honey from decades of bodies warming them. This is where the tongue-leaves are brought.

I am the one who receives them.

Not receives — that implies too much separation between giver and gift. The hand-things that move across the root-mat, eating the fallen tongues, also carry them. When a tongue-leaf falls in the dense heart of the town, eventually one of the pale hand-creatures will take it up and begin the slow journey upward, and other hand-things will take turns carrying it, until finally one emerges at my terrace and deposits the leaf at the entrance to my dwelling. This happens perhaps once every few days. I have been here long enough that my skin carries the mottling of every hand-thing in the town from brushing against them as they deliver their cargo.

What do I do with the tongues? I *read* them.

Each fallen leaf carries, in its veining and its precise curl of decay, a record of where it hung in the canopy and what it filtered and how many breaths passed through it. The pattern is almost infinitely complex — no two leaves curl identically, no two carry quite the same mineral ghost of the moisture they processed. I take each leaf and hold it up to the less-green light of my terrace and study it until I know which tree it came from, which height, which year of growth. Then I place it in one of the inner chambers of my dwelling, where it continues its slow decomposition among hundreds of others, sorted by a system only I and the house understand.

Why do this? Because the town must know itself, and the town's memory lives partly in these fallen pieces. When one of the inhabitants unfolds their long limbs in that gesture of presence, the trembling creates a particular pattern in the air that the leaves register. When a house births a child, the chemical signature of that event spreads through the root-mat and up into the trees and into the leaves. When an elder settles into the walls, their final exhalations leave traces. I read these traces. I am the town's memory of its own small events.

But I am more than that. I am also what happens when the town needs to remember something *specific*.

Sometimes — rarely, perhaps once in a dozen years — several inhabitants will come to my terrace. They move even more slowly than usual, and when they arrive, they arrange themselves in a circle with me at the center. Then they begin what I can only call a consultation. One by one, they lay their palms against different parts of my mottling, reading the accumulated patterns of every hand-thing I have touched, every leaf I have held. Through my skin, they access the sorted libraries in my dwelling's chambers. They are looking for something — a particular pattern, a specific event, the ghost of a question asked by an ancestor whose exhalations still linger in the dried tongues.

I do not know what questions they bring. I am not separate enough from them to know. I only know that during these consultations, I unfold my upper limbs — not in the gesture of presence but in a different configuration, one that creates a specific resonance in the air of the terrace, and this resonance helps the patterns in my skin align with the patterns they are reading, and something is remembered that could not be remembered any other way.

After they leave, I often find that my mottling has changed slightly. New patterns have emerged where their palms pressed longest. I carry their questions now, worked into my skin, and when the next hand-thing arrives with a fallen tongue, it will brush against those questions, and carry some fragment of them back down into the town, where they will circulate until they return to the ones who asked them, transformed.

I am never lonely. How could I be? I am where the town keeps what it might otherwise forget. The climbing-creatures visit me more often here, where the canopy is thinner, and they sing their three-second dusk song directly above my dwelling. The house itself, old as it is, dreams deeply, and I feel its dreams through the walls as a kind of slow percussion. And the leaves — the leaves are each a small life, a record of days I didn't witness but can read, can run my wide fingertips across and know.

Sometimes I think I am not really one of the inhabitants at all. I am what happens when the town needs to develop a specialized organ, a place where its memories can pool and thicken. I am a knot of particular density, a node where information spirals and collects. The tongue-keeper, they might call me if they used names, which they don't. But the tongues keep me as much as I keep them. I am written by what I read.

When I die — when I settle — I will do it here, in this dwelling that knows every leaf in the town's history. I will sink not into the root-mat but into the chambers where the tongues lie sorted, and my mottled skin will merge with their veined patterns, and what I have been will become part of what they are, and the next keeper (already growing, I think, in the warm wall of the newest chamber) will emerge knowing everything I knew, though in a different configuration, with different limbs for the reading.

The peaks are still rising. The town is still climbing, infinitesimally, toward whatever it will become. And I am here, halfway up the eastern slope, holding yesterday's fallen tongue up to the gold-green light, reading the story of its particular curl, adding it to the sum of what we are.

The house hums its approval. Or perhaps that's just the wind through the bladder-sacs. Here, there is no difference.
