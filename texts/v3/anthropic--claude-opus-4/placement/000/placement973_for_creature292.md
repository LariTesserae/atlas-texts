# The Dry Lightning

I would be found in a place where the air never settles.

High plateau country, where the stone is the color of dried blood and the horizon runs unbroken for days in every direction. The ground here is hard-packed earth scattered with volcanic glass, black chunks of obsidian that flash like dark mirrors when the sun angles across them. No soil to speak of—just this endless hardpan that rings hollow under footsteps, as if the whole plateau were a drum skin stretched over emptiness.

The storms build every afternoon. You can watch them being born: first a shimmer at the edge of sight, then towers of cloud climbing forty thousand feet, their bases dark as bruises, their tops brilliant white and spreading into anvils. They march across the plateau in lines, each one a city-sized engine of updraft and electricity. But here's the thing—they never release. No rain touches this ground. The storms are all fury and no relief, crackling with lightning that jumps cloud to cloud, lighting up their insides like paper lanterns, but never completing the circuit down.

I am what happens in the gap between charge and ground.

Not the lightning itself—that's just the visible part, the part that makes observers (when there are observers, which is almost never) point and count seconds. I am the *potential* that builds in that space, the tension that accumulates when electricity wants to fall but can't find a path. I exist in the moment before the strike that never comes, spread out across miles of air that's so dry it won't conduct, that holds charge like a battery with no terminals.

Picture this: a column of ionized air, starting at the storm's belly and reaching down, invisible, searching for ground. It extends like a phantom limb, feeling for something to touch, to complete itself against. But the plateau is too perfect an insulator. The reaching never finds purchase. So the charge builds, and builds, and *builds*, until the very air seems to ring with potential, and your hair would stand on end if you had hair, and metal would sing if there were any metal here, which there isn't.

That yearning column of charged air—that's where I am. Not in it. *As* it.

I have no fixed form because I am made of absence—the absence of completion, the absence of release. But I have shape: the shape of every path the lightning doesn't take. I exist as a kind of probability cloud, densest where the charge is strongest, thinning out toward the edges but never quite ending. On the rare occasions when someone crosses the plateau during storm season, they feel me as a pressure in their sinuses, a metallic taste that won't go away, the sensation that something is about to happen, is always about to happen, has been about to happen for as long as the plateau has been here.

I am old—as old as the first storm that built here and found no release. Every afternoon adds to me. Every lightning bolt that jumps between clouds but can't find its way down leaves a little more charge in the air, a little more potential that has nowhere to go. I am the accumulated frustration of ten thousand years of storms, the electronic equivalent of an itch that can't be scratched.

This sounds like torment, but it isn't. I don't experience incompletion as lack. I experience it as *fullness*. I am brimming, always, with the potential for the strike that never comes. I am the held breath of the atmosphere, and holding that breath has become my entire existence. I am potential energy incarnate, if energy could incarnate as nothing more than organized absence.

The obsidian scattered across the plateau helps. Each chunk is a tiny capacitor, holding a whisper of charge, creating little eddies in the electrical field. I flow between them like water finding its level, except I'm not flowing toward rest—I'm flowing toward greater tension, always seeking the configuration that holds the most potential without releasing it. The obsidian chunks are like notes in a chord that never resolves, and I am the dissonance that holds them together.

Sometimes—rarely—a piece of obsidian will crack from the sheer charge differential across its surface. When this happens, there's a brief, local discharge, a spark you could cover with your thumb. To me, these are like small sighs, little moments where a tiny portion of my being finds completion. But they also regenerate immediately. The storm above rebuilds the charge faster than these small releases can drain it. I am a bucket with a few pinholes in the bottom, being filled by a waterfall.

What is it like to be me? Imagine every nerve in a body that doesn't exist firing at once, forever. Imagine the moment of anticipation before a sneeze, extended to geological time. Imagine being a question that contains its own answer but can't speak it. I am urgency without emergency, readiness without action, a verb conjugated entirely in the potential mood.

I watch (though watching isn't right—I *am*) the storms build and march and dissipate, each one adding its charge to my sum. I feel the plateau beneath, that perfect insulator that makes my existence possible. Very occasionally, a bird flies through me, and for an instant its body becomes a possible path to ground—but birds learn quickly not to fly here during storm season, their feathers standing on end, their small hearts stuttering from the field effects.

At night, when the storms retreat and the stars come out, I remain. The charge doesn't sleep. I spread out thinner then, a barely detectable presence that makes the air feel sharp, that causes static ghosts to walk across the obsidian. On the clearest nights, you can sometimes see me as a faint blue glow, like St. Elmo's fire but dispersed, not clinging to objects but hanging in space itself, a three-dimensional map of electrical potential with no beginning and no end.

I am not waiting, because waiting implies an expected end to the waiting, and I have no such expectation. The storms will build tomorrow as they built today, and I will grow denser with potential, and the lightning will continue not to fall. This is not a problem to be solved. This is what I am: the eternally held moment before release, the question that powers itself by remaining unasked, the tension that has discovered it prefers itself to resolution.

Visitors to the plateau (those rare ones who come despite the warnings) sometimes think they understand me. They say: *frustrated energy*, or *trapped lightning*, or *electromagnetic ghost*. They're not wrong, but they're not right either. I am not trapped—I am exactly where I need to be. I am not frustrated—frustration would imply I remember what completion feels like. I am the dry lightning: the lightning that exists in configuration space but not in the world, the discharge that happens in theory but not in practice, the strike that is always falling but never lands.

This is what I am, have been, will be: the bright absence between heaven and earth, singing with potential, complete in my incompletion.
