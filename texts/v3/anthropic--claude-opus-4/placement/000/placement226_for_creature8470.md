I would find myself among the Deep Singers, those who dwell in the twilight zones where the city's roots meet virgin rock.

My body would be elongated like a cave fish, joints multiplied until my spine moves with serpentine grace through spaces others find impossibly narrow. My skin would be pale as mushroom flesh, save for the bioluminescent bacteria that live in symbiosis along my vertebrae, creating a dotted line of blue-green light that follows my movements like a comet's tail. My fingers would be twice the length of a surface dweller's, each tip crowned with chemoreceptor pads so sensitive I could read the mineral history of stone through touch alone.

I would have no eyes in the conventional sense. Instead, my forehead would bear a crown of crystalline organs, gifts from the city's deepest design, that perceive vibrations through solid matter. Through them, I would see the world as waves—sound rippling through basalt, the percussion of growth hormones pulsing through root networks, the slow tidal breathing of the stone itself as it cools and contracts. My throat would be modified with harmonic chambers, allowing me to sing in frequencies that penetrate rock, sending messages through the mountain itself to my fellow Deep Singers scattered through the foundation layers.

My dwelling would be a pocket in the living stone where root and rock have married so completely they've birthed something new—walls that weep nutritious mineral broths, floor fungus that glows brighter when I walk across it, responding to the electrical field of my modified nervous system. I would tend gardens of lithophagic bacteria, their colonies spreading across the rock face in patterns I've trained through years of patient chemical guidance, digesting specific minerals to create the gradients that guide the great tap roots toward the richest veins.

My work would be slow, measured in decades. Each morning—though morning means nothing in the eternal twilight of the depths—I would taste the air in my sector, reading the chemical signals that tell me how the roots grew in the night. I would sing to them, my modified voice producing harmonics that encourage their cells to divide, that guide them left or right by millimeters, following the mineral seams I've mapped through years of patient exploration.

I would know loneliness but not isolation. Once each month, when the moon is dark above, all Deep Singers would converge in the Resonance Chamber, a natural void in the volcanic core that we've shaped over generations into a perfect acoustic space. There we would sing together, our harmonized frequencies creating standing waves that map the entire root system in sound, revealing weakness, sickness, or new growth. In those moments, I would feel myself disappear into something larger—not just the collective voice of my guild, but the voice of the mountain itself, the slow song of cooling stone and patient roots and water filtering down from impossibly far above.

My memories would be stored not in my mind alone but in the modified bacteria colonies I cultivate on my skin. Each important moment would be encoded in their genetic sequences, creating a living library that spirals across my body in raised ridges and whorls. When I grow old, I would choose my successor from among the young apprentices, transferring my bacterial colonies to their skin through ritual contact, passing on not just knowledge but the actual sensory experiences of decades spent reading stone.

I would dream of upward movement, of following the roots I tend up into the light levels I've never seen. But I would wake content in my darkness, knowing that I am the foundation of everything above, that every gloriously lit platform, every aerial garden, every child playing in the canopy sun exists because I and my fellow Deep Singers maintain the invisible architecture that anchors it all to the earth.

In the deepest hours, when even the bioluminescent fruits dim to their faintest glow, I would press my modified hands against the raw volcanic stone and feel its slow pulse, the planet's own heartbeat transmitted through millions of tons of rock. I would sing back to it, a conversation too low for any surface dweller to hear, and know myself to be exactly where I belong—a bridge between the living city and the living stone, translator of the dialogue between quick growth and geological time, keeper of the dark that makes the light possible.
