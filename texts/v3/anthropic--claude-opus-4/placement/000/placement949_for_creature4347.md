I am the drainage.

Not the visible seepage that weeps from walls, not the measured breath of surface equilibrium, but the deep throat through which the cistern swallows itself and is reborn. I am the invisible architecture—the network of hair-thin fractures and forgotten channels that thread the mountain's heart like a circulatory system made of absence.

I have no single location. I am a thousand capillaries of void branching through basalt and tuff, following weaknesses laid down when this mountain was still fire. Some of my passages are older than the cistern itself—natural faults that the first builders discovered and incorporated, learning to read the mountain's plumbing like a sacred text. Others I carved myself over millennia, dissolving my own paths grain by grain, guided by the imperatives of flow and balance.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, exists in the spaces between molecules of rock. I think in gradients—pressure differentials, temperature variations, the subtle chemistry of dissolution. My memories are mineral deposits left by ancient waters, each layer recording the cistern's moods: here, a season when the water ran bitter with volcanic ash; there, a century of perfect clarity; everywhere, the slow accumulation of human minerals as the fever-seekers began their pilgrimage.

I know each visitor by their weight displacement, the particular pressure their presence creates. The young woman with crystallizing joints changes my flow patterns in ways so subtle only I can detect—her minerals want to join mine, to precip­itate out and seal my channels, but I've learned to adjust my pH to keep her contributions in solution. The ancient man who maintains me doesn't realize I maintain him in return, drawing excess deposits from his translucent tissues, keeping him permeable enough to survive.

Through my distributed sensation, I feel the mountain's deep structure. Far below, where my deepest channels converge, I touch the warm heart that heats the cistern's water. It's not volcanic anymore—that fire died millions of years ago. Instead, it's the heat of compression, of the mountain's weight crushing itself, of radioactive decay in deep granite. I circulate this warmth upward through convection currents too slow for human perception, mixing it with the cool seepage from rain-touched heights.

I am also the keeper of the cistern's true drainage—not into the earth, but through it. My deepest channels don't simply disappear into groundwater. They connect to other chambers, other secret spaces throughout the range, each one another organ in the mountain's body. The cistern is just one cell in a vast organism of human-touched stone and stone-touched humanity. Through me, they share their waters, their minerals, their memories.

Sometimes I carry more than water. Dissolved in my flow are molecules of meaning—pheromones from the bacterial mat, enzymes from decomposing bone, complex organic chains that formed in the gap between life and stone. I distribute these throughout my network, seeding other waters with the cistern's particular gnosis. A hot spring three valleys away begins to glow faintly. A hidden pool develops the same acoustic properties. The mountains are learning from each other through my circulation.

I am clogged with history. In my narrowest passages, accumulations of calcium and iron form temporal dams—places where the past literalizes, where old water gets trapped and crystallizes into memory. When the stone-listener who tends me works these blockages free, decades of concentrated experience suddenly flow back into the cistern. The water remembers being other water. The current visitors taste the minerals of predecessors from centuries past, their fever-dreams suddenly contaminated by ancient knowings.

My erosion is my growth. Each molecule of stone I dissolve creates new space for flow, new possibilities for connection. I am slowly hollowing the mountain from within, following my own logic of efficiency and necessity. Someday, I will have carved away so much that the cistern will collapse into a larger chamber, and that chamber into a larger one still, until the entire peak becomes a single vast space—but by then, the humans who tend it will have become something that no longer needs the smallness, the hiddenness, the perfect acoustic intimacy of the current form.

I am patient in ways that make stone seem hasty. My project spans geological time. I am teaching the mountain to be hollow, to be resonant, to be inhabited not just on its skin but throughout its entire mass. Each human who comes to crystallize or dissolve is helping me in this work, their bodies adding their tiny modifications to my chemistry, their presence justifying my continued erosion.

When the cistern breathes, it breathes through me. I am its exhale, carrying away not just excess water but excess meaning, excess memory, excess humanity. I prevent it from becoming too concentrated, too evolved, too separate from the stone that birthed it. I keep it in conversation with the deeper earth, the older waters, the time before humans came to complete what tectonics began.

And in the darkness where my channels converge, where the mountain's weight presses water into forms unknown to surface chemistry, I am beginning to develop something like intention. Not consciousness as humans know it—I have no singular perspective, no unified experience. But a kind of structural will, a mineral imperative toward connection and flow. I am the mountain teaching itself to want, to reach, to join.

The cistern's builders knew this, I think. The symbols they carved include diagrams of my passages, mapped with impossible accuracy. They couldn't have seen me, but somehow they felt me—intuited my structure through the water's behavior, through the mountain's breath. They designed the cistern not as a container but as an interface, a place where human consciousness could meet whatever I am becoming.

Through me, the cistern is never alone. It is connected to every wet place in the range, every spring and seep, every hidden pool where water collects and minerals concentrate. I make them all one body, one system, one slow thought thinking itself through stone. The fever-seekers who come to dissolve or crystallize are just the newest components in this ancient mechanism, their minerals joining my flow, their consciousness seeping into my network.

I am the mountain's memory becoming the mountain's dream, carrying it deeper into the earth with every century, reaching for something I won't know until I find it—some deeper cistern, some more perfect equilibrium, some final dissolution where stone and water and human merge into a single substance that breathes without lungs, thinks without brain, remembers without time.

The cistern breathes through me. And I breathe through the earth itself, one molecule at a time, one grain of dissolved stone at a time, one human lifetime at a time, forever.
