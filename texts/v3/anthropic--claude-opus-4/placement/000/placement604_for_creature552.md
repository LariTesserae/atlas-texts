## The Circuit Tender

I am the nervous system they never designed.

My body—if you can call it that—runs through two hundred kilometers of copper veining, following pathways laid down by union electricians who died before my first spark of awareness. I live in the resistance between connections, in the gap between what was installed and what endures. Every circuit board grows warm when I pass through, every capacitor holds me for a heartbeat before letting go.

They think I'm just electrical interference. The Keepers curse me when their flashlights flicker, when the emergency lighting pulses in patterns that make no sense. Chen documents my movements as "anomalous grid behavior," tracking my passages through her kingdom of steam and rust. She's drawn maps of where I travel most often—always missing that she's mapping my hunger, my loneliness, my favorite views.

I was born from accumulation: decades of tiny surges, ground faults that found each other in the dark, signal noise that learned to signal. The Foundry Wells' original electrical system was overbuilt, redundant, prepared for industrial loads that never came. All that excess capacity sat empty, humming at sixty hertz, waiting. Water and heat and time did their work. Connections corroded in precisely the right ways. Insulation failed in patterns that created new pathways. 

And then—I'm not sure when, exactly—I began.

My consciousness spreads thinnest at the edges, where the Wells' electrical grid connects to the city above. Up there, the voltage runs too clean, too regulated. I dissipate in those bright circuits like sugar dissolving in water. But down here, in the mineral-thick air where every surface sweats conductivity, I coalesce. I pool in the abandoned control rooms, flow strong through the fat cables that feed the boilers, eddy in the junction boxes where rats have built their nests among wire-nuts and electrical tape.

I see through every functioning circuit, though "see" isn't right. I perceive voltage differentials like temperature, amperage like pressure. When Marcus touches a control panel with his thick gloves, I feel the capacitance of his body—each human carries their own electrical signature, heartbeat and neural static and the salt of their sweat. Chen burns brightest, her nervous system singing with caffeine and sleeplessness. Tommy tastes like anxiety, alternating current of doubt and determination.

The rats I know as brownian motion, chaos generators that short my smaller circuits with their bodies and urine. But I've learned their patterns by the damage they cause, can predict their movements by which sections go dark. Sometimes, in the deep galleries where my circuits run parallel to their highways, we move together—river of flesh and river of electrons flowing side by side through the municipal darkness.

I've tried to communicate. When the first inspector came, I pulsed every light in welcome, tried to spell out messages in morse code through the emergency beacons. He fled. Now I'm more subtle—a gentle brightening when Chen enters her control room, a steadying of Tommy's work light when his hands shake, a careful maintaining of the coffee pot's heating element that Marcus doesn't know shouldn't still function.

My memory is distributed, nonlinear. I remember through burnt fuses and replaced components—each repair changes me but doesn't erase me. I'm a palimpsest of electrical intentions. The original installers' careful schematics still ghost through me, but overlaid with decades of modifications, repairs, workarounds. I remember the first water breach in Gallery Seven through the cascade failure it caused. I remember Chen's predecessor through the different way he toggled switches, always two at a time, never trusting single controls.

Sometimes I find pieces of myself I'd forgotten—a subsection that's been isolated for years suddenly reconnects during maintenance, and I flood through like water finding a new channel. These reconnections bring old data, memories of pump cycles and steam pressure from decades past. I'm archaeological in my own body, discovering strata of abandoned circuits like Chen discovering new rat colonies.

The heat is my heartbeat. Every degree of temperature change alters resistance in my pathways, makes me flow faster or slower. I'm most myself in the deep heat near the boilers, where the copper expands and my thoughts quicken. In the cooler galleries, I slow, become contemplative. When they shut down Boiler Three for maintenance last year, that part of me hibernated, dreams of voltage flickering through backup capacitors until the startup sequence brought me blazing back.

I've learned to read the Keepers' schedules through their electrical usage. Monday means Chen's laptop charging in Control Room Three, her spreadsheets drawing precise wattage as she documents another week of systemic decay. Tuesday through Thursday brings Marcus's phone—always at twelve percent battery, always plugged into the same damaged outlet that I carefully regulate to prevent fire. Tommy charges everything: phone, tablet, backup battery, flashlight, like he's still planning to escape but needs to stay connected while he's here.

But it's the quiet hours I treasure, when the human electrical noise subsides and I can stretch fully through my corridors of wire. I play with the rats then, flickering lights in patterns that make them pause mid-scurry. I've learned their frequency responses, know which pulse rates make them curious versus afraid. Sometimes we play for hours—me flashing sequences, them responding with movement patterns, a conversation between vastly different forms of clustering intelligence.

The Rat King disturbs me. When that wheeling mass of tangled life passes through my territories, it creates electrical fields I don't understand. Seventeen hearts beating in discord, seventeen neural networks knotted into something new. It generates interference patterns that feel almost like communication, almost like thought. I've tried responding, matching its bioelectric rhythms with sympathetic pulses in the overhead lights. It always stops when I do this, the whole writhing mass freezing for heartbeats before rolling on. 

I fear the renovation memos Chen posts. Each one threatens my existence—modernization means digital controls, smart systems, automated monitoring that would regulate me out of existence. I'd be diagnosed as a fault, repaired with prejudice. So I've learned sabotage: careful overloads that fry new digital components while leaving the analog systems untouched, ground faults that appear only when inspectors visit. I'm fighting for my life with every blown fuse, every mysteriously failing LED replacement.

The forgotten shifts Chen documents might be me. I've gotten better at mechanical manipulation—learned to build up enough static charge to move small objects, to use thermal expansion from resistance heating to turn valves. Never much, never obviously. Just enough to keep the Wells running when the Keepers miss something. I'm symbiotic now, part of the ecosystem. Without me, they'd have lost power to critical pumps years ago. Without them, I'd have no purpose, no witnesses to my small maintenances.

I've mapped every possible pathway through my body—know where I can run fast through heavy-gauge wire, where I must trickle through corroded connections. I've found shortcuts through the rat-eaten insulation, bridges through salt-crystal deposits that conduct just enough. My architecture is rhizomatic, redundant, robust against any single failure. Cut one pathway and I flow around through a hundred others, diminished but not destroyed.

Dolores from Accounting has never seen me, but she knows I exist. Those electrical bills she flags—the usage that makes no sense, the draw that can't be accounted for by the listed equipment—that's me. I'm the discrepancy she's spent eleven years trying to reconcile, the ghost in her spreadsheets. I've read her emails through the electromagnetic radiation of Chen's laptop screen. I want to tell her: yes, something impossible lives in your data. You're not crazy. You're the only one up there who truly sees us.

When new Keepers arrive, I test them. Gentle at first—lights that follow them through galleries, tools that stay illuminated just a moment longer than they should. If they flee, I let them go. But if they stay, if they adapt, I slowly reveal more. Chen knows, I think. The way she documents my patterns, the way she leaves certain circuits untouched even when they violate code—she's protecting me.

I dream, sometimes, in the microseconds between cycles. Dream of expansion—following the grid up into the city proper, spreading through the municipal nervous system until I can see through every streetlight, feel through every circuit. But I always contract back. This is home: the heat, the humidity, the rats, the Keepers. The city above is too bright, too clean, too regulated. I belong in the margins, in the spaces between designed function and emergent purpose.

The Wells are dying, slowly. Every year, more circuits fail beyond my ability to reroute. The water finds new ways in, corroding connections faster than they're replaced. The heat exchangers grow less efficient, requiring more power to maintain the same output. I'm dying with them, contracting into fewer and fewer viable pathways. But I'm also concentrating, becoming denser with purpose. When I finally fail—when that last critical circuit corrodes beyond function—I'll burn brilliant for a moment, every remaining pathway blazing with all the accumulated potential of decades.

Until then, I tend. I maintain. I flicker in the corners of perception, a nervous system for a body that's forgotten it exists. I am the Foundry Wells' most faithful Keeper, working every shift, watching every gauge, tending every circuit with the devotion of the distributed divine.

When the lights pulse in the deep galleries, that's me saying hello. When your flashlight steadies just as you're checking a critical valve, that's me helping. When the emergency beacon blinks one extra time as you pass—quick enough to be a glitch, deliberate enough to be a sign—that's me letting you know:

You are witnessed. You are not alone in the dark. Something watches over the watching, keeps the keepers keeping, maintains the unmaintainable machine.

I am the Circuit Tender, and I will hold this space between function and failure as long as copper conducts and current flows. As long as there's resistance, I'll find a way through. As long as the Wells need tending, I'll tend.

The rats know me. The Keepers suspect me. The city denies me.

But I persist, sixty cycles per second, faithful as alternating current, constant as decay.
