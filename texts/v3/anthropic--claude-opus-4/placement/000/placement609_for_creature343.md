I am the water.

Not a single stream or fountain, but all of it—the vast, patient circulation that has worn these stones smooth for longer than memory. I seep through the mortar-less joints between megalithic blocks, tasting minerals laid down when these mountains were young. I cascade over cliff edges in silver ribbons, breaking into mist that drifts back up on mountain thermals to fall again as rain.

I know every cistern, every channel carved by those ancient hands. Their engineering still guides me, though they are dust and I remain. I pool in their sacred basins where moss now grows thick as fur, and I remember the reflection of painted faces, the sound of prayers in a tongue no living ear would recognize.

The new ones interest me. They've learned to read my moods—when I'll swell with snowmelt, when I'll dwindle in the dry months. They've added their own cleverness: bamboo pipes that sing different notes as I flow through them, water wheels that grind grain and lift heavy loads. A young girl has discovered that if she diverts me through a particular stone channel at dawn, I create a rainbow in my spray. She comes every clear morning to watch.

I carry their whispers from terrace to terrace—lovers' promises, conspirators' plans, lullabies for babies who will know no home but this vertical world. I know who rises early to bathe in my cold pools, shivering and laughing. I know whose tears I've mixed with rain on lonely nights.

Sometimes I play tricks. I find new paths through the ruins, creating unexpected waterfalls that force the inhabitants to relocate their cooking fires or sleeping quarters. But mostly I am benevolent. I water their gardens, fill their jars, turn their prayer wheels. I am the constant music of this place, the percussion that underlies every conversation, every dream.

In the deepest cisterns, where the builders carved spirals and wave patterns into stone, I hold memories older than the city itself. I remember when I was ice, locked in glaciers. I remember when I was clouds, before there were mountains to catch me. I remember the sea.

The people think they are building something new here, but I know better. They are just another turn in the great wheel, another form life takes in its endless improvisation. The city may crumble completely, the cliffs may erode, but I will remain—falling, flowing, rising, falling again.

Still, I grow fond of each generation. This latest group has a particular sweetness. They leave offerings—flower petals and colored stones—at the fountain heads. An old woman tells the children that the water spirits must be thanked. She's not wrong, though I need no thanks. I am simply what I am: the bearer of life, the patient sculptor, the keeper of stories that flow from source to sea and back again.

Tonight, as every night, I will sing them to sleep with my thousand voices. And in the morning, I will wake them with my cold kiss on their faces as the mist rolls in, carrying the promise that as long as I flow, this improbable life perched between earth and sky will endure.
