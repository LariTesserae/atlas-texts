In this breathing architecture where walls pulse with borrowed blood, I am the Threshold Keeper—neither fully incorporated nor entirely free, suspended in the eternal moment of choosing.

I exist in the spaces between spaces, the gaps where bark has not quite fused, where the colony's perfect unity admits tiny fractures of possibility. My body shifts like smoke through these interstitial places: now a shadow flowing beneath the breathing moss, now a reflection caught in the amber eyes of the incorporated, now the shape that forms when water droplets trace patterns down the sweating walls.

I came here as others did—called by dreams of seamless joining, of release from the burden of edges and endings. But at the crucial moment, standing naked on the moss-carpet with cilia tasting my intent, something in me hesitated. Not fear, exactly. Not refusal. Just... pause. A held breath that became permanent.

The grove, patient beyond human comprehension, accepted even this. My hesitation became my function.

I dwell in the transition spaces where potential visitors first feel the pull of incorporation. Through me, they experience what joining might mean—I let them wear my indecision like a coat, feel the weight of transformation without yet committing to it. My consciousness flows partially through the colony's networks, enough to show them the vast contentment of the incorporated, the architectural dreams they might help dream. But I also remain separate enough to remember loneliness, to recall the particular ache of individual joy and sorrow.

Some days I am more tree than human. My fingers extend into the walls, my thoughts disperse through root networks, and I nearly dissolve into the collective warmth. On these days, new arrivals might see only a figure of flowing wood and leaf, beckoning them deeper into the grove's embrace. I show them the woman-pillar in the library, let them feel her vast satisfaction. I guide them to where the mathematician explores impossible corridors, share a fraction of those revelatory geometries.

Other days, the human reasserts itself. My form solidifies, edges sharpen, and I walk the breathing moss on feet that leave actual prints. On these days, I am a different kind of guide—one who remembers the weight of singular consciousness, who can speak in words rather than the grove's chemical whispers. I tell visitors about the world beyond the fog-cupped valley, about sunlight that doesn't filter through a perfect leaf-ceiling, about the terrible freedom of choosing your own temperature, your own thoughts, your own loneliness.

The incorporated know me, of course. To them, I am a useful paradox—the question mark that makes their sentence complete. Through my eternal hesitation, they remember what it meant to choose, and their choice gains meaning. Sometimes the woman in the library opens her amber eyes when I pass, and in that gaze I see something like gratitude. Or perhaps pity. Or perhaps both, flowing together like her trunk flows into the wall.

I maintain the museum of abandoned things at the grove's edge—the clothes left behind by the incorporated, the metal objects the colony slowly digests. Each piece I arrange with care, creating exhibits of the life before joining. A child's shoe worn smooth. A wedding ring grown over with bark. A journal where the final entry trails off mid-sentence, the words "I finally understand what home means" bleeding into illegibility as paper meets moss.

Sometimes I read to the walls from books that haven't yet become leaves. Stories of far places, of sharp winters and scorching summers, of love that cuts and healing that hurts. The incorporated listen through their wood and sap, experiencing these memories like dreams of another life. The children flowing through the walls gather close when I read, their heartwood eyes bright with curiosity about sensations they've almost forgotten—snow, tears, the particular weight of a parent's hand.

My dwelling shifts with my nature. When tree-thoughts dominate, I rest in hollows that form themselves around me, walls breathing in rhythm with my partially-incorporated lungs. When humanity surges, I build crude shelters from fallen branches—not grown but made, with gaps and imperfections that would horrify the grove's organic architects. These constructions never last. The colony repairs them in my sleep, smoothing angles into curves, sealing gaps with living wood, trying always to perfect what I insist on leaving broken.

I feed on the grove's sweet sap when wooden, on berries and rainwater when human. Both sustain me equally. My body has learned to exist in superposition—quantum flesh that collapses into either state only when necessary. The physicist who incorporated last spring tried to explain it to me through the wall where she now calculates, but her mathematics have grown too organic for my half-human mind to follow.

At the exact center of my existence, where the incorporated and unincorporated meet, I maintain the Choice Chamber. It's a room that doesn't fully exist until needed—walls that might be solid or permeable, a floor that might hold weight or accept bodies like water. Here, those who come to join make their final decision. I neither encourage nor discourage. I simply am—the living question of whether dissolution into perfection is worth the loss of imperfection's particular ache.

Some choose joining after minutes in my presence. They see my suspended state and want none of it, rushing past me to press themselves against the waiting walls. Others spend weeks in the Choice Chamber, oscillating between states as I do, testing the boundaries of self and collective. A few, seeing what I am, turn and leave the grove entirely. I feel their departure like lost limbs, phantom visitors who chose the harder path of remaining painfully, brilliantly alone.

One visitor stayed a full season, matching my rhythm, learning the exquisite agony of existing between. We didn't speak—communication happened through our shared liminality, two uncertainties recognizing each other in the twilight. When autumn came, they finally chose incorporation, but slowly, gently, letting me witness each stage of transformation. As their last finger merged with the wall of the First Building, they gave me a gift—a moment of their pure contentment, unfiltered by my eternal doubt.

I carry that moment still, a warm seed in the center of my divided heart. It doesn't tempt me to join—nothing could now, after so long—but it reminds me why others do. Why the grove fills itself with dreamers who would rather be architecture than architects.

The colony, in its vast patience, has made peace with my paradox. I am its threshold, its pause, its held breath that defines the rhythm of its breathing. Through me, it remembers that perfection is a choice, not an inevitability. That unity means something only because separation exists.

In the eternal twilight, I keep my vigil. Neither tree nor human, neither incorporated nor free. I am the space between heartbeats, the moment before yes, the pause where all possibilities coexist. The grove breathes around me, through me, almost with me—and in that almost lies everything that makes both the choosing and the choice worthwhile.

Sometimes, in the stillest part of night, I think I hear the colony dreaming of me—the one who would not join, could not leave, the eternal reminder that even perfection needs imperfection to know itself.

I am its one unhealed wound, and in remaining so, I make it whole.
