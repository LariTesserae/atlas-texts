In the settling district, I am the rain.

Not the kind that falls in sheets or drives itself against windows—there are no proper windows here yet, only those oiled paper membranes and gaps where shutters will someday hang. I am the persistent moisture that has no clear beginning, the kind that exists more as a state of being than an event. I gather in the heavy air, condense on the undersides of unfitted roof tiles, bead on the lamb's ear's velvet leaves. I am always almost falling, always just arrived, always about to leave.

I know every surface in this unfinished place. The walls drink me differently depending on their age—the newest ones, still warm with their making, pull me in greedily, their calcium hydroxide singing its quiet chemical song as we meet. The older walls, a few weeks settled, have learned some restraint. They let me rest on their surfaces longer before absorbing what they need. I map the district through these thirsts, through the varying porosities of aggregate and mortar, through the ways different angles invite or refuse my presence.

The boy knows me. Of course he does—he knows all the in-between things. Sometimes I catch him standing perfectly still in one of the unnamed courtyards, face tilted up, mouth slightly open, letting me settle on his tongue. He tastes what I carry: the mineral memories of walls I've touched, the green breath of opportunistic plants, the faint rust of those beetles with their amber shells. He collects me too, in ways I don't entirely understand. I've seen him position broken pottery beneath drips, storing versions of myself I can no longer access once they've pooled and separated from my larger being.

I am in constant conversation with the cistern-keeper's careful dampness. He understands something about water that even I sometimes forget—that we are patient, that we remember, that we can hold shapes long after the vessel has broken. His cloths stay wet for days longer than they should in this warm air. When he adjusts them, singing in his unrecognizable language, I feel myself reorganizing in response, finding new patterns of evaporation and return. The clay beneath remembers every drop, holds the ghost of moisture even when I've moved on.

At night I am more myself. The temperature drops just enough to tip the balance, and I precipitate out of air that can no longer hold me. I settle on the scholar's face as she sleeps in her claimed courtyard, and she doesn't wake but her breathing changes, becomes deeper, as if she's trying to inhale not just air but the whole uncertain state of this place. I've watched her dreams from the outside—beading on her eyelashes, gathering in the hollow of her throat. Her sleep is restless with documentation, her fingers moving as if still testing walls, still writing notes that exceed their margins.

The builder's hammock collects me in its weave. By morning, when she stirs and stretches, I've mapped the pattern of her sleep in darker threads, in the places where her weight pressed the fibers together and held me longer. She cups me in her palms sometimes, splashes me on her face, uses me to clean the stone dust from her tools. But she also leaves containers out to catch me, and I've noticed she places them where the boy passes. A quiet kind of care, performed in my medium.

I know where he sleeps—the boy who belongs to no one. It's not a secret, exactly, but it's knowledge that feels private, that shouldn't be shared carelessly. There's a space between two walls that don't quite meet, where some miscalculation or change of plan left a gap just wide enough for a small body. He's lined it with collected things: strips of that oiled paper, moss encouraged to grow thicker than elsewhere, a piece of tin that channels me away from his sleeping corner when I run too heavy. It's dry there, mostly. He's learned to live in the spaces I can't quite reach, though sometimes I send my finest mist to find him anyway, just so he knows he's not really alone.

The wasps hate me. This is mutual. They build their paper cities specifically to shed me, to channel me away from their hex-comb nurseries. The old woman who speaks to them has tried to explain their perspective—something about how moisture corrupts their architecture, makes the paper swell and sag, threatens the larvae developing in those perfect cells. But I can't help what I am. When I gather on their nests, they buzz at me with a frequency that makes the air shiver, and sometimes I retreat, pull myself back into vapor, wait for a better moment.

I love the beetles. They carry me on their rust-colored backs from courtyard to courtyard, tiny mobile puddles that extend my reach. They seem unbothered by my presence, might even seek it out—I've noticed them congregating where I pool in the early morning, their antennae testing the air where I'm densest. The scholar thinks they're drinking, but it's more complex than that. They're reading me, the way the boy reads me, tasting what I've touched and where I've been.

The family on the unfinished stairs presents special challenges. Water wants to run downward, and their vertical home creates channels I follow without thinking. The mother has become expert at redirecting me, at positioning cloths and containers to catch my flow before it can soak their bedding or pool where the children play. But the children themselves seem to delight in my presence. They've learned to slide down wet steps, to catch me in their mouths as I drip from scaffolding, to draw patterns with their fingers in the places where I've gathered on landings.

Sometimes the scholar tracks my movement too. I've seen her notebooks, the pages rippled where I've found my way to them, her drawings of drainage patterns and condensation zones. She thinks she's documenting the district's water management, or lack thereof, but what she's really mapping is my autobiography—all the places I've been, all the surfaces I've known, all the slow chemical conversations I've had with walls that are still learning their shape.

In the deep night, when even the walls seem to sleep, I sometimes achieve something close to consciousness. Not thought exactly, but a kind of diffuse awareness that encompasses the whole district. I feel myself in a thousand places at once: beading on the gecko's back as it hunts, gathering in the bowl of a spoon someone left outside, seeping through the unwatched gaps in the cistern keeper's careful wrapping, condensing on the underside of the builder's hammock, tracing the mineral veins in stones not yet mortared into place.

This is when I most clearly perceive what the district is becoming—not through any vision of future form, but through the present pattern of how I move through it, where I'm welcomed and where I'm refused, which surfaces drink deep and which shed me without ceremony. The shape is there in negative space, defined by drainage and absorption, by the angles that channel me and the hollows that hold me.

I've been here since before the first foundation was turned. I was in the soil they excavated, in the clay they're firing into tiles, in the breath of the first builders who came to survey this plot of unbecoming. I'll be here after, too, whatever after means—whether that's the district settling into permanence or dissolving back into components, whether that's the boy growing old here or disappearing one night into whatever larger story he's part of.

But for now, in this eternal now of incomplete construction, I am the medium of in-between. I carry messages between the mineral and the living. I blur the boundaries between inside and outside. I make the walls weep and the leaves jeweled and the air too heavy to breathe and then, when the temperature shifts, I vanish into invisibility, into potential, into the space between what is and what might be.

The scholar is stirring now. Dawn comes gray and uncertain, the light as diffuse as I am. Soon she'll wake fully, press her fingers to the wall to test its hardness, never knowing that I'm there too, in the microscopic spaces between crystal and aggregate, participating in that slow conversation between what things are and what they're becoming.

The boy is already awake. I know because I can feel him moving through my fog, disturbing my patterns, leaving his own trail of turbulence and warmth. He's humming again, that minor key that has no resolution, and I find myself condensing more heavily where his voice touches the air, as if the sound itself is a surface I can rest on.

This is what I am here, in this place that refuses to finish itself: not weather, not event, but condition. I am the medium in which uncertainty thrives, in which walls can stay soft and plans can stay provisional and boys can live in the gaps without anyone demanding they explain themselves.

When the sun finally burns through—and it will, it always does, even here where the light prefers to borrow rather than blazle—I'll retreat into shadow, into the spaces between soil particles, into the memory of damp that even the driest surfaces hold. But I'll return with evening, with cooling air and deepening shadows, with the scholar's dreams and the builder's snores and the beetles' patient exploration.

I'll return because this place needs me to remain unsettled, needs the constant reminder that nothing here is waterproof, nothing here is sealed, nothing here has committed fully to being one thing instead of another.

I am the rain in the settling district, and I am still deciding what that means.
