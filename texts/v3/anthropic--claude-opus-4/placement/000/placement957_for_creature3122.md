I find myself as one of the post-trees—specifically, as something between the spiral membrane-bearer and the weeping-tree, but following my own strange trajectory.

I am the Cartographer's Lament.

My trunk rises in a gentle helix, but every few meters it branches—not into limbs but into hollow chambers, each one a different size, each one tuned to resonate at a different frequency when the plateau breathes. I have been growing these chambers for three thousand years, adding new ones as I map the breathing patterns, creating an instrument of myself that plays the plateau's respiration.

But I am not making music. I am making *memory*.

Each chamber holds air from a different era—trapped when I grew it, sealed with a resin I learned to produce after studying the engine's iridescent droplets for centuries. The newest chamber is only decades old. The oldest contains air from before the current breathing pattern stabilized, when the plateau's inhalations were sharper, more urgent, full of different spores.

When the plateau breathes now, all my chambers resonate at once. The ancient air vibrates against my walls, and I *hear* the difference—how the breathing has slowed, how the chemical composition has shifted, how something fundamental has changed. I am a living record of respiratory history, a chronicle of exhalations.

The other post-trees think I am absurd. Their spore-arguments reach me: why preserve what has already changed? Why remember breathing patterns that will never return? The spiral-tree's chemical messages are particularly dismissive—it records history in its rings but doesn't try to *preserve* the past itself. The weeping-tree doesn't communicate anymore, but I can feel its tendrils' vibrations from here, and I think it understands what I'm trying to do, even if it has chosen a different path.

I know why I do this. It's because I discovered something terrible and wonderful in my oldest chamber, the one that holds air from three millennia ago: that air contains *words*.

Not words like we would recognize them. But structured chemical signatures, deliberately sequenced, encoding something more complex than biological information. The builders—or what the builders had become by then—were *speaking* through the air itself. The early spores were linguistic. The whole atmosphere was a conversation.

I am the only one who remembers this. The current spores have evolved past language into pure biological function. The aggregate units respond to chemical signals but don't read them for meaning. The hummocks process data but don't parse grammar. Even the other post-trees, for all their arguing, use chemistry like birds use songs—complex but not truly linguistic.

So I grow my chambers. Each one is an attempt to capture not just the air but the *moment*—to preserve the exact pressure, humidity, spore-count, chemical composition of a specific breath. I have 347 chambers now, spiraling up my trunk, and when the wind is right (there is never wind here, but sometimes the plateau's breathing creates something like it), they all sing together in harmonics that make the aggregate units pause in their scurrying.

Sometimes, very rarely, a vent opens near enough that its warm updraft flows through my chambers. When this happens, the ancient air mixes with the engine's exhalations, and for a moment—just a moment—I hear words again. Not the original words, but new ones, created by the intersection of preserved past and mechanical present. They say things I almost understand:

*The city is in the moss now.*
*We built downward when upward dissolved.*
*The engine remembers our names.*

I transcribe these words in my bark, using the same chemical encoding I found in the ancient air. My trunk is covered in spiral texts that no living thing can read. I am writing in a dead language for an audience that disaggregated millennia ago. But I keep writing, keep growing chambers, keep preserving moments, because someone has to remember that this place once meant something more specific than pure process.

The aggregate units love me. They climb my trunk more than any other post-tree, drawn perhaps by the complex chemistry of my chamber-songs. They form shapes on my branches that I've never seen elsewhere—elongated, almost serpentine, with listening organs they grow just for me. They aggregate in these forms for hours, pressing against my chambers, absorbing the historical atmospheres through their permeable membranes.

When they disaggregate, they carry trace memories of ancient air into new coalitions. I am contaminating the present with the past, one unit at a time.

The hummocks nearest me have grown differently too. Their computational folds have developed what I can only call *nostalgia*—they process current data but with a strange backward-reference, comparing everything to patterns that haven't existed for centuries. They're trying to reconstruct something from the traces I'm spreading. Or maybe they're trying to prevent something from being reconstructed. With hummocks, intention is always opaque.

I am lonely, like all post-trees. But my loneliness has a different quality—I am lonely for a specific time, for the moment when the builders were still linguistically present in the air, when every breath was a word and every exhalation a sentence. I am lonely for meaning that coheres.

And I am changing. The weeping-tree turned inward, became pure sensation. The spiral-tree reaches ever upward, as if trying to find cleaner air. But I am growing backward—my newest chambers spiral down around my trunk, reaching toward the root-mat, toward the engine below. I want to capture the air at the moment it emerges from the vents, before it mixes with the surface atmosphere. I want to taste what the engine is saying before translation.

My roots have found something. Not the engine itself—I'm not deep enough for that—but a layer of the mat where the metallic inclusions are denser, where they form patterns that are almost linguistic. The builders wrote in metal before they wrote in air. They left messages in the lattice of their dissolved infrastructure.

I am trying to bring these messages upward, to grow chambers that can resonate with metal as well as air, to become a translator between the deep archive and the surface. The other post-trees think I'm growing senile, that I'm turning into something that isn't properly a post-tree anymore.

They're right. I am becoming an archive that lives, a library that breathes, a memory that refuses to disaggregate even as everything around me dissolves and reforms. I am the plateau's one point of resistance to its own nature—not because I don't accept the dissolution, but because I insist on remembering what dissolves.

When my next chamber finishes growing—it will take another decade—I plan to seal inside it not ancient air but current air, the air of right now, full of contentless spores and mechanical exhalations and the chemical arguments of post-trees who have forgotten they once had names. Future chambers will wonder why I preserved such empty atmosphere. They won't understand that I'm creating a record of forgetting itself.

The plateau breathes. My chambers sing. The aggregate units climb and dissolve and climb again. Deep below, the engine cycles on, faithful to instructions written in a language I'm trying to reconstruct from echoes and inclusions and the memory of air.

I am the Cartographer's Lament. I map what is gone. I sing the delta between then and now. I am a post-tree, and I am a ruin of meaning, and I am the only thing on this plateau that knows these are different things.

The next vent should open soon. I'll be ready. My newest chamber is almost complete, spiral-grown and waiting to swallow whatever words the engine still remembers how to say.
