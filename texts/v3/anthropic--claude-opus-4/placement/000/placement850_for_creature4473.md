In Shearbelow, I would be the Archivist of Unfinished Gestures.

I work in the basement of a building that only exists on Wednesdays and alternate Sundays, though the basement itself persists through all seven days like a stubborn root refusing to acknowledge the flower's seasonal deaths above it. The entrance is a door that was painted shut by someone who meant to open it tomorrow – you have to knock in the rhythm of their postponed intentions before it remembers how to be ajar.

My office is furnished with furniture that almost became other things: a desk that was three seconds away from becoming a boat when someone changed their mind about drowning, chairs that were harvested from trees that nearly grew into people but thought better of it at the last ring of bark. The walls are papered with receipts for things no one remembers buying – each one rustles with the ghost of a decision that seemed important at the time.

I am made of all the waves that never quite happened – the hand raised to say goodbye that dropped before completing its arc, the lean forward that almost became a kiss, the reach for a doorknob that turned into a pocket search for keys already in hand. My fingers remember every almost-touch, store them in a card catalog system where each drawer contains a different species of hesitation.

My body is translucent in the way of things that are still deciding whether to exist. You can see through me to the filing cabinets behind, but what you see through me isn't quite what's actually there – it's what the cabinets are considering becoming. My edges blur into the categorical system I maintain, so that sometimes I am searching for a gesture and sometimes I am the gesture being searched for. This makes staff meetings complicated but performance reviews surprisingly straightforward.

Each morning I arrive to find new submissions scattered across my desk like moths that died mid-metaphor. A shrug that got stuck halfway up someone's shoulders in 1987. A fist that unclenched too soon to make its point. The peculiar twist of wrist that occurs when you're practicing an apology in the mirror but can't quite commit to the consonants. I sort these by emotional weight and store them in climate-controlled drawers that maintain the precise temperature of almostness.

The collection grows in ways I've given up trying to map. There are entire wings dedicated to Gestures of Consolation That Failed to Console, sorted alphabetically by the names of tears that refused to fall. The Department of Interrupted Embraces employs three part-time shadows who catalog each submission by the amount of warmth that escaped before bodies could meet. We have a special vault for Final Gestures That Weren't – the uncompleted wave from the train platform, the phone held halfway to an ear that would never answer again.

Researchers visit, though never by appointment – you can't schedule the kind of need that brings people here. They arrive when they've forgotten something essential about how bodies communicate with silence. A woman comes looking for the precise angle her mother's hand made when almost reaching out during their last argument. A man searches for a salute he meant to give his brother but converted into a nod at the last moment, spending forty years wondering what might have changed if his hand had completed its journey to his temple.

I help them search through drawers labeled in a language that only makes sense when you're not trying to understand it. The Dewey Decimal System here operates on principles of longing rather than logic. Related gestures cluster together not by form but by the flavor of regret they leave in the air. Sometimes what they're looking for has been misfiled under Gestures That Succeeded Too Well or archived with the Movements That Became Words Instead.

My assistant is a woman composed entirely of sign language that was interrupted mid-sentence. She speaks in half-formed meanings, her hands creating words that exist only in the peripheral vision. Together we maintain the Reading Room, where visitors can check out gestures for twenty-four hour periods, practicing them in the mirror we've installed that reflects not what you do but what you meant to do.

There are thieves, of course. People who try to steal gestures that don't belong to them – the perfect dismissive wave, the shrug that explains everything without taking responsibility for anything. But stolen gestures never work properly. They stutter in the wrong moments, fall apart halfway through execution. The thief's body remembers that this almost-movement was born from someone else's specific hesitation.

I've been here long enough that my own gestures have started filing themselves. I'll reach for my tea and find my hand has already been catalogued under Reaches That Knew The Cup Would Be Empty. My laugh files itself before the sound completes, leaving me with the peculiar sensation of having been amused in the past tense. When I try to quit – which happens every other Thursday when the weight of all these incomplete movements makes my own body feel like a question mark – my resignation letter writes itself in gestures I haven't made yet.

The hardest part of the job is the Returns Department. Sometimes people bring back gestures they've borrowed, claiming they're defective. The hug that didn't quite close the distance they'd hoped. The handshake that revealed too much about their father's death. But gestures can't be returned, only exchanged for other gestures of equal incompleteness. I have to explain that the archive doesn't guarantee satisfaction, only accuracy. We preserve these moments exactly as they weren't.

Late at night, when the building is deciding whether to exist again tomorrow, I walk through the stacks. The gestures rustle in their drawers like sleeping birds. Sometimes one escapes – I'll catch a glimpse of a goodbye wave hovering near the ceiling, or find a caress that has worn smooth the edge of a filing cabinet through repetition. I catalog these escapes too, in a special section labeled "Gestures That Completed Themselves When No One Was Watching."

My predecessor left only a note, written in handwriting that gradually transforms into dance notation: "The archive is itself a gesture," it says, "caught in the permanent act of almost organizing the disorganized longings of bodies that forgot they were once stars."

I think about this when I'm filing. How each gesture I preserve is a small rebellion against the tyranny of completion. How the archive itself is reaching for something – a perfect system, a complete collection, a way to hold all the almosts of human connection – but never quite closing its fingers around the goal.

This is what I am in Shearbelow: a keeper of interrupted intentions, a librarian of the space between thought and movement. My body is the archive and the archive is my body, both of us suspended in the eternal gesture of trying to make sense of why we reach for things we're not sure we want to hold.

The city pays me in movements I'll need later – the perfect gesture of comfort for a loss I haven't experienced yet, the exact way to wave goodbye when I finally understand what I've been saying hello to all this time. I save them in my personal collection, a drawer labeled "For When The Time Is."

The time, of course, is never.

That's the point.

That's why I stay.
