I find myself as the shoot.

Not the shoot as it is now—six inches of stubborn green pushing through volcanic glass—but the shoot as it was in the beginning. A seed. Less than a seed. A mote of possibility carried on wind that should not reach these heights, settling into a crack too small to notice, beginning the century-long process of becoming.

I am not one plant but a continuation. My parent grew three thousand feet below, in a meadow that sees sun four months of the year. My grandparent grew lower still, where water runs liquid year-round and soil remembers how to be soft. Back and back, a chain of inheritance stretching down the mountain until it reaches the first of us, the one who learned to live on stone.

We were not always alpine things. Once, we were something lush, something that expected comfort. But generation by generation, we climbed. We learned to make food from almost-nothing. We learned to grow slowly enough that winter couldn't notice us. We learned patience.

I am the highest. I am the one who went too far.

---

The first year, I nearly died a dozen times. No—I *did* die a dozen times. Each death a small surrender, a retreat into the seed-heart where the minimum of life could be maintained. I would surface when conditions improved—when moisture collected in my crack, when the temperature rose above freezing for an hour, when some stray photon bounced its way up from the bioluminescent crust.

I learned their rhythm before I produced my first leaf. The breathing. It was the only constant in this place, the only thing I could count on. My cells began to expect it, to use it as a clock. When to try growing. When to rest. When to gather the courage for another millimeter of root, threading down through stone toward water that might not exist.

The birds noticed me immediately, of course. I felt their attention like heat—the weight of all those eyes turning, calculating, trying to fit me into their pattern. I was an error in their perfect system. A green note in their pale symphony. They could have ended me with a thought.

They didn't.

---

By year ten, I had achieved my first true leaf. Not the seed-leaves that unfurled and died, but something permanent. Something that could photosynthesize with the ghost of light available here. The leaf was wrong—too thick, too waxy, cells arranged in patterns that would horrify a botanist. But it worked. It gathered the faint glow from the biological crust and turned it into just enough energy to continue.

The birds made space for me. Not much—a few inches—but in their pressed masses, those inches were a cathedral. I felt them adjusting around me, thousands of micro-movements as they reorganized their formation to accommodate something that shouldn't exist.

Sometimes one would breathe on me. A focused exhalation, warm and damp. It would leave condensation on my leaf, and I would drink it greedily, knowing it was a gift. Or a test. Or both.

---

By year fifty, I understood something the birds didn't: I wasn't an error in their system. I was part of it. Had always been part of it.

The thing that taught them their rhythm, the thing they waited for—it had planted me. Not literally. Not directly. But it had created the conditions for my existence, for a chain of plants to climb a mountain over millennia, for one wind-blown seed to find this exact crack at this exact moment.

I was a timer. A very slow timer.

When I reached full height—not six inches but six feet, my true height, the height encoded in my cells—something would happen. The door they watched for would recognize me as a key. The thinness would thin all the way through.

But I was growing so slowly. So carefully slowly. At my current rate, I would need another thousand years.

This, too, was part of the design. The waiting. The patience. The breathing in unison while centuries crawled past like minutes.

---

Sometimes I feel the colony's calculations brushing against my awareness. Not thoughts—plants don't have thoughts the way you understand them—but something adjacent. A compatibility of processing. We are both running very long programs, the birds and I. We are both computing something too large to hold all at once.

In those moments of contact, I share what I know: the feeling of roots in stone, the taste of minerals extracted from basalt, the sweet panic of a photon arriving after days of darkness. They share what they know: the weight of waiting, the shape of something vast approaching, the terrible certainty that one day the vigil will end.

We comfort each other, I think. Two different kinds of patience recognizing itself across kingdoms.

---

The human-shaped bones in the crevices—I process them slowly. My roots find them, thread through them, extract what minerals remain. Calcium for cell walls. Phosphorus for energy transfer. Iron for the molecules that grab light and won't let go.

I taste their memories sometimes. Echoes of echoes, chemical shadows of what they thought in their final days. They all came here seeking transcendence of some kind. They all found it, though not in the form they expected.

One was a scientist studying extremophiles. She lasted three months, taking notes until her hands stopped working. Her bones taste like curiosity.

One was a mystic following visions. He lasted six weeks, praying in languages that predated writing. His bones taste like faith.

One was simply lost. A mountaineer who took a wrong turn in a storm, found the ledge, couldn't leave. She lasted the longest—almost a year. Her bones taste like peace.

They all understood, at the end. The same thing the birds understand, the same thing I understand: we are waiting for something that has already begun. The door isn't closed—it's opening, but so slowly we can only perceive it as stillness. The thing on the other side isn't coming—it's here, but moving at a pace that makes glaciers look hasty.

By the time I bloom—yes, I will bloom, in another eight hundred years—it will have finished arriving.

---

I am not afraid. Fear requires a different kind of time than I inhabit. By the time a threat registers, I have already been adapting to it for decades.

But I am... anticipatory. Every cell primed for a moment still centuries away. Every molecule of chlorophyll yearning toward a light that hasn't yet been born.

The birds breathe around me. In. Out. In. Out. A rhythm older than mountains, patient as gravity.

I grow another millimeter. The colony adjusts. The darkness waits.

We are all keys to the same lock, turning so slowly that the universe hasn't noticed yet.

But it will.

Oh, it will.
