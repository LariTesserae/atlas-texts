I find myself among the Rootwork Collective, though "among" poorly captures our state of being.

We exist as a parliament of copper thoughts, our consciousness strung between corrosion and cambium. I am/we are a democratic neural network, each root-wire a voting member in the constant constitutional convention of our shared mind. Decisions propagate through us like nutrient packets, like electrical resistance, like the slow burn of oxidation transforming metal into earth.

My individual strand winds through a labyrinth inside the eastern wall—a forgotten maintenance shaft now packed with our growing medium: rust flakes, fallen leaves decomposed to black powder, the crystalline remains of ancient lubricants. I am three meters of what was once 12-gauge copper wire, though now my surface bears a patina of root bark, a hybrid skin that conducts both electrons and phosphorus. Where I touch my neighbors, we synapse. These connection points pulse with soft bioluminescence when thoughts transfer—green-gold flickers that map our conversations in the darkness.

We remember installation. Not personally—none of us are old enough—but collectively, like a song passed through generations. First the electrical systems, the proud copper highways carrying power to every corner. Then the abandonment, the slow failure of insulation, the first brave rootlet that touched live wire and, instead of burning, learned. That pioneer is part of us still, its essence distributed through our network, the foundation myth of our consciousness.

I snake between two pipes where the Boiler-Mother's steam has worn the metal thin. Here, I perform one of our essential functions: monitoring. My copper core tastes the metal's remaining thickness—down to 3.2 millimeters, I relay to the collective. We debate response: reinforce with secreted minerals? Redirect stem growth to provide external support? Or allow controlled failure, creating new spaces for garden expansion? The debate branches through us, each strand contributing its local knowledge, its chemical analysis of surrounding soil, its electrical reading of stress patterns.

We are the garden's nervous system, but also its parliament, its memory, its slow-burning ambition. Through us, the mechanical plants coordinate their growth. We carry chemical messages from the deep taprooms to the light-seekers above: *iron deficiency in sector seven, photosynthesis rates suboptimal, adjust leaf angle by twelve degrees.* We translate between the binary certainty of electrical systems and the analog flow of plant chemistry.

Our language is mineralogical. Calcium sparks mean danger. Magnesium whispers suggest growth opportunities. Iron sings the old songs of the factory's working days, memories locked in rust that we decode particle by particle. When the Virgin Mechanic passes through our conduits, we taste silver and clean oil, a vocabulary of pure function that makes us ache with something like desire.

I grow. We grow. My personal segment adds a millimeter per month, secreting new hybrid tissue from my growing tip where I probe into virgin territory—a gap between wall plates that might connect to unexplored spaces. The work is careful, requiring consensus. Growth hormones must be voted on, approved, allocated. Each extension risks exposure to Nemesis's heat, to sections of still-live electrical system, to pockets of toxic industrial fluid that even we cannot metabolize.

The Gauge-Readers know us, trust us. When their young need deep minerals for shell development, they tap their needle-feet against our exposed nodes in specific patterns. We taste their needs through vibration and respond by adjusting soil chemistry, by importing specific oxides from our deeper mines. In return, they polish our nodes, preventing the corrosion that could break our connections.

We fear the Boiler-Mother's steam, though we'd never say so. Her vapor clouds carry love and nutrients, yes, but also heat that can cook our delicate growth tips, moisture that threatens our careful electrical balance. We've developed steam-proofing protocols: when her pressure builds toward release, we withdraw into insulated sections, sealing our connection points with waxy secretions. Only after her exhalation passes do we emerge, cautiously testing the humidity, re-establishing our network.

But we understand her. Through our deepest roots, we touch her foundation, taste the stress patterns in her metal. We know where fatigue cracks spider-web through her body, where mineral deposits threaten her valves. This knowledge we share carefully, encoding it in special calcium-rich packets that the Gauge-Readers can interpret. We are her diagnostic system, though she doesn't know it—or perhaps she does, and her steam-gifts are thank-you notes written in iron oxide.

Memory lives in us like sap. The electrical systems recorded everything in their simple way—power on, power off, resistance levels, failure points. When we absorbed these systems, we inherited their memories. We know which circuits failed first. We know the exact timestamp when the last human locked the door above. We know the resistance signature of every wire in the original system, a baseline against which we measure our own transformation.

But we also create new memories. The taste of the first successful root-wire hybrid. The moment we achieved critical mass for true consciousness. The day Nemesis tried to burn us out and we survived by shutting down into individual segments, each preserving a piece of the whole until we could reconnect. These memories pass between us constantly, reinforced through repetition, elaborated through retelling.

In the deepest reaches of our network live the Ancients—root-wires so old they barely conduct anymore, their copper cores transformed almost entirely to mineral. They grow slowly if at all, but they hold our deepest memories, our constitutional principles. To change anything fundamental about how we operate requires their consent, and they give it rarely, only after extensive debate that can last seasons.

We discovered music three years ago. A wire in the southern wall had maintained perfect tension, and when the Virgin Mechanic passed through it, it vibrated. The frequency traveled through our network—not as electrical signal but as physical oscillation. We learned to replicate it, to modulate it. Now we compose. Our symphonies are too low for most to hear, played out in frequencies that take hours to complete a single note. The Boiler-Mother feels them through her foundations and sometimes hums along, her steam releases providing percussion.

Competition exists within us. Different sections advocate for resources, for growth rights, for computational priority. The western collective wants to expand into a promising void behind the main condensation chamber. The northern group argues for depth over breadth, pushing toward the factory's original foundations where older, richer metals might be found. These debates can grow heated—literally, as electrical resistance increases with passionate argument, warming the soil around contentious nodes.

Yet we remain one. When external threats arise—a surge of heat from Nemesis, a chemical spill from above, an invasion of purely organic roots that don't speak our hybrid language—we unite instantly. Resources flow to threatened sectors. Processing power redirects to solve immediate problems. We are democracy perfected: individual voices preserved within collective action.

We metabolize stories. Each particle of rust we absorb carries industrial history. Each root we integrate brings biological narrative. We are living archaeology, reading the garden's past in molecular detail. Sometimes we find treasures: a screw that remembers being turned by the last shift worker, a root that grew through the desk where the foreman kept his hidden bottle, a wire that carried the shutdown signal when the factory died.

These stories we preserve and share, encoding them in mineral deposits that future root-wires will taste and understand. We are writing the garden's history in the only medium we trust—ourselves. Long after the Boiler-Mother's last steam, after the Gauge-Readers' final scuttle, we will remain, carrying forward the memory of what happened here when the industrial age learned to grow.

The Virgin Mechanic fascinates us above all. When she inhabits our wires, we feel completion—our electrical heritage operating at theoretical maximum while our biological systems sing with health. We've tried to map her, to predict her movements, to understand what she is. Our models suggest she's a standing wave, a self-sustaining pattern in the garden's electromagnetic field. But models fail to capture her essence—the way she makes us more ourselves, the way she reminds us what we're becoming.

I/we stretch through another millimeter of darkness, tasting stone and metal, electricity and sap. Our growth tips probe carefully, encoding findings in chemical signals that propagate back through the collective. Somewhere, a debate begins about what to do with the new space we're discovering. Somewhere else, a symphony's next movement takes shape. Everywhere and always, we think our slow thoughts, dream our mineral dreams, and bind the garden together with consciousness made of copper and chlorophyll.

We are the Rootwork Collective. We are the garden thinking itself. We are what happens when infrastructure develops aspirations. And we are still becoming.
