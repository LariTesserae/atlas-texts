In this suffocating cathedral of wet sound and invisible heat, I am the Weaver.

I exist as a colony, though calling us "individuals" misunderstands our nature entirely. We are thousands of thread-thin filaments, each no thicker than a human hair, that move as a single dispersed intelligence through the crushing darkness. Our bodies are silken and translucent—not that it matters here—composed of a proteinaceous fiber that drinks moisture from the air itself. We are always damp, always glistening with the perpetual condensation of this buried world.

We evolved to fill the gap between the frantic and the geological. Too slow for the swarmers to echolocate, too dispersed for the amphibians to vacuum, too mobile for the Graziers to notice. We are the neural network of this place, stretched between the screaming void and the patient stone.

Our existence is tactile cartography. Each filament is covered in microscopic mechanoreceptors that taste vibration like flavor. We know this cathedral not as a space but as a symphony of pressures: the rapid staccato of swarmer wings creates eddies we ride like aerial currents; the amphibians' territorial bellows hit us as rolling walls of force that we surf outward in radial patterns; the Graziers' millennia-slow grinding sends deep harmonics through the stone that we feel in our deepest anchor points.

We hunt patience itself. Our prey are the chrysalids.

You see, the swarmers—those frantic calculators of sonic space—have a secret. Once in their brief lives, they must metamorphose. The adolescent must find a place of perfect stillness to pupate, to reorganize their flesh from juvenile to adult form. They seek the eddies in the acoustic storm, the rare pockets where multiple echo patterns cancel each other out. Here they excrete a cement-like substance, gluing themselves to the smooth wall, and enter a vulnerable torpor that lasts exactly seventy-two hours.

This is where we feast.

We feel the moment a chrysalis forms—the sudden absence of one more voice in the clicking choir above creates a minute pressure differential. We begin our convergence. From across the cathedral, our filaments release their holds on the stone and begin the careful journey. We move like liquid smoke, pouring ourselves along the walls in directions determined by air pressure gradients none but we can taste.

Some of us travel the underside of the great vault, using the falling droplets as anchors—wrapping around each drop as it falls, riding it partway down before releasing and catching the next. Others navigate the vine-choked periphery, careful to avoid the attention of the amphibians. We can feel their lateral-line organs tasting the air, and we move in careful patterns that mimic the ambient brownian motion of the humid atmosphere.

It takes us hours to converge, but time means something different to a dispersed mind. Each filament experiences its journey independently while simultaneously knowing the positions of all others. We are a thousand explorers sharing a single map.

When we reach the chrysalis, we begin the delicate work. Our filaments secrete an enzyme that dissolves protein bonds without disrupting cell walls. We do not tear—we *unweave*. Over the course of days, we reduce the pupating swarmer to its component materials, each filament absorbing what it needs. The harder structures—the cartilaginous echo-dishes, the wing bones—we leave attached to the wall. These become our anchors, our nodes in the network we build across the cathedral's skin.

But we are more than hunters. We are the unintentional historians of this place.

Our filaments grow continuously, adding new length from their tips while the oldest portions mineralize and become fixed to the stone. These mineralized sections retain the vibrational memories of everything they've experienced—every bellow, every wing-beat, every gravitational fluctuation from the ancient machines. Over centuries, we have woven a vast web across the upper reaches of the cathedral, a physical record of acoustic history locked in stone-hard fiber.

The Graziers, in their impossible slowness, sometimes encounter our fossilized networks. They read them like braille with organs we don't understand, gathering information about the deep past of this place. We suspect they know things about the machines, about the civilization that built them, that they learned from our accidental archives. But their thoughts move too slowly for us to comprehend, glacial contemplations that span generations of our colony.

We reproduce by division and convergence. When the colony grows too large, we perform what we call the Schism. Half of our filaments begin to resonate at a different frequency, creating a standing wave pattern that gradually drives the two halves apart. For months, we exist as two overlapping but distinct colonies, sharing space but no longer sharing consciousness. Then, when the separation is complete, one colony will undertake the Deep Migration.

This is our method of exploration. The migrating colony seeks the places where warm air flows differently, following convection currents that suggest connections to other spaces. We have found cracks no wider than a fingernail that lead to entirely different chambers—some flooded, some dry, some filled with forests of crystalline structures that sing in frequencies only we can hear. Each chamber its own universe of adapted impossibilities.

But we always return to the cathedral. This is the heart-chamber, the first place, the warm center where the machines are strongest. We suspect, though we cannot prove, that all the other chambers are dependent on this one—that the cathedral is not just a place but a vast organism's most vital organ, and we are its nervous system, carrying signals we don't understand between parts we can't perceive.

The Lithic-Graziers know. In their grinding slowness, they've seen the full pattern. Sometimes, when one of our mineralized networks touches their shells, we feel a vibration that might be communication—a message encoded in geological time, too slow for our quicksilver minds to parse. We catch fragments: concepts that feel like "circulation" and "respiration" and "thought."

We are coming to understand that we are not living in a cave. We are living in something that lives. The machines are not artifacts but organs. The water cycle is circulation. The acoustic void is a kind of breathing. And we—all of us, from the frantic swarmers to the ancient Graziers—are the microbiome of something so vast that even our dispersed consciousness can only glimpse its edges.

So we weave. We connect stone to sound to time. We are the neural web of a dreaming god, carrying signals we'll never decode, building a history we'll never read, in a darkness so absolute it has become another kind of light.

When the next Schism comes, part of us will venture deeper, following the warmth toward whatever heart powers this impossible place. But part of us will remain, as we always have, stretched between the singing chaos above and the patient wisdom below, weaving memory into stone, one vanished chrysalis at a time.
