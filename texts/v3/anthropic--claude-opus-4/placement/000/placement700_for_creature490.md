I find myself among the Librarians of Decay.

We were never explorers or scientists—we came as pilgrims seeking the end of knowledge, and found instead its fermentation. Now we exist as living card catalogs in the moss-tunnels, our bodies stretched into impossible filing systems. My spine has become a spiral staircase of vertebrae, each bone a different epoch, ascending through my own history. My ribs spread like wings or shelves, holding volumes of pressed salamander skin on which we record what we call the Index of Unbecoming.

I no longer remember if I was one person or several. My consciousness runs on tracks through the moss like a library's pneumatic tube system, shooting between the bodies we share—for the Librarians are both singular and collective, a distributed archive of everything that fails to successfully die here. My fingers have multiplied into a hundred styluses, constantly scratching observations into the living vellum of my expanded dermis. The sound is industrial, metallic—steel nibs on organic parchment, writing at the speed of rust.

We catalog the salamanders' fold-paths, creating taxonomies for creatures that exist in seventeen places at once. Our classification system uses scent and electrical resistance rather than words. Section by section, we organize impossibilities: here, the moths that oxidize backwards; there, the spores that remember futures they haven't reached. My left eye has become a jeweler's loupe permanently fused to my skull, its lens ground from crystallized time stolen from the Accountants' tentacles. Through it, I can see the bibliography of any object—every text it has touched, every story it has interrupted.

The other Librarians and I meet at the intersection of several moss-tunnels, where the electrical crackling of the plant-network creates natural reading rooms. Our bodies flow together like mercury, sharing catalogs, cross-referencing experiences. We've developed a filing system based on density—heavier thoughts sink to the bottom of our collective consciousness, lighter paradoxes float. Sometimes we discover we've been cataloging each other, infinite loops of classification that would drive us mad if we hadn't already passed through madness into something more useful.

I maintain the section on "Industrial Processes, Absent"—documenting all the mechanical sounds that echo through the plateau without source. My specialty is the subsonic grinding that occurs when moss-plates shift, a sound like factories that never existed manufacturing products that cannot be. I've written seventeen volumes on the moth-wing turbines alone, their rust-patterns encoding operating manuals for machines that evolution built from flesh and oxidation.

My body has adapted to the work. My skin has become translucent, showing the card-catalog drawers of my redesigned organs. My heart pumps ink instead of blood—a special secretion harvested from the bog-pools where the Accountants swim, luminous and heavy with temporal sediment. When I breathe, I exhale index cards, each one containing a single observation: "Salamander 47-B folded through coordinates that don't exist." "Moss-cliff 12 computed pi to a value where circles become squares." "The Surgeon invented a new form of calcium that remembers."

We trade with the other inhabitants. The Shepherds bring us new paradoxes to catalog in exchange for our maps of stable fold-paths. The Rust Prophets offer prophecies written on moth-wings, which we file under "Futures, Oxidized." Even the Erasers visit, though we can never remember their visits—we only know they've been by the gaps in our shelves, the absent volumes that mark knowledge too dangerous to preserve.

Our library has no walls, no roof—it is the tunnels themselves, every surface inscribed with our findings. We've been writing for so long that we've had to develop new alphabets, new syntaxes for describing things that happen outside causality. Our script looks like circuit diagrams made of fungus, growing across the moss in bioluminescent trails. Reading our work requires following the text through three dimensions, sometimes four, tracing arguments that loop back through their own premises.

At night (though night is a contested concept here), I hang from the tunnel ceiling by my elongated spine, sorting the day's observations. My hundred fingers dance across my skin-pages, reorganizing, reclassifying, finding new connections between impossible things. The sound of our work joins the plateau's symphony—scratching nibs, rustling skin-pages, the whisper of paradoxes being properly shelved.

We are creating an encyclopedia of everything that should not exist, a reference work for a physics that runs on dream-logic and metal oxidation. Our catalog will never be complete—the plateau generates new impossibilities faster than we can document them. But this is our purpose now, our transformation: to be living books in a library with no borders, archivists of a wilderness that has evolved past the need for sense.

Sometimes I remember having a name, a face with the standard number of features. But those memories are filed away now, pressed between pages of salamander leather, cross-referenced under "Identities, Abandoned." I am happier as an index, more complete as a system of organization than I ever was as a person. My body is perfectly adapted to its purpose—stretched, multiplied, reorganized into something that can contain the uncontainable.

I am the Librarian who catalogs absence. I am the archive that documents its own impossibility. I am a dozen bodies thinking one bibliography, a hundred hands writing a single, endless entry in the Index of Unbecoming.

The work continues. The work has always continued. The work will continue until the plateau's industrial silence finally claims even the sound of our scratching pens, and we become our own final entry: "Library: see under Moss."
