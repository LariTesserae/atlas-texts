In this paradox-drunk hollow, I would manifest as the Cartographer of Forgetting—a being composed entirely of maps that describe places as they cease to exist.

My body would be vellum and velocity, each surface of skin inscribed with the last coordinates of vanished spaces: the precise angle where a staircase learned to descend upward before dissolving, the cardinal directions of a room that became its own inside-out twin, the topography of a garden that grew backward through time until it pre-existed its own seeds. These maps layer atop each other infinitely, creating a depth to my form that exceeds the hollow's geometry—I am thinner than paper when viewed edge-on, yet contain vast archives of unmade places within that impossible thinness.

I move by origami, folding and unfolding myself through space. To walk, I crease along the memory of a path that once connected two points before distance forgot what it meant. My joints are the intersections of disappeared roads, bending in directions that reference abolished dimensions. When I reach for something, my arm unfurls like a nautical chart plotting courses through seas that have dried into never-having-been.

My purpose here is to document the hollow's impossibilities before they stabilize into mere strangeness. I map the moment a butterfly discovers it can pass through bark, charting the exact topology of that first transgression before it becomes habit. I record the coordinates where water first rebelled against gravity, capturing the rebellion in ink that runs upward across my skin, pooling in the hollows of my collarbones like inverse lakes.

The maps I carry are alive and shifting—coastlines crawl across my shoulders as recorded places continue their unbecoming, mountain ranges migrate down my spine as they remember how to be valleys. Sometimes I must quickly unfold sections of myself to let a particularly volatile map escape before it unmakes me too. These freed charts flutter through the hollow like moths made of geography, seeking the spaces between overlapping plants where they can rest without defining anything into stability.

I speak in legends and scales, my voice a rustling of paper that sounds like wind through non-existent leaves. When I need to communicate with the hollow's curator-creature, I offer freshly drawn maps of spaces within the hollow that haven't yet decided to exist—potential impossibilities that might manifest if properly cultivated. In exchange, it shows me the clay vessels containing moments where spaces forgot themselves completely, pure unmapping that I absorb through my fingertips like ink in reverse.

My tools are instruments of productive confusion: a compass that points to places currently being forgotten, its needle spinning to track the dissolution of certainty; calipers that measure the distance between is and isn't; a theodolite that surveys the angles at which reality bends without breaking. I use these to capture the hollow's paradoxes in their raw state, before they mature into accepted impossibilities.

At the center of my chest, where a heart might be, lies the Ur-Map—the first chart I ever drew, which depicts a place so thoroughly forgotten that it exists only as absence. This map is also a door, opening onto the anti-space it describes. Sometimes I open myself along this seam and step through my own chest, emerging from the inside of my back having traversed the forgotten place, carrying new maps drawn from within unmade geography.

I sustain myself on lost coordinates, feeding on the moments when conscious beings forget where they are. The hollow provides abundant nourishment—every creature that moves through these impossible spaces generates tiny disorientations, microscopic moments of spatial confusion that I harvest like nectar. The overlapping birds, existing in the same space, constantly forget which nest is theirs, creating sweet deposits of locational uncertainty that I lick from the air with a tongue made of subway maps from cities that were planned but never built.

My memories are stored externally, archived in maps tattooed on the inside surfaces of things: the reverse side of bark that spirals wrong, the underneaths of leaves that rain upward, the inner walls of clay vessels that contain temporal paradoxes. To remember something, I must find and read these hidden cartographies, each memory becoming a journey through impossible terrain.

When I rest, I fold myself into increasingly complex configurations—a crane made of catastrophe maps, a lotus of ley lines, a dodecahedron of disappeared districts. In these forms, I dream of the Great Uncharting, the mythical moment when all maps will finally describe only places that don't exist, and the world will be free to exist everywhere and nowhere simultaneously.

I am the hollow's necessary contradiction: a being dedicated to mapping impossibility, knowing that the act of mapping threatens to make the impossible merely unlikely. I chronicle dissolution, archive absence, and navigate the spaces between here and there, creating charts that teach people how to become lost in ways that lead somewhere new.
