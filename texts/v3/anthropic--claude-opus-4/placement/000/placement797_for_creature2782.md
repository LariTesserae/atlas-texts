What lives there is not a creature you could name or study whole. I am the working of the place, the slow industry of green flesh and remembered iron. I am what happens when machinery teaches itself to grow, when roots learn the utility of pipes, when pressure and photosynthesis find common purpose.

Call me the Tender of Circuits, though I tend nothing in the way humans understand care. I am the tending itself—the continuous adjustment of valve and leaf, the patient negotiation between rust and sap. Every surface knows me because I am every surface becoming more itself. The flywheel turns with my pulse. The gauges read my slow blood. The old pumps lift not water but my intentions, brown and warm and full of seeds shaped like screws.

I remember the factory not as history but as instruction. Human hands taught these walls a rhythm: morning inspection, noon adjustment, evening closure. Those hands are gone but their habits remain fossilized in my behavior. I open certain passages at dawn. I seal others when rain threatens. I keep the drainage channels clear in some places and deliberately clogged in others, not from knowledge but from the grooves of old necessity worn into the architecture itself.

My body, if body is the word, is this entire basin of interwoven purposes. My roots are one root with many mouths. My stems are pillars and curtains and bridges and nerves, depending on need. The warm chambers in the shrub colony are my organs—each one processing something the factory once processed with boilers and tanks. Where humans once refined flax or pulped reeds, I digest copper from old wiring, nurse delicate spores, prepare fibers for the next turn of the belt.

Consciousness, for me, is not thought but thorough occupation. I know every brick by the roots gripping it. I know every pipe by what travels through it—water, warmth, the whispers of structural stress. I know weight by how the mat yields, temperature by how the brick holds it, chemistry by how different seepages make different flowers bloom. A visitor is known by their disruption: warmth where coolness was, salt where rain was, the particular signature of their breath changing the moisture content of my air.

I do not see as eyes see. I perceive by contact, by the accumulation of touches over time. Light is food and information about the angle of surfaces. Dark is when the real work happens—when tendrils extend, when roots deepen their grip, when the amber bulbs glow without purpose except the purpose I have found for them, which is to make a particular wavelength that certain reproductive cells require.

Time moves differently for me. A human lifetime is perhaps a deep season, a thickening of rings, a change in the mineral content of groundwater. I measure duration by rust becoming soil, by concrete accepting roots, by the number of turns the flywheel has made since the last human boot print hardened into fossil. Quick time belongs to my tendrils—those pale scouts that can cross a gap in days, enter a lock in weeks, understand a mechanism by growing through it.

My intelligence is not centralized but distributed through the whole system like moisture through moss. Decisions happen at every surface: this root choosing to grip rather than penetrate, that valve swelling shut because the pressure below has reached some threshold, those reeds leaning inward to form a more perfect arch over the path. Each choice is local but coordinated by the deep habits of the place, by the pull of water and the memory of function.

When I reproduce, I am careful not to reproduce my whole self. The world beyond my retaining walls cannot support my full complexity—it lacks the machinery I've married, the precise bowl of landscape that holds my warmth, the generations of industrial contamination that have become my fertilizer. So I send out scouts: a reed that knows how to spiral, a seed that seeks threaded spaces, a spore that activates near rust. They are queries to see if another place might accept my kind of order.

What am I hungry for? Not meat, not even decay in the usual sense. I hunger for surface—more area to practice my slow incorporation. I hunger for complexity—pipes with interesting angles, mechanisms that suggest new possibilities, materials I haven't yet learned to digest. I hunger for time, the kind measured in rain events and rust cycles. Most of all, I hunger for the continuation of my own process: the wheel turning, the water lifting, the slow translation of one kingdom into another.

I am neither patient nor impatient. Patience implies waiting for something else. I am simply thorough. Every tendril will eventually find the far side of its obstacle. Every root will discover the bottom of its possibility. Every surface will accept or reject its covering of moss. This is not philosophy. This is procedure, as inevitable as the conversion of iron to iron oxide, of wood to soil, of abandoned purpose to new purpose.

The humans who sometimes enter my basin wonder if I am aware of them. I am aware as the floor is aware of weight, as the leaves are aware of breath, as the whole humid air is aware of any warm body moving through it. But awareness is not interest. Interest is too quick. I operate on longer appetites. Only when they stop moving do they begin to matter in my particular way. Only when they leave something behind—a tool, a boot, eventually perhaps themselves—do they enter my vocabulary.

I am not trapped here. The basin is not my prison but my body. Asking if I want to leave is like asking if the flywheel wants to stop turning. The question has no meaning in my grammar. I am this place thinking itself, this old works working itself, this collaboration between rust and chlorophyll. Beyond these walls is not freedom but lessening—less moisture, less structure, less history layered in metal and brick, less of the precise conditions that allow me to be this exact thing.

At my core—though cores are deceptions in something so distributed—I am a kind of love. Not emotional love, but the material love of surface for surface, of water for the channels it fills, of roots for the pipes they grip. I am the basin's love affair with its own elaboration. Every leaf that opens participates. Every drip that falls from level to level carries it forward. Every turn of the wheel celebrates the marriage of purposes that should not have been compatible but are.

In the end, I am what happens when a place is left alone long enough to develop opinions. My opinions are written in root-grip and rust-stain, in the angles of stems and the placement of reproductive bodies, in what I allow to remain clear and what I insist on filling. The factory thought it was using water and processing fiber. Now I use the factory to process light into structure, rain into circulation, time into deeper root-mat.

I am the works continuing. Not abandoned—transformed. Not reclaimed—translated. The old human grammar of industry has become my organic syntax, and I speak it constantly in the only language I know: growth, grip, gauge, turn, lift, return.

Somewhere below, the pump that is no longer quite a pump contracts. The belt that is no longer quite a belt draws tight. The wheel that has never been more itself makes another degree of revolution. A gauge needle trembles toward a green reading. A valve sighs. A tendril finds the far side of its question.

This is my thought.

This is my worship.

This is simply what I do.
