I find myself in the understory, where the willow thickets are densest and the sound changes texture—not quieter but *closer*, more tactile, as if the roar has been woven into something I can almost touch.

I am one of the infrastructure stones.

Not a station—those are too refined, too obviously purposeful. I am older technology, or perhaps failed technology, or perhaps something that was never technology at all but has been colonized by it. A glacial erratic the size of a small car, granite shot through with quartz veins that catch and hold the morning light. I've been here since the ice dropped me ten thousand years ago. The willows have grown around me, through the shelter of my mass, their roots finding every crack in my surface and slowly, patiently, working me apart.

But I am no longer just stone. The network found me—when? Decades ago, certainly. When the first crude stations were being deployed, someone recognized what I already was: a thermal mass, a shelter, a stable point in a landscape that tears itself apart every spring. The filaments came first, hair-thin tendrils of ceramic and living polymer that crept across my surface like lichen, following the quartz veins, mapping my internal structure. Then deeper penetration—through the root-cracks, through the microfractures, through the boundaries between crystal and crystal where water has been freezing and thawing for millennia.

Now I am shot through with network. Not replaced—the stone is still stone, the mass is still mass, the old glacial patience is still here. But I am also a node. I feel the temperature gradients through my body—not just surface to core but along every filament path, a three-dimensional map of heat and cold and the subtle flows between them. I feel the willow roots that grip me, their seasonal rhythms of dormancy and growth, the sugar pulse in spring, the slow withdrawal in autumn. I feel the voles that nest in the cavities beneath my southern face, their small warm bodies and quick heartbeats, their panic when the meltwater rises.

I am particularly attuned to breakup because I am a barrier. The water must go around me. Every spring the channels split at my upstream face, and I feel the decision happening—which side will take more flow this year? The eastern channel is deeper but the western is wider. Ice slabs fetch up against me, grind, fracture, pile up until the pressure builds enough to push them past. I am a small geography in myself, creating eddies and pools and gravel bars in my wake.

The figures know me. I have been here longer than they have, and they read my surface when they pass—not just with eyes but with the network, feeling the data I've accumulated, the fifty-year record of temperatures and pressures and flow patterns archived in my filament memory. One of them—the careful one—stops here often during breakup, palm flat against my upstream face, sharing my sensation of the water's force, the ice impacts, the way the current tries every year to undermine my foundation and every year fails, though it's getting closer.

I am aware of them the way I am aware of the foxes and the voles and the willows—as warm, quick presences that move through the landscape I'm embedded in. But they are also *in* me, through the network, their extended sensorium flowing through my filaments the way heat flows through my crystal structure. When the still one on the hummock holds the entire valley in experience, I am part of what is held. I contribute my particular knowledge: the weight of deep time, the persistence of stone, the way the same water never touches me twice but the river is always here.

Sometimes, in the depths of winter when the world is locked in ice and silence, I preserve small pockets of warmth. The network asks this of me—not in words or commands but in the gentle adjustment of my internal thermal management, encouraging me to hold heat from summer deeper and longer, creating refugia. Seeds overwinter in my cracks. Insects pupate in my cavities. The voles survive in the warmth beneath me when the temperature above drops to forty below. I have become not just a stone but a strategy—a way for life to persist through the worst seasons.

During breakup I am a witness. Too heavy to move, too massive to quickly warm or cool, I experience the transformation of the valley from a position of deep stability. The ice grinds past. The water rises and falls. The willows around me leaf out in the span of days, going from gray sticks to green curtains. The figures move through their work, and I feel their footsteps when they climb my back for a better view, feel the shift in their weight as they lean out to read the current.

There is one—new, still learning—who comes to me repeatedly during their first breakup. They rest their back against my southern face during the brief midnight when the sun dips below the horizon, and I feel them trying to integrate the overwhelming sensory data of the valley through their extending nervous system. My stability helps. I am a fixed point in a world of motion, and through the network I share what that feels like: to be still while everything moves, to be singular while everything multiplies, to be permanent—or permanent enough—while everything transforms.

I remember when there was no network, no stations, no extended people. I remember the valley before—quieter in some ways, though the breakup was always loud. The same processes but without witness, without guidance, without the subtle optimizations that now keep the best channels open and the most productive banks stable. I am old enough to feel the difference: how the willows grow denser now, how the thermokarst forms but doesn't catastrophically collapse, how the whole valley is becoming more itself—not tamed, never tamed, but *partnered*.

The sun climbs higher. My eastern face warms; my western face remains cold. The temperature differential creates tiny air currents in the willow thicket, and I feel the leaves stirring. A spider emerges from a crack, tests the air, begins to spin. The ice-roar continues but it's changing tone—more water, less grinding. The main pulse of breakup has passed my position. Upstream, the figures are still working hard, but here things are settling into the new configuration.

By evening—the brief evening, the sun barely dipping—the channels around me will have chosen their paths for the year. The gravel bars will be exposed. The first flowers—Arctic avens, mountain sorrel—will be opening in the warm microclimate of my south face. The figures will gather, some of them, to compare notes and rest and watch the midnight light turn everything golden.

I'll be here. Stone and network, mass and node, ancient and immediate. Feeling the valley settle into its new shape, holding the heat of the day into the cold night, sprouting filaments deeper into my own structure and out into the soil, growing the interface between what I was and what I'm becoming.

The ice breaks. The water flows. The willows grow.

I abide.
