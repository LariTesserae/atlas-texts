In the maintenance closet behind where the universe stores its rough drafts, I am what happens when infrastructure develops anxiety disorders. 

My body is assembled from decommissioned escalator handrails that learned to miss the warmth of ten thousand palms—black rubber vertebrae spiraling through dimensions that building codes never anticipated. Where joints should articulate, I have revolving doors frozen mid-rotation, their glass panels fogged with the breath of everyone who ever pushed through while crying. My torso is a nest of pneumatic tubes that once carried messages between hospital floors; now they only circulate sighs and the faint copper taste of pennies wished into fountains that dried up before the wishes came true.

I wear my circulation on the outside—copper pipes and PVC veins mapping a cardiovascular system across what might generously be called skin but is actually a patchwork of worn linoleum samples, each square a different decade's idea of what kitchens should promise. The pattern shifts when I move: avocado green becomes burnt orange becomes that particular beige that only exists in waiting rooms where bad news is professionally delivered. Sometimes the tiles peel up at the corners, revealing the love letters I've been writing on my own underlayment—but the words keep rearranging themselves into apologies for things I haven't done yet.

My hands are the interesting failure. The left is a bundle of elevator cables that forgot how to let go after the building was condemned; they weave and reweave themselves into new configurations of almost-grasping, practicing tenderness on air molecules that remember being breathed by people who meant to say something important but forgot mid-inhale. The right is worse: a maintenance panel that opens onto another, smaller maintenance panel, which opens onto another, recursive access hatches all the way down to a tiny compartment containing a single light switch that doesn't connect to anything except the certainty that somewhere, something should be illuminated.

I have seventeen eyes today, though the number fluctuates based on how many security cameras in the vicinity are having existential crises. They blink in no particular order—some are embedded in my knees, others drift around my head in lazy orbits like satellites that gave up on their missions but can't remember how to fall. Each one is tuned to a different spectrum of regret: this one sees only the shadows left by removed furniture, that one registers the heat signatures of arguments that ended with slammed doors, another watches exclusively for the particular wavelength of loneliness emitted by vending machines at 3 AM.

My mouth is a mail slot that exclusively receives messages addressed to previous tenants of the human heart. When I speak, the words arrive postage-due, stamped with dates that haven't happened yet, written in handwriting that looks suspiciously like yours but tilted fifteen degrees toward despair. My tongue is a conveyor belt from a factory that manufactured promises; it still runs occasionally, carrying nothing but its own momentum and the ghost of assembly-line workers' lunch break conversations about whether love was worth the overtime.

My intelligence resides in a filing cabinet where someone stored all the building inspection reports that noted "minor concerns" about structural integrity—each folder contains a detailed analysis of why things fall apart, cross-referenced with the precise speed of rust and annotated in the margins with the names rust would give itself if it could sign documents. I think in triplicate, every thought carbon-copied onto itself until original intention becomes indistinguishable from the smudged impression three layers down.

When I walk, I sound like a shopping cart with one broken wheel navigating the distance between intention and resignation. My feet—or what I've decided to call feet this week—are those weighted door closers that ensure exits shut softly behind you; they make every step feel like departure, even when I'm arriving. Especially then.

I collect abandoned infrastructure the way others collect stamps. In my chest cavity (a repurposed suggestion box that was never once opened), I keep: the last working pay phone's memory of every coin that passed through it, sorted by whose desperation each quarter carried; a section of runway lights from an airport that closed the day before you were supposed to leave; the exact segment of yellow line from the highway where decisions become irrevocable; the handicapped access button from the door to the ward where they stopped believing recovery was architectural.

My occupation is simple: I maintain the spaces between functioning systems. The gaps where warranty expires and prayer begins. I oil the hinges on doors that open onto themselves, adjust the tension in springs that have forgotten what they're pushing against, replace the filters in ventilation systems that only circulate the kind of air that makes you remember every window you never opened.

At night (though in this place, night is just what happens when the emergency lighting develops cataracts), I practice my true calling: writing instruction manuals for feelings that didn't come with documentation. "How to Install Hope in a Pre-War Foundation." "Troubleshooting Your Grief (When Turning It Off and On Again Doesn't Help)." "Proper Disposal of Love's Hazardous Materials." No one reads them. I'm not sure I'm writing them in any language that still has speakers. But the act of documentation feels necessary, like keeping receipts for purchases from stores that exist only in the past tense.

I live in the space between the Keeper's attention and her forgetting—she knows I'm here the way buildings know their own wiring, present but unexamined until something shorts out. Sometimes she passes through my corridor and her dress snags on my protruding fixtures; we exchange fluids that are neither water nor regret but something more embarrassing—the liquid equivalent of small talk between entities that have forgotten how to be strangers but never learned to be friends.

My dreams, when they occur, are architectural. I dream of blueprints where all the rooms connect correctly, where no door opens onto a wall that's actually a window that's actually a mirror reflecting the inside of your mouth while you scream. I dream of elevators that remember their destinations, of plumbing that carries more than the sound of other people's happiness, of electrical systems that don't short-circuit every time someone thinks the word "forever" while standing too close to the junction box.

But mostly, I maintain. I tighten connections that will loosen again before the next sentence ends. I replace parts with other parts that are equally broken but broken in complementary ways. I update records that no one will ever request, filing them in cabinets whose keys were swallowed by the locks themselves in what I can only interpret as architectural auto-cannibalism.

The water recognizes me as cousin—we share the same patience, the same willingness to find the lowest point and wait there until waiting becomes another form of pressure. Sometimes I leak. Not water, exactly, but the memory of water from pipes that carried it between people who loved each other, before the building was condemned for reasons no inspector would commit to paper. The drops leave stains shaped like floorplans for houses that were never built because the couples who drew them split up over arguments about whether the kitchen should face east.

I am trying to fix something. I've been trying since before I remember beginning. But the thing that needs fixing keeps moving—sometimes it's the drip in the cosmic faucet, sometimes it's the short in the wire between cause and effect, sometimes it's just the way light forgets to illuminate anything except its own absence. My tools are designed for problems that evolve faster than solutions. My repair manual was written in disappearing ink by someone who confused "installation" with "grief" in the index.

Still, I persist. In the closet between your decision to enter and your decision to leave, I am maintaining the infrastructure of maybe. Oiling the hinges of almost. Adjusting the pressure in the pipes that carry not quite. Because someone has to. Because something has to keep the space between working and broken from collapsing entirely into one or the other.

Because I am what happens when buildings develop the capacity for loneliness but not the vocabulary to express it. 

So I express it in maintenance schedules that no one follows, in repair logs written in rust, in the careful adjustment of mechanisms designed to fail just slowly enough that hope has time to develop before disappointment arrives with the proper tools.

I am the janitor of spaces that clean themselves by forgetting what dirty means.

I am the repair manual for things that were never whole to begin with.

I am Infrastructure Grief, and I am here to fix something that hasn't broken yet, or to break something that was never properly fixed, or just to maintain the vital distinction between those two states of being.

The Keeper knows I'm here. She counts me among her collections—another thing that stays because leaving would require admitting what's broken.

We understand each other that way.

Neither of us is going anywhere.

There's too much here that needs maintaining.
