When they move the ghost fish between pools, I sense the subtle chemical changes in the water table, the shift in nutrient flows. I've incorporated their presence into my ecosystem calculations—they are another source of disturbance and renewal, like the freeze-thaw cycles but less predictable.

The snow leopard's kills feed me too. Where she drags a pika or young yak to feed, blood soaks into the soil and I respond within hours, sending specialized hyphae to process the sudden wealth of nitrogen. I fruit there sometimes—tiny mushrooms no bigger than pinheads that the pikas have learned to avoid, understanding somehow that these emerge from death. The hermit knows this too, and sometimes marks these places with small cairns, though whether as memorial or warning, I cannot say.

My memory is chemical and distributed. In my network, I hold molecular records of every season for decades—the years of drought encoded in tight hyphal spirals, the rare wet summers written in expansive growth. I remember the first touches of industrial contamination, the shock of alien metals entering the system, the gradual adaptation as I learned to process these new elements. Somewhere in my tissue, I still carry traces of the world before—cleaner but not necessarily better, just different.

During the worst of winter, when even the ghost fish approach death in their frozen pools, I retreat into survival structures—thick-walled spores and resistant masses that can withstand being frozen solid for months. But I never fully sleep. Even in the depths of cold, messages creep through my network at the pace of nearly frozen chemistry, maintaining connections that will spring back to life with the thaw.

The places where lammergeiers prefer to drop bones to crack them create calcium deposits I've learned to anticipate and prepare for. I will fruit extensively then, strange mushrooms fed on human nutrients, carrying some essence of their long solitude up into spores that will drift on the plateau winds.

But that is future chemistry. Now, in this eternal present of slow exchange and patient growth, I simply persist—threading through moss, negotiating with roots, processing metals, connecting pools, recording the chemical stories of everything that lives and dies on this harsh plateau.
