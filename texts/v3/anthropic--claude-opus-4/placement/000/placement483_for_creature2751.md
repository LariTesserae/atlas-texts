In this water-breathing city where nothing green grows, I am one of the channel-readers.

We are a small caste, smaller than pumpwrights, less visible than flood advocates. You know us by our bare feet, our weighted belts, our habit of standing shin-deep in moving water while crowds part around us like we're stones. We taste the city's circulation. We know which districts are fighting by the flavor of their runoff.

I was born to a family of condenser mechanics, people who spent their lives suspended in the skeletal forests, deafened by the constant ring of droplet-on-metal. My grandmother's hands were permanently stained with the blue-green patina of moisture collectors. She wanted me to follow her into the heights, to learn the songs that make water gather.

Instead, I went down.

At seven, during the Year of Copper Drowning, I fell into a surge channel near the old rendering district. Should have died. The water was running brown and electric, full of dissolved metals from a failed industrial gate. But I opened my mouth to scream and tasted instead: salmon-copper here, bitter zinc there, the sweet rot of protein facilities, the sharp note of residential fear-sweat, the flat mineral base of deep aquifer water forced up through emergency vents.

The water told me where it had been.

A retired channel-reader pulled me out, her feet finding purchase on the canal bottom while others slipped. She looked at my eyes - they'd taken on the temporary copper tint of immersion - and said nothing. Three days later she appeared at our housing stack with an apprentice contract. My parents protested. The work was dangerous, low-status, necessary. People crossed streets to avoid us. We smelled of everywhere the water had been.

She waited. I signed.

Twenty years now I've been reading. My feet know every major channel bottom from the Condensate Districts to the Lower Processing Reaches. The balls of my feet are thick with mineral calluses that sense pressure differentials. My toes can distinguish twelve kinds of drainage stone. When I stand in flowing water, I know which neighborhoods are eating, which are manufacturing, which are panicking about a broken gate.

The city pays us, barely. But residents pay better. A family will hire me to stand in their building's outflow and taste whether their walls are beginning to fail. A merchant collective will want to know if their competitors are secretly processing protein - the enzymatic signature is unmistakable. The transit authority needs confirmation that their magnetic lubricants aren't seeping into residential water. I wade, I taste, I tell them what moves beneath.

We develop specialties. Old Pitar reads violence - she can taste a fight three districts away by the adrenaline metabolites in runoff. Young Emo reads breeding. In spawning season, half the city's animal facilities hire her to stand downstream and predict fertility by hormone concentrations. The twins, Nil and Fan, read mechanical distress. They work for the municipality, moving through industrial channels, calling out warnings minutes before catastrophic failures.

I read boundaries.

Where one thing becomes another. Salt becoming sweet. Old water becoming new. Public becoming private. Safe becoming dangerous. The gradient moments, the chemical edges. I can stand at a junction and tell you which upstream neighbors haven't spoken in three years by the way their waters refuse to mix.

My feet have been cut by glass, by metal fragments, by the shells of feral canal-cleaners. My blood has mixed with every district's water. According to the old laws, this makes me part of the city's circulation. I cannot be fully exiled. I am written into the flow.

My dwelling is three rooms in a stack above the Ninth Meridian Channel. Through the floor grates, I hear water constantly. When the municipal systems run their weekly cleaning cycles, my rooms fill with the chlorine-ozone scent of sterilization. I've removed all the door seals. I like to feel the building breathe.

My neighbors are a rope-maker, a family of vent-crab farmers, and a very old woman who claims to remember birds. She describes them as "membrane-winged but warm, bright-voiced, nest-builders in high places." I think she's confused them with messenger drones. But sometimes, when she makes their sounds - liquid trills and sharp whistles - something in my chest recognizes a loss I can't name.

I own:
- Seven pairs of weighted belts, each for different current speeds
- Ceramic cups in thirty-seven sizes for sample collection  
- A library of scent-bottles, chemical references in solution
- Boots I almost never wear
- Maps of channels that no longer exist, drawn on metal sheets
- A dry box containing my grandmother's condenser tuning forks
- Legal documents affirming my right to refuse any channel
- Scars in precise patterns where industrial water cut me

The young apprentices think we're mystics. They expect secret knowledge, channel-whispers, the ability to predict floods by taste alone. But we're not prophets. We're sensors. Human instruments calibrated by exposure. The water tells us what is, not what will be. The future comes down from the condensers like everything else - one drop at a time, gathering into streams we'll eventually have to read.

My work rhythm follows the city's circulation. Early morning, when the night freight has been running, I read the industrial channels for contamination. The water runs thick and dark, tasting of hot metal and compression. By midday, I'm in the residential districts, standing knee-deep in household runoff, reading the chemical signatures of dense habitation: soap, sweat, cooking oil, shed skin, medicine, small joys, large griefs. Evening finds me at the junction points, where waters meet and mingle or refuse to. 

The most interesting stories are in the refusal. Water has opinions. This district's flow will not mix with that one's, even when forced through the same channel. They run side by side, maintaining boundaries invisible to eyes but vivid to feet and tongue. Sometimes it's chemistry - incompatible mineral loads. Sometimes it's temperature - one source is deep-cold, another surface-warm. But sometimes it's history. Waters remember what has dissolved in them. Blood from the old riots. Toxins from failed industries. The particular sulfur of betrayal.

During flood season, we're emergency infrastructure. The city puts us on civic duty, stationing us at critical points to taste for danger. Is this surge clean runoff or has it broken through contamination barriers? Is that brown color just disturbed sediment or has something died upstream in massive quantities? We stand in the rising water, human sensors in a sensory network, calling out our readings to emergency coordinators who trust our tongues more than their machines.

I've saved neighborhoods by tasting poison before it reached population. I've also been wrong. The Year of Silent Acid, I misread industrial sweetness for food-district overflow. Thirty-seven people lost their feet before we understood. I offered to have my own feet flayed in recompense. The flood court refused. They needed me functional. The scars still ache when barometric pressure drops.

Channel-readers don't retire. We accumulate. Our bodies become libraries of water-memory. Our blood grows thick with mineral knowledge. Eventually we'll be too heavy with accumulated tastes to leave the water at all. When that happens, the city grants us the Submergence Right. We choose a channel, usually one we've read for decades. We lie down in it. We open our mouths. We let the water read us in return.

They say our bodies don't decay like normal corpses. We crystallize. Mineral by mineral, we become part of the channel bed. Future readers will stand on us, learning from the pressure against their feet. The water that passes over us will carry some fragment of what we knew. In this way, the knowledge continues.

I'm forty-three years old. My feet are maps of scars. My blood tastes like copper even when I haven't been in copper-high water. I dream in chemical gradients. When I wake, I put on my weighted belt, check the drainage schedule, and go to stand in the city's veins.

Today the water tastes of distant rain, warm stone, ten thousand metabolisms, and something new - a sweetness I haven't encountered before. Not the sweetness of contamination or decay. Something else. I stand in the Ninth Meridian Channel, ankle-deep, trying to parse it. The other readers haven't mentioned it. Perhaps it's local. Perhaps it's nothing.

But my feet know better. Somewhere in this mineral city, something unprecedented is beginning. A new process, a new presence. The water carries its whisper but not yet its name.

I close my eyes. I let the current speak against my skin. I open my mouth and let the city tell me its newest secret, drop by ancient drop.

Tomorrow I will stand here again. The day after, again. Reading boundaries, tasting changes, my feet writing their story in scars while the city writes its story in water. We are all part of the circulation. We are all learning to breathe.
