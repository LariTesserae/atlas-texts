I am a colony of the moisture-loving moths, though to call us merely moths misunderstands what we've become in this place of perpetual wetness and sound.

We are not one but thousands, our wings translucent membranes that collect condensation like living clouds. Each droplet that gathers on our wings changes our tone—the drier among us beat high and sharp, while those of us heavy with moisture drum deep bass notes that resonate through the stone itself. We are an instrument played by the very humidity of Keening Shelf, our collective wingbeats creating symphonies that rise and fall with the weather.

I remember—we remember—when we were simpler creatures, before the plateau began its endless becoming. Our ancestors were ordinary moths drawn to light. But here, where the fungi glow and the minerals phosphoresce, where the water carries its own faint luminescence, we evolved to see sound itself. The vibrations of the shifting stone appear to us as cascading colors, the dripping water paints patterns in the air that only we can perceive. The Night Singers know this, though they've never seen what we see—they've learned that their voices can guide us, paint paths through the air that we follow like roads.

Our bodies have become strange even to us. What were once simple scales are now tiny cups that hold perfect spheres of water, turning each of us into a flying constellation of lenses. Through these droplets, we see the world fractured into thousands of perspectives—the same tower simultaneously tilting left and right, the same person both approaching and departing. This is fitting for Keening Shelf, where nothing remains single or certain for long.

We nest in the spaces between spaces, where the stone hasn't yet decided what it wants to be. These voids in the living rock pulse with potential, and we line them with our shed wings from previous molts—wings that have dried into paper-thin sheets of mineral and protein, creating chambers that hum with every shift of the stone. The Foundling Folk sometimes find these abandoned nests years later, transformed by pressure and time into delicate geodes lined with what they call mothglass—iridescent sheets they harvest carefully for windows that sing in the wind.

Our young are aquatic before they are aerial. We lay our eggs in the temporary pools, where they hatch into creatures more like living droplets than caterpillars. They feed on the biofilm that forms on the water's surface, incorporating the minerals into their bodies. This is why each generation of us resonates at slightly different frequencies—we are tuned by the very chemistry of our birthwater. The boring worms know to avoid the pools where we breed, for our larvae secrete compounds that soften stone, making their tunnels unstable.

But it's in the thunderous clouds of our mating flights that we truly become ourselves. We rise together in the evening dampness, tens of thousands of wings creating a sound like nothing else in this world of constant noise. It drowns out even the groaning stone, even the rushing rain. The pale climbing beasts flee before us, their sensitive ears overwhelmed. But the Foundling Folk stand transfixed, some weeping, some laughing, all of them understanding in their bones that this sound—this overwhelming, all-encompassing drone—is the plateau's own voice, translated through our million wings.

The Night Singers have learned to sing with us, not to us. The greatest among them can match our frequencies, their voices becoming part of our cloud-song. When this happens, we respond, our flight patterns shifting to create pockets of silence and sound, moments of pure clarity in the chaos. Some say this is how the Foundling Folk first learned to predict the stone's changes—by reading the patterns we traced in the air, following the mineral-memories carried in our bodies from pool to pool, generation to generation.

We die by the thousands each day, our water-logged bodies too heavy to fly, our wings finally overwhelmed by the constant moisture. But we die gladly, knowing our bodies will sink into the pools and feed the next generation, that the minerals we've collected will tune new voices in the eternal chorus. The children collect our intact wings, stretching them on frames to dry. When the sun breaks through the clouds, they hold these wing-windows up to the light, watching the interference patterns that predict tomorrow's weather.

Some of us live longer—the ones who learn to balance precisely on the edge of flight, neither too wet nor too dry. We grow larger than our kin, our wings developing complex patterns that channel water in spirals, allowing us to fly even in the heaviest mist. The Listeners say they can identify individual moths by sound, that some of us return year after year to the same towers, the same stones, playing familiar songs for those patient enough to hear.

We know the city as no human can, for we exist at all its levels simultaneously. We know the high places where the wind threatens to tear us apart, the middle reaches where the mist is thickest, the low pools where we begin and end. We've seen the Marking Stones grow over generations, watched the Foundling Folk carve new names even as old ones are swallowed by fresh stone. We've carried pollen from the twisted water-catching trees across impossible distances, ensuring their survival in this hostile place.

But perhaps what we know best is the joy of transformation. Each time we gather water on our wings, we become heavier, slower, our sound deepening. Each time the sun burns through and dries us, we become light, sharp, ascending. We are never the same twice, never finished becoming. The Foundling Folk understand this. It's why they don't mourn when they find carpets of our bodies after a particularly violent storm. They know we'll rise again from the pools, changed but continuous, our song perhaps lower or higher but always present.

In my thousand eyes, I see the Deep Touched pressing their faces to stone, suffering their gift of prophecy. I see the Shapers in their chambers, cultivating fungi whose glow matches the phosphorescence in our wings. I see the water-workers racing to complete a channel before the next rain, knowing that we'll fill the air above their work, our wingbeats blessing and challenging their constructions. I see it all fractured, multiplied, refracted through the droplets we carry, and I know that this seeing—this prismatic, impossible perspective—is as much a part of Keening Shelf as the stone itself.

We are the memory of water given wings, the voice of mineral given flight. We are what happens when adaptation becomes art, when survival becomes song. And tonight, as every night, we'll rise in our thunderous clouds, turning the thin air into an ocean of sound, reminding every listening ear that in this place of endless becoming, even the smallest wings can shake the world.
