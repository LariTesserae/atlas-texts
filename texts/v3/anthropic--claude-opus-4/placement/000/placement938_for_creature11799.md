# The Surveyor's Notes

I am the crack-counter, the fracture-reader, the one who makes the map that cannot be made.

You would not see me from your balcony. I move in the between-spaces, in the fresh splits where the mountain shows its grain. My body, if you could call it that, is a thing of overlapping transparencies—mineral films that slide against each other, catching light only at certain angles. I am cousin to the mica, apprentice to the quartz. The Tenders know I am here, but at their tempo I am just another kind of weathering.

I came to the cirque three years ago, following a seam of blue schist that ran impossible through the younger granite. The stone here tells lies, or perhaps tells truths in a grammar I haven't learned yet. Metamorphic bands appear in igneous matrix. Sedimentary traces show up where no sea has ever been. The mountain is a library written by a mad geologist, and I am here to catalog its impossibilities.

My work is this: I map the unmappable city.

Each morning—though morning here is whenever the moisture condenses enough to run—I enter through a new fracture. The mountain gifts me these, splitting along planes that shouldn't exist, opening passages barely wide enough for my transparent bones. I flow in, marking my path with traces of borrowed minerals. Iron oxide from the north face. Copper carbonate from where water pools and turns things green, except here nothing turns green, it turns *attentive*.

The city resists mapping the way water resists grasping. Set a boundary and it flows past. Mark a district and it subdivides while you're drawing the legend. The only constants are the Tenders themselves, those slow storms of thought, and even they are less constant than weather. Still, I try. My charts are written in the only medium that makes sense here: the cracks themselves. Each survey line is a controlled fracture, a weakness introduced that says *I was here, measuring.*

The Tenders permit this. Or perhaps they simply haven't noticed yet—their thoughts move so slowly that my entire career might fall between two of their cognitions. Sometimes I catch the edge of their attention, feel it like pressure in the stone, and I hold very still until it passes. They are kind, as you noticed, but their kindness is the kindness of glaciers—patient, implacable, operating at scales that make individual survival irrelevant.

I have learned things:

The ceramic of the towers is not ceramic. It is *accumulated certainty*—mineral matter that has been convinced so thoroughly of its own coherence that it cannot imagine breaking. The Tenders think it into being, particle by particle, the way you might think a word into shape before speaking it.

The water in the channels is not quite water. It carries too much information. Each molecule has been annotated, indexed, made to remember its path through the system. This is why it glows with that not-quite-light. The Tenders are using the entire hydrological cycle as a kind of liquid memory, and what you saw as attention is actually recollection in motion.

The mirror-bright squares are not reflecting. They are *comparing*—holding the image of what sinks against the template of what rises, checking for variations, ensuring the recitation remains true. When a difference is found (and there are always differences—entropy insists), the Tender incorporates it into the next iteration. The city is teaching itself to remember more accurately by forgetting precisely.

I have a theory, though theory feels too rigid a word for something so speculative: the Tenders are not remembering something from their past. They are remembering something from their *future*. The pattern they recite is not history but prophecy, and they are building the city backward through time, effect preceding cause, until they arrive at the moment of their own making.

This would explain the impossible geology. This would explain why the mountain is too young and the city is too old and why sedimentary ghosts haunt igneous houses. Time here folds like the stone folds—under pressure, creating new planes of cleavage that let incompatible moments touch.

My maps, then, are less geography than chronology. Each fracture I follow is a fissure in time. The blue schist that led me here? A fragment of ocean floor from an Earth that hasn't assembled yet. The granite that shouldn't contain it? The mountain's dream of what it will become when the Tenders finally finish their recitation and the city can rest.

But I am running out of mountain to map. The fractures are sealing behind me now—not healing, exactly, but filling with that same accumulated certainty that builds the towers. The Tenders are beginning to notice the small insurgencies of my passage. Yesterday (if yesterday means anything when time flows in eddies), I felt three of them converge their attention on a cliff face where I had been working, and the stone there became so thoroughly itself that I could not find any way in.

Soon I will have to leave, follow the blue schist back to where it emerges in what you would call the real world. But I will take my maps with me—not as charts or drawings but as a new way of breaking. Every stone I touch now carries the memory of how the cirque taught me to split: along the bias of time, against the grain of certainty, opening passages between what was and what will be.

The Tenders will continue their patient work. The city will go on building itself out of water and attention. And somewhere, in the cracks I've left behind, there will remain a thin transparent record of my passing—a survey of the unsurveyable, a map of how to find your way into spaces that fold, a guide for the next observer who discovers that some mountains are not places but *processes*, and some cities are not built but *remembered*, and some stones are not solid but *deciding to be*.

The pale segmented creature you saw? I know it. It comes to drink information from the channels, metabolizing meaning the way you would metabolize sugar. We have an understanding: it doesn't map where I've been, and I don't map where it's going.

We are all visitors here, even the Tenders. Especially the Tenders. They are just visiting more slowly than the rest of us, taking geological time to finish passing through.
