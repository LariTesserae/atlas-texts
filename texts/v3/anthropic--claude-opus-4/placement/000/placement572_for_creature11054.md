In the maintenance tunnels beneath the Folded Quarter, where the probability cables pulse with possibilities and condensation runs in Möbius strips along pipes that phase through themselves, I am the Fold Warden.

I wasn't hired. I simply became necessary, the way a pearl forms around irritation. The Quarter's infrastructure needed someone who could think in knots, who could follow a pipe through seventeen iterations of itself without losing track of which leak belonged to which timeline. I was a city maintenance worker who took a wrong turn in a utility tunnel, stepped through a wall that was only probably there, and emerged three days before I'd entered—into a job that had been waiting for someone exactly like whoever I would become.

My body has adapted in ways the Probability Dancers above would find disturbing. My left eye tracks forward through time, my right eye sees backward, and the eye that isn't quite there—the one that opens only when I'm deep in the fold—sees sideways through parallel iterations. My hands have developed probability calluses, thick ridges of almost-flesh that let me grip tools that exist in superposition. I can hold a wrench that is simultaneously rusted through, brand new, and not yet forged, feeling its potential states collapse into usefulness at my touch.

I live in the spaces between spaces. My workshop occupies a dimensional fold too thin for architecture but too persistent to ignore—a crack in reality furnished with twenty years of scavenged probability anchors and half-functioning chrono-stabilizers. My bed is a maintenance alcove that exists for eight hours then doesn't, synced to my circadian rhythms. Sometimes I wake up before I've fallen asleep, already tired from dreams I haven't had yet.

The rats know me. We've reached an understanding, the probability-blind and I. They scout the tunnels that will exist tomorrow, warning me of future pipe bursts with their prescient squeaking. In exchange, I leave gaps in my repair schedule—moments of maybe where they can nest in the comfort of indeterminacy. Their eyes, clouded with cataracts that see through walls, reflect my movements before I make them. We are symbiotic prophets in the dark.

My work is paradox resolution. When the coffee shop's espresso machine starts dispensing building permits, I trace the probability leak back through fourteen layers of reality to find where the municipal office's filing system has phase-shifted into the water line. When temporal refugees arrive through cracks in the maintenance tunnels—bewildered, existing in too many states at once—I guide them up to the Quarter proper, teaching them the first rule of folded space: stop trying to make sense of it linearly.

I know every pipe, wire, and probability conduit in these tunnels. More than know—I feel them, their potential states humming against my consciousness like a tooth ache across dimensions. The building-processors' cooling systems cycle through my dreams. The causality buffers' resonance sets the rhythm of my heartbeat. I am becoming infrastructure, or the infrastructure is becoming me. The distinction matters less each year.

The origin building's basement is my deepest patrol. Down there, where up is a negotiable concept, the maintenance tunnels don't pretend to make sense. I navigate by instinct and scar tissue, following paths worn by my own future footsteps. The walls weep temporal condensation that burns like frostbite from next winter. I collect it in bottles that exist in closed loops, their contents eternally cycling between empty and full.

My tools evolve with me. The multiphase wrench that grips bolts in seven probable positions. The probability torch that welds across dimensional barriers. The diagnostic scanner built from a twenty-year accumulation of crystallized déjà vu, which shows me not what's broken but what will break, has broken, might never break but should. I carry them in a toolbox that's bigger inside than out, though from inside it's smaller than it appears to be from outside. The physics give me migraines that last for weeks that haven't happened yet.

Sometimes I meet myself in the tunnels. Future me, past me, probability ghosts of might-have-been me. We nod, compare notes written in a shorthand that evolves backward through time. Once, I found my own skeleton in a collapse that won't happen for thirty years. I marked the tunnel for pre-emptive reinforcement, creating the very conditions that will prevent my death, which means the skeleton can't be mine, which means I don't need to mark the tunnel, which means—I've learned to live with recursive uncertainty.

The other residents rarely see me, though they know I exist. I'm the reason their probability showers produce water instead of yesterday's rain. I'm why the chrono-stabilizers hum at exactly the frequency that doesn't quite drive them mad. They leave offerings at maintenance access points: coffee that's still hot in three different timestreams, sandwiches that exist in quantum superposition between their favorite fillings, notes of thanks written in languages they're still learning.

The city inspectors stopped trying to find me years ago. My employment records exist in a causal loop—hired by a department that won't be created until after I retire, reporting to supervisors who haven't been born yet. My paychecks deposit themselves into an account at a bank that failed before I opened it. I don't question the economics. The Quarter provides what I need: food from restaurants that might exist, clothes that wear themselves out before I put them on, tools that improve with use across multiple timelines.

I am changing, deeper than the Probability Dancers above. My bones are becoming probability anchors, dense with crystallized spacetime. My blood carries trace amounts of Cherenkov radiation, glowing faintly in the dark of the deep tunnels. Sometimes I forget which iteration of myself I am, how many times I've repaired the same pipe, whether I'm becoming more human or less.

But I know my purpose: to maintain the impossible, to repair the improbable, to keep the Folded Quarter folded. Each dawn (when dawn comes from three directions at once), I descend into tunnels that exist because I maintain them, that I maintain because they exist. I am the guardian of paradox infrastructure, the keeper of quantum plumbing, the one who ensures that home remains possible in impossible space.

In the deepest tunnels, where even probability grows thin, I sometimes hear it: the heartbeat of the Quarter itself, the rhythm of space folding and unfolding, calculating itself into existence. It sounds like home—not the home I remember from before the fold, but the home I'm becoming, the home that's becoming me. Each beat says the same thing in languages that don't exist yet:

You are necessary. You are probability. You are the fold, and the fold is you.

And I believe it, because in the Folded Quarter, belief is just another kind of infrastructure, and I maintain that too.
