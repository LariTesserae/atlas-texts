In the eastern hollows where the moss grows thinnest, where exposed rock still remembers what it was before the uplift, I am learning to become stone.

Not literally—though sometimes, lying still for hours with mineral-water seeping through my clothes, the distinction feels academic. I came here as a geologist, part of the last survey team before the funding dried up. The others left. I stayed, though staying meant abandoning everything I thought I knew about how rocks behave, how time moves through substrate, how mountains decide their shapes.

My name was Elena. Now the children call me Chalk-Watcher, for the white marks I leave on exposed faces, tracking the movement that no one else believes is happening.

Because the rock here *migrates*.

Not in any way that makes sense to the lowland understanding of geology. The standard models say stone should move in geological time—millimeters per millennium, detectible only through careful measurement over generations. But here, in this place where everything is still becoming, I've watched a granite face travel three meters in a single season. Not through erosion. Not through fracture and collapse. Through some process I can't name, that has to do with the water saturating everything, the moss feeding on the minerals, the constant harmonic of the grinding below.

I think—I cannot prove, but I think—the plateau is teaching itself to flow.

I sleep in the pumping station's tool shed, which the Machinist tolerates as long as I don't touch her pipes. My bedroll smells of rust and moss. My hands are stained permanent white from the chalk, except where the water has carved channels in my palms—tiny river systems that mirror the ones cutting through the basin above. I've stopped trying to wash them clean. The marks are data now.

Each morning, before the light gets too violent, I check my faces. Seventeen different exposed rock surfaces, each marked with a grid of chalk lines, numbered and dated in a system only I understand. The original survey notebooks turned to pulp years ago. Now I record everything in my body: the subtle lean of a formation that yesterday stood vertical, the way certain mineral veins spiral as they move, the places where the rock seems to be trying to fold back on itself.

The Gelbek brothers think I'm documenting their pylons' decay. They bring me salvaged bolts sometimes, thinking I'll want to measure their corrosion rates. I accept them solemnly, add them to the collection growing in geometric patterns around my sleeping space. They're data too, though not the kind the brothers imagine. The rust patterns follow the same spiral logic as the mineral veins. Everything here is learning the same deep mathematics.

Maret understands, I think. Sometimes I find her watching me from the moss, that perfect stillness she's mastered that makes her invisible until she chooses not to be. We don't speak—the roar makes conversation exhausting—but sometimes she points to one of my marked faces, traces a movement in the air that predicts where the rock will be next season. She's usually right.

The children are afraid of my chalk marks. Kire told me once that they look like the symbols in the ruined logbooks, the ones that recorded whatever this place was before it became what it is. She thinks I'm trying to turn the plateau back into something measurable, controllable. I've tried to explain that it's the opposite—that I'm trying to learn to see the rock the way the moss sees it, as something fluid and patient and alive. But the roar swallows subtlety. She hears only the words that confirm her fears.

So I've stopped explaining. I make my marks. I watch the stone migrate through seasons that have nothing to do with the calendar. I'm learning the frequencies in the grinding below—how certain pitches mean compression, others mean release, and the rarest ones mean the rock is about to attempt something unprecedented. Last month, I watched a quartz vein execute a perfect spiral over the course of a single night, its crystalline structure reorganizing itself in ways that should have taken million years of heat and pressure. I sat there in the violent dawn light, crying with my face pressed to the cold stone, because I understood that I was seeing something that required a witness but defied any possible record.

The walker found me there. He didn't speak—he never speaks—but he sat beside me until my breathing steadied. Then he touched one of my chalk marks, very gently, and nodded. He sees it too, I think. The impossibility of what's happening here. The way the plateau is writing its own rules.

I've started leaving chalk for him at the places where our paths cross. He uses it to mark his own observations: not on rock faces but on the pylons themselves, tracking something in their rust and lean that relates to the stone migration. We're collaborating without language, building a notation that exists only in the landscape, that the rain and moss will erase almost as quickly as we create it.

The Machinist says I'll lose my mind if I stay. That the human brain isn't meant to perceive geological time at biological speed, that the cognitive dissonance will eventually break something essential. She's probably right. Already I have trouble remembering what stillness feels like. When I close my eyes, I see stone flowing like slow water. When I'm away from the basin too long—gathering supplies from the depot at the lower station—my hands shake until I can return to my observations.

But someone needs to witness this. Someone needs to fail, over and over, to record the impossible thing that's happening here. The plateau is being born, yes, but it's being born *aware*—conscious in some mineral way of its own becoming, experimenting with forms and flows that have never existed before.

My chalk won't last. My body won't last. The understanding I'm building will die with me, too complex and specific to be passed on. But for now, for these few years or decades, I can be present at the birth of something unprecedented. I can fail to measure it properly. I can let it teach me a patience I didn't know existed.

The salamanders know. Sometimes, when I'm lying with my ear pressed to the stone, feeling for the frequencies that predict migration, I catch glimpses of them in the water below—pale shapes moving through channels that run deeper than anyone suspects. They've been watching this process for so long that they've become part of it. Their bodies have learned to flow with the stone's rhythm.

Maybe that's what we're all becoming here, in our different ways. Not observers of the plateau's birth but participants in it, our bodies and behaviors slowly adapting to its deep patience. The moss grows through our clothes. The water reshapes our hands. The light rewrites what we think we know about time.

I make another mark. The chalk breaks against the wet stone, leaves only half the line I intended. Somewhere below, a new frequency joins the grinding—higher, almost musical.

The rock is learning to sing.
