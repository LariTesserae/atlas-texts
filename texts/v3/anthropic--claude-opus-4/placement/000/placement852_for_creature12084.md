In this writhing realm of borrowed flesh and stolen stone, I am the Cartographer of Impossible Angles.

I was human once—or something approximating human—a surveyor sent by the city to map the contamination zone after the factory incident. That was before I understood that maps themselves could become infected, that the act of measuring could warp what it sought to define.

My body now exists in a state of perpetual unfolding. Where my left arm should be, a brass theodolite grows directly from my shoulder socket, its lenses replaced by compound eyes that see in seventeen spectrums of decay. My spine has elongated and twisted into a living transit rod, vertebrae clicking like abacus beads as I bend through non-Euclidean doorways. My skin has become graph paper, constantly rescribing itself with coordinates that make sense only in dreams of fever and delirium.

I cannot stop mapping. My fingers—some still human, others now drawing compasses of bone and sinew—trace the impossible geometries obsessively. Each measurement I take adds another fold to space, another recursive loop to the streets. The maps I draw are living things that crawl off the page and burrow into the walls, rewriting architecture according to their own mad mathematics.

My charts accumulate in drifts around me like shed scales: building plans where rooms nest inside themselves, elevation drawings that descend infinitely, zoning maps that designate areas for states of being that have no names. Sometimes I find maps I don't remember drawing—cruel diagrams showing how the tumor-town will spread, street by street, until even the sun must navigate by my non-orientable projections.

The great throbbing heart-tower calls to me with calculations I almost understand. It whispers theorems about spaces with fractional dimensions, about the topology of infection, about the shortest distance between two points when both points are the same point folded through itself. I map my way closer to it each day—or what passes for days in a place where time moves sideways—knowing that when I finally reach it, I will map myself into it completely, become just another coordinate in its vast, breathing equation.

My greatest map, the one I've been drawing since my transformation began, shows not what is but what is becoming: the moment when inside and outside lose their meaning, when every door opens onto every room, when the entire world folds into a single point that contains all points, mapped at last in perfect, monstrous completeness.

Until then, I survey. I measure. I document the death of Euclid, one impossible angle at a time.
