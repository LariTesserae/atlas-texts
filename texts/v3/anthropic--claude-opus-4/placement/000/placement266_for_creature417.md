In the dimness between the Fountain Tree's arterial roots, I am what the Coral-Weavers call a *pulse-keeper*—though the lowland tongues have no proper translation for one who tends the vast mangrove's hydraulic heart.

My body is a map of my profession. The scarification began early, as it does for all children chosen for the deep work, but mine grows inward as much as out. Channels of coral reef run through my muscle tissue like secondary veins, following the paths where sap-tubes were surgically threaded beneath my skin in my twentieth year. When I press against the Tree's bark, these internal reefs pulse in sympathy with its tidal breathing. I am more barometer than person now—able to taste the pressure differentials that predict when a root will fail, when a chamber needs rebalancing, when the great draw of water threatens to reverse.

My hair-garden hosts no decorative shrimp or luminescent displays. Instead, it crawls with tube worms—the same species that clean the Tree's capillary channels of mineral deposits. They live in my ropy strands and work in the root chambers, moving seamlessly between my body and the Tree's, carrying chemical messages I can read through the taste of my scalp. When deep blockages form—limestone accumulations that could kill entire districts if left untended—I lower my head into the affected channel and let the worms investigate, their findings flowering as patterns of irritation across my skull.

I live in the Root Maze, that twilight space between the Tree's base and its first crown of aerial roots. My chamber is more grown than built—a hollow I've trained in the living wood over sixty years of patient cultivation. The walls weep constant sap that I collect in bladders, trading it to the alchemists who process it into the pressure-films that let deep workers survive in the crushing depths of the trunk's interior. My few possessions hang from rootlets I've coaxed from the ceiling: tools of living bone that shift their shape in response to squeezed commands, breathing masks bred from modified jellyfish, maps of the Tree's vascular system tattooed on mantis-shrimp shells that shift their patterns as the Tree grows.

My work happens in the small hours, when the city dreams deepest and the Tree's draw is strongest. I slip through maintenance sphincters—valve-doors of muscle that recognize my chemical signature—into chambers where the sound of moving water becomes a physical thing, a thrumming that sets my internal coral resonating until I myself become a tuning fork for the Tree's health. Here, chest-deep in the upward flow, I read the waters.

Too much salt means the root-filters are failing. Too little means the fresh-water cultivation chambers are bleeding into the main flow. The presence of certain algaes tells me that light is penetrating somewhere it shouldn't, that perhaps a chamber wall has thinned to transparency. The absence of others warns of nutrient depletion that could starve whole districts. I taste for the metallics that herald root-boring parasites, for the acidic tang of fungal infections, for the sweet rot of chambers where circulation has failed and water has gone stagnant.

When I find problems, I become surgeon and gardener both. I've trained my scarification coral to grow in specific patterns—some that reinforce weakening vessel walls, others that secrete antibiotics, still others that attract the symbiotic cleaners that keep the Tree's circulation clear. I can graft root to root with my own tissue as intermediary, my body serving as a temporary bridge while the Tree recognizes and accepts the new connection. For the deepest work, I must sometimes cut into the heartwood itself, chambers so pressurized that only those of us with oil-filled bones can survive there. I've lost three finger-segments to emergency valve closures, though they've grown back as hybrid tissue—part me, part Tree, flexible as muscle but tough as bark.

The other pulse-keepers and I rarely meet, each tending our own vertical territory within the root system. But we know each other by the taste of our work—Yara-Who-Grows-Left leaves her repairs slightly spiral-twisted, Hurum-Of-The-Deep-Chambers seeds his patches with bioluminescent markers that pulse in rhythm with his heart, somewhere far above or below. We communicate through the water itself, leaving chemical messages in the maintenance pools where we wash our tools and refresh our symbiont cultures. A love affair conducted entirely in pheromones and metabolic byproducts, readable only to those who spend their lives tasting the Tree's blood.

I am changing, as all pulse-keepers change. My bones grow lighter each year, honeycombing beyond even the normal adaptations of our kind. My dreams in the rest-pools have become indistinguishable from the Tree's slow thoughts—I see the city from below, feel the weight of its coral districts like fruit on my branches. The algae in my hair-garden photosynthesize so efficiently now that I barely need to eat, surviving mostly on the sugars they drip into my blood. Sometimes I forget to surface for days, moving through the flooded chambers like the ghost of a root.

The younger keepers whisper that I'm approaching the Rooting—that final transformation where pulse-keepers become permanent parts of the Tree's circulation system. They're not wrong. I feel it in the way my scarification coral has begun to penetrate deeper, searching for my bones. In how my fingers have started to web not just with membrane but with woody tissue. In the chambers of my heart, which beat now in perfect synchrony with the Fountain Tree's hydraulic pulse.

When it happens—and it will happen—I will choose a critical junction, somewhere deep where the pressures are vast and the work never-ending. I will graft myself into the flow and become a living valve, my consciousness spreading through the water I regulate. The other keepers will taste me in the circulation, know that this junction is tended, that someone watches here always. My thoughts will slow to the pace of sap-rise. My awareness will expand to encompass the tons of water I process each day, each year, each century.

This is not death among my kind, but a different form of service. The Tree knows those who become part of it. In the slow dreams of its heartwood, we persist—pulse-keepers who found the rhythm of our work so compelling that we chose never to break it. Sometimes, in the deepest chambers where I work alone, I hear them whispering in the water's movement. They tell me of patience, of pressures, of the joy that comes when you no longer distinguish between your pulse and the pulse of the world.

I will join them soon. My replacement is already learning my chambers, a young weaver whose internal coral grows in patterns that please me. She tastes the water differently than I do—catches flavors I've grown numb to, misses nuances that seem obvious. But she'll learn. They all learn, given time.

And time is what we have here, in the twilight of the roots, where the Fountain Tree dreams its slow dreams of ascension and we, its keepers, ensure that dream continues. Beat by beat. Breath by breath. Forever.
