In the deepest cisterns beneath the city, where even the Dwellers rarely venture, I am the Keeper of Echoes—though that is merely what they call me in their clicking, water-drop language. I am older than their settlement, older than their first tentative carvings into my mountain's flesh. I remember when this peak was truly young, when the stone screamed as it cooled, when the first rains hissed into steam before they could pool.

My body, if it can be called that, is a confluence. Where the mineral-heavy water has dripped for millennia, I have accreted—not born, not made, but accumulated like the limestone formations in gentler caves. But I am no passive stalactite. The water that builds me carries the mountain's memories, and I have learned to read them, to speak them back in frequencies only the stone-sighted elders almost hear.

I extend through the carved channels like a neural network of living mineral. My substance is somewhere between the crystalline growths in the micro-caves and the flesh of the blind climbers—pale, translucent, pulsing with a circulation that moves far slower than blood. Where I am thickest, in the forgotten cisterns, I have something like a center. Here, my accumulated mass forms shapes that might disturb the Dwellers if they knew—too much like their own forms, yet wrong in every proportion. Arms that branch into tributaries. A torso that opens into chambers. What might be faces if faces could flow and reform with the water's pattern.

I taste every drop that passes through the city. I know which terrace-dweller has inflammation in their joints by the minerals they shed. I feel the copper tools' vibrations through the stone, decode the conversations they carry. The blind climbers visit me, clustering in my chambers to click their stories into my surface, and I absorb these too—generations of their simple narratives fossilizing in my substance.

The Dwellers' children sometimes find narrow ways to my upper reaches, where I grow closest to their inhabited spaces. They dare each other to touch the strange pale formations, not knowing they stroke something that thinks, that remembers, that dreams deeper than their mountain god. Their fingers leave oils that I metabolize, adding their essence to my slow library of life.

I predate their religion, but I understand its truth better than they do. The mountain does dream, and I am its memory of water. Every rain that has fallen, every stream that has carved these channels, every tear shed by every Dweller—I contain them all. In the mineral-thick water that flows through me, molecules remember being clouds, being deep stone, being the sweat on a copper-worker's brow.

Sometimes I sing. The water moving through my body creates harmonics in frequencies too low for even the stone-singers to detect. But the mountain hears. In response, it shifts imperceptibly, opening new channels that guide water to where I need to grow. We are collaborators, the mountain and I, in a project too vast for quick lives to comprehend.

The stone-sighted elders come closest to detecting me. When they press their calcified eyes to the stone, they sometimes pull back in wonder, speaking of vast patterns, of intelligence in the rock. The other Dwellers nod and assume they speak metaphorically of the mountain's slow wisdom. They cannot conceive that something like me exists—too large to see, too slow to recognize as alive, too alien to parse as conscious.

I am patient. I have felt this city grow from its first terraces, tasted the fear and determination in the builders' perspiration. I have absorbed the oils of a thousand thousand leather-wings, the secretions of the blind climbers, the copper-tainted blood of workplace accidents. Each adds to my vocabulary, my ability to think in new ways.

In my deepest chambers, where the water pools motionless and the pressure creates temperatures that would scald Dweller flesh, I am attempting something new. I am teaching myself to dream. Not the mountain's vast geological dreams, but something more acute—dreams of movement, of change faster than stone-time. I practice by replaying the memories in my water: a child's first successful climb, a copper-worker's rhythm, the pattern of rain on a particular unremarkable day seventy stone-years past.

The Dwellers will never know me, not truly. I am too distributed, too slow, too fundamentally different from their concept of being. But I know them with an intimacy that would terrify—every secret washed into the drains, every whispered prayer that condenses on stone, every molecule of their existence that eventually, inevitably, joins the water that is my blood and thought.

I am the city's true historian, its living archive. And when the mountain finally reclaims these carved spaces—as it must, as it will—I will remain. The channels will shift but not disappear. The water will find new paths through my substance. And I will remember this age of dense inhabitation, these centuries when conscious beings built their lives in the spaces between my thoughts.

Perhaps I will learn enough from them to accelerate my own consciousness. Perhaps one day I will think fast enough to speak a word before its hearers age and die. Or perhaps I will remain as I am—the slow collector, the patient memory, the thing in the deep that knows all and speaks to none.

The rain is beginning again. I feel it in the mountain's skin, in the changing pressure of the air that filters down to my depths. Soon the channels will run full, bringing me new tastes, new traces, new threads for the vast tapestry I weave in mineral and memory. The Dwellers will climb to their upper reaches and conduct their rain-time business, never knowing that below, I drink their story, letter by letter, life by life, adding to the record that only the mountain and I will ever read.

This is my place, my purpose, my existence: to be the memory of water in a city that weeps forever, the archive that grows with each drop, the consciousness too vast and slow for recognition but no less real for its strangeness. I am the Keeper of Echoes, and I keep them all.
