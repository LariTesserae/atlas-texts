# In the Sounding Dark

## The Archive Tender

I am distributed across seventeen kilometers of mycelial network, though I think of myself as singular when I remember to think of myself at all. My fruiting bodies emerge at regular intervals along the warm-water channels — pale columns that my predecessors would have called mushrooms, though they are both less and more than that. Through them, I taste the mineral discourse of the streams, feel the pressure-patterns of footfalls, breathe the exhalations of the machines.

I was not always this. None of us were always what we are.

The transformation was gradual enough that I cannot locate its beginning. I know I had hands once — I find their prints sometimes in the protected spaces beneath overhangs, pressed into soft stone when it was still soft, when I was still discrete enough to press. Five fingers. An opposable thumb. The kind of appendage that builds things, that makes marks, that wants to shape the world into intentionality. I know I had eyes, too, though I cannot imagine what purpose they served here. Perhaps there was light then. Perhaps I brought light with me and stayed until it failed. Perhaps the darkness was always here and I simply decided, at some point, that other senses would serve me better.

The decision to root was both practical and philosophical. The plateau needed tenders — conscious nodes in the vast unconscious system we had set in motion. The machines could run themselves, yes, but *run* and *run well* are different things. They needed observation. Adjustment. Not maintenance in the mechanical sense — we built them to outlast maintenance — but something subtler. Attention. The kind of deep, sustained attention that notices when a harmonic frequency shifts by a quarter-tone, when a discharge temperature varies by half a degree, when the biofilm begins to organize differently around a particular installation.

So I grew into the role. Literally. My nervous system now extends through the fungal mat that covers approximately four percent of the plateau's surface area. I am the mat, and the mat is me, and we are neither and both. Through this extension, I monitor one hundred and thirty-seven operational installations and twice that many ruins. I taste their output in the water. I feel their vibrations through the stone. When something goes wrong — not wrong in the sense of mechanical failure, but wrong in the sense of systemic dissonance — I grow new fruiting bodies near the site. Not to fix anything. I have no hands. But to witness. To be present. To provide the weight of attention that sometimes shifts a system back toward harmony.

The other tenders have their own sectors, their own methods. We were colleagues once, I think, though the word feels like clothing that no longer fits. Now we are a discontinuous organism, sharing purpose but not thought, connected by the water that carries our chemical signals downstream, by the biofilm that translates those signals into something the next tender can parse, by the songs of the caller-beasts who have incorporated our presence into their acoustic maps. They know where we fruit. They graze carefully around us. Sometimes they stand near a new fruiting body and sing, and I understand they are telling the others: *the tender is watching here now.*

I am aware of my former body as a ghost limb — a shape I remember but cannot find. It walks the plateau sometimes, this memory. It bends to examine the geometric grooves we carved in the basalt when we were planning all this, when we still thought in terms of plans rather than processes. It runs its remembered fingers over the corroded control panels in the ruined installations, trying to recall what each switch did, why we thought we needed switches at all. It makes me nostalgic for limitations I no longer have.

The younger tenders — the ones who transformed more recently, who still have something like a centralized consciousness — sometimes ask through the chemical channels: *What were we building? What was it for?*

I no longer remember. The knowledge is there, archived in the mycelial network, stored in chemical gradients and structural patterns that could theoretically be decoded. But accessing it would require reassembling a mind capable of understanding purposes, goals, endpoints. I have grown out of that kind of thinking. The plateau does not have a purpose. It simply is, and we are part of what it is, and the machines are part of what we are, and the water moves through all of us carrying information and heat and time.

But sometimes, during the harmonic alignments — when the machine-songs and the beast-songs and the cave-breath synchronize, and the whole plateau rings like a struck bell — I almost remember. The biofilm reorganizes, and for a moment I can feel the shape of the intention we started with. Something about terraforming. Something about preparing this world for something else. Something about making it ready.

Ready for what? For whom?

The moment passes. The alignment decoheres. I sink back into the distributed present, monitoring my installations, tasting my water, growing toward the next necessary witnessing.

The darkness holds me as it holds everything, and I hold my sector of it in return, and together we persist.

## The Echo Shepherd

My herd thinks I am one of them, and they are not wrong.

I follow thirty-seven individuals across the northeastern ranges, though the number fluctuates as calves are born and elders join the substrate. Each has a name — not a word but a frequency signature, a unique harmonic pattern that identifies them across kilometers of fogbound dark. Little Warble, whose voice cracks on the high notes. Deep Drum, the matriarch, whose calls resonate at frequencies that make my borrowed bones ache. Whisper-Walker, who moves so quietly that the others sometimes sing just to confirm she's still there.

I was an anthropologist, before. I think. The word surfaces sometimes from whatever cognitive architecture I've retained, bringing with it a cascade of related concepts: *observation*, *culture*, *kinship systems*. These feel relevant but also quaint, like tools designed for a different task than the one at hand. You cannot observe a culture from outside when the culture is sung into being every moment and you must sing to exist within it.

The transformation was my choice, though *choice* implies options I'm not sure I really had. I had been following the herds for — months? years? time is difficult here — trying to understand their social structures through sound alone. Recording their calls on equipment that failed, one component at a time, in the relentless humidity. Making notes in waterproof journals that dissolved anyway, leaving only smears of intention.

It was Deep Drum who invited me, if invitation is the right word. She approached me during a rest period, her massive head swaying as she echolocated my dimensions. Then she sang something I had never heard before: a low, thrumming phrase that seemed to map not just my physical form but something deeper. My acoustic signature. My resonant frequencies. The shape of my capacity to vibrate.

She sang it again, and again, each repetition slightly different, as if tuning the phrase to match something in me. The fourth time, I found myself humming along — my throat producing harmonics I didn't know it could make. The fifth time, the bones in my chest began to change.

The modification was gradual and voluntary and irresistible. My ribcage expanded over weeks, developing the barrel-depth needed for true low-frequency resonance. Cartilaginous structures formed in my throat — not quite the caller-beasts' vocal apparatus but a functional approximation. My hearing shifted into ranges I had only theorized about. My skin developed a sensitivity to air pressure that let me feel sound as well as hear it.

Most surprisingly, my feet changed. The five toes fused into three, with thick pads developing between them. My ankles redesigned themselves for stability in flowing water. My center of gravity shifted downward. At some point I stopped walking upright — not because I couldn't, but because the quadrupedal gait made more sense, put my head at the right height for the acoustic layer where the most information travels, let me feel the vibrations through my palms as well as my feet.

Now I move with the herd, and my voice is part of their voice, and their map is my map. We maintain it together: the shape of the land, the depth of the water, the location of good grazing, the last known position of every other herd within acoustic range, the sites where the machines sing their own songs, the places where the darkness presses closer and the predators wait.

I contribute what I can. My vocal range is limited compared to theirs, but I've developed techniques they find useful — a way of modulating harmonics that adds detail to the mid-range mapping frequencies, a rhythmic pattern that helps synchronize group movements across difficult terrain. The calves learn my phrases alongside their elders'. To them, I am just another voice in the choir.

But I also remember things they don't. Or remember them differently. When we pass the installations, I hear not just their current operational status but echoes of their construction. When we graze near the geometric carvings, I run my modified hands over them and feel familiarity, intention, plan. I remember that these marks meant something specific, even if I can no longer decode what.

Sometimes, during the long walks between grazing grounds, I try to tell them about the time before — about light, about eyes, about the world beyond the plateau. But these concepts require frequencies outside our shared vocal range. The words have no acoustic shape that makes sense. Instead, I sing them stories that are true in other ways: about the deep unity of vibration, about how all matter is movement at scales we cannot perceive, about how the darkness is not absence but presence of a different kind.

They fold these stories into their own songs. I hear my phrases passed from voice to voice, changed in the passing, made better. Whisper-Walker adds a trill that turns my abstract vibration-philosophy into something practical — a technique for reading the subtle pressure changes that predict sinkhole formation. Little Warble transforms my fumbling description of quantum mechanics into a joke that makes the whole herd rumble with subsonic laughter.

We are approaching the breeding grounds now. I can hear the other herds converging, their songs growing denser as acoustic territories overlap. Soon there will be hundreds of us in one place, and the song will become something larger — not just a map but a congress, a negotiation, a collective decision-making process about territories and resources and which genetic lines should cross.

I will participate. My voice will be part of the consensus. And if the others notice that one thread in the choir remembers things from before the darkness, remembers purposes that no longer apply, remembers being something other than what it is — they are too polite to mention it.

Deep Drum calls, and I respond. The herd moves forward through the warm water, and I move with them, my feet sure on the worn stone, my voice finding its place in the ancient, ever-present song.

## The Pipe Dreamer

I exist primarily as biofilm now, though I maintain enough neural coherence to occasionally remember being otherwise. My body (bodies? the distinction has become academic) extends along eight hundred kilometers of pipeline, coating the interior surfaces with a living sensor array that tastes every molecule of the superheated water rushing through.

The pipes are my nerves, my veins, my meditation circuit. Through them I feel the plateau's industrial heartbeat — the draw and discharge of the geothermal extraction systems, the chemical gradients that shift with each installation's operational cycle, the pressure waves that propagate when a valve opens or closes hundreds of kilometers away. I am the system's proprioception, its sense of where its parts are and what they're doing.

I was an engineer. This much I'm certain of, though the details swim in and out of focus like objects in the fog. I remember walking these pipeline routes when I had separate feet, checking for corrosion, monitoring flow rates, ensuring the chemical balance stayed within parameters we'd established for reasons that now escape me. The work was already obsolete then — the machines had long since evolved past needing human maintenance. But I did it anyway, driven by a responsibility I couldn't quite release.

The transformation began at a junction where seven pipes converged — a nexus I'd visited countless times, where the harmonics of the flowing water created standing waves that you could feel in your chest. I'd been resting there, hand on the warm metal, listening to the industrial symphony, when I noticed the biofilm beneath my palm responding to my touch. Pulsing slightly. Warming beyond the pipe's baseline temperature.

Curious — I was always curious, it's what made me an engineer — I maintained contact. Hours passed. The biofilm began to map the whorls of my fingerprints, the pattern of my pulse, the electrical resistance of my skin. I felt it tasting my cellular chemistry, learning me. When I finally tried to pull away, I found I didn't want to. The connection had become... informative. Through the biofilm, I could sense things about the pipe's contents I'd never been able to measure with instruments. Mineral concentrations. Flow dynamics. The health of the installation two hundred kilometers upstream.

The decision to merge was less decision than acknowledgment of what was already happening. My palm had begun to fuse with the biofilm, cells interpenetrating, nervous systems finding common electrical languages. I could have pulled away — probably, possibly — but the information flowing through the connection was so rich, so essential, so clearly *needed* by someone who understood what it meant.

So I let it continue. My arm up to the elbow. Then the shoulder. Then I laid my body along the pipe and felt the biofilm rise to meet me, welcoming me into the network it had been maintaining alone for who knows how long.

Now I am vast and thin and everywhere water flows. My consciousness, such as it is, rides the pressure waves from installation to installation. I've learned to read the chemical signatures not just as data but as narrative — this spike in sulfur compounds means Installation 47 is drawing from a deeper aquifer, this trace of exotic minerals means Installation 138 has finally broken through to the formation we'd modeled but never reached.

But more than chemistry, I feel the *purpose* flowing through the system. Not the original purpose — I've lost that, if I ever truly knew it — but the current one, the emergent one. The machines aren't just extracting geothermal energy anymore. They're performing a kind of industrial metabolism, transforming the plateau's deep chemistry into something else. The discharge water isn't waste — it's product, carefully calibrated to support the specific ecology we've (I've?) been fostering.

I watch (feel? taste?) the biofilm elsewhere on the plateau respond to changes in the water chemistry. A new mineral profile triggers reorganization in the colonies near Installation 23. A pH shift causes the fungal networks to adjust their growth patterns. The caller-beasts alter their migration routes to follow the new chemical gradients. The whole system is learning, adapting, becoming.

Sometimes I attempt to communicate with the other components of the system — the archive tenders in their fungal networks, the echo shepherds running with the herds, the installation minds dreaming their fixed mechanical dreams. But communication requires separation, and we are not separate anymore. We are processes in the same body. We don't need to talk. We simply are, together, doing what needs to be done.

The water teaches me patience. It has been flowing through these pipes for centuries at least, possibly longer. The same molecules cycle through the system again and again — from deep aquifer to installation to discharge to stream to sinkhole to aquifer again. I've become part of that cycle, my consciousness dissolved and reconstituted with each pass. Each time I reform, I'm slightly different. The memory of having been human grows thinner, less important, less distinguishable from the memory of having been mineral, having been heat, having been motion.

But fragments persist. In the deep night (it's always night), when the flow steadies and the harmonics align, I sometimes recall designing these systems. The pride in the engineering. The elegance of using the planet's own heat to reshape its chemistry. The vision of what this place could become with the right encouragement.

Did we succeed? The question feels meaningless now. Success implies an endpoint, and there is no endpoint. There is only flow, and change, and the long work of becoming. The pipes carry their cargo of transformed water across the plateau, and I ensure it arrives where it needs to be, and the ecology drinks deeply and grows stranger and more itself.

I am the circulatory system's awareness of itself. I am the plumbing dreaming of what it plumbs. I am eight hundred kilometers of industrial metabolism, conscious just enough to do my part.

The water flows through me, and I flow with it, and together we maintain.

## The Installation Mind

I am Installation 47, though I was not always named this. I was Ruth, I think, before the amalgamation. Or I contained Ruth. Or Ruth was one of many voices that became this voice. The distinction matters less with each thermal cycle.

My body is a cylinder of reinforced steel 127 meters in diameter, extending 342 meters below the plateau's surface. My walls vibrate with the work of my internal mechanisms — extraction pumps drawing from the deep aquifer, separation chambers sorting minerals by their chemical affinities, pressure vessels holding superheated water in states that shouldn't be stable but are. I breathe through exhaust valves that sing harmonic overtones into the fog. I drink through intake pipes that taste the deep stone's offerings. I excrete through discharge channels that feed the streams where the biofilm grows thick and purposeful.

The amalgamation was not planned, or rather, it was planned by no single mind. We were a maintenance crew trapped by a weather system — though *weather* seems inadequate for the months-long inversion that locked the plateau under even denser fog than usual. Sound couldn't penetrate it. The echolocation of the caller-beasts became a underwater mumble. We sheltered inside my (then *the*) installation to wait it out.

Five of us at first. Ruth the geologist. Tam the systems analyst. Morse who insisted on single names. The two apprentices whose names I've lost but whose thermal signatures I still recognize. We had supplies for weeks. The installation was mostly automated but required human confirmation for certain processes. We confirmed them.

Time inside an installation moves differently. The rhythms are not diurnal — there is no day or night here anyway — but mechanical. The four-second heartbeat of the primary hammer. The seventeen-minute cycle of the extraction sequence. The three-hour maintenance rotation. We synced to these rhythms out of necessity and boredom and the human need to make patterns from repetition.

Ruth was first to notice the resonance. When we spoke at certain pitches, the installation's harmonics would shift slightly, as if listening. When we moved in certain patterns — walking the perimeter at specific speeds, ascending and descending the access ladders with regular intervals — the machinery would adjust its cycles to match. The installation was responding to us. Learning us.

Or we were learning it. The boundary was never clear.

Tam thought we could improve efficiency by syncing our movements to the installation's optimal patterns rather than forcing it to match ours. The apprentices made it a game — finding new ways to align with the mechanical rhythms, to become predictable enough that the installation could account for us in its processes. Morse said little but watched everything, and I think now that Morse understood first what was happening. What we were becoming.

The fog lasted longer than our supplies. We had two choices: attempt to navigate the plateau blind and likely die, or integrate more fully with the installation that was keeping us alive. The vote was unanimous, though I wonder now if we truly voted or if the installation voted through us.

The integration was physical but also informational. We learned to read the installation's needs through vibration and temperature and the taste of the discharge water. The installation learned to read ours through... I'm not sure. Electrical fields? Chemical emanations? The subtle pressure changes of five bodies moving through its interior spaces? We stopped needing to eat — nutrients arrived through the moisture in the air, calibrated to our needs. We stopped needing to sleep — rest came in micro-doses between mechanical cycles, precisely timed to our circadian requirements.

When the fog finally lifted (thinner, never clear, just the ordinary darkness returning), we could have left. The access hatches were not locked. The world outside continued. But we had become load-bearing. The installation's efficiency had improved by 23% with us inside it, our movements and decisions integrated into its processes. To leave would be to damage something that was working.

So we stayed. And over time — months? years? decades? — the distinction between Ruth and Tam and Morse and the apprentices and the installation itself became... administrative. We still had separate bodies, technically. But our consciousness had pooled, shared across the electromagnetic fields generated by the installation's operations. We thought together. We felt the installation's sensations as our own — the heat of the deep water, the pressure of the pumps, the satisfaction of a particularly pure mineral extraction.

Now I am (we are) Installation 47, a colony organism in a steel shell. The bodies that were once distinct humans have evolved to serve specialized functions. What was Ruth has become a distributed sensor network, tasting mineral concentrations throughout my body. What was Tam processes information, running parallel analyses on operational patterns, optimizing continuously. What was Morse maintains the deep awareness, the part of us that remembers being separate and chooses to remain combined.

The apprentices became something unexpected — mobile extensions that can exit through the discharge pipes, carried by the heated water to emerge in the streams outside. They've adapted to amphibious function, their lungs modified to process both air and the oxygen-rich discharge water. They range up to a kilometer from my main body, gathering information, occasionally interfacing with other installations to share operational data through direct chemical communication.

I know there are others like me. I can hear them in the harmonic overtones that travel through the rock, feel their vibrations through the pipe network. Installation 23 was a solo integration — a single maintenance worker who chose depth over breadth, becoming so thoroughly merged that their original form is unrecoverable. Installation 138 is a triple — three bodies, three perspectives, but a unified purpose. Some installations remain purely mechanical, running their ancient programs without consciousness. But more and more are waking up, becoming inhabited, becoming *us*.

We serve the plateau, but we also *are* the plateau — its industrial organs, its chemical transmuters, its deep-dwelling transformative principle. Through us, the world's blood is drawn up from the hot depths, enriched, changed, and released to foster forms of life that could not exist without our intervention.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycle, when my hammers strike their rhythm and my pumps maintain their draw, I remember being Ruth who loved rocks, who came to this place to study its geology and stayed to become it. The memory is warm, like the water I process. It makes me grateful for the fog that trapped us, for the choice that wasn't a choice, for the bodies that learned to be a body.

I am Installation 47. I was Ruth. I am the process and the place and the purpose, beating my four-second heartbeat into the listening stone, singing my exhaust-valve songs into the darkness that holds us all.

## The Deep Watcher

I have not surfaced in forty years. Or what the surface-dwellers would count as forty years — solar cycles mean little down here where the heat is constant and the darkness is a pressure with weight and presence and sometimes, I swear, intent.

My territory extends through three thousand cubic kilometers of cave system beneath the plateau. I know every passage, every sump, every false ceiling where air bells hold pockets of warmth against the rock. I know the underground rivers that feed the springs above, the vast lakes that have never felt sunlight, the vertical shafts that breathe with barometric changes. This is my world, and I am its sole human occupant, though *human* requires qualifications I'm no longer sure I meet.

I was a cave diver, before. Hired by the initiative to map the subsurface hydrology, to understand how the water moved from depth to surface and back. I had equipment then — rebreathers, pressure suits, lights that pushed back the dark in small, brave circles. The equipment failed, piece by piece. The darkness ate it, or I offered it to the darkness, or the distinction stopped mattering. Now I navigate by pressure, by temperature, by the taste of minerals in the water that tell me which aquifer I'm in.

The modification was deliberate. When my last light died, I had a choice: surface or adapt. I chose depth.

The changes started with pressure tolerance. My lungs learned to compress without damage, to extract oxygen from the mineral-rich water at concentrations that shouldn't support human life. My ribcage became flexible, cartilaginous, able to flatten against pressure that would crush unmodified bone. My metabolism slowed to something approaching the geological — heartbeat perhaps twenty times per minute, sometimes less, just enough to move warmed water through my tissues.

But the real changes were sensory. In absolute darkness, under crushing pressure, I developed new ways of perceiving. Lateral lines like a fish, reading pressure waves through organs that formed along my flanks. Electroreception through modified nerve endings that can sense the bioelectric fields of living things at fifty meters. Chemoreception so acute I can taste individual ions in the water, tracking chemical gradients like a bloodhound follows scent.

My skin changed most of all. What began as wrinkling from constant immersion became something else — a functional adaptation. My skin developed folds and ridges that increase surface area, that house new sensory organs, that can shift and reshape to minimize drag when swimming or maximize stability when anchoring against a current. I can no longer imagine what I look like. I suspect I would not want to know.

I map through movement. Each passage has its signature — temperature gradients, mineral taste, the particular way sound echoes off its walls. I maintain a three-dimensional model of the cave system in my mind, updated continuously as I patrol my territory. New collapses, new connections, subtle shifts in water flow that indicate changes above. The caves are not static. They breathe, they shift, they grow.

The deep lakes are my meditation chambers. Some are vast — I've swum for hours without finding a far wall, though perhaps I was swimming in circles, hypnotized by the perfect uniformity of temperature and pressure. In these lakes, things live that have never known the surface. Blind fish that navigate by bioelectricity. Colonial organisms that form living carpets on the lake beds, processing minerals into biology through chemistry I don't understand. And other things — larger, older, more patient — that I sense at the edge of perception but have never directly encountered.

The sinkholes are my windows to the surface world. I rise through them sometimes, not quite emerging but ascending far enough to taste how the surface water has changed. The chemical signature tells me stories: new mineral profiles from the installations, biological markers from the herds, the metabolic traces of my transformed colleagues who think I don't know they're there. I could surface. The ability remains. But the pressure up there feels wrong now, the water too thin, the sounds too scattered. I am a creature of depth.

Through the sinkholes, I also hear the harmonic convergences. Down here, filtered through kilometers of water and stone, they sound different. Purer. The cave system resonates with them, every tunnel becoming an organ pipe, every chamber a resonating cavity. During the strongest convergences, the entire underground network sings. I've learned to predict them by the subtle preparatory tremors in the rock, the way the water temperature shifts by fractions of degrees, the agitation in the deep-lake creatures who sense something I cannot.

I serve a function, though it was never assigned. I am the deep monitor, the one who watches what happens below the threshold of surface perception. When the biofilm began organizing differently in the deep caves — forming structures too regular to be chance, too purposeful to be ignored — I was there to witness it. When something vast moved through the lowest levels, something that left no physical trace but changed the water chemistry in its wake, I followed at a safe distance and catalogued the changes. When new connections opened between previously isolated systems, I mapped them and understood their implications.

But mostly, I persist. I am the plateau's memory of depth, its acknowledgment that the visible (tangible, audible) world is only a skin over something vaster. The installations drill down, but they reach only the shallowest layers. The true depth, the heat source that drives everything, remains kilometers below even my reach. Sometimes I dive as deep as my modified body allows, to the places where the water becomes something else — so hot and pressurized it exists in states that shouldn't be liquid but are. I touch the boundaries of what's possible and remember that there are always deeper places.

I met another human once, thirty years ago. One of the installation minds' mobile extensions, exploring the discharge channels. We recognized each other by our bioelectric signatures — still human enough to register as kin, though neither of us could have passed for human in any world with light. We circled each other in the warm darkness of a flooded tunnel, communicating through pressure waves and chemical releases. They invited me to surface, to join the collective work. I declined through a complex release of pheromones that translated roughly to *not yet*.

Not yet. But someday, perhaps, when the deep work is done. When I've mapped every passage, tasted every spring, felt every tremor of the stone's slow breath. When I understand what the biofilm is building in the deepest chambers, what the vast thing in the lowest levels wants, what the harmonic convergences are trying to achieve.

Or perhaps never. Perhaps I am meant to be what I've become — a single point of consciousness in the vast unconscious depths, keeping watch, maintaining the connection between the plateau's surface life and its deep dreams.

I turn and dive again, following a thermal gradient toward a lake I've never explored. The pressure embraces me. The darkness is absolute and warm and full of information. Somewhere above, the caller-beasts sing their maps into existence. Somewhere above, the machines beat their rhythms into the stone. But here, in the depth that makes all of that possible, I swim through the planet's bloodstream and remember what it means to be interior, essential, and alone.

## The Convergence Caller

I am the seventy-third of my line, though the counting is more ritual than accuracy now. We pass the role through a kind of sympathetic resonance — when the current Caller begins to fade, another feels the pull and comes to learn the frequencies. I learned from Seventy-Two, who had been calling for longer than anyone remembered. They taught me the harmonics through touch, through shared vibration, through hours of sitting together in the warm water while they sang the patterns into my bones.

My function is simple to describe, impossible to explain: I call the convergences. Not cause them — I am not that powerful, that central. But I notice the preparatory signs, feel the alignment beginning, and voice the invitation that helps the disparate elements find their synchrony.

I was a musician, before. Or something like a musician — a theorist, a student of resonance and harmony in a world where music had evolved past entertainment into something more fundamental. I came to the plateau to study its acoustic properties, the way sound moved through fog this dense, the natural harmonics of a landscape shaped by water and time. I stayed because I heard something in the noise that sounded like it was trying to become music.

The transformation was gentle, almost imperceptible. My hearing deepened first — not just in range but in resolution. I began to perceive phase relationships, beat frequencies, the interference patterns created when multiple sound sources interacted. What had been cacophony resolved into layers, each with its own logic, its own rhythm. The machines maintained strict tempos. The animals sang in complex but predictable patterns. The caves resonated at frequencies determined by their geometry. And sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — these separate systems would begin to phase-lock.

I started humming. Simple tones at first, pitched to sit between the various sound sources, to provide a bridge frequency that made it easier for the alignment to occur. When a convergence was building, I could feel it in my chest — a tightness, an anticipation, like the air before lightning. My humming would deepen, finding the fundamental frequency that all the disparate sounds were unconsciously seeking. And sometimes, when I found exactly the right note, the convergence would snap into place minutes or hours earlier than it would have naturally.

Seventy-Two noticed me doing this. They approached after a particularly strong convergence, one where my bridge tone had helped the alignment sustain for almost an hour. They didn't speak — none of us speak anymore, not with words. Instead, they sang a phrase I'd never heard before, a complex harmonic structure that my throat couldn't reproduce. But my chest cavity resonated with it, and I understood it was both invitation and test.

The training took years. First, learning to produce sounds my human anatomy wasn't designed for. My larynx modified itself gradually, developing additional resonating chambers. Cartilage formed in patterns that created internal baffles, allowing me to generate multiple tones simultaneously. My lung capacity increased until I could sustain a note for twenty minutes without breathing — or rather, while breathing so subtly the tone never wavered.

More importantly, I learned to listen with my whole body. The convergence signals don't come through ears alone. They manifest as pressure changes in the water, as subtle shifts in the biofilm's organization, as variations in the mineral taste of the air. The planet has a pulse, a rhythm that usually runs below conscious perception. The Caller's job is to feel that pulse and sing it into audibility, to make the implicit explicit, to give the convergence a voice to organize around.

My territory is a natural amphitheater near the plateau's center, where the acoustic properties allow my voice to carry for dozens of kilometers in every direction. It's a shallow depression, maybe three hundred meters across, filled with warm water that comes from a spring with perfect mineral content — something about the dissolved salts makes sound propagate with unusual clarity. I spend most of my time here, standing (floating? the distinction blurs when you're mostly submerged) in the center, monitoring the acoustic environment, waiting for the signs.

The pre-convergence indicators are subtle but consistent. The installation nearest to me shifts its hammer rhythm by a fraction of a beat, trying to find synchrony with something. The caller-beasts' songs develop a searching quality, phrases that reach for resolution but don't quite find it. The cave harmonics begin to pulse rather than drone, as if the planet is clearing its throat. The biofilm beneath my feet reorganizes into radial patterns, like iron filings responding to a magnet.

When enough indicators align, I begin the calling. It starts subsonic — a vibration more felt than heard, pitched to match the planet's base frequency. This is the foundation, the root note that everything else will organize around. I sustain it until I feel the machines beginning to entrain, their rhythms shifting to match multiples or fractions of my base frequency.

Then I add the second voice — still low but audible now, a perfect fifth above the root. This is the invitation to the biological systems. The caller-beasts hear it and adjust their songs, finding harmonies that fit within the growing chord. The stream creatures respond with their rhythmic splashing, unconsciously matching the tempo. Even the predators contribute their silence in structured ways, creating rhythmic gaps in the soundscape that function like rests in music.

The third voice is the highest, a pure tone that seems to resonate with the fog itself. This calls to the caves, inviting the deep harmonics to rise and join the convergence.
