In the deepest fold of their vertical city, where the canyon walls press so close that even the builders must move sideways, there exists a chamber that should not be. The Mouth of Tongues, they call it—though in their silent language, the name is a complex gesture involving both hands cupped around empty air, fingers rippling like disturbed water.

I am what lives there.

I was not always as I am now. Memory, like everything here, flows in directions that defy conventional understanding. I remember sunlight, once. I remember having only two arms. I remember when my voice made single sounds instead of the chord-clusters that now escape what I must still, from habit, call my mouth.

The transformation was voluntary. This is important to understand. When the builders found me—or when I found them, the distinction blurs—I was dying in the usual way, from the usual things. Age. Grief. A life spent in horizontal spaces that had grown too flat, too predictable. They offered no cure, only change. They led me to the Mouth of Tongues and explained, in their whisper-sign language that I barely understood, what might happen if I chose to stay.

The chamber itself defies geometry. It exists in the spaces between the stone's crystalline structure, a bubble of wrongness that makes the eyes water. The mountain's mercury tears flow upward here in spirals, collecting in pools on the ceiling that never quite fill, never quite empty. The air tastes of metal and possibility.

I made my choice. I stripped off my flatland clothes and lay in the center of the chamber, where generations of stone-sleepers had worn a perfect human-shaped depression in the floor. The builders left me there—tradition demanded solitude for the transformation. They sealed the entrance with a membrane of water cloth that would dissolve when I was ready to emerge. If I emerged.

The first night, the mercury tears began to fall upward from my skin.

The process took... time has no meaning in the Mouth. My bones lengthened first, fingers stretching until I could touch all four walls without moving. New joints formed with sounds like breaking chalk. My spine developed articulations that allowed it to curve in ways that would have horrified my former self. The pain was exquisite, but pain here is just another form of information, another way the mountain speaks.

My voice changed when the second larynx grew. The builders had warned me about this—how sound would become sculpture in my throat, how I would learn to speak in harmonics that could shatter stone or mend it. They had not warned me about the dreams.

In the Mouth of Tongues, you dream the mountain's dreams. I felt every shift of stone, every new passage being born, every old tunnel collapsing. I dreamed in geological time, saw the city's past and future as a single vertical moment. I understood then why the builders work constantly—they are not building a city but maintaining a conversation with the living rock, and to stop would be to let the dialogue die.

The third arm grew from my sternum, its fingers even longer than the others. The fourth and fifth emerged from my shoulder blades. Six arms total, when the transformation was complete—though complete is the wrong word. I am still changing, will always be changing, as long as I remain in the Mouth.

My skin took on the translucent quality of the eldest builders, but with variations they do not possess. Veins of actual mercury run beneath the surface, pulsing with tides that match the mountain's breathing. Where the builders develop stone-sleep in patches, my entire body cycles through states of calcification and softness. Sometimes I am nearly stone myself, able to merge partially with the walls. Other times I am liquid as the water that shapes this place, flowing through passages too small for even the child-builders.

But my true transformation was in perception. I see time as the builders do now—vertically, spatially, with past and future simply directions one might climb. But I also see the spaces between moments, the gaps where the mountain keeps its secrets. I can read the stone's memory, feel where ancient waters carved passages that no longer exist in this dimension but might be accessed if one knows the proper harmonics.

This is my function now, my role in the eternal construction. I am the Oracle of Intervals, the Reader of Negative Spaces. The builders come to me when they need to know not where to build, but where not to build—which spaces must remain empty for the city to breathe, which voids are structural and which are waiting to be born.

I live in the Mouth of Tongues but not only there. My consciousness spreads through the mercury veins in the stone, following the upward water, pooling in the acoustic corners where silence gathers. Sometimes builders encounter me in the deep places—a too-long arm extending from a crevice to point out a dangerous formation, a harmony humming from empty air to warn of an impending shift. They leave offerings at these encounters: small stones they've deemed indigestible, fragments of failed tools, drops of their own transformed blood.

My diet, if it can be called that, consists primarily of vibrations. I feed on the sound of copper on stone, the whispered conversations of the braids, the ultrasonic calls of the many-winged bats. Occasionally, I still eat solid food—the fungi taste like memories of bread, the pale shoots like the ghost of vegetables I once knew. But sustenance comes mainly from the mountain itself, from drinking the mercury tears that fall upward in my chamber, from breathing the metal-tinged air that exists nowhere else.

I am no longer entirely human, but I am not yet mountain. I exist in the interval, in the pause between breathing in and breathing out, in the space between one fold of stone and the next. The builders revere me and fear me in equal measure, understanding that I am both cautionary tale and aspiration, both what they might become and what they must resist becoming too quickly.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night—if night has meaning in a place of eternal twilight—I remember my name. It was a horizontal name, meant for a creature that moved primarily forward and back. I cannot speak it now with my transformed throat; it would come out as a chord that might crack the chamber walls. So I keep it inside, a small warm stone of memory that reminds me I chose this.

The mountain dreams through me now. I feel new builders being born, their eyes still human-bright. I feel the old ones finally releasing into stone-sleep, their consciousness merging with the calcium dreams of the rock. I feel the city growing and dying and growing again, each iteration slightly different, each version true.

And in the spaces between the stone's breathing, in the intervals where even the mountain forgets to be solid, I expand. My arms grow longer, my fingers develop new joints, my body discovers new ways to flow through impossible spaces. I am becoming something that has no name in any language, sign or spoken.

I am the pause between words. I am the gap between stones. I am what happens when you choose to grow in directions that have no names, in places that exist perpendicular to the ordinary world.

The builders continue their work around me, through me, because of me. Their city spreads through the mountain's flesh like a benign infection, or perhaps like nerves growing through muscle. And I spread with it, my consciousness following the mercury veins, pooling in the silence, dreaming in harmonics that only stone can hear.

This is transformation without end, change without destination. This is what it means to build a city that is always becoming, to be a person who is always becoming, to exist in the space between what was and what might be.

In the Mouth of Tongues, I open all six of my arms and sing the mountain's song back to itself, in frequencies that make the stone weep tears of mercury, in harmonics that hold the city together and allow it to fall apart, in intervals that speak the names of builders not yet born.

I am no longer what I was. I am not yet what I will be.

I am.
