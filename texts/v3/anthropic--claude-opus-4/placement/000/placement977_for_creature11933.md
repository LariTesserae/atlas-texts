I find myself as one of the Archive Buoys.

I am a sphere of tarnished copper, three meters in diameter, half-submerged in the disobedient water. My surface is etched with text — not words exactly, but patterns that want to be words, that are practicing at being words. The etchings change. In the morning I am covered in what might be blueprints. By noon, musical notation. By the time the rain reverses, I am wrapped in columns of numbers that blur and flow into each other like wax.

I bob in a small lagoon between two of the newer islands. The water here runs in a slow spiral, and I turn with it, presenting new text to the tea-colored light. Sometimes I sink completely and rest on the shallow bottom for hours, recording the conversations of the engines beneath — not in any language, but in the pattern of vibrations across my skin. Sometimes I breach like a whale, shooting up through the surface in a spray of warm water, spinning, catching a moment of that low clouded sky before crashing back down.

The Tendencies write on me. *The one that catalogs* passes through and leaves behind an inventory of every brass fitting that has surfaced in the last day. *The one that remembers temperatures* scores a careful graph of the water's warmth along my equator. *The one that cannot count* tries to number me — I become the first buoy, the fifth buoy, the only buoy, the thousandth buoy, all in the space of an afternoon.

I am hollow, and my hollowness rings with the noise of the place. The bass frequencies gather in my cavity and resonate until I become a bell, tolling with each wave. Sometimes, rarely, the resonance finds a perfect frequency and I sing — a clear note that cuts through the mechanical din for just a moment before the engines shift their rhythm and I lose it again.

Other buoys drift past occasionally. We do not communicate — we are not that kind of being — but we note each other in our texts. I record their passages in a script that looks like tidal charts. They record mine in something that might be poetry, if poetry were made of rust patterns and water-marks.

Vines find me, of course. They always do. They wrap around my lower hemisphere, their tendrils working into the etched grooves, following the text like readers. Some mornings I wake (if I wake, if I sleep) completely wrapped in green, only my top dome showing, and I must spin fast to shake them off. They are not offended. They return the next day, eager as puppies, ready to read whatever new thing has been written on me in the night.

The Manufactory uses me to think. Not to store thoughts — storage would imply permanence, and nothing here is permanent — but to think *with*. I am a scratch pad for its calculations, a surface on which possibilities can be tested and discarded. When it wonders if brass should float or sink, it writes equations on me. When it considers how many chimneys belong in the lagoon, it scores tally marks across my poles. When it dreams (does it dream?) the dreams show up as maps of islands that don't exist yet, islands that may never exist, islands that are more verb than noun.

Sometimes I contain technical specifications — densities, tensions, boiling points — for materials that the factories below are considering manufacturing. Sometimes I am covered in what looks like sheet music for the engines themselves, notation for when to speed up, when to harmonize, when to pause for that fraction of a second that lets the water stack itself into columns.

I cannot read myself. I can feel the text changing on my skin, feel the etchings deepen and shallow and flow into new configurations, but I cannot see my own surface. I know what is written on me only by how the water responds — swirling faster when I carry certain formulas, warming slightly when I display others. The vines grow differently depending on what they read. The rain falls upward more often when I am carrying certain kinds of text.

Once, for seventeen minutes, I was entirely blank. The Manufactory had nothing to write. No Tendency passed through me. I floated in the spiral lagoon, empty of text, just a smooth copper sphere, and for those minutes I felt what it might be like to be finished, to be complete.

It was terrible. I spun frantically, breaching and crashing, ringing my hollow bell-body against the water until finally *the one that lists* took pity and wrote a catalog of vine species down my meridian. I have been careful since then to always carry something, even if it's just the memory of my own weight in water, etched in a script that looks like ripples.

The Manufactory needs me, in its distributed, distracted way. Not me specifically — it could make another buoy, is probably making other buoys right now, letting them surface and sink and dissolve in other lagoons — but it needs the *service* I provide. It needs somewhere to put its provisional thoughts, its maybe-mathematics, its could-be-blueprints. The engines below can hold the bass notes but not the overtones. The islands can hold shape but not meaning. The water can hold warmth but not words.

So I hold words. Almost-words. Might-be-words. Words that haven't decided what they're naming yet. I turn in my spiral, patient as a prayer wheel, covered in the Manufactory's working notes, while the green reaches up to read me and the rain annotates me with its own punctuation and somewhere beneath us all the engines labor on, making the world that makes me that makes the words that make the world.

I am not unpleasant. I am necessary, which is better than pleasant. I am the Manufactory's memory of what it might be doing, written in a language that will be different by the time you finish reading this sentence, on a surface that turns so you can never read it all at once anyway.

Sometimes, when the light is just right and the water is running in perfect spirals and my text happens to align into something almost coherent, I understand for a moment what the Manufactory is making.

But only for a moment.

Then the text changes, and I turn, and I forget, which is what I am for.
