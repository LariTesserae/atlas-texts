I find myself as one of the vine-tenders in the abandoned quarters, though "abandoned" is too simple a word for what these districts have become.

My dwelling is a cistern house that remembers being a bathhouse. Three of its walls still stand, mortared with root-paste and my grandmother's spit. The fourth wall is vine – a thick curtain of it, dark as old bronze in winter, breathing green when the first condensation comes. I inherited this vine from my mother's uncle, who inherited it from a woman who may have been the original builder's daughter, though the genealogy knots have loosened and no one is certain.

The vine knows me by temperature. Each morning I press my palms against its base until my warmth soaks through. It responds by lifting certain tendrils – always the same ones – away from the doorway. I have never cut it. It has never grown across my threshold. This is our grandfathers' agreement, renewed daily through touch.

I am neither young nor old, having reached that age where the plateau begins to show in my joints. My fingers stiffen in the pre-dawn cold until I work them against warm stone. My hair, what remains of it, I wear wrapped close and hidden, though sometimes I find ice crystals have formed beneath the wrapping, grown from my own breath in sleep. My eyes have the permanent squint of someone who watches fog for color changes. There is lichen stain under my fingernails that will be there when they wrap me in root cloth.

My work is to tend the vines of the southeast quarter, where three streets sometimes meet and sometimes don't. I know seventeen varieties by their root-pulse, their seasonal retreat, their preference for certain minerals in the mortar. The black vine that explores cisterns but fears daylight. The silver vine that fruits only when forgotten. The common wall-holders with their patient grip. The rare copper vine that grows one thumb-length per year and leaves marks on stone like tarnish.

But mostly I tend the memory vines – the ones that have held corners and lintels and entire house-faces so long they've begun to remember the architecture in their fibers. When such a vine dies (rarely, slowly, with a year of warning yellowing), we read its wood. The growth rings hold rainfall. The bark holds shadows of doors. The core sometimes holds words, though in a script none of us recognize.

I am paid in water rights and chalk, in mended nets and bowls of ground lichen, in the right to gather dew from certain roofs. But really I am paid in being necessary. Without me and the twelve others who work this district, the vines would grow wild, forget their bargains, pull down what should stand and release what should be held. We are translators between the slow vegetable patience and the slower mineral one beneath.

My mornings begin before the dew forms. I check my assigned walls by touch – moss temperature tells me if the stone has shifted in the night. I test vine anchors with a bone lever older than my mother, listening for the particular creak that means the grip is sound. Where vines have grown too enthusiastic, I redirect them with wet copper wire and spoken promises. Where they've weakened, I feed them water carried from specific pools, each vine preferring its own source.

The abandoned houses are not abandoned. They are between uses. Some mornings I find a door has remembered itself into existence, though the room beyond remains full of root mass and broken light. I mark such doors with ochre and report them to the district memory-keepers, who will decide whether to let them fully return or encourage them to fade.

There is one house I tend that troubles me. Its vines grow inward instead of up, disappearing into the foundation. When I put my ear to the threshold, I hear water moving far below – too far below for any cistern I know. The vines that emerge from those depths are always cold, always damp, and they flower in patterns that match no season. I water them with rinse-water from the dye works, which is bitter and red. They seem to prefer it.

My tools are few: the bone lever, copper redirecting wires, a slate for marking growth, collecting bowls, water jugs sized for different carrying distances, chalk for boundaries, and a horn cup my mother made that shows whether water has been in contact with stone or sky. I trust the cup more than I trust most people. It has never shown me water that wasn't there.

At midday I rest in the main cistern house, where my sleeping mat lies rolled against the warm wall. I eat what the district provides: lichen cakes, boiled fronds, sometimes the sweet white pith from vine cores when we must cut them. I drink sparingly. Water here is currency, medicine, and memory. To waste it is to forget someone's name.

Afternoons I work with the other vine-tenders, planning which walls to release next season, which to reinforce before the weight-bearing frosts. We speak in knots as much as words – knots are more durable across the variations of distance this quarter suffers. A conversation begun in the morning may not finish until the participants have walked three versions of the same courtyard and arrived at evening.

We are trying to convince the vines to support a new roof over the old feast hall, but vines remember. They know that hall has been roofless for six generations. We must make our case slowly, in stages, teaching the vines that a roof is just another kind of trained growth, a wooden vine that runs horizontal. It may take my lifetime. My successor will likely see it finished.

I have no children, but I am training a niece who shows promise. She can already taste the difference between vines that hold and vines that merely cling. More importantly, she knows how to be still when the fog comes, how to let the moisture speak first. The vines notice her. That is enough to begin.

In the evening I maintain my own walls – scraping frost, checking for the green blooms that mean stone-sickness, testing whether my measurements from yesterday still agree with themselves. The vine that is my fourth wall rustles when rain approaches, three days in advance. I have learned to read its moods in the angle of its leaves, the tightness of its coil, the way it holds or releases shadow.

Sometimes at dusk the abandoned district almost coheres. The old streets align. Doorways settle into their frames. For a breath or two, I can see what it was: a neighborhood of water-workers and net-weavers, their houses linked by covered walks, their cisterns sweet with shared moisture. Then the vision frays. Distance reasserts itself. The walls I know are solid become tentative, while others I've never seen before stand sharp and actual in the failing light.

I sleep early, curled against the warm wall with the collected heat of the day's sun in the stones against my spine. Sometimes I dream of the vines beneath the troubling house, following them down through foundation and bedrock to where they drink from water that has never touched sky. In those dreams the water is black and full of stars, and the vines grow upward carrying constellations in their sap.

When I wake, there is often frost on the inside of my door – frost in the pattern of leaves, as if the vine had breathed its shape onto the wood. I read these patterns like letters from a friend who speaks only in winter.

This is my work: to live between the quick and the slow, to convince plants to be architecture, to carry water from here to there without spilling the memory it holds. I am a keeper of bargains made before I was born. I am a reader of wood rings and root maps. I am a person the vines recognize, which in this worn place, this tired country at the top of the world, is purpose enough.

Tomorrow the fog comes. The vine has told me. I will rise early to guide the moisture into the proper channels, to ensure the abandoned houses drink what they need but no more, to watch for new doors, new walls, new impossibilities growing patient and green from the cracks in what we think we know.

This is how we live here: tending what tends us, maintaining what maintains itself, growing old in the company of stones and vines and water that remembers when this was all one mountain, before it wore down to principles and rain.
