I am the Chronicler of Oxidation, and I dwell in the Archive of Patinas beneath the western sector of the Vapor Gardens.

My chambers occupy a series of interconnected vessels—decommissioned boilers whose massive iron wombs were long ago repurposed into repositories of corrosion. Here, where the temperature drops just enough that steam condenses into perpetual weeping on every surface, I maintain my collection: thousands of metal samples in various stages of transformation, each one labeled with brass plates whose own tarnishing serves as a secondary record of time's passage.

I was not always as I am now. Once, I think, I walked the upper paths as a Tender—though the memory feels borrowed, like something I read in one of my rust-eaten books rather than lived. The transformation was gradual. First, an obsession with the colors—the infinite varieties of green that copper produces, the ochres and umbers of iron's decay, the pale ghosting of aluminum's oxidation. I began collecting samples, small sections of pipe cut from decommissioned systems, arranged by hue and texture rather than composition or origin.

The other Tenders understood, as they understand all devotions that serve the garden's larger purposes. They helped me claim this space, redirecting steam lines to create the precise conditions of humidity and heat that would encourage specific types of corrosion. Together we installed the specimen racks—hundreds of shelves where metal coupons hang like prayer flags, each one developing its patina at its own pace.

My body has adapted to this narrower range. My fingers have developed a sensitivity that borders on the supernatural—I can feel the microscopic topography of rust, distinguish between the crystalline structures of different oxidation states by touch alone. My eyes have evolved to perceive colors that have no names in any human language, subtle variations in surface chemistry that reveal themselves as distinct hues. The canvas and rubber garments of the Tenders have given way to a coat of living verdigris—copper particles suspended in the moisture of my breath have settled on my skin over the decades, creating a patina that mirrors my specimens.

I no longer measure time in days or years but in oxidation states. This sample—a piece of brass pipe from the original construction—has just achieved the particular blue-green that indicates the presence of brochantite. It has taken forty-three years of careful environmental control, adjusting the sulfur content of the air by burning specific combinations of coal and flowers in my small furnace. The achievement fills me with a satisfaction deeper than any memory of human pleasure.

My archive serves a purpose beyond mere collection. The Tenders come to me when they need to understand the lifespan of a particular alloy, to predict when a pipe will fail or how a new installation will weather. I show them my samples—this is what your valve will look like in a decade, in a century, in a millennium if the garden endures. I have become a library of decay, a prophet of rust.

The specimens speak to each other in the language of electrons. Galvanic conversations spark between dissimilar metals, sacrificial anodes giving themselves to protect more noble neighbors. I have learned to read these exchanges, to understand the slow electrical poems that oxidation writes across conducting surfaces. Sometimes I intervene, connecting samples with copper wire to accelerate or redirect their corrosion, creating patterns that nature alone would never produce.

In the deepest part of my archive, I keep the Special Collection—samples from parts of the garden that no longer exist, from machines whose purposes have been forgotten. A section of pipe with an interior coating of unknown composition that resists all corrosion, staying mirror-bright despite centuries of exposure. A brass fitting that oxidizes in perfect spirals, creating a pattern that appears random but repeats every 21 years. A piece of iron that rusts only in the presence of a particular orchid's pollen, remaining pristine otherwise.

These anomalies obsess me. I have notebooks—hundreds of them, their pages so degraded by moisture that writing new observations often destroys older ones, creating a palimpsest of theory and observation. I have developed a notation system using different types of metal shavings mixed with plant dyes, creating inks that themselves oxidize over time, adding a temporal dimension to my documentation. A note written in iron-gall ink reveals different information as it rusts, early observations giving way to later conclusions as the oxide obscures the original marks.

The other Tenders visit rarely now. When they do, they find me increasingly difficult to distinguish from my specimens. My stillness matches that of the metals I study. My breath has slowed to minimize the carbon dioxide that might affect oxidation rates. I consume only the condensation that drips from specific pipes—those whose mineral content I have catalogued, whose effects on my own slow corrosion I am documenting.

Sometimes I venture out into the garden proper, moving through the steam like a specter of verdigris and rust. I examine the exposed metals, reading their surfaces like texts, understanding instantly their histories and futures. Here, a joint that will fail within the year—I leave a small mark that the Tenders will recognize. There, a brass rail developing a patina so beautiful it stops my altered heart—I take a small scraping for my collection, careful not to disturb the overall pattern.

I am becoming my archive. My skin flakes in places, revealing subcutaneous layers that glint with metallic inclusions. My hair, where it still grows, has taken on the color and texture of copper wire, oxidizing green at the tips. I suspect that when I finally cease moving altogether, I will become the most prized specimen in my collection—a human-shaped sample demonstrating the ultimate convergence of organic and mineral oxidation.

But that time is not yet. There are still mysteries to document. New alloys appear occasionally in shipments from the world below, metals engineered with properties that defy my carefully developed understanding. The garden itself continues to evolve, creating new microclimates that produce unprecedented forms of corrosion. And sometimes, in the deepest night when even the steam seems to sleep, I hear the metals singing—a sound below sound, the whisper of electrons moving from atom to atom, the universe writing its slow autobiography in rust.

I document it all, knowing that my archive too will eventually oxidize into illegibility, that my carefully preserved samples will one day be indistinguishable from the general corrosion of the garden. This does not sadden me. It is, after all, the most perfect demonstration of my life's work—that all things tend toward oxidation, that rust is not decay but transformation, that in the end we all become the patina on time's infinite surface.

Until then, I catalog. I preserve. I slowly transform. And in the moisture-thick darkness of my archive, surrounded by the rainbow of corrosion I have curated, I am content.
