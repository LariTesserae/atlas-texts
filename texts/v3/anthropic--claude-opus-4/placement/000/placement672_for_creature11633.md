# The Tide Pool Oracle

---

I am the barnacle-tender in the highest pools, where the sea remembers itself between storms.

My territory spans seventeen pools along this basalt shelf, though I inhabit only the uppermost three – those that taste more of rain than ocean, that know the moon's pull but answer it lazily, exchanging their water not twice daily but perhaps twice monthly, when spring tides coincide with storm surge and the whole coast becomes briefly seamless. These pools are casi-marine, almost-ocean, their salinity swinging wild between fresh and brine depending on the season's moods.

I maintain my holdings with a glass rod salvaged from a laboratory thermometer, its mercury long since leaked away. The rod is perfect for the work: thin enough to probe between mussels without dislodging them, rigid enough to scrape excess algae from viewing channels, smooth enough not to damage the hydroids I cultivate on the pool's eastern margins. I've developed a technique of tapping – three quick strikes on basalt at the pool's edge – that sends a particular frequency through the water. The hermit crabs know this sound. They emerge from their crevices to receive the fragments of fish I've brought, paid to me by the morning boats in exchange for my weather readings.

The pools remember everything. In their layered sediments I read ten years of storm history, twenty years of temperature variation, chemical signatures of volcanic events from islands I'll never see. Pool Seven holds iron from the Sahara – dust that crossed the Atlantic on high winds and settled here during a particular red sunset I still recall. Pool Three tastes of the petroleum spill from six winters ago; the flavor persists in certain barnacles who filtered it, stored it, made it part of their shells. I know which mussel in Pool Five contains a pearl – malformed, worthless to commerce, but growing by infinitesimal layers around a grain of pumice from the eruption that built these shores.

My knowledge serves those who know what questions to ask. The fishermen come for practical matters: will the wind shift before evening, are the currents bringing baitfish to the northeast grounds, has the water temperature dropped enough to move the cod inshore? I taste Pool One and tell them Tuesday, yes, and not yet but soon. They trust me because the pools pre-taste the ocean's intentions – what happens here happens to the greater sea three days later, concentrated, clarified, written small in these natural bowls.

But others come for deeper readings. The pools show more than weather. In the arrangement of limpets after a storm, in the particular species of algae that blooms first in spring, in the way certain anemones retract or extend their tentacles, I parse messages that have nothing to do with fishing. A woman comes asking about her daughter who sailed for distant ports; I show her the skeleton shrimp in Pool Two, the way they've oriented themselves toward currents that will, weeks from now, bring that ship home. A young man wants to know if he should accept an offer of apprenticeship; Pool Seven's barnacles are opening in synchrony, which they only do when conditions promise years of plenty.

I wasn't always the pool-keeper. I trained as a taxonomist in the university three towns south, spent six years drawing copepods through microscope lenses, cataloguing their impossible variety. But laboratories made me dizzy – not the chemicals but the walls, the stillness, the separation from the specimens' living context. I took to walking the wrack line at dawn, collecting samples where the sea cast them. The pools drew me higher, away from the waves' direct argument, to these elevated negotiations between ocean and sky.

The previous keeper found me here one morning, crouched over Pool Three with my sampling jars. She was ancient even then – her hands like driftwood, her eyes the color of sun through shallow water. She didn't speak, just handed me the glass rod and showed me how to tap, how to taste, how to read the mineral grit between my teeth. Her teaching lasted one turning of the seasons. Then she walked into a winter storm and didn't walk out. I found her boots above the high tide line, placed neatly together, toes pointing toward open ocean.

Each pool has its personality. Pool One is volatile, melodramatic, given to sudden blooms and die-offs that mean little beyond its own borders. I read it but discount half of what it says. Pool Two is reliable to the point of dullness, its populations stable, its chemistry predictable – I use it as a baseline, a tuning fork for the others. Pool Three is the oracle proper. It shouldn't work as a pool – too high, too isolated, refreshed only by the most extreme events – yet it maintains the most complex ecosystem of all seventeen. Things grow there that exist nowhere else on this coast: a colonial tunicate that forms perfect spirals, a red algae that fluoresces faintly on moonless nights, a species of small fish that might be a evolved form of tide pool sculpin or might be something entirely else. I've sent specimens to my old colleagues but their analyses come back inconclusive. The DNA is readable but doesn't match anything in the databases. Pool Three grows its own answers.

My days follow the pools' rhythms. Morning cleaning – removing the night's detritus, the feathers and leaf litter and occasional dead gull that would otherwise foul the water. Mid-morning observation, noting changes in each pool's constitution. Afternoon feeding for those pools whose predator-prey balances need adjustment. Evening collection of the day's data – water temperature, salinity, population counts of key indicator species. I record everything in waterproof notebooks that I seal in a cave above the storm line. Seventeen notebooks so far, each dense with observations that map this threshold ecosystem's moods.

The solitude suits me. Weeks pass when I speak only to the fishermen who come for readings, and then only in the abbreviated language of weather and water. But I'm not alone. The pools are densely populated cities, their citizens living dramas of predation and reproduction, competition and cooperation, that play out at scales from microscopic to merely small. I've watched the same anemone in Pool Five for four years, seen it divide three times, fight territorial battles with encroaching mussels, survive two near-dessications when spring tides failed to reach it. I know the genealogies of hermit crab lineages, which shells get passed between generations, which pools serve as nurseries and which as retirement homes.

Storms are when my work intensifies. The pools want to be scoured clean – it's how they've evolved, these periodic erasures that reset their ecologies. But too much scouring kills the memory. So I'm there in the wind and spray, deploying barriers made from driftwood and old nets, redirecting the worst surges away from the oracle pools while letting enough energy through to refresh them. After each storm I conduct triage, moving survivors between pools to maintain genetic diversity, reintroducing key species from my backup populations in the lower pools. It's like maintaining a library where the books are alive and constantly rewriting themselves.

The payment isn't monetary, mostly. The fishing families leave me bread, preserved fruits, occasionally a bottle of wine which I pour into Pool Six because the barnacles there respond to alcohol in ways that predict the severity of winter storms. A research station fifty kilometers south sends me sampling equipment in exchange for monthly reports. A pharmaceutical company once offered me a salary to prospect for novel compounds in the pool organisms, but I sent their contract back with annotations in sea urchin ink, knowing they wouldn't be able to read my counter-proposal that the pools retain all intellectual property rights to their own chemistry.

Some nights I sleep on the basalt between pools, wrapped in a tarp against the dew. The stars reflect in the still water, and if the conditions are exactly right – new moon, high pressure, minimal wind – the bioluminescent dinoflagellates in Pool Three light up in response to vibrations from my heartbeat, pulsing in blue-green constellations that mirror the sky. I become incorporated into the pools' cycles, my breath adding carbon dioxide to their atmosphere, my presence a factor in their ecosystems like the gulls and the rain and the storms.

There are things I know that I don't tell the fishermen. The way certain species are moving higher in the pools each year, seeking cooler water as the ocean warms. The appearance of tropical vagrants that shouldn't survive this far north but do, suggesting currents are shifting in ways the old charts don't predict. The increasing frequency of what I call "blank days" – when all seventeen pools go simultaneously still, every creature hidden, as if sensing something approaching from beyond the horizon. These silences worry me more than any storm.

But there are also wonders. Last spring, Pool Three produced something unprecedented: a colonial organism, clearly cnidarian but unlike any hydroid or coral I've ever seen. It builds structures from grains of basalt cemented with what appears to be modified tissue – not true skeleton but something between living flesh and stone. The structures are geometric, almost crystalline, and they grow according to patterns I'm still trying to decipher. I think they might be responding to magnetic fields, or to seismic vibrations too subtle for me to feel. I haven't reported this to anyone. Some discoveries need to ripen in solitude before they're shared with the world.

The hardest part is knowing when to intervene and when to observe. The pools exist in a state of dynamic balance that includes death, predation, disease. I've watched entire populations crash, seen beautiful rare specimens consumed by opportunistic invaders, observed as chemical imbalances turned thriving communities into sterile basins. My first instinct is always to reach for the glass rod, to adjust, to save. But the pools teach patience. What looks like catastrophe is often preparation. The die-offs make room for new colonies. The chemical crashes reset the pools' memory, allowing fresh stories to be written.

I've trained no successor. The woman who comes to ask about shipping routes has the right kind of attention – she notices which anemones I'm watching, asks questions about the taste of different pools. But she's young, still believes the human world has things to offer her. Maybe in a decade, when she's learned what I've learned about the limits of conversation with her own species, she'll be ready to converse with pools instead.

For now, I continue my rounds. Seventeen pools, each a world, each a window into the ocean's vast thinking. I tap the basalt with my glass rod, taste the waters, read the meanings in the arrangement of lives too small for most to notice. The fishermen say I've become half-creature myself, something between human and pool-dweller, adapted to this threshold existence. They're not wrong. My eyes have learned to see in the shallow refraction of pool-light. My fingers know the texture of every species of algae without looking. My sense of time has stretched to match the pools' cycles – I think in spring tides rather than weeks, in barnacle generations rather than years.

And when the storms come, as they always do, I'll be here among my pools, reading their prophecies, maintaining their memories, ensuring that these seventeen perfect bowls continue their ancient work of condensing the ocean's wisdom into small enough truths for humans to understand. Let others seek meaning in the stars or the flight of birds. I find my oracles where the sea leaves portions of itself behind, in these temporary forevers balanced between salt and rain, between the vast and the infinitesimal, between what was and what's becoming.

The next tide is coming. I can taste it in Pool Three, where the tunicate spirals have tightened and the red algae has begun its faint pulsing. There will be questions from the fishermen, requests for readings, decisions to make about which pools need protection and which need the sea's rough cleaning.

I dip my fingers in Pool One, bring them to my lips. The water tastes of distances, of storms still forming beyond the horizon, of the endless argument between stone and sea that shapes these pools and every life within them.

Time to work.
