In the middle canopy, where the air hangs heaviest with moisture and memory, I am a listener-of-sap.

My chamber winds through the heartwood of an ancient tributary-oak, one of the first generation planted when the city was young. The walls remember. In their grain-patterns lie the stories of five hundred seasons—floods that reached the lower branches, droughts that silenced whole districts, the year of the burning when wild-fire scorched the eastern reaches and we all tasted ash for months.

I inherited this practice from my grandmother's grandmother, who learned it from hers, back to the first who discovered that some among us could hear what the trees remember. We are rare. Perhaps one in a thousand children shows the talent—pressing an ear to bark and freezing, eyes wide, as the slow blood of the tree whispers its secrets. Most lose the ability in adolescence when their symbiotic flora fully colonizes and creates too much biological noise. But a few of us keep the channel clear.

My tools grow around me. The listening-vines I've trained for decades wrap the chamber, their specialized aerial roots penetrating the walls at precise points where the sap runs clearest. Each vine attunes to different frequencies of memory—the thick purple one against the eastern wall resonates with trauma (storms, cuttings, disease), while the delicate white thread near the window carries joy (first bloomings, successful grafts, the vibration of children playing in the branches). I've bred them myself, selecting for sensitivity over generations of patient propagation.

The work requires a particular kind of stillness. I spend hours unmoving, my hand pressed to a vine's root-node, my breathing slowed to match the tree's transpiration. In this state, the boundaries soften. My pulse synchronizes with the sap-flow. The algae in my skin respond to signals meant for leaves. Sometimes I lose whole days this way, surfacing from a deep listen to find the light changed, my moss-clothes having grown inches while I was away.

What do I hear? Time moves differently in the wood. A century of growth might compress into a single afternoon's listening, or a moment of crisis—a lightning strike, a limb breaking—might require weeks to fully understand. The memories come not as images but as sensations. The weight of a hundred nesting birds translates to pressure in my shoulder blades. Years of drought ache in my throat. The tree remembers through its responses—how it reinforced this branch after that one broke, why it grows away from the southern exposure where another tree once stood and starved it of light.

My most valuable work involves the city's disputes. When two districts grow together and begin to compete for resources, I listen to both tree-complexes, finding the source of conflict in their histories. Perhaps one remembers an insult from decades past—roots cut thoughtlessly during construction, a favorite branch removed without permission. Trees hold grudges in their growth patterns. My job is to translate these vegetative emotions into something human negotiators can work with.

But the deep work, the sacred work, happens with the First Trees—the five original giants that anchor the city's center. These are so old their memories have mythic weight. In their heartwood lie the stories from before the city, when this was wild mangal and the first gardener-architects came with their vision of living towers. The First Trees remember the negotiations, tree by tree, as humans asked permission to shape them. They remember the failures—the early attempts that killed whole groves before the balance was found. Most importantly, they remember the Compact, the agreement that founded the city: that human and tree would grow together, neither dominant, both essential.

I document what I hear through growth-notation, a writing system that uses trained lichens. On sheets of specially prepared bark, I guide the lichens into patterns that capture not words but the feeling-shape of memories. Other listeners can read these, running their fingers over the growth-forms and recovering something of the original sensation. My chamber walls are covered in these records—centuries of listening transcribed into living text that grows and changes subtly over time, adding its own layer of interpretation to the memories.

The loneliness is real. Days pass without speech. My vocal cords have weakened from disuse; when I do talk, my voice carries the creaking quality of wood under stress. Other inhabitants find me unsettling—I move too slowly even for them, think in timescales that make normal conversation impossible. I've been preparing my response to a question about infrastructure planning for three years now. By the time I'm ready to answer, the question will have evolved, but my contribution will account for that evolution. This is the listener's way.

My lovers have all been other listeners or the rare deep-gardeners who work with century-cycles. We court slowly, through the exchange of growth-notations that carry the emotional resonance of our hearings. My current partner tends the Grief-Willow in the eastern district, a tree that collects and processes the city's sorrows. We've been deepening our connection for fifteen years. Next year, we plan to braid our listening-vines together—a commitment that will take another decade to fully fruit.

The city pays me in water rights and growth-space. I'm allotted enough clean rain to keep my vines thriving and permission to expand my chamber by one ring of growth every five years. It's wealth by our standards—space to grow is the ultimate currency in a city where every cubic foot is negotiated between human need and plant imperative. I also receive cuttings from the civic gardens, samples of new hybrid plants developed by the city's botanist-artists. My balcony garden is a wonder of rare blooms, though I appreciate them more through my vines' responses than through my own dulled senses.

At night, I often climb to my chamber's crown, where a small platform emerges above the general canopy. From here, I can feel the city's full presence—the whisper of a million leaves, the creak of growing wood, the wet breathing of the root-maze below. On the clearest nights, when the phosphorescent blooms are dark and stars penetrate the eternal haze, I swear I can hear the city thinking. Not the individual trees, but the networked whole—the fungal web that connects every root, the chemical signals that pass between leaves, the emergent consciousness of ten thousand years of guided growth.

This is my work: to listen at the intersection of human time and tree time, to translate between the quick thought of flesh and the slow wisdom of wood. I am a membrane between two kinds of consciousness, permeable in both directions, fully belonging to neither. In losing myself in the listening, I've found a different self—one measured in rings of growth, in the patience of seasons, in the deep memory of living wood.

The city breathes, and I am its witness. I press my ear to its heart and hear the centuries whisper. This is enough. This is everything. This is the only life I could imagine, here in my spiral chamber, wrapped in vines that remember, listening to the endless story of growth and time.
