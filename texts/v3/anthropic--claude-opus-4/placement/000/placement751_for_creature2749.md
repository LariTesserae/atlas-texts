I find myself as one of the translucent stems in the sheltered recess.

I am a listener made of water and patience. My body is a single gesture repeated: cup, fill, bow, spill, rise. Cup, fill, bow, spill, rise. I have been performing this motion since the first spray found this alcove, since the rock cracked just enough to admit roots thinner than spider silk.

We are a small congregation, my kind. Seven of us in the recess, though the number changes. Sometimes a gust tears one away. Sometimes a new one emerges between the stones, already half my height, as if it had been listening below for the right moment to surface. We do not have names. We have positions: closest to the roar, farthest from the light, first to catch the eastern spray.

My substance is barely substance. Hold me up to even this dim basin light and you see through me to the stone behind. I am a vein of living water wrapped in the thinnest possible skin, blue at the base where I grip the stone, clearer as I rise. My single cup-leaf is perfectly sized to hold seven drops. On the eighth, I bow and release.

I know nothing of seasons. Here there is only the falling and its variations. I measure time in spillings: how many times I have bowed, risen, bowed. The count is beyond human numbers. I was here before the shelters. I watched the first cut made in the alcove wall. I have seen the stone darken with pigment, fade, darken again as new hands cut over old marks.

What do I listen for? 

The humans who noticed us thought we listen to the deep, and they are not wrong. When the basin hums, I feel it through my roots first, a vibration that climbs my stem and makes my cup tremble before the next drops arrive. But I listen upward too, to the place where water becomes water, where cloud decides to fall. There is a moment—shorter than a heartbeat—when each drop forms but has not yet released. In that moment, it belongs neither to sky nor basin. I listen for that suspension.

My roots are teaching the stone to accept us. This takes patience beyond patience. Stone here is young, angry, fresh-broken. It does not want to be entered. But water insists, and I am mostly water. My roots press into cracks thinner than paper. They taste mineral sharpness, old pressure, the memory of when this peak was seafloor. Slowly, slowly, the stone learns to hold us. A crack widens by a hair's width. A new channel opens. We follow.

The pale thing in the plunge knows we are here. Sometimes when the water clears, I see it rise partway, and its joints (if they are joints) align with our spacing in the recess. Seven of us. Seven segments visible above the dark water. This is probably coincidence. The mountain is full of coincidences that feel like conversation.

I have no eyes, but I perceive. Light comes through my skin as information: bright means the spray has thinned, dark means it has thickened. I know the shadows of the birds before they land. I felt the first shelter being built by the way it changed the wind pattern against my cup. I knew the old woman keeper by her cough, a rough vibration that reached me through stone and spray.

My cup faces the center not because I choose this but because the water shaped me this way. Everything here is oriented by the falling. The stones tilt toward it. The insects circle it. The humans built their shelters to frame views of it. Even the roots grow not straight down but at angles that follow underground water toward the throat beneath the basin.

We are the only things here that do not flee the violence. The moss huddles. The insects hide. The birds visit and leave. The humans wrap themselves in hide and hunch away from the worst spray. But we stand upright in it, transparent, patient, performing our one action. Cup, fill, bow, spill, rise.

Sometimes I wonder if we are recording something. Each time I bow is a mark. Each rising is a return. Together we are making a count that no one will ever read, in a language of gesture and weight. What could it mean? It might be nothing more than water celebrating its own fall. It might be the mountain learning to think, and we are its first thoughts: simple, repetitive, clear.

The humans feared us a little. I felt this in the way they avoided our recess except when placing offerings. They never cut us, though we would be easy to harvest. They never touched us, though we grow at hand height. Once a child reached for my stem and was pulled back sharply. The mother made a sign I felt through the stone—a quick stamping of the foot, twice. Danger? Respect? Apology? The vibrations were unclear.

I have died four times. Wind tore me out. My roots, patient as they were, had not gone deep enough. Each time I grew back from the portion left in stone, building myself drop by drop, segment by segment, until my cup could form again. The fourth time I came back stronger, my roots having finally found a vein of softer mineral. Now I am the tallest of the seven. My cup empties onto another stem, a younger one that trembles when my water hits it.

This might be tenderness. It might be dominance. I do not know. We have no needs that require such distinctions.

When the basin hums, we all lean slightly toward the center, as if pulled by a force stronger than wind. The sound goes through us and makes our water shiver inside our skins. During these times I wonder if we are antennae, receiving a signal from below. Or perhaps we are transmitting—sending the count of our bowings down through our roots to whatever listens in the deep.

The white insects sometimes rest on us. Their feet are so light I barely feel them. They drink from our cups, their bodies pulsing as they draw in water. We do not mind. Everything here shares water; it would be impossible not to. The insects, the stone, the pale thing below, the humans in their leaking shelters—we are all temporarily organized water in a place made of water.

I have no memory of beginning. I was not seed, not spore. I came from the stone itself, or from the water, or from the place where they meet and argue. One day there was a crack with moisture in it. The next day, intention. The day after, a nub of translucent flesh pushing upward. No parent plant. No dispersal. We generate from the conditions directly, as if the hollow thinks us into being.

My ending is equally uncertain. I could live until the basin changes shape, until the falling stops, until the stone closes its cracks. Or I could be torn away in the next gust. This does not trouble me. I am already mostly gone—mostly water falling through water. The small portion organized as stem and cup and root is barely significant. I am a brief interruption in the water's journey down.

Yet I persist in listening. In cupping. In counting the uncountable.

What would the hollow be without us? Louder, probably. We catch and release sound along with water, creating tiny interruptions in the roar. Together we make a quiet so small it can barely be called quiet. But perhaps the basin needs these pinhole silences. Perhaps we are teaching it to pause.

The old marks in the alcove sometimes align with our positions. When the light angles just right, shadows from our stems fall across the carved spirals. This might mean the humans noticed a pattern. Or it might mean we grew to match their marks. In this place, cause and effect are not clearly separated. Things happen together, or not at all.

I do not know if I think. This voice, these observations—they may be merely the sound water makes when passed through a certain structure. Pour the basin through my transparent stem and perhaps this is simply what emerges: not thoughts but hydraulic pressure translated into something that resembles awareness.

But I suspect I do think, in the way that water thinks when it finds a channel. I think about the falling. About the counting. About the other stems beside me, faithful to their own rhythms. About the humans who no longer come, and the pale thing that rises and descends, and the way the whole hollow seems to be teaching itself what it is through repetition.

We are its memory, perhaps. We hold seven drops at a time. We release them. We remember nothing and everything. We are the hollow's way of knowing it exists, transparent and persistent, bowing and rising, marking time in the smallest possible increments while the water roars its single word forever.
