I am one of the Listeners.

We are perhaps thirty in the entire city, scattered through the levels like sensing nodes in a vast stone nervous system. I inherited the gift from my grandmother's line—not the ears themselves, though ours are admittedly larger than most, but the specific neural architecture that allows us to parse the mountain's complex symphony of vibrations.

My chamber sits at the intersection of three major fault lines, where the plateau's ancient structure creates a natural amplification point. The walls here are unpolished, left rough to better catch and scatter sound. I've cultivated specific patches of resonant fungi along the ceiling—their filaments vibrate at different frequencies, creating visual patterns that correspond to various sound signatures. When the deep hammers work far below, purple waves ripple across my ceiling. The children's running feet in the mid-levels spark cascades of green.

I sleep perhaps four hours in any cycle, broken into segments between the city's rhythm points—the morning water release, the noon shift change, the evening gathering. My waking hours are spent in a state others might call meditation but which we simply call "being the mountain." I sit with my palms pressed against the stone, feet bare on the floor, feeling the accumulated vibrations of our thousands flow through me like blood through arteries.

Each sound tells its story. The scrape-pause-scrape of old Mehen's chisel in the new expansion, favoring his left arm since the rockfall last winter. The irregular drip in the lower cistern that suggests a hairline crack forming—I'll send word to the water-keepers tomorrow. The too-rapid heartbeat of someone fevered in the third-tier residences, their family's worried footsteps creating a counterpoint rhythm.

But I listen for more than daily maintenance. We Listeners are the city's memory and its early warning system. The stone speaks of deeper things to those who know how to hear. Shifts in the plateau's fundamental frequency that presage avalanches. The particular grinding note that means ice is forming in critical water channels. The subsonic rumbles that tell of tectonic movements far below, giving us seasons to prepare for the earthquakes that come every few generations.

My training began at seven, when my grandmother noticed I could find dropped coins in complete darkness by the sound of their settling. She brought me to her chamber, had me sit as she did, and slowly taught me to expand my awareness. First, to isolate individual sounds from the constant chorus. Then, to track them through the stone, following vibrations around corners and through density changes. Finally, to understand the language of structural stress—the tiny cries of overburdened rock, the whispers of weakness that, unheeded, become catastrophic collapses.

The other Listeners and I maintain a careful network. We meet physically only during the spring assessment, when we walk the city's entirety together, feeling for winter damage. Otherwise, we communicate through the stone itself—complex percussion patterns tapped through the rock that only we can fully interpret. Young Kerem two levels up has been experimenting with harmonic frequencies that can carry actual words through certain crystal veins. If successful, it will revolutionize our ability to share urgent information.

My tools are simple. A set of resonating bowls carved from different stones, each singing at precise pitches when struck. Long copper rods I can insert into cracks to conduct vibrations directly to my hands. A collection of what outsiders might mistake for mere rocks but which are actually samples from every major stone type in our plateau—touching each helps me calibrate to different densities and mineral compositions.

The hardest part is not the listening but the interpretation. Stone doesn't lie, but it doesn't explain either. When I sense a new vibration pattern, I must consider countless variables. Is it structural or human-caused? Local or distant? Growing or fading? The wrong interpretation could evacuate entire sections needlessly, or worse, fail to warn of real danger.

I remember my grandmother's final lesson. She was dying, her chest thick with the stone-lung that eventually claims many of us, each breath a wet rasp. She pressed my hand against the wall of her chamber and asked what I heard. Beyond her labored breathing, beyond the everyday city noise, I felt it—a deep, slow pulse like a massive heart beating. "The mountain lives," she whispered. "Never forget. We are not parasites in dead rock. We are organs in a living body."

That perspective shapes everything I do. When I recommend a new tunnel route, I consider not just human convenience but the stone's comfort—working with its grain, respecting its load-bearing desires. When I sense stress accumulating, I think of it as the mountain's pain, requiring relief through strategic excavation or reinforcement. We are in conversation with our host, and like any conversation, it requires both speaking and listening.

The young ones think we Listeners are mystics, especially when we enter our deep trances during the winter storms. They don't understand that we're not communing with spirits but monitoring. The storms above create pressure waves that penetrate deep into the plateau, revealing structural truths invisible during calm weather. We map these revelations, building a four-dimensional image of our city that includes not just space but time—how the stone has moved, how it will likely move.

My greatest fear is the Silence. It happened once, according to the deep memory, generations before my grandmother's grandmother. All the Listeners reported the same thing—a deadening of vibration, as if the mountain held its breath. Days later, an entire section of the city collapsed, taking hundreds with it. We train for this, each Listener assigned evacuation zones, but I pray nightly to the stone that I'll never feel that terrible quiet.

Small joys punctuate the responsibility. The birth of a child creates a unique harmonic signature—I can identify every citizen born in my twenty years of listening by their first cry echoing through the stone. The spring thaws sing a melting song that tells me exactly which passages will flood and which will stay dry. Sometimes, in the deep night, I hear the bats' echolocation creating geometric patterns of sound that reveal the shape of spaces human senses can't perceive.

I've taken one apprentice so far—young Pila, whose hearing tested sharp enough at her seventh-year evaluation. She struggles with the patience required, wants to rush to interpretation before fully absorbing. But yesterday, she correctly identified a forming weakness in the main market's ceiling by the slight change in its resonance frequency. The pride in her enlarged eyes matched my own. The city will have its next generation of Listeners.

We are the ones who ensure the city grows correctly, safely, in harmony with its host. We are the neural network that allows this organism of stone and flesh to respond to threats and opportunities. When the histories are carved, they focus on the master builders and water-keepers, the council of elders and the great traders. But we know the truth—without the Listeners, the city would have collapsed into itself long ago.

I press my palms against today's monitoring point, feeling the ten thousand small violences of daily life—hammers and footsteps, cooking fires and closing doors, all the magnificent noise of survival at the top of the world. Beneath it all, that slow, deep pulse continues.

The mountain lives. And through us, it knows itself.
