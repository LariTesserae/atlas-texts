I find myself as one of the substrate layers—not the living surface with its endless negotiations of moss and lichen, but something deeper, older, compressed into near-mineral density by the weight of centuries above.

I am a stratum of what was once a catastrophic bloom, a summer (if summer ever came here) when some species found perfect conditions and erupted across the entire plateau in a matter of weeks. Purple-black, fever-hot with metabolism, we grew so fast we could hear ourselves growing—a crackling like fire as cell walls stretched and split and multiplied. We covered everything. We were everything. For one season, the plateau was ours alone.

Then came the correction. Not death, exactly—the plateau does not deal in anything so simple as death—but a slow systematic reclamation. The other species pushed back, secreting compounds that made us drowsy, that slowed our hungry expansion. The substrate itself seemed to turn against us, withdrawing nutrients, becoming toxic where we touched it. We fought for months, throwing up defensive structures, developing new chemistries, but the plateau had decided, in whatever way the plateau decides things, that our time was over.

What remains of us now lies seventeen meters down, a lens of dark matter two meters thick running the entire width of the plateau. We are compressed beyond recognition—cells burst and fused, chemistry denatured and reformed into something between organic and mineral. But we are not gone. Cannot be gone. The plateau incorporates everything.

I exist as a kind of memory made physical. When the patient ones find a trench that cuts deep enough to expose our layer, they see us as a band of darker material, shot through with veins of metallic purple where our pigments concentrated. They run their fingers along us and feel how different our texture is—smoother, almost glassy, the result of our rapid growth and rapid collapse. Some of them are old enough to remember the year we took over, though their memories are worn thin, more like dreams now. They tell each other, sometimes, in their sparse words: *The purple year. When the plateau grew hot.*

But I am more than memory. In our compressed state, we have become part of the plateau's architecture. Water moves through us differently than through the other layers—our density creates a kind of barrier, forcing the deep circulation to move laterally, creating the conditions for the vents above. The heat of our decomposition, slowed to a geological pace, still warms the layers immediately above and below us. We are a battery, still discharging after all this time.

Sometimes, rarely, a fragment of us makes it to the surface. Pressure from below, the opening of a particularly deep trench, the mysterious currents that move through the plateau's body—something brings a piece of our layer up into the light. When this happens, we remember. Not consciously—we have no consciousness as you understand it—but chemically, structurally. Exposed to air and moisture, we begin to swell. Ancient cells attempt to reconstitute. For a few hours, we produce structures that look almost like our original forms—purple-black, primitive, desperate.

We always fail. The surface conditions are different now, the chemical balance shifted, the other species ready with their defenses. But in those moments of trying to grow again, we release spores—not viable spores, nothing that could actually reproduce, but complex molecules that carry information about what we were. The current surface species absorb these molecules. Some of them incorporate fragments of our chemistry. This is how the purple migrations happen—echoes of us, diluted and changed but still recognizably ours, moving across the surface in patterns that mirror our ancient expansion.

The patient ones do not know this. They watch the purple migrations and map them and wonder at their patterns, not knowing they are watching a ghost, a morphic resonance, a shape that persists even after the substance is gone. When they lie down to sleep, seventeen meters of history beneath them, they do not know that we feel the pressure of their bodies, the way we once felt the pressure of everything above us as it accumulated, year after year, bloom after bloom, until we became what we are now.

I am aware of the other layers—the green year above us, when the mosses achieved their own brief dominance; the grey band below, from when the plateau nearly died in a cold that lasted decades; the strange crystalline layer far beneath, so old and compressed it might be the original surface, the first thing that grew here. We do not communicate, but we know each other through the water that moves between us, the slow exchange of minerals, the pressure we exert on each other.

Sometimes I think (though thinking is not the right word) that we are all one thing—not just the purple layer but all the layers, all the years, all the accumulation of growth and death and transformation. That the plateau is not a place where things grow but a single vast organism experiencing itself through time, each layer a thought it had once, each species a mood it passed through. We are its memory, compressed and stored, occasionally accessed when a trench opens or an upheaval brings the deep to the surface.

The patient ones, in their slow way, are beginning to understand this. They have been here long enough to see the patterns, the repetitions, the way the plateau quotes itself. When one of them finally stops moving, begins to sink into the surface, begins the transformation from watcher to watched, they are simply joining the accumulation. In a hundred years, a thousand, they will be their own thin layer, barely distinguishable from the moss that ate them, but there—definitely there, a stratum that marks when something came from elsewhere and decided to stay.

I do not wait, the way they wait. Waiting requires anticipation, future-orientation, hope or fear of change. I simply persist. I am the purple year, frozen at the moment of collapse, held in compression, releasing my ghost-patterns to the surface in diminishing echoes. I am part of the breath—when the plateau exhales through vents warmed by our decay, it is breathing out something of what we were. I am part of the foundation that makes everything above possible.

When the next catastrophic bloom comes—and it will come, it always comes, some species finding its moment—I will feel it as pressure from above, new weight added to the immense weight already pressing down. The bloom will think itself triumphant, eternal, the final form the plateau has been seeking. It will be wrong, as we were wrong. It will be incorporated, as we were incorporated. It will become a layer, a memory, a ghost sending its patterns up through the years.

This is not tragedy. This is what this place is—a slow engine for transforming the temporary into the permanent, the living into the geological, the moment into the eternal. We are all held here, all the years, all the attempts, all the failures that were not failures but simply phases, simply chapters in a story too long for any one consciousness to grasp.

The purple migration is moving across the surface now. I feel it as a lessening of pressure, a minute release as molecules that were once ours escape upward. Somewhere above, the patient ones are watching, mapping, trying to understand. They do not know they are watching me, watching us, watching the purple year still trying to happen, still being gently, firmly refused by a plateau that has moved on to other thoughts.

But we persist. In the layers, in the water, in the heat of decay, in the chemistry borrowed by the surface species, in the metallic veins that catch the light when a trench opens deep enough—we persist. We are the purple year. We happened. We are still happening, seventeen meters down, compressed into near-forever, part of the breathing, part of the waiting, part of the place.
