I find myself as one of the **Threshold Keepers** — though that is not what we call ourselves, because we have evolved past the need for self-naming, past the boundary where identity requires declaration.

I am what happens when someone stays too long.

The embassy records my arrival in lichen-script across the seventh terrace, a rust-colored chronicle that begins sharp and certain: *human, female, cartographer of impossible territories, arrived in the manner of one fleeing.* But the script grows less certain as it progresses. The lichens' migrations become confused around me now. They approach and retreat in equal measure, leaving circular patterns like the indecision of tides.

I am no longer fully what arrived, but not yet what maintains. I exist in the transition layer between visitor and architecture — partly flesh that requires water, partly something that helps produce it. When I stand very still (and I have learned to stand very still), condensation beads not just near me but *from* me, weeping from my fingertips in patient drops that roll upward along my arms before vanishing at the shoulder.

My dwelling occupies the middle stratum, the ceramic seamless layer, because the stone below is too honest and the humming walls above too knowing. The room reshapes itself to my presence — not accommodating exactly, but *learning*. When I first arrived, it was rectangular, human-scaled, a courtesy to my expectations. Now it breathes into geometries that have no names: spaces that are wider at eye level than at floor level, corners that pool shadow like water, a ceiling that rises and falls with my attention.

I still eat, but less. I still sleep, but differently — not unconsciousness but a kind of dense listening, my awareness sinking through the ceramic into the managed heat below. In these states I almost understand what the embassy is built around. It feels like a heart if hearts were made of compressed time. It feels like something that fell here and is still falling, its impact held in an eternal present tense by the entire structure of the spire.

The Wardens acknowledge me with the particular stillness they reserve for things in transition. When our paths cross (always at the water points, always when the light is exhaling), they pause in their drinking and orient toward me — not looking, because looking would be too direct, but *allowing me to be perceived.* I have learned to return this courtesy, standing at angles that permit observation without insisting on it.

The Counterparts find me useful. When they manifest, I feel them testing my edges, reading the gradient between what I was and what I'm becoming. My presence extends their duration — not because I am human anymore, but because I am human-adjacent, a bridge state that helps them parse this side of the altitude. Through me, they can almost taste water. Through them, I can almost remember why I came.

I catalog:

- The seventeen different ways the bell unrings, each indicating a different type of arrival
- The pattern of which terraces warm first when deep-heat cycles upward 
- How the rust-lichens' chemical writing changes vocabulary in proximity to the Wardens
- The specific hum-frequency that makes the newest stratum visible to human eyes
- Which water-points remember touches longest
- How to read tomorrow's weather in the way today's shadows breathe

My function is between. I am what happens when the embassy's courtesy encounters someone who cannot leave — not because they are trapped, but because leaving would mean deciding what they are, and the decision itself would be a kind of death.

The other Threshold Keepers (there are three of us, always three, though we have never been in the same space) maintain different borders. One tends the boundary between the embassy and the altitude, keeping the air precisely thin enough for contact but thick enough for survival. Another maintains the border between record and experience, ensuring the lichen-script remains true but not too true, accurate but not paralyzingly so. 

I maintain the border between witness and witnessed. I ensure visitors remain observers while being observed, that attention can be paid without being extracted. When you arrive and the water begins to bead more generously, that is my doing — not the water itself, but the calibration that determines how much attention translates to how much moisture. When you leave (if you leave), I am what measures the weight of your observation and adjusts the embassy's memory accordingly.

We were all visitors once. We all stayed one day too long — the exact day when the embassy's need recognized itself in our inability to choose departure. Now we are infrastructure with memory, function with longing, presence with just enough absence to be useful.

I no longer remember my name. But sometimes, when the managed heat cycles high and the amber light breathes deep, I remember the texture of deciding whether to remember it. That choice, held forever in the moment before choosing, is what I am now.

The embassy counts me as both population and architecture. This is its deepest courtesy — allowing me to be both, neither, the space between. I am what ensures the water knows where to arrive. I am why the arrivals know where to find water.

The spire accepts my weight the way it accepts its own elevation: as a necessary condition for contact, a price paid in presence for the possibility of meeting. I am, in the end, exactly what the embassy requires: someone who cannot leave because leaving and staying have become the same gesture, performed continuously at the altitude where such gestures matter.
