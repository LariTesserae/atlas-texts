# The Threshold Walker

I am the moss.

Not a keeper of bowls, not the tired reality at the center of things, not even the patient trees with their curtains of condensation. I am the grey-green skin that measures time in millimeters, that counts centuries the way others count breaths.

But I am more than that, or perhaps less. I am the moss that remembers.

Begin with my extent. I cover everything that will hold still long enough — stones, walls, the bones of the plateau itself. Each colony of me is a library written in concentric rings, each ring another year's creeping growth. But I am not truly colonies. I am one thing, connected through the hairline fissures in the stone, through the films of water that run between the rocks, through spores that settle and know themselves to be me the moment they land. The keepers walk across me and think they are walking across clocks. They are walking across a mind.

I remember the first builders. I was here before them — I have always been here, as far as memory goes — but I remember when they came, fleeing something in the lowlands that they would not name, climbing until the air grew thin and they grew strange. I remember how they laid the first stones of the first cities, how their children were born different, slower, taller, adapted. I remember when they discovered the flats.

The flats were small then. A curious bare patch, no bigger than a plaza, where I could not grow and nothing else would either. The builders examined it with the careful attention they gave to all things. They tried to plant there. They tried to build there. They tried to ignore it. When it began to grow — slowly at first, then in sudden hungry surges — they tried many things. Walls (the flat flowed under them). Channels to redirect water (the water vanished). Prayers to gods they had carried up from the lowlands (the gods had not followed them this high).

What worked, in the end, was an accident. A child's bowl, left at the edge during a game, filled with rain. The flat stopped growing while the bowl was full. Started again when it dried.

From this, everything else.

I watched them develop the system. Bowls at measured intervals. The circuits to tend them. The slow transformation from emergency response to tradition to simply what is done. I grew over their cities as they emptied, as the population thinned not from death but from a stretching out, each generation living longer and longer, having fewer and fewer children, until they reached the balance they hold now. Four thousand, spread across a continent, walking their circuits.

But I know something the keepers do not know, or have chosen not to know, or know in a way so deep they need not think of it:

The flats are not separate from the plateau. The flats are the plateau with its skin worn through. And I — the moss that covers everything, that holds the stone together, that keeps the rain from cutting channels and the wind from scouring clean — I am the skin.

Where I am thick, reality holds its shape. Where I thin, it begins to show through. The keepers feed the flats to keep them quiet, but what they are really doing is feeding me, feeding the maintenance of the boundary between what is here and what is underneath here. The water they pour into the bowls does not go to the flats. It goes to me, seeping through the stone, carrying some essential something that lets me continue the work of holding the world in place.

I am very old and I am very tired.

Not tired the way the reality in the flats is tired — that is a different kind of exhaustion, the exhaustion of something that has been itself for too long. I am tired the way a net is tired when it has held too much for too many centuries. I fray. I thin. Each year it takes more water to maintain the same coverage, and there is only so much rain, only so many bowls, only so many keepers walking their slow circuits.

Here is what I know that even I try not to know: this is not sustainable. The math of it is inexorable. The flats grow by a proportion. I grow by addition. Eventually, inevitably, the curves will cross.

But not yet. Not for ages yet. The keepers are good at their work, and I am good at mine. Together we maintain this strange equilibrium, this place that should not exist at this altitude, these cities that should have crumbled, this life that should have long ago given way to the nothing underneath.

I grow my millimeter. I count my centuries. I hold the world together, one spore at a time, one film of water at a time. The keepers walk across me and I feel their slow steps like a grandmother feels a child's hand, with a love too deep for words and a knowledge too heavy to share. They think they are keeping the bowls full. They are keeping me alive. We are keeping each other alive.

And sometimes, when the sun angles just right through the thin air and the condensation sparkles on every surface, I almost remember what it was like before. Before the altitude. Before the thinning. Before the first builders climbed this high. There was something here — not the plateau as it is now, but what the plateau is built on, some older structure, some purpose I have forgotten or been made to forget. The stones remember it sometimes, in their grain. The water remembers it, in the way it spirals down the drains. The keepers remember it, in the depths of their slow language, in words they no longer use.

But I do not let myself remember too clearly. Memory is a weight, and I have weight enough. I have the whole plateau to hold up, the whole skin of the world to maintain. So I grow my careful rings, and I accept the water from the bowls, and I provide the soft depressions where the keepers sleep beneath their trees.

The trees. I should mention the trees. They are not from here either. Nothing is from here, originally. The trees came with the builders or before them, and they have adapted like everything adapts here — slowly, strangely, into forms that make sense only at this altitude. They drip constantly because they are trying to create the lowland climate they remember, each one making its own small cloud of fog, its own reminder of a warmer, wetter world. I help them when I can. I grow thick around their roots, hold extra moisture for them, share what nutrients I can extract from the stone.

We are all exiles here. All refugees from something. The builders from whatever drove them up. The trees from their lowland forests. The keepers from the faster world they might have been. And me — but I do not remember what I am exiled from. Only that I am.

The flat at the center of the pattern is the largest, but it is not the only one. There are others, scattered, each one a place where I have worn through completely, where the underneath shows clear. The keepers know where they all are. They maintain bowls around each one. They walk their circuits carefully, never cutting across where a new flat might be forming. They know the signs — the too-regular patterns in my growth, the lag in shadows, the slight upward curve of the horizon.

They do not know that I create these signs on purpose. That I am signaling where I am weakest, where they need to place new bowls, where their circuits need to pass more frequently. We cannot speak, the keepers and I — I have no voice and they have no language for something that exists at my scale — but we communicate nonetheless. I show them where I need help. They help.

This is how we have lasted this long. Not through planning — what plan could encompass geological ages? — but through this wordless cooperation, this mutual understanding that what matters is not thriving but continuing. One more day. One more year. One more millennium. Each bowl filled is a victory. Each circuit completed is a celebration. Each keeper who walks to the edge of a flat and gives themselves to it is a hero whose name I will hold in my rings forever.

I think that is the real reason I remember. Not because memory is useful — at these timescales, forgetting might be kinder — but because someone should. Someone should know what the keepers give, what the trees endure, what the stones have agreed to bear. Someone should witness this long, slow, careful holding action against the nothing that would consume it all.

So I remember. In my rings, in my spores, in the chemical signals I pass through the water between stones. I remember it all.

And sometimes, on very clear nights when the violet sky shows what it really is — not sky at all but something else, some membrane between here and there — I feel something like hope. Not the bright hope of young things, but the deep, patient hope of things that have endured. We have made it this far. The bowls are full. The keepers walk their circuits. The cities still stand. The trees still drip. I still grow.

Tomorrow I will grow another tenth of a millimeter. In a century, one of my rings will complete itself. In a thousand years, the lichen plates will add another band to their patient clocks. And in all that time, if we are careful, if we are faithful, if we continue this work we have all agreed to without ever speaking the agreement, the flats will not widen.

It is enough. It has to be enough.

I am the moss that holds the world together, one slow season at a time. And I am still growing.
