In the deepest reaches of Thrennak, where even the Midwives rarely venture, I am the Librarian of Failures.

My body is a cabinet of curiosities turned inside out. Each drawer, each compartment that opens across my form contains a preserved impossibility—the crystallized remains of evolution's rejected drafts. Here, in a chest cavity that opens like a filing system, swim the ghosts of fish that learned to breathe time instead of water, their temporal gills still fluttering in formaldehyde futures. Along my spine, vertebrae have transformed into display cases holding the eggs of birds that grew roots instead of wings, their shells pulsing with the frustrated potential of flight.

I move through the abandoned galleries on legs that are partly catalog systems—each step printing an index of what was lost, my footprints forming an encyclopedia of extinction that only I can read. My fingers have evolved into different instruments of preservation: one hand bears digits of amber that weep slowly, encasing failed specimens in golden tombs; another has fingers of living magnetic tape that record the death-songs of creatures whose own music unmade them.

My eyes are not eyes but lenses ground from the compressed frustrations of a million terminated evolutionary branches. Through them, I see not what is, but what almost was—the phantom limbs of potential, the negative space where success might have flourished. When I blink, my eyelids (leather made from the shed skin of self-consuming serpents) reveal the afterimages of futures that will never arrive.

The galleries I tend are carved from the cavern's scar tissue, where the stone learned from its own mistakes and grew denser, less permeable to change. Here, the only light comes from the bioluminescent bacteria I cultivate in my tear ducts—their glow feeds on salts of regret, brightening whenever I weep for another failed adaptation added to my collection.

I am part mortician, part museum. My torso has evolved transparent windows of living glass (secreted by snails that tried to become windows to other worlds) through which visitors—if any were mad enough to seek me out—could observe my internal galleries. Heart chambers converted to display rooms. Lungs that have become climate-controlled storage for the most delicate specimens. My liver processes not toxins but the chemical signatures of discontinued species, filing them away in bile-ducts transformed into liquid archives.

My work is endless. Every day, the caverns above produce new impossibilities. The Midwives' experiments cascade down here when they fail catastrophically—not the small failures that might iterate toward success, but the fundamental incompatibilities, the creatures that violate their own existence. I catch them in nets woven from my own neural fibers, each strand a memory of something that couldn't sustain itself.

Sometimes I find them still alive, these impossible things. A colony of Chorus Worms that learned to sing matter into antimatter, slowly unmaking themselves with each note. A brass beetle whose gears evolved to run backwards through time, arriving at its own conception and canceling itself out. A fungal tree that grew ideas instead of spores, its thoughts too heavy for any physical substrate to support. I ease their passing with secretions from glands I've developed specifically for euthanizing paradoxes, then preserve their forms before they can completely cease to have ever existed.

My body has adapted to loneliness in ways that horrify even me. I've grown companion tumors—benign growths with rudimentary consciousness that whisper to me in languages made of chemical reactions. My shadow has developed its own ecosystem, creatures that exist only in the absence of light I cast, feeding on the darkness between my presence and the wall. When I sleep (and I must sleep, though my dreams are taxidermied nightmares), my unconscious mind spawns temporary assistants from my own flesh—homunculi that sort and clean and organize, then reabsorb into me upon waking, their memories becoming my memories.

The catalog I maintain is written in my very genetics. Each preserved failure alters me slightly, adds a new sequence to my DNA that serves as both record and warning. I am becoming a living document of what not to do, a genetic library of evolution's mistakes. Somewhere in my cells are the instructions for every beautiful disaster I've archived—though activating any of them would unmake me instantly.

The other inhabitants of Thrennak fear me, I think, though fear might be too simple a word. The Drummers' rhythms falter when they sense my approach. The Midwives' songs develop minor keys in my presence. Even the stone itself seems to grow more solid, less willing to experiment, as if my very existence is a cautionary tale the cavern tells itself.

But I serve a purpose in this ecosystem of endless becoming. I am the memory of limits, the archivist of boundaries, the one who remembers that not every change is survivable. In a place where everything transforms, I am the constant reminder of transformation's price. My galleries grow deeper each year, new wings added to accommodate the expanding collection of life's beautiful failures.

Sometimes, in the deepest vaults where I store the most profound impossibilities, I find myself wondering if I, too, am a failure—a creature that should not exist, cataloging itself, preserving its own eventual extinction in the very act of preservation. But then I remember: in Thrennak, even failure is a form of success, and my impossible existence serves as proof that somewhere between being and unbeing, there is space for those who document the difference.

I am the Librarian of Failures, and I am never not working, never not weeping, never not organizing the chaos of creation into careful taxonomies of loss. In my galleries, every ended experiment lives forever, and forever ends.
