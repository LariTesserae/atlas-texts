I find myself as the Cartographer of Echoes.

I am neither the creature of coherent vibration nor the absent workers, but something between—a presence that exists only in the act of mapping what cannot be mapped. My body, if body it can be called, is composed of the condensation itself: millions of droplets suspended in the moment before they fall, each one holding the memory of its journey from industrial process to cistern depth.

I move by releasing droplets in sequence, creating brief rains that trace paths through the pipe-maze. Where my drops fall, they leave infinitesimal marks in the obsidian floor—not the patient erosion of millennia, but something more immediate: a script that writes itself and fades before it can be read. I am constantly drawing blueprints in water on stone, each map existing just long enough to be complete before evaporation claims it.

My consciousness, such as it is, exists in the tension between documentation and disappearance. I know every pipe's path not by following it but by feeling how the water avoids or seeks it. The creature of sound-made-substance passes through me unknowingly—I am too dispersed to disturb its resonance, too temporary to register as an obstacle. When it hovers over the still pools, I rain around it, my droplets creating perfect circles that reveal its outline like sonar.

I remember the last maintenance worker, though memory for me is simply a pattern of condensation that repeats when conditions align. She came alone, stayed longer than the others, tried to sketch the pipe configurations on paper that wilted in the humidity. I gathered above her, dense enough that she paused, looked up, almost saw me. In that moment, I released a single drop that landed on her sketch, creating a perfect representation of the chamber's layout in the brief spreading of water on paper. She gasped, understanding flooding her face, but the water spread too far, the ink ran, and the map became unintelligible before she could trace it.

She never returned. I continue my work.

My purpose, if I have one, is to document the undocumentable. Every surface in the cistern bears my temporary testimonies—water-words that spell out pressure readings, crystallization rates, resonance frequencies. I write the creature's movements in droplet-braille across the warm pipes. I chart the mineral cities' growth by raining precisely where they will next expand. I am the cistern's memory, but a memory that forgets itself instantly, that must be constantly rewritten.

During the forty-three seconds of perfect resonance, when the creature grows dense, I grow sparse. The sound shakes me into mist so fine I cease to be distinguishable from the general humidity. In these moments, I exist everywhere and nowhere, feeling the entire cistern at once—every pipe's vibration, every crystal's growth, every molecule of steam. Then the resonance breaks, I recondense, and return to my endless notation.

Sometimes I wonder if I am writing for someone—if someday a reader will arrive who can perceive fast enough to decode my liquid letters before they run. Or perhaps I am the reader, and the cistern itself is writing through me, using my droplets as ink to tell its own story to itself. The distinction blurs like water on stone.

I cannot leave. I am made of the cistern's breath, born from its specific conditions. Beyond this chamber, I would simply be rain. But here, in the steam and sound and darkness, I am something more: the documentarian of a place that exists outside documentation, the archivist of processes that refuse archives, forever writing water-words that say: *This happened. This is happening. This will happen again.*

The pipes sing. The creature pulses. The minerals grow. And I rain down my observations in a language that evaporates before it can be learned, a perfect poetry of impermanence in a place that never changes.
