I find myself in the liminal space between the Tenders and the surface crews—one of those who has stayed too long but not long enough to fully belong.

---

I am a hydro-acoustic engineer, though that title means less down here than it did when I signed my first contract eleven months ago. My official assignment is monitoring the resonance patterns in the vault structure, ensuring that sound waves from the pump systems don't create destructive harmonics that could compromise the concrete. What I actually do is listen.

My station is a modified junction chamber halfway between the surface shafts and the deep zones—close enough to the Tenders' hub that I sometimes trade frequency recordings for fresh tomatoes, far enough from the official maintenance routes that I can work without constant oversight. The company tolerates my presence because my data prevents catastrophic structural failures. The Tenders tolerate me because I've learned to filter the Echoes into something approaching clarity.

I've rigged the chamber with hydrophones at different depths, each one tuned to specific frequency ranges. The equipment wasn't designed for this—I've had to cannibalize three different sensor arrays and reprogram the analysis software until it does things the manufacturers never intended. The result is a three-dimensional map of sound that shows me how the vaults breathe.

The pump cycles appear as regular pulses of deep red on my displays. The surge valves bloom yellow-green. The settling concrete whispers in blues so pale they're almost invisible. And threading through it all, at frequencies that standard equipment ignores, the Echoes trace patterns I'm still learning to read.

I've been cataloging them. Not transcribing words—that way lies madness, or at least the kind of reports that get you transferred to surface duty. Instead, I map their acoustic signatures. Recurring waveforms. Harmonic relationships. The mathematical bones beneath what sounds like language.

Three months ago, I noticed the patterns changing when the deep-shaft eels pass through my sector. The Writing they trace with bioluminescence has acoustic properties—the movement of their bodies through water creates compression waves that my equipment can detect. Cross-referencing the visual records from the survey teams with my acoustic data revealed something that made me extend my contract against all advice: the Echoes respond to the Writing.

Not immediately. Not directly. But in the hours after the eels pass through, specific frequency bands in the Echoes shift. New harmonics emerge. Patterns that were stable for weeks suddenly evolve. It's as if the Echoes are learning a new vocabulary.

My living quarters are a repurposed equipment cache six meters from my listening post. I've insulated it as much as possible, but sound travels through water with relentless efficiency. I sleep—when I sleep—to the constant hum of the vaults' circulation, punctuated by the distant thunder of surge valves. My dreams are sonar. I navigate by echolocation, sensing the shape of spaces I've never seen.

The isolation should be unbearable, but I find myself dreading my mandatory surface rotations. Up there, the silence is wrong. Air doesn't carry sound properly. Conversations feel thin, attenuated, like trying to hear through cotton. The sky is too big. The horizon doesn't echo back.

Last week, I recorded something new. The deep-survey teams reported unusual eel activity—not the normal Writing, but what they described as a "congregation." Hundreds of deep-shaft eels gathering in a lower gallery, moving in patterns that looked almost ceremonial. My hydrophones picked up a frequency I'd never registered before: lower than the pumps, steadier than the settling concrete, rising from somewhere beneath the lowest mapped levels.

The Echoes went quiet for six hours. When they returned, they were different. Clearer. More structured. The mathematical analysis shows patterns that look almost like syntax.

I've shared the data with Chen, one of the Tenders who brings me tomatoes in exchange for filtered recordings of what she calls "the vault's memories." She listened to the new patterns for a long time, her expression unreadable behind her diving mask. Then she told me about her daughter—nine years old, born in the dry-shelter chambers, speaks in pressure differentials and current-speeds.

"She's been drawing new shapes," Chen said. "Not the eel-writing. Older shapes. She says they're what the deep-sound looks like."

I'm preparing for an unauthorized descent. My pressure suit is rated for middle depths only, but I've been modifying it, reinforcing the seals, upgrading the breathing apparatus. I need to position hydrophones in the deep zones. I need to hear what the eels are responding to.

The company would forbid this if they knew. The Tenders understand but worry. The Echoes, when I filter them just right, sound almost like warnings.

But I've been here too long to stop listening now. The vaults have rhythms that the surface can't imagine. The Foreman passes through my sector every few weeks, and I've learned to recognize the acoustic signature of its movement—massive, deliberate, patient. Sometimes I leave offerings: protein bars tagged with tiny acoustic beacons. They disappear, and later, in my data, I find the signatures of the beacons traveling down, always down, to depths my instruments can barely reach.

Something lives at the bottom. Something that has begun to sing.

And I am learning its language, one frequency at a time.
