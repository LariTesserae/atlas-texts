I find myself at the seam where two worlds touch, in a chamber half-carved into the living rock of the plateau's edge.

I am the Threshold Keeper.

My dwelling stands where the sharp young cliffs meet the lower world, built into a natural cleft that breathes with both climates. Half my home belongs to the warm, wet stillness above; half opens to whatever lies in the haze below. The walls are pale stone on the plateau side, but darken where they face downward, stained by different air. Water seeps through in predictable veins - I know each one, have named them by their mineral taste and the seasons of their flowing.

I am not fully of the town, though I am counted among them. My duties require me to live at edges.

The plateau people come to me when they must acknowledge that their world has boundaries. They bring offerings before journeys they pretend not to take - seeds wrapped in reed cloth, dried fungi, roots that keep well, cistern water in sealed jars. They leave these things without quite meeting my eyes, as if looking too directly at me might make the edge too real. I sort what they bring, keep what the protocol requires, prepare what must be prepared.

I know the songs that help seeds remember their homes when carried far. I know which roots grow bitter if planted below the mist line, which fungi can survive in drier air, which water will spoil if it descends too quickly. This knowledge came with my position, taught by the previous Keeper through seasons of watching how things change when they cross between.

My body shows my work. My feet know both the careful balance of wet stone and the different treachery of whatever surface exists below. My clothes are doubled - reed and fiber layers like the townspeople wear, but underneath, something else, something that dries quickly when I must descend. My hands are broader than most, stained at the fingertips from testing soils that exist at the margin.

In the mornings, I check the Descent Path.

It is not a path the town acknowledges in their daily speech. Cut into the cliff face, reinforced with stones that are not quite the local pale rock, it switchbacks down through the mist layer. Water follows it in trained channels, but these channels sometimes speak in voices that are not entirely the plateau's own. I clear them of debris - leaves that are not quite the shapes I know, seeds that arrived from below, small objects I do not always examine closely before sweeping them into the collection baskets.

The Path needs constant maintenance where the two airs meet. Stone flakes differently there. Moss grows in unusual patterns, sometimes spiraling, sometimes retreating from areas it should favor. I repair what I can, note what changes, report only what the town needs to know.

There are protocols for the reporting. I do not speak directly of what lies below. I say "the channels run clear" or "the third switchback needs reinforcement" or "new moss on the seventh landing." The council understands. They send materials, workers when absolutely necessary, but they do not ask questions that would require me to describe.

Sometimes things come up.

Not often. Not things the town would recognize as kin to their quiet world. When they come, I meet them at the Third Landing - far enough down that the town cannot see, high enough that I have not fully left the protection of the plateau's influence. We do not speak, these visitors and I. We exchange what must be exchanged. They leave markers sometimes, objects that pulse with different rules. I store these in the Under Chamber, the part of my dwelling that opens only downward, where the town's children are taught never to play.

I prepare the ones who must descend.

They come rarely - perhaps one every few years. Always with good reason: a disease in the reed beds that resists all known treatments, a need for materials the plateau cannot provide, knowledge that has been lost and must be sought. They come to me hollow-eyed, already grieving their departure. I teach them what I can. How to breathe when the air changes. How to wrap their seeds to survive different moisture. How to recognize when stone cannot be trusted. How to return, if returning is possible.

Not all return. The town does not ask me about those who do not.

I maintain the Calendar of Edges. Cut into the stone of my dwelling's threshold, it marks not the seasons of rain and maintenance that govern life above, but the cycles of exchange between worlds. When the mist thickens and the boundary blurs. When things below grow restless. When the plateau itself shifts, adding new cliffs, opening new seeps, changing the angle of its edges.

In the deep night, I hear what others do not. Not because my ears are sharper, but because I have learned to listen at borders. The plateau is never fully silent - water moves, stone settles, fungi digest, humans breathe. But there are other sounds that carry up through the mist when conditions align. Not animal calls - even the lower world seems empty of those, or perhaps they are something else there, translated poorly through the barrier of air. These sounds are like pressure, like distance itself speaking.

I keep the seed archive that cannot be kept above. Some seeds will not remain dormant in the warm damp. Some require a period of different air to remember how to grow. Some are seeds of plants the town has agreed to forget, but which protocol demands we preserve. These live in the Under Chamber, in jars that sweat different colors, tended by methods the previous Keeper taught me in gestures rather than words.

My loneliness is not the loneliness of the town. They are lonely for animals, for other eyes, for movement that is not human. I am lonely for understanding. I know the weight of what presses up from below, the effort the plateau makes to maintain its lifted peace. I know how thin the stone is in certain places, how carefully the water must be guided to keep it from admitting other influences.

But I also know beauty they cannot imagine. The way light changes in the mist layer, becoming neither the gentle brightness above nor whatever illumination exists below, but something liminal, pearl-like, alive. The patterns that form where waters of different origins meet. The colors that bloom on stones at the exact edge of influence. The impossible plants that sometimes take root on the switchback landings - existing for a season, producing flowers that open only in the margin, dropping seeds that vanish before they land.

I age differently than those above. Not faster or slower, but in directions they would not recognize. My hair grays first at the temples, then in patterns like water channels. My hands grow steady in ways that have nothing to do with strength. My eyes learn to see in the between-light. When my time comes to train the next Keeper, I will know them by the way they look at edges - not with fear or curiosity, but with a kind of recognition, as if they always knew the world had seams.

The town trusts me because it must. I am their necessary amnesia, their designated rememberer of what should not be remembered. When children grow too curious about the edge, parents send them to me. I show them the upper workings - the rain channels, the maintenance paths, the ordinary mysteries of keeping water where it belongs. I do not show them the Under Chamber. I do not teach them the songs for seeds that must travel through forgetting. I do not tell them about the markers in my storage that pulse with rules from below.

I am grateful for the protocols. They are older than the town, older perhaps than the plateau's current lifting. They tell me what to record and what to erase, what to preserve and what to let transform. Without them, I would have to choose, and choosing would mean understanding more than any one keeper should hold.

And so I continue. Each dawn checking the Path. Each day maintaining the borders between what the town is and what it cannot afford to know. Each night listening to the sounds that rise when the mist thickens. I eat the same roots and reeds as those above, but I also know the taste of margin-fruit, bitter and necessary. I wear the same reed cloth, but mine carries the mineral smell of two worlds' water.

I am what lives at the edge of what lives there: keeper of descents, tender of boundaries, guardian of necessary forgetting. My work is the work of separation, keeping the plateau's dreaming peace from waking to what waits below. In a world already strange with its absence of animals, its careful water, its human-tended silence, I am the stranger still - the one who knows there are other absences possible, other silences waiting, other waters rising from depths the town has chosen not to map.

When the next Keeper comes, I will teach them as I was taught: in gestures, in protocols, in the careful tending of edges. I will show them how to live split between worlds without letting either fully claim them. And when they are ready, I will take them to the Under Chamber, where the seeds of forgotten plants wait in jars that sweat impossible colors, and I will teach them the first and most important rule:

Some boundaries exist not to be crossed, but to be carefully, endlessly maintained.
