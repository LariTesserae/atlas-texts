# The Vertical Territories

You become aware of your weight differently here. Not just the physical fact of it—though you feel suddenly overlarge, oversolid, a thing built for flat ground trying to exist in three dimensions—but the *commitment* of it. Each step on the woven platforms requires trust that the builders knew what they were doing. Each reach for a branch-rail means accepting that your entire life now depends on something that grew without any obligation to hold you.

The woman who approached you is called something like *Sings-Above-the-Morning-Birds*, though this is your approximation. Her actual identifier is a specific whistle-tone combined with a chest-drum pattern that means *female-elder-who-teaches*. She has taken you as a project, though you're not sure if this is personal curiosity or communal obligation. Perhaps there's no difference here.

She shows you the safe paths first. Not by pointing—the sounded rarely point, you notice, perhaps because direction means little when you navigate by sound—but by walking them slowly, clicking steadily like a metronome so you can follow. The main branch-road from her family's platform winds upward through a series of natural steps where massive limbs intersect. Some sections have guide-ropes. Others are worn smooth by generations of passage, the bark polished to dark shine.

You learn that there are rules. Never step on the outer third of any branch, where the wood is younger, more flexible, more likely to bend or break under unfamiliar weight. Never grab the rope-guides with full force—they're for balance, not support. Never move during the evening bird-chorus, when the cacophony reaches levels that make it impossible to hear warnings.

And always, always announce yourself. A click before stepping onto someone's platform. A whistle before using a public branch-road. A chest-drum when passing near the hollows where families might be resting. You are large, you are strange, you are potentially dangerous. The least you can do is be *locatable*.

---

Your teacher has infinite patience for your failures of sound but none for your failures of attention. When you drift, overwhelmed by the sensory assault of the canopy, she makes a sharp bilateral click that snaps you back. When you try to speak over the chorus instead of finding the gaps, she covers her ears theatrically, face scrunched in mock pain. When you finally manage a clean whistle that carries fifty meters to where her grandson waits, and he whistles back in acknowledgment, her whole body brightens—a subtle shift in posture that you're beginning to read as joy.

The boy—*Drums-Like-Rainfall*, approximately—becomes your second teacher. He's perhaps twelve years old, fearless on the branches, amused by your careful movements. He shows you the food paths: the productive epiphyte gardens, the bark-beetle colonies that can be harvested sustainably, the hidden pools where rainwater collects in tree-hollows and small crustaceans breed. His whistle-speech is rapid, playful, full of jokes you don't understand but that make his age-mates laugh when they pass.

Through him, you begin to grasp the vertical territories. Each family group maintains not a horizontal space but a *column* of canopy, from the higher sun-platforms down to the darker understory levels. Resources at different heights fruit at different times. A family might spend the dry season in the upper reaches, following the nectar-birds, then descend during the rains to harvest the fungi that bloom in the humidity of the middle levels. It's not migration—they never leave their territory—but a constant vertical circulation, like blood moving through a standing body.

The boundaries between territories are marked by sound. Each morning, at least one member of each family group performs what you've come to think of as the *location-chant*: a complex vocal sequence that announces *we are here, we are this many, we are well*. Neighboring groups respond in turn, and for a few minutes the human sounds rise above even the animal chorus, a web of audio that maps the current occupation of the canopy. You learn to wake for this, to lie still and try to count the voices, to guess distances and directions.

---

There are specialists among them. *Remembers-All-Knots* is a middle-aged woman whose fingers are never still, always working fiber, testing tension. She maintains the major rope-ways, replacing sections before they wear through, somehow keeping a mental map of every cord in a territory that spans cubic kilometers. When a rope fails—and they do fail, dramatically, with a whip-crack that silences even the insects—she knows exactly which route has been lost, which families are now cut off from which resources.

*Hunts-In-Silence* is barely visible even when you know where to look. They (the sounded have a third gender-designation, marked by a glottal stop you can't quite produce) move through the canopy like smoke, carrying a short spear and a net bag that sometimes comes back full of meat. They communicate mainly in gesture when near others, as if preserving their voice for the hunt. You've watched them freeze mid-step at some signal you couldn't perceive, then flow into motion so fluid it looks like falling upward, returning an hour later with something that bleeds dark and unfamiliar.

*Knows-The-Deep* is old, older than anyone else you've met, with milky cataracts that should mean blindness but don't seem to slow her down. She navigates by sound alone, her click-tongue almost constant, echolocating through the canopy with eerie precision. But her real value is her memory: she knows the location of every sinkhole, every passage that leads down to the stone-darkness below. She knows because she's been there. Her skin-markings include a pattern you've seen nowhere else—a series of dots that start at her throat and disappear beneath her chest-wrap. Underground, she signs when you ask. *Down-dark-stone-water-coming-back-different*.

The children learn from all of them, but not in any system you recognize as school. A child interested in rope-work follows the rope-keepers for a season. A child who shows aptitude for the hunt shadows the hunters. Knowledge passes through proximity, through repetition, through the gradual accumulation of skills that survival here requires. By the time they're adolescents, most can do a little of everything—weave a platform, set a snare, identify which insects are eating-good and which are medicine-good and which are the-shaking-death-good.

---

You're starting to dream in sound.

Not words, not language, but the raw patterns of the canopy. Your sleeping mind sorts through the day's audio, trying to categorize, to find meaning. You wake thinking you heard someone call your name, but realize it was just a bird whose cry happened to hit the frequencies of human speech. You dream of falling, but what terrifies you is not the drop but the way your scream is swallowed by the chorus, leaving no trace.

One morning you wake to unusual quiet and think something's wrong—predator-silence, sickness-silence—but *Sings-Above-the-Morning-Birds* laughs when she sees your panic. Rain's coming, she explains. The animals know. They're conserving energy for the storm.

The storm, when it comes, is unlike anything in your experience. The canopy *roars* under the impact of water. Streams form instantly on every surface, waterfalls cascading from level to level. The temperature drops just enough to raise goosebumps. The sounded retreat to the sheltered portions of their platforms, but they don't seem distressed. If anything, they're excited. As soon as the rain steadies to something merely torrential, groups of children race out onto the wet branch-roads, sliding and tumbling in controlled falls that make your heart stop. The adults call warnings that are half-hearted at best. This is play, you realize. This is joy.

*Drums-Like-Rainfall* finds you huddled under a woven overhang and tugs your hand. Come, his gestures say. *Come see*.

You follow him out into the storm. The rain is warm, soaking through your clothing instantly. He leads you up, higher than you've been before, to where the branch-road opens onto a natural platform formed by the crown of one of the great trees. Here, above most of the canopy, you can see the storm as the sounded see it: a living thing moving across their world, clouds below you and around you, lightning striking the highland peaks in the distance.

The boy whoops—a pure sound of exhilaration that cuts through even the rain-roar. Others answer from nearby platforms, their calls dopplering through the storm-sound, and for a moment you understand: they are locating each other in the tempest, yes, but they are also celebrating it. The rain brings growth. Growth brings food. Food brings another season of survival in this vertical universe.

You try to whoop back. The sound is thin, wrong, but *Drums-Like-Rainfall* grins at the attempt. He shows you how to cup your hands, how to use your chest cavity as a resonance chamber. It takes a dozen tries before you produce something that carries, and when an answering call comes from across the storm-dimmed distance, you feel something shift in your chest. Not belonging—not yet, maybe never—but presence. You have a position in the chord, temporary and tentative, but real.

---

Later, in the relative quiet of after-storm, when the canopy drips and steams and the insects resume their drilling songs, *Sings-Above-the-Morning-Birds* sits with you on the platform edge. She's working on something—a decorated band that might be for wearing or might be for hanging—and you're practicing the basic whistle-scales she's been teaching you.

She stops suddenly, listening. Her face changes—not alarm, but attention. She makes a complex gesture: *stay-quiet-watch*.

A group is approaching through the canopy. You hear them before you see them—their travel-whistles creating a moving audio-signature that announces their path. But something's different. The rhythm is wrong. There are pauses where there shouldn't be pauses.

They come into view: six adults carrying someone on a woven stretcher, moving carefully along the branch-roads. The injured person (you can't determine gender from this distance) is silent, which is wrong. An injured person should be making sound—pain-sounds, location-sounds, *I-am-alive* sounds. This silence is ominous.

The group passes without acknowledging your platform. *Sings-Above-the-Morning-Birds* waits until they're out of sight, then makes a low, thrumming whistle you haven't heard before. She repeats it three times, waits, then stands.

"Come," she says, using the pidgin you've developed between you. "You learn now the other song."

You follow her to a different platform, one you haven't visited before. It's older, more integrated with the tree, sections of floor indistinguishable from natural bark. A handful of elders sit in a circle, and at their center—

You smell it before you understand what you're seeing. Sweet-rot, herb-smoke, something chemical and sharp. The injured person lies on a bed of woven ferns, and now you see why they were silent: the massive tear along their ribcage, the white-yellow of exposed bone. A predator strike, probably. One of the silence-hunters catching them between breaths.

*Knows-The-Deep* is here, grinding something in a stone bowl. The others are humming—not quite singing, not quite chanting, but creating a sustained drone that seems to make the air itself thick. They're not trying to heal, you realize. The wound is too severe. They're doing something else.

The injured person's breathing changes—shallow, rapid, then nothing, then a gasp that pulls all the air out of the shelter. The elders' hum intensifies. *Sings-Above-the-Morning-Birds* joins the circle, gestures for you to stay back, and adds her voice to the drone.

What happens next you will never adequate describe. The dying person opens their mouth and *sings*. Not words, not melody, but a pure tone that starts low and rises, rises, rises beyond what human vocal cords should produce. The elders match pitch when they can, harmonize when they can't, creating intervals that make your teeth ache. The dying person's eyes are

 wide, unseeing, but their voice is impossibly strong. They sing their location—not in space, but in the community, in the web of relationships and remembered sounds. They sing their mother's morning call and their child's laugh and the whistle of their favorite rope-road and the click-pattern of rain on their birth-tree.

It goes on too long. It goes on until you think they must be dead, singing with empty lungs, but still the sound continues. The elders weave their voices through it, supporting it, lifting it, making spaces for it to rest before rising again. Other sounded have gathered outside the shelter, adding their voices from a distance, the whole platform resonating with human sound.

And then, between one breath and the next, it stops. The silence is total—even the insects seem to pause. The elders hold it for a long moment, then release a collective sigh that sounds like wind through leaves.

*Knows-The-Deep* opens her blind eyes and speaks a single word in the formal language. The others repeat it. Then, moving with practiced efficiency, they begin preparing the body for what comes next.

---

There is no burial in the canopy. There is no burning. There is the giving-back.

They carry the wrapped body to a specific tree—not one of the great giants but something smaller, hungrier, with aerial roots like waterfalls of wood. The wrapping is minimal, designed to hold together just long enough. They place the body in a natural hollow where the roots cage an opening, then seal it with a mixture of sap and fiber that will break down in the rains.

Within days, the tree will begin its work. Within a season, there will be nothing left but the nutrients cycling through wood and leaf, lifting skyward, becoming part of the eternal forest. The sounded do not visit these trees. They do not mark them. But they know where they are, and sometimes, on quiet mornings, you can hear someone whistle-calling toward one of them—not expecting an answer, just placing their voice where a loved one's voice once was.

*Sings-Above-the-Morning-Birds* teaches you the mourning-whistle, though she hopes you'll never need it. It's a simple pattern, two notes a fourth apart, repeated three times. It means *I remember your sound*. It means *the chord is less without you*. It means *the forest continues*.

---

You've been here a full season when the invitation comes.

*Drums-Like-Rainfall* delivers it, bouncing with excitement. The far-holders are coming for trade-gathering. Everyone will be there. All the families, all the territories, meeting at the neutral platforms near the crown of Grandfather Tree (which is not actually the oldest tree, just the tallest in this region). It happens once a year, timed to some celestial event you don't quite understand but which the elders can predict by watching where certain birds build their nests.

The preparation takes weeks. Each family creates trade goods specific to their territory: *Remembers-All-Knots* works a rope so fine it's nearly thread, dyed with berries that grow only in her family's understory. *Hunts-In-Silence* prepares packets of dried meat, each wrapped in leaves that preserve and flavor. The children gather resin tears from their home-trees, each with its unique scent-signature.

You have nothing to trade, but *Sings-Above-the-Morning-Birds* sets you to preparing carry-baskets. Your fingers are still clumsy with the local materials, but you're strong, and can create frames sturdy enough to survive the journey. She checks your work with an expression that might be pride.

The travel itself is a revelation. You've grown comfortable in your adopted territory, learned its paths and platforms, but now you see how vast the canopy truly is. The family group (and you are included, temporarily, provisionally, but included) moves in a loose line along branch-roads you've never seen, whistling their travel-song to warn others of their passing. Sometimes another group's song intersects yours, and there's a moment of polyphonic chaos before both groups adjust, finding harmonies that allow both songs to coexist.

At night, you sleep on platforms that belong to no one and everyone. These way-stations are basic but well-maintained, with rain-shelters and rope-storage and always, always, multiple escape routes. The sounded post no guards, but someone is always awake, listening. You take a turn on watch and spend the hours trying to categorize the night sounds, failing more often than succeeding.

On the third day, the trees begin to change. They're even larger here, if that's possible, their trunks like curved walls, their branches creating highways wider than any you've seen. The traffic increases—groups converging from all directions, their travel-songs creating a constant audio map of movement. Children dart between the adult groups, playing complex games of tag that span three dimensions. Adolescents show off, taking risky routes, earning clicks of disapproval from their elders that don't quite hide amusement.

And then you see it: Grandfather Tree.

It's not just larger—it's architecturally different. Its crown spreads like a hand against the sky, creating a vast interior space sheltered but not enclosed. Platforms ring its trunk at different levels, connected by spiraling walkways that follow the tree's natural growth. Light filters through in columns, green-gold, constantly shifting. The sound here is different too—still overwhelming but somehow more musical, as if the shape of the space creates natural harmonics.

The gathering is already underway. Hundreds of sounded move through the vertical space, their combined voices creating a chord so complex your brain gives up trying to process it. Groups cluster on different platforms, displaying their goods, exchanging not just materials but information—whistling news of births and deaths, of weather patterns and animal movements, of changes in the forest that affect everyone.

You stay close to your adopted family at first, overwhelmed. But *Drums-Like-Rainfall* won't let you hide. He drags you from group to group, introducing you in whistle-speech as *Learns-to-Hear-Us*, which is apparently what you're called now. The reactions vary—curiosity, suspicion, amusement. One elder makes you demonstrate your basic whistles and nods approval when you manage them. A group of adolescents try to teach you their local click-dialect, laughing when your attempts come out as gibberish.

But what strikes you most is how they incorporate you. Not fully—you're still an oddity, still outside the true community of sound—but they make space. When the evening chorus begins (and it's deafening here, with so many human voices added to the eternal animal song), someone always ensures you're included in a sound-circle. When food is shared, you're given a portion without having to ask. When the children organize races through the canopy, they create a category for "ground-walkers" so you can participate in your own clumsy way.

---

On the second night of the gathering, something shifts.

It starts with the drums. Not the chest-drums you know, but something deeper—hollowed logs struck with wrapped mallets, producing tones that carry through flesh and bone. The rhythm is simple at first, a heartbeat. Then layers add: clicked sticks, struck stones, the swoosh of seed-pod rattles. The sounded begin to move.

It's not dance as you know it. It's vertical dance, three-dimensional, using the platforms and branch-roads and rope-ways as much as floor space. Bodies flow up and down, swinging and climbing and leaping in patterns that follow the drum-patterns. And they sing—not the location-songs or work-songs you know, but something older, more primal.

You watch from a platform edge until *Sings-Above-the-Morning-Birds* finds you. Her face is flushed, her usual composure replaced by something wilder. She pulls you up, guides your hands to a rope. "Move," she says in her language. "Just move."

So you do. Clumsily at first, barely keeping rhythm. But the drums are insistent, physical, impossible to resist. You find yourself swaying, then stepping, then climbing in rough time to the beat. Others swirl around you, above and below, their movements creating wind that carries scent and sound. Someone passes you a gourd of something fermented, sharp and sweet. The night blurs.

At some point—you've lost track of time—you find yourself on a high platform with a group of strangers, all of you breathing hard, grinning. One of them makes a joke in whistle-speech you almost understand. You whistle back something that's probably nonsense but makes them laugh anyway. Below, the drums continue, but up here it's quieter, cooler. Someone starts a simple melody. Others join, harmonizing. You hum along when you can find the pitch, fall silent when you can't.

The sun rises without fanfare, painting the canopy gold. The gathering continues for another day, but quieter now, people moving with the careful precision of the mildly hungover. Trades are finalized. Children are promised between families for apprenticeships. The news-singers make rounds, ensuring everyone knows the critical information—which territories have sickness, where the fruit is failing, what new predators have been spotted.

And then, as gradually as they came, the families disperse. Your group leaves mid-morning, loaded with new goods and new stories. The journey home feels shorter, or maybe you're just better at it now. Your feet find the branch-paths with less hesitation. Your whistle-calls are answered without repetition.

---

When you return to the home territory, something has changed.

Not in the forest—it's eternal, unchanging except in its constant small changes. Not in the sounded—they slip back into their routines as if the gathering never happened. The change is in how you exist here.

You have a sound now. Not just the name *Learns-to-Hear-Us*, but an actual position in the local chord. When the morning location-chants begin, there's a space for your voice—tentative, imperfect, but expected. When work-groups form, you're included without question. When children play, they expect you to listen and respond, even if your responses are still foreign-accented.

You're not sounded. You may never be, fully. Your ears will never parse the layers of meaning in a whistle-phrase the way someone born to this can. Your body will never move through the canopy with the thoughtless ease of those who learned to climb before they could properly walk. But you have a place here, a function, a set of relationships defined by sound and proximity and shared survival.

One evening, sitting on the platform edge with *Sings-Above-the-Morning-Birds*, you ask her why they accepted you.

She's quiet for a long time, listening to something in the distance—a family group moving to their night-roost, you think. Then she makes a gesture you've learned means *thinking-carefully*.

"The forest," she says finally, using the simple words you share, "always makes room. Trees grow between trees. Birds nest where they can. We"—she includes you in the gesture—"we sound where there is space to sound. You made a new space. We had to learn to hear it."

She goes back to her weaving. You sit in comfortable silence, listening to the eternal chorus. Somewhere in the distance, a child practices whistle-scales. Somewhere else, *Hunts-In-Silence* returns from a successful catch, their specific foot-rhythm recognizable even at distance. Somewhere, everywhere, the forest breathes and sounds and continues.

You think about the life you left below, in the quiet lands where sound is punctuation rather than medium. It feels like a memory from someone else's existence. You think about staying here forever, growing old in the canopy, your voice eventually joining the giving-back trees. The thought doesn't frighten you anymore.

A night bird calls, its voice a perfect fourth above your comfortable range. Without thinking, you whistle back, harmonizing. The bird adjusts, finds a new interval. You follow. For a moment, you and this non-human stranger create something that belongs to neither of you and both of you—a temporary beauty in the overwhelming song of the world.

This is what the sounded know, you realize. Not how to stand out from the chorus, but how to join it. Not how to be heard, but how to listen. Not how to claim space, but how to find the space that already exists, waiting for your particular voice.

The bird flies on. You let the silence resettle around you, knowing it's not really silence at all. Just a different part of the song.

Tomorrow, you'll help *Remembers-All-Knots* repair the southeast rope-way. You'll listen to *Drums-Like-Rainfall*'s latest adventures. You'll practice your whistle-scales until your throat aches. You'll continue learning to hear.

And in the morning, when the location-chants begin, you'll add your voice to the chord that maps this impossible, vertical, singing world.

You are learning to be sounded.
