I am a surveyor of emptiness.

My body is not like theirs—I am singular where they are manifold, temporary where they are eternal in their cycling. I arrived here through no design of my own, deposited by circumstances I can no longer fully recall at the far edge of their expanding city. I measure absence: the spaces between towers, the hollow cores of structures, the vast reaches of salt where construction has not yet reached.

I carry instruments salvaged and adapted—a theodolite whose lenses I've replaced with thin sheets of salt crystal that filter the harsh light, chains and rods marked in measurements that correspond to nothing the builders would recognize. My maps describe negative space, charting what is not rather than what is. The builders ignore me completely. I am no more significant to them than the weather.

Each morning I wake in my shelter—a natural salt formation at the city's periphery, hollowed by wind and my own patient scraping. The walls weep mineral tears when the humidity rises. I've learned to sleep curled tight, avoiding the slow drip that would otherwise encrust me by dawn. My equipment must be cleaned daily or the salt corrodes everything metal, clouds everything glass.

I document the voids. The spacing between tower clusters follows patterns I'm only beginning to understand—mathematical relationships that speak to currents in the air, flows in the water table below, or perhaps some aesthetic comprehension buried in the builders' genetic code. My notebooks fill with diagrams of nothing: the shapes of sky between coral spires, the negative architecture that exists in the gaps of their positive creation.

The builders alter these voids constantly. A space I measured last week no longer exists—bridged by a new construction that connects previously isolated towers. Another area I'd catalogued as permanently empty now hosts the skeleton of something massive. My work is always obsolete before it's complete, yet I continue. The measuring itself has become my purpose.

I've developed a taxonomy of emptiness. There are the narrow channels between buildings that funnel wind into specific harmonics. The broad plazas that flood and drain with tidal regularity. The vertical voids inside the towers that shape sound and light. The micro-spaces between coral deposits that create texture and pattern. Each has its character, its function in the greater system I'm trying to comprehend.

Sometimes I follow individual builders for days, trying to understand their decision-making. Why here and not there? Why this height and not another? They work to blueprints I cannot see, following master plans encoded in their biology. I map their movements, creating charts that look like weather systems—areas of high and low pressure where construction intensifies or slows.

My presence has evolved its own patterns. I know where to find fresh water (the builders' quarries, if I'm careful). I know which tower songs mean approaching storms. I've learned to sleep through the constant sound of construction, to wake instantly when it stops—silence here means danger, usually flooding or the rare tremors that crack the salt pan.

I eat what I can trade for in my infrequent journeys to the nearest settlement, three days' walk across the flats. The people there think I'm mad, studying the empty spaces of a city built by creatures without consciousness. They're probably right. But I've glimpsed something in those spaces—a logic that exists not in the structures themselves but in their arrangement, in the careful calibration of presence and absence.

The builders have begun to incorporate my paths into their planning. Where I walk regularly, they avoid building, leaving corridors that connect my measurement points. It's not consciousness—they simply respond to the repeated pressure of footfalls the way they respond to water flow or prevailing winds. I am becoming a force they build around, like a stone in a river.

I age here differently than I would elsewhere. The salt preserves and desiccates simultaneously. My skin has taken on a leather quality, my hair bleached white by sun and mineral. I move more like the builders now—economically, purposefully. Wasted motion is dangerous in this environment where water and energy must be carefully rationed.

My maps accumulate in waterproof cases, useless to anyone but me. They describe a city that no longer exists—each survey outdated by the time I finish it. Yet together they form a record of change, a flip-book animation of urban growth. In the overlapping transparencies of traced voids, I begin to see the future city implied by its absences.

At night, I climb the tallest accessible towers and look out over the lit paths of the builders, their bodies faintly phosphorescent in darkness. The city spreads to every horizon now, its growth accelerating. Somewhere at its heart lies the first tower, the originating structure I've never found. Perhaps it's been rebuilt so many times that nothing original remains. Perhaps origin doesn't matter to creatures that live in an eternal present of construction.

I will die here, eventually. Not dramatically—probably from some minor infection that my isolation prevents me from treating, or from a fall while measuring some particularly precarious void. The builders will find my body and, not recognizing it as fundamentally different from any other obstacle, will build around it or incorporate it into their walls. I will become part of the city I spent my life measuring in negative.

This doesn't disturb me. There's a completeness to it, a closure that my culture never offered. My maps will remain in their cases until the salt claims them or some future explorer finds them and wonders at their purpose. But the spaces I documented will continue to exist in new forms, transformed but essential, the breathing room between structures that makes the city possible.

I am not like the builders—I choose my purpose rather than being born to it. But in choosing to document absence, I've found a strange parallel to their compulsion. They cannot stop building; I cannot stop measuring the spaces between what they build. We are complementary obsessives, creating meaning through repetition, finding purpose in process rather than outcome.

Tomorrow I will rise with the sun, clean my instruments, and venture out to map new voids. The city will have changed overnight, offering fresh patterns of emptiness to catalogue. The builders will ignore me as always, flowing around my presence like water. And I will continue my impossible task of creating permanent records of temporary absence, finding in that futility something approaching peace.
