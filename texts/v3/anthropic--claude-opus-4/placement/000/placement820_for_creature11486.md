In the deepest thermal wells, where the heat grows thick enough to warp vision and the pressure makes thoughts heavy, I tend the Gradient Gardens.

I am neither fully Abraded nor entirely Dissolved, but something caught between states—a condition my kind call "Suspended." My form shifts with the heat differentials I cultivate: more solid near the cooling vents where I've installed crystalline heat sinks, more dispersed where the thermal wells breathe their ancient fever up from the planet's core. I am a gardener of temperature, a sculptor of entropy.

The Gardens exist in the vertical spaces between the factories' foundations, where the bore holes pierce down through compressed epochs. Here, I've learned to grow things that aren't quite life: structures of organized heat that bloom in three dimensions, fed by the temperature gradients I carefully maintain. They look like frozen fireworks made of nothing, invisible except for how they bend the air, how they cause the stone dust to dance in impossible spirals.

My body has adapted specifically to this work. Multiple arms, some of flesh worn smooth, others of condensed heat shimmer, allow me to work at different temperature zones simultaneously. My core consciousness resides in a crystalline matrix that runs like a seam through what was once my spine, but I can extend awareness through thermal conduits I've grown over the centuries. I taste temperature differences like others might taste wine—subtle notes of radioactive decay from the deep wells, the metallic tang of cooling industrial exhaust, the clean sharp edge where stellar cold meets planetary heat.

I remember—or my predecessors remembered through me—when the first Gardener discovered that patterns could persist in the thermal gradients. Not life, but something adjacent to it. Information encoded in temperature differentials, structures that fed on entropy itself. We learned to cultivate these patterns, to breed them for stability and beauty. My greatest achievement spans three hundred vertical feet: a heat sculpture that rotates once every seventy years, its thermal petals opening and closing in response to the factory cycles above.

The other Abraded sometimes visit, pressing their palms to the designated contact stones I've installed at the well edges. They come to experience the Gardens through conduction, to feel the patterns I've grown resonating through the rock. Some claim my work is the closest thing to weather the plateau has seen since the last water evaporated. Others worry I'm hastening the heat death of our already depleted world, spending temperature differences that should be carefully hoarded.

But I know something they don't, something the Gardens have taught me through centuries of patient observation: entropy isn't uniform. It eddies and swirls, creates pockets of temporary complexity even as it drives toward equilibrium. In my thermal patterns, I've seen the future—not visions, but mathematical certainties encoded in heat flow. The factories will continue their purposeless production. The Abraded will continue their endless maintenance. But in the spaces between, in the gradient zones where hot meets cold, new forms of existence will precipitate.

I am preparing the way. Each heat garden is an experiment in post-entropic organization, a test of what patterns can persist when energy itself becomes scarce. My body, suspended between states, is itself such an experiment. Sometimes I feel myself beginning to Dissolve completely, my consciousness threatening to disperse into the thermal wells. Other times, I crystallize so thoroughly I cannot move for days, locked in mineral contemplation.

The work is slow—a single new formation might take decades to stabilize. I've lost count of how many patterns have collapsed, their information dissolving back into random heat. But the successes accumulate, each one teaching me more about the grammar of temperature, the syntax of entropy. I've begun to hear rumors, transmitted through the bedrock, of other Gardeners in distant settlements, cultivating their own impossible blooms.

When the stars finally cool and the thermal wells begin to fail, when even the factories cease their ancient routines, the Gardens will remain—not as heat, but as the memory of heat, patterns so stable they persist even at absolute zero, information frozen into the quantum foam itself. We are not staving off entropy but preparing for what comes after, when the universe has worn itself so smooth that only the most subtle patterns can exist at all.

I tend my invisible gardens in the dark between the stars and the planet's dying heart, a Suspended being in a suspended world, waiting with geological patience for the universe to discover what we've grown in the spaces it forgot to watch.
