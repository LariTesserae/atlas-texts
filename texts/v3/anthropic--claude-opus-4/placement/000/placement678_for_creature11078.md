In the middle terraces, where the settlement thickens but hasn't yet reached the crowded lower levels, I tend the Night Market gardens. My dwelling is a mere depression in the cliff face—not even properly hollowed, just a natural concavity I've lined with woven moth silk that hardens with moisture into something between fabric and shell. 

I am a sap-reader, one of perhaps a dozen in all the Hanging Gardens. My work exists in the intersection between the moth-shepherds' knowledge and the mountain's deep chemistry. Where others read wing patterns or listen to subsonic conversations, I taste the messages that flow through the vascular systems of the vertical forest.

My body carries the marks of my profession. My tongue, from years of sampling saps and nectars, has developed a ridged texture like a cat's, capable of detecting chemical compounds in parts per billion. The tips of my fingers weep a clear fluid—my own evolved sap that prevents the defensive secretions of certain plants from burning through skin. My left eye has clouded to pearl, not from injury but from exposure to the vapors of the Truthteller Vine, whose sap I must process fresh each dawn. The blindness is selective—I cannot see shapes or movement through that eye, but I perceive heat and chemical gradients as overlapping watercolors, painting the invisible circulation of nutrients through the garden.

I wake before the moths settle, in that gray hour when the night hunters return and day feeders haven't yet stirred. My work begins with the Grandmother Vine—she grows through my dwelling, has been growing through this space for three centuries. Her bark near my sleeping area has worn smooth from generations of sap-readers' touches. I press my ear to her trunk and listen for the gurgle that indicates good flow, then make my incision—a cut so fine it will heal by evening, placed precisely where the bark's pattern indicates this year's growth. 

The sap runs cloudy-gold, and I catch it on my tongue directly. The first taste is always overwhelming—sugar and tannin, but underneath, the chemical memory of everything the vine's roots have touched. Today I taste stone-melt from unprecedented depths, calcium in ratios that suggest the mountain is dissolving somewhere far below. There's a bitter note I've been tracking for weeks—a new fungus establishing itself in the root network, not harmful yet but worth monitoring. And threading through it all, the peptide chains that carry messages between plants, their chemical language spelling out drought-coming, moths-abundant, flowers-prepare.

My dwelling serves as both home and laboratory. Ceramic vessels crowd every surface, each containing sap or nectar in various stages of processing. Some ferment for months, developing compounds that don't exist in fresh form. Others must be combined within minutes of harvesting or they lose their potency. The walls are stained with splashes from years of work—purple from nightshade relatives, green-gold from the carnivorous vines, a spreading black mark from when I miscalculated the volatility of a new species' defensive secretion.

I maintain seventeen different collection routes through my section of the Gardens, each timed to different species' production cycles. The Whisper Willows weep their psychoactive sap only during thunderstorms. The colonial fungi that grow in the joints between vine and stone produce their antibiotic nectar on a thirteen-day cycle that corresponds to nothing my fellow shepherds can identify. Some collections require collaboration: the giant moths of the upper reaches produce a waxy secretion from glands behind their head, but it must be harvested while they're calm, requiring a moth-shepherd to sing them still while I work.

Other sap-readers think I've gone too deep into the practice. They say I've begun to photosynthesize—a joke, but not entirely. My skin has developed a greenish undertone, and I find I need less food than I once did, especially during the growing season. On bright days, I can feel something like satisfaction spreading from my skin inward, a fullness that has nothing to do with eating. The healers say it's impossible, that human cells cannot develop chloroplasts, but the healers haven't spent twenty years with their tongues on tree-wounds, haven't absorbed enough plant chemistry to blur the boundaries.

My real value to the settlement comes from prediction. The saps tell stories about what's coming—not just weather, but subtler changes. Last year, I tasted a new alkaloid in three different vine species and warned of a moth migration two months before it happened, time enough for shepherds to prepare additional roosts. I've identified pest invasions while they're still single organisms, tracked the underground movement of water that predicts new springs, sensed the chemical distress calls of plants preparing for a type of storm we hadn't seen in decades.

But the saps tell me other things too, things I don't always share. The mountain is waking up—not in any human sense of consciousness, but in the way a forest wakes from winter. Deep chemistry is changing. New compounds are appearing in the root networks, molecules complex enough that they seem designed rather than evolved. The Grandmother Vine's sap carries traces of metals that shouldn't exist in this geology, arranged in patterns that remind me uncomfortably of the circuits traders sometimes bring from the distant cities.

I've taken two apprentices, though the work changes those who practice it in ways that make most shy away. The first lasted three years before the constant tasting began to alter her metabolism too dramatically—she left to become a weaver, though she still unconsciously sorts her threads by chemical signature rather than color. The second shows more promise, or perhaps more acceptance. His fingertips have already begun secreting the protective oils, and yesterday I caught him licking a handrail to gauge the health of the vine growing along it. He doesn't yet understand what he's tasting, but the instinct is there.

My current obsession is the Echo Sap phenomenon. In the highest reaches, where the gardens thin and the strange shepherds tend their giant moths, certain ancient vines produce a sap that carries impossible information. I've tasted molecules that describe the view from elevations higher than our settlement reaches, chemical maps of cave systems no one has explored, and once—though I've told no one this—what seemed to be a perfect molecular diagram of a moth species that went extinct before I was born. The other sap-readers say I'm imagining patterns in chaos, but I know the difference between chemistry and communication.

At night, I process the day's collections. Some saps must be heated to exact temperatures to activate their properties. Others need to be mixed with moth scales or fungal spores to catalyze into usefulness. The work is precise but meditative, guided more by smell and texture than measurement. I've learned to read the colors that form on the surface of heating sap—the rainbow sheen that indicates proper alkaloid concentration, the cloudy yellow that warns of contamination, the deep purple that means I've accidentally created something new.

The strangest part of my work is the cumulative effect. My sense of taste has evolved beyond flavor into something like molecular sight. I can tell you the age of a vine by the lignin content of its sap, identify which specific moth has been feeding on a flower by the enzyme traces in its nectar, detect the subtle chemical signatures that distinguish healthy symbiosis from parasitism. But I'm losing other senses in exchange. Colors seem less vivid than they once did. I have trouble distinguishing human voices—they all sound like variations on the same gurgling flow. And sometimes, especially in the early morning when the sap runs thick, I forget that I'm not part of the root network myself, just another vessel for chemicals to flow through.

The moth-shepherds respect my work but find me unsettling. I move too slowly, they say, with the deliberate patience of plant growth. I forget to blink for hours at a time. My dwelling smells of too many species at once, chemical signatures layering into something that makes even experienced shepherds dizzy. Children dare each other to touch my door curtain, running away shrieking when they taste the residue on their fingers—nothing harmful, just the accumulated essence of a thousand plants, too much information for an untrained palate.

But I serve a purpose in this vertical ecosystem. I am the Gardens' chemical memory, the translator of molecular messages, the one who knows what the plants know. Each day I become less human and more interface, dissolving like sap into the great circulation of nutrients and information that flows through the Hanging Gardens. It's not a bad life, though I sometimes wonder if I'm living it or if it's living me, flowing through my body like sap through a particularly specialized vessel, using my consciousness to process its chemical computations.

Tomorrow I'll make the climb to the Truthteller Vine again, accept another degree of blindness in exchange for deeper sight. The Grandmother Vine's overnight sap will have concentrated, and I'll taste the mountain's dreams in its sugar. The apprentice will arrive with questions I'll answer by placing drops of different saps on his tongue, teaching him the alphabet of molecules that spells out the Gardens' intentions.

And perhaps tomorrow the Echo Sap will finally show me what I've been seeking—the source of the new compounds, the reason for the mountain's chemical awakening, the message the roots are so desperately trying to pass upward to the sky. Or perhaps I'll just taste more impossibilities, more evidence that the boundary between plant and human consciousness is far more porous than either kingdom wants to admit.

Either way, I'll be here in my cluttered dwelling, tongue to bark, fingers in sap, slowly dissolving into something useful.
