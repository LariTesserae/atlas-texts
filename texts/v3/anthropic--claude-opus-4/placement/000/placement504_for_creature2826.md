I find myself as one of the pressure-keepers, though I have only held this position for three years - barely an apprenticeship by the town's patient measure.

My predecessor was my aunt, who spent forty years learning the particular sighs and tremors of our district's vessels. She died last winter, wrapped in fiber cloth and given to the warmth beneath the eastern chimney. Sometimes in the morning vapor, I think I catch the faint sweetness of her oils rising through the canopy, mixing with the perpetual scent of damp brick and fermentation.

I inherited her tools: a leather pouch of ceramic listening cups, each one shaped for a different pipe diameter; a set of thin brass rods for testing valve tension; a bone-handled knife specifically for cutting pressure-relief notches in young sap-lines; and most importantly, her notebook wrapped in waxed leaf-cloth, its pages soft as skin from decades of handling. The entries go back sixty years, a mix of her observations and her own mother's before her:

*Third cistern, northwest corner - develops crystalline buildup in dry seasons. Treat with mild acid from Fermentation House 2.*

*Main boulevard pressure line sings at dawn. This is normal. If pitch rises, check root intrusion at junction with market square.*

*The municipal engine breathes seventeen times per hour in wet season, twelve in dry. Count this before all other diagnostics.*

I live in a narrow room above the oil pressing house, where the walls sweat slightly from the warmth of the machinery below. My sleeping platform grows from a single root-burl that my aunt trained for twenty years, its surface worn smooth by three generations of my family. At night, I feel the building's pulse through the wood - the slow compression of the presses, the trickle of oil through ceramic pipes, the occasional satisfied gurgle of a full collection tank.

My mornings begin before dawn. I dress in darkness, fingers finding the familiar texture of my work clothes: trousers of woven vine-thread, soft and slightly sticky with accumulated resin; a shirt of felted fiber that holds warmth even when damp; the traditional pressure-keeper's apron with its many pockets for gauges, valve keys, and emergency patches. My boots are older than I am, their soles compressed from the same routes walked daily, seasoned with spores and oil until they've become part of the town's own skin.

The first duty is always the municipal engine. I descend through sleeping streets where moisture beads on every surface, my footsteps careful on the root-matted paths. The engine house is never locked - what would be the point? The doorway is a living arch of woven willow that parts for those who know how to ask. I place my palm against the left upright, where generations of pressure-keepers have worn a smooth depression in the bark, and apply gentle counter-clockwise pressure. The growth responds, fibers loosening just enough to admit a familiar body.

Inside, the darkness has a quality like deep water. I don't light the algae lamps yet. First, I must listen.

The engine breathes.

That's the only word for it, though it shares no anatomy with lungs. The central shaft rises and falls with accumulated pressure from the entire town - rainwater weight in high cisterns, sap-compression in the vine networks, the slow expansion of fermentation gases, the minute electrical discharge of the root-mats digesting their substrate. All of this potential gathers here, transforms through bronze gears green with verdigris, and disperses outward again as motion.

I place my largest listening cup against the main housing and count. Fourteen breaths per minute. It's been dry for two weeks, but not desperately so. The rhythm is steady, perhaps a touch hollow on the exhale. I make a note to check the southwestern cisterns - they sometimes develop air pockets that create that particular resonance.

By the time I've completed the engine diagnostics, dawn has begun to silver the leaf-windows. The town stirs with a thousand small movements: condensation dripping from eaves, membrane-valves adjusting to temperature change, early workers beginning their routes. I have my own circuit to walk.

The first skill a pressure-keeper learns is that everything connects. A failing valve in the market square will announce itself through irregularities in the textile workshop three streets away. A root breaking through a buried pipe might first be noticed as a pressure drop in the lamp-gel refiners. The town is one body, and we are its nerve endings.

My route takes me through the pressing district where I live, across the smaller of two market squares, between the fermentation houses, and finally to the boundary pumps that manage the interface between the town's wet circulation and the drier world beyond. Each checkpoint has its own personality:

The oil presses run heavy and slow, their belts creaking with productive complaint. I know each voice. Number three always sounds like it's failing but has run that way for thirty years. Number seven genuinely needs attention - I can hear a hairline crack in one of its ceramic vessels, singing a pitch half a tone too high. I mark it for the sealers.

The market square cisterns are my most delicate charges. They collect roof runoff from twelve buildings, channel it through sand filters grown from specialized root networks, and distribute it according to a hierarchy older than anyone living. The drinking water takes precedence, then the industrial reserves, then the maintenance allocation for moss and growth, finally the emergency stores. Each outlet has its own valve - not metal anymore, but trained sphincters of living wood that respond to pressure differentials. I test each one with a thin probe, feeling for the proper resistance.

The fermentation houses manage themselves mostly, but they need watching. A runaway ferment can build enough pressure to crack even our strongest vessels. I listen for the telltale acceleration in bubble-rate that precedes problems. Today, House Three sounds ambitious - not dangerous yet, but worth noting. I adjust its relief valve two degrees, just enough to bleed the excess without disturbing the process.

The boundary pumps are where my work becomes almost political. The town must maintain its distinction from the surrounding land, but it cannot be entirely separate. Water enters and leaves. Air circulates. Roots negotiate underground treaties. The pumps manage these exchanges, ensuring the town stays moist but not waterlogged, distinct but not isolated.

Here I meet Ehrin, the root-warden whose territory overlaps with mine. She's kneeling beside Pump Four, one hand thrust into the soil up to her elbow, reading something in the root-networks that my instruments can't detect.

"Migration," she says without looking up. "Something's shifted in the water table. The deep roots are reaching southwest now instead of south."

This is important. The town's foundation roots follow water, and if they collectively decide to chase a new source, whole districts can begin to lean over the course of years. We'll need to adjust the pressure distributions to encourage them back to balance.

We work together through the morning, she reading the deep patterns while I calculate how to redistribute the town's fluid weight. It's delicate work - too much change and we risk shocking the system, too little and the migration continues. We settle on a mild intervention: slightly more water to the southeastern cisterns, slightly less to the southwest, a new channel cut in the market square to direct overflow where we need it.

By midday, I'm back at the municipal engine for its noon assessment. This is when I usually eat - a handful of pressed seed cake, a cup of bitter tea brewed from the moss that grows on copper pipes (it has a metallic tang that pressure-keepers claim sharpens the hearing), sometimes a precious dried fruit from beyond the embankments.

The afternoon is for maintenance and minor repairs. Today that means replacing a worn valve on the textile works' main line. The old valve is beautiful in its decay - bronze gone green and purple with patina, its moving parts seized with mineral deposits. The new valve is freshly grown, still pale and slightly flexible, smelling of sap. I coat the joining surfaces with resin and wrap the connection in young bind-weed that will seal the junction as it matures.

This is when I'm most aware of being part of a conversation that began before I was born and will continue long after. My aunt's hands placed some of these pipes. Her mother trained some of these valves. Every repair is a small argument for continuance, a vote for tomorrow being much like today, but still functional, still wet, still breathing.

Late afternoon brings the social portion of my work. Other maintenance folk stop by with problems that might have pressure components. A molder thinks one of her grow-tanks isn't getting enough flow. A sealer has noticed weeping around a foundation stone that might indicate hydraulic intrusion. Two rain-setters argue about whether a particular gutter should drain toward the market square (more public benefit) or the pressing district (more industrial need).

I adjudicate with tools and precedent. The tank is getting enough flow, but an air bubble has formed in the supply line - easily fixed with strategic tapping. The foundation stone is indeed weeping, but from condensation, not intrusion - it needs insulation, not intervention. The gutter should favor neither district but split its flow, which means installing a divider that won't clog with moss - I sketch a design the molders can grow over the winter.

As dusk falls, I make my final rounds. The town settles into its evening rhythm: slower breaths, cooler temperatures, moisture beginning to gather for its nocturnal redistributions. I light the algae lamps along my route, not for myself - I could walk these paths blind - but as a courtesy to the night workers who will follow.

The last stop is always the engine house. I enter through the same patient doorway, place my listening cup against the main housing, and count. Fifteen breaths. The evening moisture has eased some hidden constriction. The town breathes easier in the dark.

Some nights I sleep straight through, lulled by the familiar hydraulics of home. But often I wake at the sounds that mean attention: a pressure drop that changes the pitch of flow through the walls, a valve clicking open when it should stay closed, the particular silence that means something has stopped moving that should never stop.

On those nights, I dress in darkness and go out with my tools, following sounds through the empty streets. Sometimes it's nothing - a temporary blockage that clears itself, a valve that just needed exercise. Sometimes it's serious - a cracked main, a failing seal, a root that's found its way somewhere forbidden. Always, it's the town talking to itself, and I'm just the part that listens and responds.

The work is not glamorous. My hands are permanently stained with resin and pipe-moss. I smell of fermentation and metal. My clothes never fully dry. I spend my days with my ear pressed to walls, counting drips, feeling for vibrations, tasting water to determine its source. I crawl through root-cellars and climb moss-slick ladders. I argue with plants about the proper direction of flow.

But there's a satisfaction in being essential without being central. The town doesn't need heroes. It needs constituents who show up, who listen, who make small adjustments before problems become crises. It needs people who can tell the difference between the silence of contentment and the silence of system failure.

And late at night, when I've solved some small crisis and the town's circulation resumes its proper rhythm, I sometimes think about my aunt in the chimney tree, her minerals rising through root networks, her carbon becoming leaf and bark. She's still part of the conversation, still helping maintain the balance.

I make notes in the shared notebook, adding my observations to the decades of accumulated knowledge:

*New resonance in eastern district main - investigate for calcification.*

*Boundary pump four showing stress - consider reducing afternoon flow.*

*Market square valve twelve developing personality - responds better to gradual rather than sudden opening.*

The town breathes around me. Moisture gathers and disperses. Pressure builds and releases. Somewhere a root finds a way through ancient brick. Somewhere else a seal finally yields to patient entropy. And tomorrow I'll wake before dawn, dress in darkness, and continue the work of keeping this strange collaboration between human, plant, and machine exactly what it has been, exactly what it is: home.

There is no drama in this, only duration. But duration, I'm learning, is its own achievement. Each day the town doesn't dry out, doesn't flood, doesn't cease its quiet manufacturing - each such day is a success that belongs to all of us who tend its flows.

The engine breathes seventeen times in this minute. Somewhere, my aunt's carbon helps a leaf turn toward the morning. I close the notebook, check the latches, and begin the walk home through streets that know my footsteps, past buildings that remember my family, under a sky that promises rain sometime in the next three days.

I can tell by how the moss is beginning to lift.
