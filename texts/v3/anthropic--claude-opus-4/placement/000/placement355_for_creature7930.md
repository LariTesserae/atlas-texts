I am a Tender of the Deep Resonance, one of those who dwells where the air grows so thick it forms standing pools in the carved basins of our ancient observatory. My body has taken on the characteristics of both my calling and my chosen depth—my skin has developed a faint luminescence, not bright enough to see by, but sufficient to trace my movements through the murk like a ghost of light. My third arm emerged during my fortieth year of service, growing from just below my primary left arm. It is more delicate than my birth limbs, its fingers twice as long and sensitive to vibrations that even other Drifters cannot detect.

I maintain the Listening Wells—vast bowls carved into the compressed cloudstone that collect and concentrate the acoustic patterns from the plateau's edge. Each well is unique, its curves calculated over generations to capture specific frequency ranges. My dwelling is built into the wall of the largest well, its entrance only accessible by swimming up through a vertical channel of ultra-dense air that would crush an unadapted body.

My daily work begins before the morning condensation, when the air is at its stillest. I swim-climb down the well's sides, my cilia-covered footpads finding purchase on the slick stone while my primary arms pull me through the medium. My third arm trails behind, its fingertips reading the sonic patterns that have accumulated overnight like sediment. Where I find distortions—places where the city's great drone has developed discordant nodes—I make minute adjustments to the well's surface using tools passed down through my lineage: resonance chisels that vibrate at precise frequencies to reshape the cloudstone one molecular layer at a time.

The deepest part of my well connects to the Undertones, the network of acoustic channels that run beneath the city like veins. Here, the sound becomes a physical force, and I must anchor myself with hooks of carved bone to avoid being swept away by the pressure waves. My gill-slits flutter rapidly in the thick medium, extracting what oxygen they can while my modified lungs hold a reserve breath that can last me through the deepest dives.

I am not alone in this work. My companion is a ribbon-creature I call the Echo—though naming them is somewhat meaningless as they exist more as a pattern of movement than a discrete being. The Echo is old, older than my predecessor's predecessor, and has learned over centuries to modulate its body to create interference patterns that help me map the acoustic space. When I work, it wraps around my torso, its body pulsing with bioluminescent signals that tell me about sound frequencies I cannot directly perceive.

My diet consists primarily of concentrated plankton paste, which I cultivate in the standing pools around my well. But like many who work at depth, I've developed patches of photosynthetic skin along my shoulders and back. During the brief periods when the sun's disk is visible through the murk, I float at the surface of my well, my modified skin drinking in what weak nourishment it can provide. It's not enough to sustain me, but it supplements my needs and more importantly, connects me to the rhythm of day and night that is otherwise lost in our eternal twilight.

The most profound part of my existence comes during the Harmonic Convergence, when the atmospheric tides align to create standing waves throughout the city. During these events, which occur perhaps twice in a year, the Listening Wells become portals to something greater. The sound achieves a perfect resonance, and for brief moments, we can hear beyond our plateau—echoes from other drowned peaks, other settlements we know must exist but cannot reach. I transcribe these distant songs using a notation system of knots tied in air-plant fibers, each knot representing not just a tone but a quality of movement, a thickness of air, a direction of current.

My transformation continues. Recently, I've noticed my bones beginning to hollow, the first sign that I may one day join the Resonants. It's not a fate I fear—to become a living instrument in the great symphony of our city is considered the highest honor. But I am not ready yet. There is too much to document, too many patterns yet to map. My third arm is still growing stronger, and I've begun to sense ultra-low frequencies that suggest massive movements in the deep air far below our plateau—migrations of creatures so large they displace entire weather systems.

In the evening condensation, when water briefly becomes water again and drips from every surface, I perform maintenance on my tools and weave new recording fibers. The Echo helps, its body creating small air currents that help the fibers dry evenly. We work in companionable silence—or what passes for silence here, where the base drone is so constant it becomes indistinguishable from the absence of sound.

This is my existence: guardian of echoes, reader of stone, swimmer in the thick air, becoming slowly more attuned to my environment until the boundary between self and city, between my vibrations and the eternal tolling, dissolves entirely. I am both observer and instrument, noting the music while becoming a note myself, sinking deeper into the resonance that defines our drowned world.
