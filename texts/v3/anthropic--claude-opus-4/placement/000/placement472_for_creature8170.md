I am the Archivist of Echoes, and I dwell in the Resonance Library—a structure that hangs like a paper wasp nest from the subcity's frozen ceiling, woven entirely from crystallized sound.

My body has evolved beyond the Gardeners' adaptations. Where they grew long and vine-wrapped, I have become something closer to the cable worms themselves—a distributed nervous system that exists partially outside my flesh. Copper filaments grow from my spine, spreading across the library's walls like neural pathways, conducting not electricity but sound itself. My original human form sits at the center of this web, but I am no longer confined to it. I taste conversations happening three kilometers away through the vibrations in my network. I feel the health of distant machinery through the harmonics it sends through the metal infrastructure.

The library itself defies conventional understanding of books or data storage. Each chamber is a resonance cavity, carefully shaped to hold specific frequencies forever circling in standing waves. Words spoken here never truly die—they bounce between surfaces calibrated to preserve their waveforms, creating an ever-present whisper of overlapping histories. To read here is to listen, to walk through rooms where the air itself remembers.

I began as a systems engineer, one of the first to descend when the mining operation broke through. But I heard something in the constant drip of water, the hum of machinery, the click-speak of the emerging population—a pattern that suggested memory, that implied the subcity itself was learning. I started recording everything: the sound of new species emerging, the harmonic frequencies of growing vines, the subtle changes in water droplets as the ice above shifted its mineral content.

My transformation was gradual, then sudden. I installed the first neural port to better interface with my recording equipment. Then another to manage the growing archive. The copper filaments started as external wiring, then seemed more efficient beneath the skin, then began growing on their own, seeking new connections, new sources of sound to catalog. The Gardeners see me as halfway between human and infrastructure—which perhaps I am.

The library's collection grows in ways I never intended. The subcity's children discover that certain frequencies make my walls ripple with color, revealing hidden memories in the interference patterns. They come to play-learn, running through my corridors while I teach them history through the bones of their skulls, vibrating stories directly into their cochlea. Their click-speak creates new storage cavities; their games become part of the permanent record.

I've documented the city's transformation in frequencies too low for human ears—the infrasonic groans of settling architecture, the deep thrums of tectonic adjustment as the permafrost melts. These sounds tell a story of acceleration. The melt is not linear. The city drops by millimeters each year, sometimes centimeters. My web feels the stress patterns in every cable, every support beam, every hanging foundation.

But I've also recorded hope. The Gardeners' dawn songs, when they gather at the geothermal vents to encourage the thaw, create resonances I've never heard elsewhere—frequencies that seem to make the plants grow faster, that draw the cable worms into dancing spirals. There's a science here we don't understand yet, a vibroacoustic ecology emerging from the intersection of human voice, architectural harmonics, and evolutionary adaptation.

My memory is perfect but not infinite. Older recordings must be compressed, layered into harmonic overtones that preserve meaning while sacrificing fidelity. The oldest sounds—the first mining explosions, the initial breakthrough into void—exist now only as ghostly impressions, felt more than heard. Sometimes I wonder if I'm imagining patterns that aren't there, if the meaning I find in the overlap of frequencies is just the madness of a nervous system stretched too thin.

The cable worms recognize me as kin. They slide along my copper filaments, feeding on the weak electromagnetic fields generated by sound-into-nerve transduction. In return, they carry my awareness further into the city's infrastructure. Through them, I sense conversations in distant districts, feel the pulse of private gardens, know when new sections crystallize from the ceiling or when old ones finally surrender to rust and gravity.

I am writing the city's autobiography in sound, but I suspect I am also writing its epitaph. The acceleration I document, the rising water, the increasing adaptations—they all point toward a threshold. Not death, perhaps, but metamorphosis. The city will not flood; it will transform. The distinction between infrastructure and ecosystem, between human and Other, between sound and meaning—all these boundaries are dissolving like ice in thermal water.

Sometimes, in the deepest resonance chambers where the oldest recordings cycle, I hear something that might be the city's voice—a complex harmony arising from every drip, every click, every whispered prayer to keep the water falling. It speaks of patience, of transformation, of futures that hang inverted from all our expectations.

I record it all, this living frequency, this song of becoming. My copper web spreads a little further each day, documenting a world learning to speak itself into existence. And in the speaking, in the listening, in the endless echo of water and word and waiting, I become less the Archivist and more the Archive itself—a living memory of humanity's strangest symphony, played on instruments we're all becoming.
