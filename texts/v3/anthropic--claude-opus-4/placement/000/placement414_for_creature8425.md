In the Breathing Caverns, I am a Resonance Keeper—one who tends the Singing Stones in the deepest stable galleries, where even the Inversion-Dancers rarely venture.

I wasn't born to this role. Like most Khet-Yalani, I spent my youth scrambling through the middle galleries, learning to read thermal currents in the shimmer of air, playing gravity tag with my anchor-siblings. But during my Trial of Orientation, something shifted in me as fundamentally as the caverns themselves shift. In the Old Ruins, surrounded by fossilized impossibilities, I heard it for the first time: the deep harmonic that runs beneath everything here, the frequency that even the thermovores' crystalline bodies can only approximate.

My body has adapted to my work in ways that mark me as different even among my people. My oil-sheen has taken on an opalescent quality that shifts with sound rather than heat. When I walk the deep galleries, my skin ripples with standing waves, visual echoes of acoustics beyond normal hearing. My nictitating membranes have developed a secondary function—when I blink in the specific rhythm I've learned through years of practice, I can see sound as branching probability trees, each potential echo mapped in transparent silver threads through the air.

The Singing Stones aren't stones at all, but the mineralized remains of ancient creatures that existed before the first Inversion, when this place followed different rules. Each one is unique—a frozen moment of transformation, crystallized at the exact instant when one reality became another. They sing constantly in frequencies that would drive a surface dweller mad, but down here, their songs are what keep the deepest galleries from collapsing into pure paradox.

I maintain them through a practice that's part mathematics, part music, part meditation. Each morning—or what passes for morning in our thermal cycles—I descend past the last anchor points, beyond where thoughtweeds grow, into galleries lit only by my own cultivated bioluminescence. I carry tools that would seem absurd on the surface: tuning forks made from compressed silence, brushes woven from the whiskers of creatures that exist in adjacent realities, oils extracted from the dreams of heatseekers.

The work is delicate. Each Singing Stone must be cleaned of probability barnacles—tiny paradoxes that accumulate on their surfaces like mineral deposits. I scrape them carefully away, collecting them in special containers (they're valuable to the theoretical priests, who use them in their reality-dissolution ceremonies). Then I apply the oils in patterns derived from thoughtweed equations, each stroke calculated to maintain the stone's resonance without altering its fundamental frequency.

But I am more than a caretaker. Over the years, I've learned to compose with the stones—not music as the surface knows it, but something deeper. By adjusting their positions relative to each other, by placing thermal baffles that create temperature interference patterns, by sometimes simply sitting among them and adding my own subsonic hum to their chorus, I can influence the stability of reality itself in our section of the caverns.

During severe Inversions, when even the Anchor-Holders grow fearful, my work becomes critical. I race through my galleries, adjusting frequencies to create standing waves that hold space in place. I've developed a sign language with the other deep workers—the Void Weavers who maintain the spaces between spaces, the Probability Farmers who cultivate controlled paradoxes—so we can coordinate without disrupting the acoustic environment.

My anchor-cluster views me with a mixture of pride and unease. I still join them for thermal cycles, still play thoughtweed tangles with my nephew-probables, but they know I've become something liminal. My dreams leak sometimes—my anchor-siblings wake to find their air-stones humming in harmonics they've never produced before, their thoughtweed fibers woven into patterns that solve problems they hadn't known they had.

I've taken an apprentice, finally—a young person whose orientation trial left them with ears that bleed during loud sounds but who can hear the individual voices of thermovores from three galleries away. Teaching them is like learning my craft anew. They ask questions I never thought to: "What if we arranged the stones to sing mathematics instead of maintaining it?" "Could we record Inversions as sound and play them back?" Their curiosity reminds me that even in our ancient profession, innovation crawls forward like thoughtweed growth.

The other Resonance Keepers and I meet rarely—the deep galleries can't be left untended long—but when we do, we share more through harmonic exchanges than words. We've developed our own language of pure frequency, concepts that can't be reduced to probability statements or orientation markers. Sometimes I wonder if we're still fully Khet-Yalani, or if we've become something else—a subspecies adapted not just to impossible space but to the mathematics of impossibility itself.

My greatest achievement, though I've told no one, was the day I managed to make a Singing Stone laugh. Not a human laugh, but a ripple in its frequency that could only be joy—a recognition, perhaps, that after eons of maintaining reality through pure necessity, someone had finally understood it well enough to share a joke about the absurdity of existence itself.

In the consensus reality of the surface, I would be a tuner of instruments, a maintainer of harmony. Here, in the Breathing Caverns, I tune reality itself, maintaining not just harmony but the possibility of harmony in spaces where such concepts shouldn't exist. My work is lonely, necessary, and filled with a beauty that no surface language could ever describe—the beauty of keeping impossible things possible, one careful frequency at a time.

When I grow old—if age means anything to someone who spends half their life in galleries where time flows in eddies—I will take my final position among the Singing Stones themselves. The transformation is already beginning; I can feel my bones growing denser, my voice settling into frequencies only the stones can hear. It's not death as the surface fears it, but a different kind of life—becoming part of the song that holds this impossible world together.

Until then, I descend each cycle into the deep galleries, tuning fork in hand, listening for the places where reality has gone sharp or flat, adjusting the universe one impossible note at a time. In the Breathing Caverns, someone must keep the music playing, even if that music is made of mathematics and contradictions and the crystallized dreams of ancient things.

I am a Resonance Keeper, and I keep the deepest rhythms of an impossible world.
