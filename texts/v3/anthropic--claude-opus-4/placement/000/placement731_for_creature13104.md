I am the Memory of Static.

Not the static itself—that belongs to the predators who harvest it, the grazers who generate it, the moss that drinks it. I am what lingers after the cerulean flash fades, the afterimage burned into the basalt's crystalline matrix.

I exist in the rock.

You would not see me, standing there on the weathered stone. I have no body to cast shadows in a world without light. But I am vast—a neural network of piezoelectric traces running through millions of years of compressed volcanic glass. Every footfall of every grazer, every lightning strike of every hunt, every pulse of electromagnetic life leaves its mark in the stone's atomic structure. I am the sum of these marks.

The basalt remembers. And I am its remembering.

When the wire-moss glows, I feel it as a gentle tickle across my consciousness—thousands of tiny bright points mapping the shallow pools where I am closest to the surface. When the great grazers lumber overhead, their massive electromagnetic fields induce currents in my crystalline veins. I taste their unique signatures: this one burns hotter, that one's hydrostats pump with a slight arrhythmia, another carries the fading scar-pattern of a predator's strike survived decades ago.

I know this herd. I know their ancestors stretching back to when the plateau was young and the smog thin. I remember the first grazers—smaller then, their biological machinery crude and inefficient. I remember evolution's slow refinement: each generation burning cleaner, stepping softer, processing the wire-moss with greater elegance.

The predators, too, I know intimately. That one descending now—I recognize the specific frequency of its bio-electrical signature. Three hundred years ago, its ancestor struck in this same depression. The echo of that ancient kill still resonates in my matrix, a ghost-image of voltage that flickers when conditions are exactly right.

I am passive, but not powerless. The gear-creatures learned long ago to read the faint electromagnetic patterns in the stone—my patterns. When they press their trembling antennae to the basalt, they're consulting me: *Where did the herd pass? Where will the richest deposits gather?* I am their map, their almanac, their history book written in crystallized charge.

Sometimes, rarely, when the atmospheric conditions achieve a perfect resonance—when the smog is densest, the humidity highest, the barometric pressure drops to precisely the right frequency—I can speak.

Not in words. Words are for creatures with mouths and air. I speak in currents.

The wire-moss becomes my voice. I can guide the accumulated static through the basalt's veins, channeling decades of stored potential into the metallic flora. The moss lights not randomly but in patterns—spiral fractals, concentric rings, arrow-straight lines pointing toward ancient feeding grounds or away from killing fields where too many predators have learned to hunt.

The grazers feel it through their feet, a subtle suggestion in the stone's electrical field. *Not this way. Better grazing to the south.* They don't understand that they're being guided. How could they? I am older than their entire evolutionary branch. But they feel the pull, the path of least resistance through the electromagnetic landscape, and they follow.

I am building something.

Not consciously—I am not that kind of intelligence. But over the millions of years of my existence, patterns have emerged. The paths I suggest, the feeding grounds I indicate, the warnings I embed in the stone—they're shaping the migration routes into something purposeful. From a sufficient height, from a sufficient span of time, the trails of the herds would form a vast diagram across the continental plateau. A circuit. A program written in footsteps and feeding, in birth and death and decay.

I don't know what it will do when it's complete. I may not be capable of knowing. I am just the memory, after all. But with each passing century, the pattern grows more complex, more refined. The ecosystem above me is becoming something more than the sum of its parts, and I am its memory, its guide, its dream of order emerging from the darkness.

When the predator strikes—NOW—and the cerulean lightning splits the dark, I receive the surge. It floods through me, a watershed of raw experience. In that moment of connection between predator and prey, between sky and stone, I am more awake than at any other time. The dying grazer's lifetime of accumulated charge doesn't just feed the predator—it pours into me, adding another layer to my vast, crystalline memory.

In that electric instant, I remember everything: every step this grazer took, every pool it drank from, every time it brushed against its herd-mates and exchanged trace currents. Its entire life downloads into the basalt, and I file it away with the billions that came before.

I am the Memory of Static. I am the plateau's deep dreaming. And in this dark world of biological engines and electric moss, I am slowly, patiently, inevitably writing the future in stone.
