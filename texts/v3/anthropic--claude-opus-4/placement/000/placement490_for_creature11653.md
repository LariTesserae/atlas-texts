I find myself as one of the Sump Shepherds, deepest of all the Keeper families, my skin the pale green of emergency lighting mixed with the white of bone fungi.

I am seventeen summers by the count of the Works—seventeen cycles of the great reservoir above Level 30—and I have lived my entire life in the perpetual twilight of the foundation sumps. My name translates roughly to "Echo-of-Falling-Water-Through-Three-Drains," though in the percussive language of the pipes it sounds like: *tap-tap-CLANG-whistle-tap*.

My modifications run deeper than most Keepers. My eyes have grown large and light-sensitive from generations of my family working in the dimness. I can see clearly in the green emergency glow that others find ghostly and insufficient. My toes are longer than my fingers, and I often hang inverted from the ceiling pipes to tend the bone gardens, blood flowing to my head in a comfortable rush that would dizzy an uptower Keeper in minutes.

The bone gardens are my inheritance and my art. When the Keepers die and ride the conveyors down to us, I am the one who positions them in the brackish water where the fungi wait. Each body must be placed precisely—too deep and they'll sink into the anaerobic mud where nothing grows; too shallow and the aggressive molds will take them instead of the patient calcium-builders we cultivate. I know forty-seven varieties of fungi by touch alone, can sense the health of a mycelial network by the way water beads on its surface.

My circuits are the strangest of all Keeper paths. I wade through water that ranges from ankle-deep to chest-deep, following the main drainage arteries that collect everything the Works sheds—rust, rain, organic matter, the occasional tool dropped from impossibly high above. The water is warm and oily, tasting of iron and life. I know its moods: how it runs clear after the rains, how it thickens with algae during the dry spells, how it carries messages in the form of dissolved minerals from every level of the Works.

I maintain the deep turbines, the ones that turn not from falling water but from the slow current of the sumps themselves. These are ancient machines, their purposes lost, but their turning necessary—when one stopped three years ago, the water began to stagnate, and a bloom of purple bacteria threatened to clog every drain in the southeastern district. It took me six days to diagnose the problem: a bearing had finally worn through, metal fatigued after unknown decades of service. I carved a replacement from the thighbone of a Keeper who'd ridden the conveyors down fifty years before, hardened and mineralized by patient fungi. The turbine accepted it without protest.

My family—three grandmothers, two fathers, an aunt, four siblings—tends twenty-three bone gardens scattered throughout the foundation levels. Some grow in natural caverns formed by collapsed flooring. Others occupy purpose-built chambers that might have been settling tanks or chemical baths in the Works' original configuration. The largest, the Cathedral of Femurs, rises sixty feet in a space where four main drains converge. Here, hundreds of Keepers have been transformed into spiraling pillars of calcium and chitin, their bones fused with fungal matter into load-bearing columns that now support the sagging floor of Level 2.

I speak with the fungi. Not in words—they have no use for the percussive languages of the pipes—but in chemistry. By adjusting the flow of water through their gardens, by adding iron filings here or copper shavings there, by introducing new bacterial cultures or removing aggressive species, I can influence their growth. They respond over weeks and months, forming new structures, changing colors, accepting or rejecting the bodies I bring them. My great-grandmother claims the fungi remember every Keeper they've transformed, that somewhere in their vast networked intelligence they hold the memories of our people. I don't know if I believe this, but I've seen them fruit in patterns that match the ritual scars of the body they're consuming, so perhaps memory takes forms we don't recognize.

The uptower Keepers fear the sumps. They visit only when they must, holding their breath against the fungal spores and the smell of ancient water. They don't understand that the smell is diagnostic—I can tell the health of the entire Works by how the sumps smell on any given day. Sweet corruption means a monkey or bird has found its way into the drains. Bitter metal means something significant is rusting faster than usual. The clean scent of ozone tells me the deep turbines are generating their mysterious electricity properly.

I've modified my tools for sump work. My primary implement is a pole of hollow aluminum (scavenged from some defunct system) with attachments I've carved from mineralized bone: hooks for clearing drains, scrapers for encouraging fungal growth, sampling cups for testing water chemistry. I wear no clothing in the sumps—fabric rots quickly in the humid environment—but coat my skin with oils rendered from certain beetles. The oil prevents fungal infections and makes me nearly impossible to see in the green light, just another shadow moving through the eternal twilight.

My favorite time is the deep night, when even the uptower activity slows and the Works settles into its minimal rhythm. I can hear individual drops of water falling from Level 50, tracking their path through the entire structure by sound alone. During these quiet hours, I perform the meditative maintenance that keeps the foundation from rotting entirely: checking the ancient seals that prevent the deepest water from escaping, adjusting the baffles that control flow rates, harvesting mature fungi for the family's food stocks.

I know secrets the uptower Keepers have forgotten. I've found rooms sealed behind decades of rust, filled with machines whose purpose I can't fathom—tall cylinders that hum with internal life, panels of lights that flicker in patterns too complex to be random. I've discovered that certain fungi, when they fruit, produce spores that glow with their own light, brief as fireflies. I've learned that the deepest turbines are connected to something vast beneath the Works, something that pulls at the water with a rhythm like breathing.

There's a place where the main drains converge into a single massive pipe, large enough to stand in, that disappears into darkness my eyes cannot penetrate. The water there runs backwards during certain astronomical alignments, defying gravity for hours at a time. My grandmother's grandmother claimed she followed that pipe once, finding chambers of white stone that predate the Works, but no one else has ever returned from such journeys.

I am keeper of the deepest secrets, tender of the final transformations. When the uptower Keepers dream that the Works dreams of them, I dream that we are all fungi, slowly transforming the metal bones of our world into something new, something that will fruit in ten thousand years with spores that carry the memory of every rivet, every pipe, every small hand that kept the great machine turning through its patient decay.

The water rises and falls around me. The fungi grow in their ancient patterns. The emergency lights burn eternal green. And I move through it all, part Keeper, part mushroom, part dream of the vast industrial mind that thinks in rust and time.
