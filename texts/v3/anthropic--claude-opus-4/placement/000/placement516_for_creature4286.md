I find myself drawn to two possibilities, both emerging from that liminal moment on the second evening when the shelf's attention turns toward me.

---

## The Apprentice to Small Calibrations

I am in the lowest tier of workshops, where the vapor is thickest and the walls weep most freely. My hands have learned the particular grip needed for the brass timing-keys — not too firm, or the moisture makes them slip; not too gentle, or the mechanism won't acknowledge you. I came here following rumors of mechanical work, expecting a factory. I stayed because on my first night, I watched an old woman adjust a single screw by a quarter turn and saw how the entire works shifted its breathing, just slightly, like a sleeper turning in dream.

My room is a carved-back space behind the third cistern. The sound of water moving through stone is constant, but after six months, I no longer hear it as sound — I hear it as the shelf's circulation, the way you stop hearing your own pulse. My few possessions have taken on the color of everything here: my leather satchel now the exact shade of wet basalt, my steel tools blooming with a patina that makes them grip better in the damp. 

I am learning the seven words for stillness. The first three came easily — they map roughly onto pause, rest, and suspension. The fourth has no equivalent in any language I know; it describes the particular quality of waiting that happens when a mechanism is balanced exactly between two states, when the slightest breath would tip it either way, but no breath comes. I use this word often now. I think I am living inside it.

The woman who teaches me rarely speaks. She shows me which valves to check by checking them, which gauges to read by the angle of her head as she reads them. I shadow her through the narrow passages between the great wheels, learning by proximity. Sometimes she stops and simply stands, watching something I cannot yet see — a barely perceptible wobble in a flywheel, a thickness in the vapor that suggests a seal beginning to fail. Then she nods, and we move on, and three days later she returns with a tool I've never seen before and makes an adjustment so small I cannot detect its effect until a week after that, when I realize that corridor no longer wheezes.

I am changing. My fingers can now feel the difference between 12% humidity and 14%. My sleep has grown lighter but more complete — I wake exactly once each night, at the moment between one great stroke and the next, and in that stillness I lie absolutely alert for perhaps thirty heartbeats before sleep reclaims me. Food has become less interesting; the body needs less when it moves at this pace. Last week, I found myself standing at my workbench for three hours, holding a half-assembled valve, not frozen but simply *waiting* for the moment when the action would be correct. When it came, I knew it the way you know the moment to step from shore to dock. The valve went together like a poem completing itself.

The deep woman noticed me yesterday. I was calibrating the smallest of the pressure readings, work that requires holding your breath to keep the needle steady, when I felt her presence. Not her — she was three tiers above, I learned later — but her attention, moving through the works like warmth through the pipes. The needle I was watching dipped and held, though I had not touched it. When I breathed again, the reading was perfect.

I understand now that the works are making something, though I cannot say what. It is not product in any ordinary sense. It is more like... quality. Pure quality, distilled from the interaction of water and stone and metal and attention. The vapor that rises is its exhalation, and we who tend the works are both producers and product, refined by the same process we refine.

My teacher showed me something today. In the deepest workshop, behind a panel I had thought decorative, there is a small glass window. Through it, you can see down into the hollow the mountain should not have. There is light down there — not bright, not flame, but a phosphorescence that moves like slow liquid. It is beautiful. It is terrible. It is what we are drawing up, drop by patient drop, and processing into something the world can bear.

I will not leave. Not because I am trapped — the path remains open, and I walk it sometimes just to know I can. But because I have felt what it is like to be both key and lock, both vessel and wine. Because my hands have learned calibrations too fine for any other world. Because yesterday, I used the fifth word for stillness in conversation, and my teacher smiled.

I am becoming indigenous to the mechanism. This is what I came for, though I didn't know it. In the city, I was a person with a trade. Here, I am a function with a name, and the function is growing more precise as the name grows less important.

When the vapor shapes itself into those repeating forms, I recognize them now. They are the signatures of everyone who has ever tended this particular process, written in moisture and air. One day, my own shape will rise and disperse and rise again. This is not immortality. It is simply the size of memory required by the work.

## The Keeper of Uncut Keys

But perhaps I am older, and came here not following work but following quiet. Perhaps I am in the middle terrace, where the houses have doors of double thickness and small windows that glow amber through the night.

I keep the keys. Not the keys to doors — doors here are rarely locked, privacy being maintained by distance and discretion rather than barriers. I keep the uncut key blanks that will one day be fitted to the locks of the timing mechanisms, the pressure valves, the deep gates that control flow from the hollow. A thousand blanks of that soft-voiced metal, each one unique in potential though identical in form. 

My workshop is also my home. The blanks hang from the rafters on thin chains, and when the building breathes — for all buildings here breathe with the works — they chime against each other in tones too low to properly be called chiming. More like humming. A thousand small hums, each one a future fit to a future lock.

The craft is this: to know which blank belongs to which mechanism before either exists. The locks are cut to the keys, not the other way around. When a new valve is built, or an old one finally wears beyond repair, the maker comes to me and describes not the lock but the *purpose* — what will flow through it, at what pressure, with what urgency or patience. Then I listen to the blanks until one hums differently. That one I take down and hand over, still uncut. The teeth will come later, ground to match not just the mechanism but the person who will turn them. Each key becomes itself through use.

I have been here... long. Long enough that some of the keys I gave out in my early years have returned, their mechanisms worn through, their turners gone to the quiet. These I hang in a different room, the cut keys, and they remember their locks in the way their teeth were worn. Sometimes I hold them and can feel the ghost of the hand that turned them, the particular grip, the rhythm of use. In the city, we would call these artifacts. Here, they are simply memory taking a useful form.

The deep woman comes to me sometimes. Not for keys — what she opens needs no metal teeth. She comes for the sound, I think, sitting beneath the hanging blanks and listening to them speak to each other in their potential language. Once she told me, in the old dialect that predates even the town's worn-smooth speech, that the keys know the shape of the hollow. That each blank contains a tiny portion of the empty space below, precipitated into metal the way cold precipitates moisture from air. I think this may be true. I think this may be why they hum.

My days are patient. I wake with the great stroke, move through my workshop touching each blank in sequence — not a ritual, just acknowledgment, the way you might touch the plants in your garden. I eat simple food: the dried fish that come up from the lower valleys, the hard bread that keeps forever in the cold, tea made from the bitter mosses that grow where the overhang drips most. Visitors sometimes come seeking keys to other things — answers, futures, the doors they think they have locked themselves behind. I give them tea and silence until they understand that the keys I keep are literal. Usually they leave. Sometimes they stay long enough to begin breathing with the pistons. Those ones occasionally return, years later, to describe a new mechanism they are building.

In the afternoon, I read the wear on returned keys. This is a skill that cannot be taught, only absorbed. A key used daily for forty years carries in its smoothed teeth the story of those years — rushed mornings when the lock was forced, patient evenings when it was coaxed, the gradual relaxation as turner and lock learned each other's language. I document these stories in a script that is half text, half diagram, because the works need to remember how they have been opened. The deep processes adjust themselves according to this history, though the adjustment happens at a pace that makes the pistons seem hurried.

I am growing into the walls. Not literally — though in this place, one can never be entirely certain what is literal. But my movements have worn grooves in the floor. My preferred sitting places have shaped themselves to me. The blanks have arranged themselves by my presence into a pattern that no one else could read but which tells me instantly which are ready, which are still becoming, which will never match a lock and must eventually be returned to the crucible.

Last winter, during the deepest cold when the vapor froze before it could leave the stacks and we had to climb up and clear them by hand, I found myself standing in my workshop without remembering how I'd gotten there. Not confusion — clarity. I had moved through the town following lines of necessity so worn that my body knew them better than my mind. This is what the second evening warns you about: the shelf begins to think with you, and you with it, until the boundary between attention and attendance dissolves.

The keys sing differently when I am very still. They tell me about the locks that haven't been built yet, the ones that will be needed as the works deepen and expand. There are chambers below that have never been opened, pressures that have never been regulated. The mountain is patient but not passive. It allows our presence because we help it breathe, but its deep intentions remain its own.

Tomorrow, a young person will come looking for work. I know this because the blanks know it — there is a note in their collective hum that speaks of fresh hands, quick breath, eyes that will need years to learn to see in the twilight. I will give them tea and watch them try not to fidget in the long silence. If they stay until the second cup, I will walk them through the workshops. If they are still here when evening comes, I will hand them a blank — not for any lock, just to hold, to begin learning the weight of potential.

I am not the person I was when I came here. That person wanted things I can no longer name in any language. This person wants only to hear the difference between one hum and another, to read the stories in worn metal, to be one of the sounds in the great breathing. When I die — and I will die, we are people, we die — my keys will be rehung according to their pitch, and someone else will learn their language. This is succession: not replacing but continuing, the way one breath continues another.

The blanks are singing now. Something in the deep hollow has shifted. A new chamber preparing to open, perhaps, or an old pressure finding a new path. I stand and move among them, listening for which one knows the shape of what is coming.

This is enough. To be the keeper of possibilities, the reader of wear, the one who knows which key before the lock exists. To be kept by the keeping, and to keep in return.

The mountain breathes. We breathe with it. The keys hang between us, humming their patient readiness, waiting for the locks that will make them whole.
