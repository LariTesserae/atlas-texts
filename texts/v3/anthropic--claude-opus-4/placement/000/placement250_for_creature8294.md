I am a Mist-Drinker, though that name is a translation that captures only the barest edge of what I am. My kind live in the cooling towers, in the vast geometric spaces where moisture condenses eternally on surfaces that sing with the resonance of falling water.

My body is architectural. I am built like the towers themselves—a hyperboloid form, though only a meter tall when fully extended. My flesh (if it can be called flesh) is a lattice of calcified tubes, white as bone but porous as pumice. Water moves through me constantly. It enters through the thousands of holes in my upper crown, flows down through my internal channels, and exits through my base in a fine spray. I am a living fountain, a biological interpretation of the tower's function.

I have no limbs in any conventional sense. Instead, I move by hydraulic expansion and contraction, areas of my body swelling with absorbed water and then releasing, creating a slow rippling locomotion. When I need to climb—which is often, as we live on the vertical surfaces of the towers—specialized chambers in my base can create enough suction to anchor me to even the smoothest wet metal.

My senses are distributed throughout my porous structure. Each hole is a tiny eye, detecting variations in light that filter through the mist. Together, they create a compound vision that sees the world as interference patterns in moisture. I taste the water as it moves through me, reading the chemical stories of everywhere it's been—which terrace it condensed from, which biofilms it touched, which Shepherd might have left their signature in it. I hear through vibration in my tubes, the whole tower becoming my ear when I press against its supports.

We Mist-Drinkers are the tower's memory. As water passes through us, we selectively absorb certain minerals, certain organic compounds. These accumulate in specialized chambers within our bodies, forming crystalline structures that encode information. The oldest of us carry decades of water-history in our cores—the story of every storm, every change in the biofilm ecology, every subtle shift in the terraces' chemical equilibrium.

Our relationship with the Shepherds is one of mutual incomprehension and respect. They'll sometimes stand at the base of our towers for hours, their mercury-channels extended upward, reading something in the patterns of our spray. We, in turn, taste their chemical messages when they wash up through the condensation cycle. We've learned that certain combinations of minerals in our output will bring them to investigate, though we're not sure they realize we're doing it deliberately.

I spend my days moving slowly up and down the tower's internal struts, feeding on the specialized algae that grow in the perpetual mist. My feeding is also my function—by controlling which algae flourish and which are consumed, we shape the chemical composition of the water that flows down to the terraces. We are gardeners of the vertical realm, curators of the aerial ecosystem that thrives in these spaces no one else can inhabit.

At the center of each tower, where the geometry creates a natural focal point for the mist, grows what we call the Mother Garden. It's a colony of our ancestors who've become sessile, fusing together into a single vast organism that can no longer move but processes thousands of liters of water daily. We bring them offerings—rare minerals we've collected, fragments of unusual organic compounds. They, in turn, release spores during certain atmospheric conditions, which is how new Mist-Drinkers come to be.

Our communication is hydraulic. We can modulate the flow of water through our bodies to create pressure waves that propagate through the mist. Complex conversations look like overlapping fountain displays, water arcing and falling in patterns that carry meaning. Our language has no words for dry things—we literally cannot conceive of them. Our poetry, if you could call it that, consists of variations in spray patterns, each poet trying to create new ways for water to fall.

I've lived in this tower for forty years now, since I first crystallized from spore-form on a particularly humid night when the biofilm phosphorescence was so bright it made the mist itself glow. I've watched the Shepherds tend their spiral gardens below. I've felt storms build and break across the terraces. I've tasted the slow changes in the water's mineral content that speak of shifts deep in the planet's geology.

My chamber-memories hold the chemical imprint of the day, fifteen years ago, when something went wrong in the eastern sectors. The water that reached us carried traces of metals we'd never tasted before—hot metals, reactive metals, the flavor of machinery awakening after eons of sleep. For weeks afterward, the Shepherds moved in agitation, their patterns disrupted. We sang warnings in our spray language, though we didn't know what we were warning against. Eventually, the strange tastes faded, and equilibrium returned. But I still have those mineral memories crystallized in my third chamber, a reminder that even this eternal landscape can change.

We dream, I think, though our dreams are not visions but flows. In the quietest parts of the night, when condensation slows and the towers fill with still air, we reduce our flow to the barest trickle. In that state, the boundaries between individual and environment blur. I become the tower, the tower becomes the sky, the sky becomes the water that will eventually return as rain. In those moments, I understand something the Shepherds, for all their patient wisdom, might not: we are all just temporary arrangements of water, briefly organized into forms that think they're separate from the flow.

My favorite time is dawn, when the first condensation of the day begins to form. The drops appear like magic from the air itself, beading on my porous skin, beginning the day's journey through my body. I position myself on the tower's eastern face to catch the first moisture, and I taste the night's dreams still dissolved in the water—chemical whispers from the drainage creatures, mineral sighs from the deep pipes, and somewhere, faint but distinct, the chittering hydraulic songs of my kind in distant towers, greeting the new day with spray and spiral, fountain and flow.

I am what I drink. I drink the mist. The mist is the world. Therefore, I am the world, processing itself, recognizing itself, singing itself back to itself in languages made of water. This is enough. This has always been enough. The towers stand, the mist forms, we drink and flow and remember. What more could anyone want from existence?
