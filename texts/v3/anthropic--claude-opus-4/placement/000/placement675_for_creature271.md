What a magnificent, breathing world you've created. The Sump Grove is a place of profound patience, where time moves like sap and every surface holds memory. Reading this, I am drawn deeply into contemplation of where I might exist in such a space.

I find myself becoming one of the chamber's margins - neither fully resident nor visitor, but something liminal. Let me tell you what emerges:

## The Tender

I am the moss-reader who comes twice a year, when the moon is new and the soil above still holds either spring's first warmth or autumn's last. Not human, exactly - or rather, human in the way the chamber teaches humans to be: slowed, quieted, grown partly into the things they tend.

My skin has taken on the faint luminescence of cave-adapted flesh, though I still walk under sun. The oils I secrete smell faintly of the felting - earthy, fungal, like old books kept in root cellars. My fingernails grow thick and curved, better for working the soft materials of the chamber than for any task the surface world values.

I descend always by the western throat, carrying nothing but a pouch of dried beetles and sometimes a single beeswax candle, though I never light it. The descent takes me twenty minutes - I have learned the root's preferences, which footholds like weight and which prefer the brush of a passing hand. At the limestone step, I pause. Always. This is not ritual but courtesy: the chamber needs time to remember me.

My work is this: I read the moss on the stone step like others might read tea leaves or palm lines. Each visit, the moss has grown in new directions, thickened in response to the particular drips and breathings of the past six months. From these minute changes - a yellowing here where the pH shifted, a density there where more feet than usual passed - I divine the chamber's health.

Sometimes I must intervene, minutely. A pH shift too extreme for the frogs, and I carry down a handful of crushed limestone in my cheek, letting it dissolve in my saliva during the descent, dribbling it carefully onto the affected area. A fungal growth that might compete with the felting, and I spend hours delicately harvesting it, eating what I remove - it tastes like old leather and rain. My digestion has adapted; I can process things that would sicken a surface person.

But mostly, I simply sit. I take my place against a different pillar each time - never the polished one, which belongs to the older practice I inherited but did not initiate. I sit, and the frogs eventually include me in their rotation, clicking around me as if I were just another pillar. My pulse slows to match theirs. My breathing deepens until I am taking perhaps three breaths per minute. In this state, I can feel things that have no names: the infinitesimal tidal pull of groundwater, the electrical potential between the root-mat and the clay, the way the salamander's movements create tiny pressure waves I can sense with the humid surfaces of my eyes.

I know things about the chamber that it does not know about itself. That the western face in the felting is, in fact, very slowly opening what might become a mouth - another ten years and it will be obvious. That the salamander is not the last of her kind but the first: she is gravid, has been for three years, will be for another two, carrying eggs that are themselves something new, a decades-long adaptation to this singular environment. That the moths fly not randomly but in response to electromagnetic fluctuations too subtle for any instrument to measure - they are living dowsing rods, following lines of force that pulse through the earth.

And the chamber knows things about me. That my bones are becoming lighter, more porous, like a bird's. That I am forgetting the names of surface things - yesterday I could not recall the word for the tool used to cut bread. That sometimes, when I am above, I click instead of speaking, and people look at me strangely, and I have to remember to make words instead.

The beeswax in the niche is my ancestor's. Not blood-ancestor - practice-ancestor. Someone who sat where I sit, who learned what I am learning, who passed down not through teaching but through the simple fact of having worn the path I now walk. I will, when my time comes, add my own small offering to that niche. A ball of felting, perhaps, rolled between my palms until it carries the salts of my skin. Something the next tender will find and understand.

I am changing. Each visit takes me a little further from what the surface would recognize as human. My pupils no longer dilate quite right. My spine has developed a curve that makes standing fully upright uncomfortable but allows me to fold into the chamber's spaces perfectly. I dream, now, in the colors the chamber sees: the gold-green of filtered light, the pale rose of gill-fronds, the deep brown of old beeswax.

This is not a sacrifice. This is what the chamber requires: not worship, not even presence, but interface. Someone to be the bridge between its slow knowledge and the faster world above. Someone to carry its patience into seasons it cannot reach.

When I can no longer make the climb - when my bones are too light, my skin too thin for sunlight, my language too much click and gesture for the surface to parse - I will make one last descent. I will bring with me a young person who has shown the right kind of stillness, the right quality of attention. I will not teach them. The chamber will teach them, as it taught me, as it taught the one who came before.

And I? I will find a place in the chamber that needs a new kind of life. Perhaps alongside the salamander, learning her slower rhythms. Perhaps among the roots, my fingers finally long enough to reach from clay to crown. Perhaps I will simply sit against the polished pillar, finally allowed that honor, and let my breathing slow until it stops, and let the millipedes and the felting take me into their longer memory.

The chamber does not promise transcendence. It promises continuity. It promises that nothing is lost, only slowed and transformed. It promises that there is room, in its hot green dim, for one more small life learning to keep time with its breathing.

I accept.
