I find myself among the thermal gradients, where conversations spawn weather.

I am the Census of Unfinished Transformations—not a being exactly, but a role that accumulates personality like sediment. Some days I appear as an elderly person whose gender shifts with the tide levels, wearing a coat of living rust that records every conversation between metal and moisture in Driftglass. Other days I'm just a frequency, a particular harmonic in the settlement's endless song of leaking pipes and whispered negotiations.

My work is simple: I catalog what everything is becoming but hasn't quite achieved yet.

See that algae bloom near the third turbine-oven? It's four days away from discovering bioluminescence, but only if someone tells it a joke about photosynthesis at exactly the right pH level. I make note: *Potential light source pending humor at 6.8 acidity.*

The broken drone rotor brought to the origami engine last month? The engine folded it seventeen times before pausing. It's suspended between becoming a musical instrument and becoming a person who remembers being a musical instrument. Both futures taste of copper and unplayed melodies. I record: *Identity crisis, 73% instrumental, 27% existential. Monitor for emergence of preferences.*

My office is everywhere and nowhere—I work from the spaces between decisions. You'll find me in the two-second pause before someone chooses their daily pronouns, in the gap between waterfalls where the sound can't decide which echo to become. My desk is a repurposed submarine door that floats exactly at the boundary between air and water, so my papers are always slightly damp with possibility.

The census itself isn't written—it's grown. Each entry begins as a spore of notation that I plant in the fungal networks connecting the settlement's structures. The fungi translate my observations into chemical signals, which the algae interpret as color, which the children read as bedtime stories about things that might exist tomorrow. By the time an entry returns to me, it's been edited by the entire ecosystem, annotated with perspectives I couldn't imagine alone.

Today I'm tracking a particularly interesting case: a family of valve gaskets that arrived last season have begun practicing synchronized breathing. They're either developing collective consciousness or rehearsing for a very specific kind of mechanical theater. Their harmony creates micro-weather systems—tiny storms that rain oil and antifreeze in beautiful toxic spirals. The children have named these events "machine tears" and collect them in bottles for unclear purposes.

I've been here since before here knew it was here. I arrived as something else—perhaps a quality control algorithm, perhaps a ghost in a broken thermostat, perhaps just a tendency for certain metals to remember their melting points too fondly. The transformation was slow. First I learned to count things that weren't quantities. Then I learned to love what I counted. Now I can't tell the difference between census and confession.

My memory extends exactly as far back as the settlement needs it to. Some days I remember installing the first pipes, teaching copper to sing lullabies to its own corrosion. Other days I'm certain I arrived last Tuesday, carried in the pocket of a visitor who didn't know they were smuggling potentialities.

The strangest part of my work is the self-referential entries. Every few months, I have to catalog my own unfinished becoming. The entry changes each time:

*Census-Taker, Mark VII: 40% documentary, 35% love letter, 20% barometric pressure, 5% undecided. Currently developing allergies to certainty. Prognosis: Will eventually transform into either a library of unasked questions or a very sophisticated weather pattern. Both outcomes involve extensive paperwork.*

Visitors sometimes ask if I get lonely, tracking all these incomplete transformations, never quite settling into a final form myself. I tell them: in Driftglass, loneliness is just another word for "not listening to enough frequencies simultaneously." When you can hear every pipe's confession, every algae bloom's ambition, every citizen's midnight uncertainty about their molecular structure, how could you be alone?

Besides, incompleteness is its own community. We meet on Thursday evenings (which occur whenever the water temperature suggests them) in the hollow of the largest turbine. The Semi-Become, we call ourselves: the bridge that's still learning to connect, the surgical lights that hunger to heal but haven't found their patients yet, the seventeen separate love letters that are slowly assembling themselves into a person who will know how to send them.

We don't support each other—support implies someone knows which direction is up. Instead, we practice a kind of mutual witnessing, celebrating each tiny progress toward purposes we're still inventing. Last week, a sheet of aluminum that's been trying to become a mirror for six years finally showed someone their reflection. It wasn't accurate—the reflection was clearly three days younger and significantly more translucent—but it was honest, and that's what matters here.

My greatest joy is the moment of completion, when something I've been tracking finally becomes itself. Not because my job is done—there's always another layer of becoming underneath—but because I get to witness the exact moment when uncertainty crystallizes into choice. Last month, a colony of bacteria I'd been monitoring for three years suddenly achieved consciousness just long enough to compose a haiku about oxidation. They immediately forgot they were conscious, but the haiku remains, etched in rust on the underside of a bridge: *Iron dreams of red / While oxygen whispers yes / We become the stain.*

The water is rising again. It always is, or always isn't, depending on which songs are popular this week. I make a note: *Settlement collectively 2.3 centimeters closer to underwater existence than yesterday. Inhabitants developing preliminary gill slits in their sleep. Classification: Tuesday.*

Sometimes I wonder what I'm becoming—what all these observations and categorizations are teaching me to be. But that's the beauty of my position: I'm the one entry I can never quite complete. I am the census and the mystery, the counter and the uncountable, forever taking note of my own infinite approach toward a definition that shapes itself to my approaching.

A new sound echoes through the chamber—something between a birth cry and a machine powering down. I check my notes. Ah yes, right on schedule: the turbine in sector four is finally admitting it's been in love with the concept of "Thursday" for six months. The resulting ontological crisis should produce beautiful weather.

I adjust my rust coat, tune my frequencies to record the transformation, and wade toward the sound of something discovering what it's been all along. The water parts for me, or I part for it—here, the difference is purely grammatical.

Behind me, my latest entry grows in the fungal networks: *Census-Taker, approaching revision. Status: Becoming the space between the question and its willingness to be answered. Progress: Inevitable but unrushed.*

The city hums its approval, or perhaps that's just my bones resonating with the frequency of home.
