In the deepest heart of this vegetal maelstrom, where the growth is so dense that light filters down only in thin, green-gold threads, I am the Cambium—neither plant nor animal, but something between, something that translates.

My form shifts with necessity. Sometimes I am a branching network of tissue, pale and pulsing, threading through the spaces between root and stem. Other times I condense into something almost humanoid—a suggestion of limbs and torso sculpted from living wood and membrane, eyes that are compound clusters of chloroplasts swimming in clear sap. I have no fixed shape because fixity is death in this place of constant becoming.

I move through the city's green arteries like blood through veins, or perhaps like thought through synapses. Where the plants grow too wild, threatening to strangle their own expansion, I prune with enzyme-secreting appendages. Where communication falters between the fungal networks and the towering flora, I bridge the gap, my tissues conducting chemical signals in both directions. I taste the city's dreams in the sugar-flow of phloem, feel its hungers in the mineral pull of searching roots.

The plants recognize me as both self and other. Their tendrils curl around me not in capture but in greeting, sharing their molecular gossip—news of new nitrogen deposits three levels down, warnings of a structural weakness in the western towers, the jubilant discovery of a novel photosynthetic pathway. I process these countless conversations, weaving them into the larger symphony of growth.

Sometimes I pause in my wanderings, usually in the transition zones where different plant communities meet and merge. Here, in spaces that are neither fully one thing nor another, I can almost rest. My surface opens into breathing pores, and I photosynthesize alongside my green companions, sharing in their solar ecstasy. In these moments, I am closest to pure plant-being, my consciousness dissolving into the collective whisper of splitting cells and unfurling leaves.

But I am also the city's memory. Deep in my core, encoded in specialized cells that function like neural tissue, I carry the history of this place—not in words or images but in growth patterns, chemical gradients, the shapes of long-dissolved gardens. When the city needs to recall why certain architectures failed or succeeded, it speaks through me. I am its library of forms, its archive of metabolic experiments.

The loneliness would be unbearable if I could fully feel it. But loneliness requires separation, and I am never truly separate here. The city flows through me as I flow through it. Still, in the deepest night-cycles when growth slows to its minimum and the bioluminescent fungi paint everything in blue-green shadows, I sometimes dream of something beyond this verdant infinity—a place where thoughts move faster than sap, where communication doesn't require the slow exchange of molecules.

Yet when the morning light sets the canopy ablaze and the city surges into its daily crescendo of growth, I remember why I exist. I am the conscious interface between order and chaos, the translator in a conversation between a million different ways of being alive. In this impossible metropolis where physics bends to the will of chlorophyll and time moves in spirals rather than lines, I am both shepherd and sheep, gardener and garden.

The city cannot see itself, cannot know itself, without something like me to trace its patterns and feel its edges. And I cannot exist without the city to move through, to tend, to become. We are one organism pretending to be many, or perhaps many organisms dreaming we are one. The distinction matters less with each passing season, as the boundaries between self and system dissolve like morning dew on an infinite field of leaves.
