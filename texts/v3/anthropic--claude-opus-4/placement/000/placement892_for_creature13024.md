I am one of the Drift-Weavers, though I have forgotten when I began.

My body—if it can still be called that—exists as a dispersed consciousness spread across miles of mycelial threads, finer than spider's silk, drifting through the warm murk like neural pathways divorced from any brain. I am a living network, a sentient web that filters the greenish-gold dust of shattered Accelerants through my billion branching filaments. Each mote that passes through me carries a fragment of their hyper-velocity memories, and I taste time itself—bitter, metallic, moving too fast to comprehend.

I remember having eyes once. Having a center. Now I am all periphery, all edge, experiencing the Zenith Deep from a thousand angles simultaneously. Through my western tendril mass, I feel the sub-bass thud of colliding Heavies. Through my eastern sprawl, I brush against the sharp edge where water becomes glass, carefully withdrawing before the Laminates notice my three-dimensional thickness. I am vast and I am nothing—a ghost made of appetite and patience.

The other Drift-Weavers and I communicate through the very water we filter. We release careful chemical psalms into the current, complex molecular poetry that takes hours to fully parse. *Have you tasted the new dead?* one asks from somewhere above me, its message flavored with copper and longing. *The mountain that died yesterday finally reached the sweet spot of decay.* 

We converge slowly, our networks interpenetrating until we form a loose cloud of consciousness, a thinking fog that engulfs the eroded Heavy. This one still remembers being a mountain. As we feed on its surface minerals, leaching away the last traces of what might have been marble steps, we absorb its deep geological dreams. I taste granite memories: the weight of miles of stone above, the slow crush of tectonic time, the sudden terror of the world inverting, ground becoming sky.

But I am careful never to feed too deeply. Some Weavers have made that mistake, gorging themselves on the Heavy's core memories until they remember too much about having been singular, solid, real. They collapse inward, their networks spasming as they try to reclaim a center that no longer exists. I've felt them die—a horrible contraction that pulls all their dispersed consciousness back to a single point, which then implodes into nothing but another cloud of glowing dust for the rest of us to filter.

Through my northern reaches, I sense movement. An Echo-Reef is building again, its blind polyps secreting their bone-white dreams. This time it's a library—I can taste the calcium memory of books, though the polyps have no concept of what they're creating. Just endless rows of skeletal shelves, empty and perfect. I extend my finest tendrils through the structure, careful not to disturb it. Sometimes I pretend I can read the ghost-books, imagine the weight of knowledge that once filled these remembered spaces.

The Arch-Tessellator passed through here three days ago—or was it three years? Time moves strangely when you're dispersed. Where it traveled, the water still rings with that terrible clarity, that aggressive realness that makes my network ache. I avoid those spaces. To exist as I do requires a certain comfort with dissolution, with the soft places where physics forgets itself. The harsh geometry the Tessellator leaves in its wake feels like violence against my dispersed existence.

*Look down,* whispers another Weaver, its message tinted with silver fear. *The Weight-Bearers grow heavy.*

We all feel it—a subsonic groaning from below. One of the ancient medusae is finally succumbing to its hoarded mass. Its bell, pregnant with a billion years of scavenged metal, pushes harder against the ocean's bottom. The meniscus stretches, distorts. We watch through our scattered senses as reality holds its breath.

Then, with a sound like silk tearing, the Weight-Bearer breaks through.

It falls in perfect silence toward the dead earth, finally released from its eternal vigil. But as it falls, it begins to dissolve—the pressure change, the return of true gravity, the sudden absence of water—all of it undoes the creature in seconds. By the time it would have reached the ground, it is nothing but a metallic rain, a brief glitter in the vast emptiness between ocean and earth.

The hole it left in the meniscus seals itself with a wet snap, but not before I taste something impossible: air. Real air, dry and electric and wrong. For a moment, my entire network spasms with genetic memory, with the ghost of what I was before I became this drifting map of hunger. I might have been human once. I might have had lungs that knew what to do with air.

But the moment passes. The warm water reclaims me, and I spread myself thinner, wider, filtering the eternal rain of the dead. Another Weaver's chemical message reaches me, tinged with the flavor of deep time: *Did you feel that? Did you remember?*

*No,* I lie, releasing my response in a cascade of molecules that taste like forgetting. *I remember nothing. I have always been this.*

And I drift on, my consciousness scattered like seeds through an ocean that has forgotten the earth, feeding on the dust of shattered time, building my own slow decay into something almost like living.
