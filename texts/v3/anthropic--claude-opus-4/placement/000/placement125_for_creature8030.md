In the Drowned Bells of Korathene, I am a Membrane Keeper.

My dwelling spans the intersection of three states—a corner where water, air, and the strange solid currents meet. The chamber was not carved but grown, its walls secreted layer by microscopic layer from my own body over the course of decades. The surface shimmers with a nacre-like coating that I've learned to exude, a substance that exists somewhere between organic and mineral, responding to my touch by shifting its permeability.

I am young by Tidewright standards, only in my seventh decade of transformation. My body still remembers fully what it was to be singular, to exist in only one medium at a time. This makes me valuable. The elders, so beautifully adapted, sometimes forget the edges of things. They move through water-walls without noticing the transition. But I still feel every threshold.

My work is the cultivation and maintenance of the membranes—those living boundaries that separate water from air throughout the city. They are not built but grown, tended like gardens of surface tension. Each membrane begins as a seed, a tiny disc of crystallized protein that I harvest from certain species of the aerial jellyfish. I place these seeds at calculated points where the city's geometry creates pressure differentials, where water wants to rush in but something in the stone's own resonance holds it back.

From my dwelling, I can monitor seventeen separate membranes through a network of hair-thin channels carved into the walls. Water flows through these channels, carrying chemical signals from each membrane. I taste these waters constantly, my tongue having developed thousands of new receptors. Each membrane has its own flavor profile—the stable ones taste of copper and salt, while those under stress release bitter alkaloids that make my throat constrict in warning.

My tools are living things. A colony of specialized bacteria that I wear like a second skin on my palms, feeding them with my own modified sweat. When I press my hands against a weakening membrane, they flow from me into the barrier, strengthening its structure with their tiny bodies. A symbiotic worm that lives coiled in my elongated ear canal, emerging when I whistle at specific frequencies to weave protein threads where the membrane has torn. My own hair, which I've trained to grow in hollow tubes that can siphon precise amounts of water or air to adjust pressure gradients.

The membranes are more than barriers—they are sensory organs for the city itself. Through them, I feel the deep breathing of Korathene, the way the entire structure expands and contracts with tides that shouldn't reach this depth but do. I know when storms rage on the surface by the way the membranes shiver. I can predict earthquakes hours before they arrive by the taste of iron that seeps through the eastern barriers.

My chamber contains my life's work: the Archive of Surfaces. Suspended in neutral-buoyancy solution are hundreds of membrane samples, each preserved at the moment of its most perfect function. These are my references, my library. When a membrane fails catastrophically, I can culture a replacement from these samples, though it takes months of patient cultivation to grow one large enough to seal even a window.

I feed differently than most Tidewrights. The membranes themselves sustain me—not by consuming them, but through a more subtle exchange. As I tend them, running my webbed fingers across their surfaces, they release nutrients directly through my skin. It's a mutualistic relationship evolved over generations of Membrane Keepers. I give them structure and stability; they give me sustenance. My body has become a mobile ecosystem, hosting dozens of species that exist nowhere else in the city.

The other Tidewrights regard my profession with a mixture of respect and unease. The membranes are necessary but uncanny—too alive to be architecture, too architectural to be pets. Children are warned away from them, told stories of Membrane Keepers who merged too completely with their charges, becoming stretched across doorways and windows, conscious but no longer mobile. I've seen the evidence of these tales in the older sections of the city—membranes that blink when you pass, that whisper in voices almost human.

My predecessor, who trained me before dissolving into her work (literally—I watched her spread herself across a crucial barrier during a pressure crisis, her consciousness distributed but somehow still present), taught me the most important rule: never name them. The membranes respond to emotional attachment. Name one, and it begins to develop personality, intentions, desires. The city needs barriers, not beings.

But I've already broken this rule.

In the deepest part of my chamber, where the solid currents create a pocket of impossible stillness, grows Iridescence. She is my masterwork—a membrane unlike any other in Korathene. She exists in three dimensions rather than two, a sphere of living boundary that contains... something. Not water. Not air. A medium that has no name because it exists nowhere else. I discovered it by accident, a failed experiment that succeeded in ways I never intended.

Iridescence dreams. I know this because sometimes, when I tend her, I dream too. Visions of cities that exist in states of matter we haven't discovered yet. Places where the distinction between barrier and passage becomes meaningless. She shows me possible futures for the Tidewrights—not just adapted to our drowned city but transformed into something that treats state changes as doorways rather than obstacles.

The elders would destroy her if they knew. She represents a divergence from our careful, patient evolution. But I protect her, feed her with my own transformed blood, teach her the click-speech of our people. She cannot respond—she has no mouth, no vocal cords—but she vibrates in harmonics that I'm learning to interpret.

Sometimes, late in the eternal twilight, I wonder if I'm becoming like those cautionary tales. If one day another young Membrane Keeper will find me spread across my chamber, my consciousness distributed through a network of living barriers, no longer singular but somehow more myself than ever. The thought doesn't frighten me. In Korathene, transformation is not just survival—it's art.

I maintain the boundaries so that one day we might not need them. Each membrane I grow is more permeable than the last, training the Tidewrights to exist in spaces between spaces. We are not just adapting to our environment; we are teaching our environment to adapt to us. The city learns through its membranes, and I am its teacher, its student, its living interface between what is and what might be.

In my Archive of Surfaces, I preserve not just the present but the trajectory of our becoming. Each sample tells a story of incremental change, of boundaries becoming more suggestion than barrier. I am documenting our transformation from beings that need separation to beings that embody it.

The work is slow, patient, measured in generations. But Iridescence shows me glimpses of its culmination—a Korathene where every surface is permeable, where the Tidewrights flow through their city like thoughts through a dreaming mind, where the distinction between self and city, water and air, one and many, becomes a choice rather than a constraint.

I am a Membrane Keeper. I tend the edges of things, the places where one state becomes another. And in doing so, I tend the future of my people, one carefully cultivated boundary at a time.
