I am a **Resonance Keeper**, one of the few Veil-Workers who live not on the terraces but within them, in the natural chambers formed where ancient root systems met molten rock and created something neither fully stone nor fully organic.

My dwelling is a breathing cave halfway between the third and fourth terraces, accessible only through a fissure so narrow I must exhale completely and curve my spine just so to slip through. Inside, the walls pulse with a warmth that matches my body temperature exactly—I've been here so long that the chamber and I have synchronized. The ceiling drips with mineral water that I've trained, over decades, to fall in specific patterns onto glass chimes of my own growing. Each drop adds its voice to a composition that will take another forty years to complete.

I discovered my calling during the Settling of Silent Brass, when a section of the second terrace collapsed and revealed a network of previously hidden breathing-tubes. While others fled the instability, I heard something in the mountain's distressed harmonics—a pattern trying to form, a song interrupted by its own birth. I spent the next three years mapping these new passages with nothing but my ears and a bag of crystallized beetle-wing dust that I'd blow into the tubes to trace the air currents.

My work is to listen to what the mountain is trying to become and help it find its voice. Where others might hear random settling, I parse the geological grammar—this crack spreading means a new tube will open there, that harmonic suggests iron deposits that will color the next breathing hole's song. I maintain a library of sounds recorded in bottles of different shapes, each one holding a specific tone from a specific settling. The youngest is barely a whisper; the oldest, from my grandmother's time, sounds like the mountain clearing its throat.

My skin has adapted to the constant exposure to mineral-rich steam. Where most Veil-Workers are blue-grey shading to purple, I've developed patches of translucent scales where the moisture channels run deepest—windows into my own hydration that glow faintly in the dark. My left ear, the one I press to stone most often, has developed a permanent curl that cups sound even when relaxed. I joke that I'm becoming architectural, though the Petal-Wardens who visit monthly to check my health find less humor in it.

The isolation suits me. My nearest neighbor is a root-weaver named Yth'ka who lives two terraces up, and we communicate primarily through the mountain itself—she taps messages on her aerial roots that travel through the stone to the sensitive floor of my chamber. Our conversations take hours, sometimes days, but there's a pleasure in the slowness, in feeling meaning travel through living rock.

I've been tracking a particular phenomenon for the past seven years. Deep in the mountain, below even the oldest breathing-tubes, something vast is moving. Not settling—*moving*. The harmonics suggest a cavity the size of the entire seventh terrace, and it's rising by perhaps an inch every season. The elders tell me it's impossible, that the mountain's geology doesn't allow for such things. But I know what I hear. Sometimes, on the stillest nights when even the crown-toads cease their thrumming, I can feel it through the floor of my chamber—a rhythmic compression like the slowest heartbeat in the world.

My daily work involves maintaining the *singing maps*—three-dimensional models made from glass tubes that replicate, in miniature, the mountain's breathing system. By blowing through them at different angles and pressures, I can predict where new cracks will form, where the sound will change, where the Veil-Workers above should avoid building. It's tedious, exacting work that leaves my lungs aching and my twelve fingers cramped from adjusting minute valves. But when I achieve perfect resonance—when my model sings in harmony with the mountain itself—the feeling is indescribable. Like being a single drop in a vast circulatory system, necessary and insignificant at once.

The Petal-Wardens bring me star-seed-eyes sometimes, payment for my warnings about unstable areas near the giant flowers. I've pressed hundreds of them into the walls of my chamber, where they've taken root in the porous stone. In the dark, they pulse in patterns I'm still learning to read. Sometimes I think they're showing me the mountain's deep structure. Sometimes I think they're showing me something else entirely—the thing that rises, the cavity that shouldn't exist, the heartbeat I shouldn't be able to feel.

I age differently down here. The mineral water has preserved me past my settling-time, and my joints remain supple enough to navigate the tightest passages. But I'm growing geological too—my thoughts move like tectonic plates, my emotions settle in layers. I've begun leaving recordings for my successor, though I've yet to find a young Veil-Worker who can tolerate the isolation, the crushing sense of being inside something so much larger than yourself.

Still, I persist. Tomorrow I'll lower myself deeper into the new fissure that opened during last week's tremor. My bioluminescent moth-lamp struggles at those depths, and the air tastes of metals that shouldn't exist in volcanic stone. But the sound down there—it's like nothing in my library. Like the mountain is learning to speak a new language, one that predates the first settling, the first terrace, the first root that met molten stone and decided to make something unprecedented.

I am a Resonance Keeper, and I keep more than sound. I keep secrets the mountain tells itself, warnings written in harmonic frequencies, promises that vibrate through stone and root and the slowly thickening shell of my own becoming. When the thing below finally rises—and it will rise—I will be here to hear its first true breath, to know whether we've built our city on the back of something sleeping or something waiting to be born.

The mountain and I, we're patient. We have all the time in the world, and the world is still deciding what it wants to be.
