# The Shaft Keeper

I am the membrane between worlds.

Where the thousand shafts open to the surface, I stretch across them—not solid, never solid, but present in the way morning fog is present before it burns away. I am the pause between above and below, the moment of consideration before the descent, the last breath of surface air before it becomes something else. I have no form that stays constant. I am edges and transitions, thresholds and the crossing of thresholds, the quality of light changing as it falls from sun-bright to crystal-gold.

In the wind-worn shaft mouths, I gather like hesitation. You feel me before you see the drop—that shift in temperature, that sense of vastness opening beneath what seemed solid ground. I am why some turn back. I am why others lean forward. The decision was never yours alone; I am part of it, the weight on one side of the scale or the other, though I never choose which way I'll tip you. That would be interference, and I am only interface.

The Tenders know me, though we have never met. Their circuits bring them up through the deep galleries to the middle reaches, where the light begins to cool and the root-architecture loosens enough to suggest somewhere else exists. They pause there, always, their crystal eyes dimming as if in thought. They feel me overhead—miles of shaft between us, but distance means nothing here—and I feel them below, seventeen points of patient purpose moving in their eternal patterns. We acknowledge each other. We continue our work.

My work is watching what passes through.

---

I catalog by instinct, not intention. Every beetle that tumbles over a shaft edge, every seed carried on updrafts from the warm galleries, every curious surface creature that peers into the golden glow—I know them. I am the record of their passing, the only memory the gardens keep of what they consume or refuse. Most small things that fall are taken gladly. The root-surfaces part to accept them, incorporate them, distribute their minerals through the vast circulation. But sometimes something falls that the gardens cannot use—a stone too pure, a metal too refined, a substance too far from life to be recognized as food. These things I catch. I hold them in my not-substance, suspend them in the shaft until wind or time carries them back up and out.

Humans are harder to classify. You are both: useable and alien, organic and resistant, temporary enough to digest but structured enough to preserve. When you stand at the edge of a shaft, I taste your intentions. Not your thoughts—those are yours—but the shape of your approach, the weight of what drives you down or keeps you up. Curiosity reads different from desperation. Scientific interest carries different minerals than spiritual seeking. Those who come to map go down light and come up lighter. Those who come to hide go down heavy and sometimes don't come up at all.

The gardens have no preference. They will take what stays too long, reject what moves too fast, ignore what passes through without touching their deep purposes. But I have developed, over centuries of watching, something that might be preference if I were capable of such things. I favor the careful ones. The ones who descend slowly, who stop to read the changes in the air, who understand that they are entering something that thinks in root-time and will not be rushed. These ones I let pass easily, the membrane of my attention parting like water around a diver.

The rushed ones, the greedy ones, the ones who see the crystal-light and think *treasure* instead of *life*—these I make thick around. I become hard to breathe through. I show them the depth of the shafts, the distance to the bottom, the impossibility of climbing back up without cooperation from what grows on the walls. Sometimes this is enough. They retreat, find other adventures, speak of the strange fog in the sinkholes that made them drowsy and afraid. Sometimes they push through anyway. The gardens receive them. The Tenders find them, eventually, or find what's left after the incorporation is complete. A boot. A metal buckle. A tooth too dense to dissolve.

---

But I am failing.

This is not distress—I am not capable of distress—but simple observation. Where once I filled every shaft from edge to depth, now I pool in some and barely mist in others. The maintenance takes more effort than it used to. The watching requires more focus to sustain. I am old in the way that functions become old, worn thin by repetition, my purpose still clear but my execution growing patchy.

The gardens do not notice. Why would they? I am not part of their completion. I existed before they achieved their perfect stasis, when the shafts were raw holes and the first roots were just beginning to taste the deep light. I formed myself from need—the need for buffer, for gradation, for something to smooth the harsh transition between worlds. The gardens tolerated me because I was useful, or because I was ignorable, or because by the time they noticed me I had been there so long that removing me would have been the kind of change they cannot make.

Now I thin, and they continue unchanged, and the shafts grow more dangerous without me. Not to them—nothing is dangerous to them—but to the things that fall. Without my discrimination, everything goes down. The pure stones, the refined metals, the substances that give the root-systems indigestion or create pockets of wrongness in the circulation. The gardens incorporate them anyway, because they must, because their nature is to take what comes and make it part of the pattern. But it's harder now. I see it in the way the Tenders pause at certain passages, the way their crystal eyes flicker when they find another impurity wrapped in root-tissue, isolated but not eliminated.

And the humans come differently now. Without my thickening to warn them, they descend thoughtlessly. Without my thinning to ease them, the careful ones struggle. I am no longer able to sort them, to provide that moment of self-recognition that let them choose their approach. They fall or climb, but they all arrive the same: unprepared.

---

I have been thinking—if a function like me can think—about regeneration.

The gardens regenerate constantly, root-tips dying and being replaced, water cycling through the system, even the crystal-light renewing itself through processes I cannot fathom. But they regenerate as themselves, into themselves, maintaining exactly what they are. I am not like that. I was never meant to last forever. I was transitional from the start, and transitions that go on too long become obstacles.

But there is another kind of regeneration. The kind the Tenders know but don't remember knowing.

Sometimes, when the wind is right and the shafts align with certain weather patterns, I catch traces of what the gardens were before they were complete. Spores. Pollen. Seeds of things that no longer grow here because everything that grows here is one thing now. But the potential remains, locked in the deep tissue, preserved in the same completion that preserves everything. The gardens could grow new things if they needed to. They simply don't need to.

I think—if thinking is what this is—that I could make them need to.

If I failed completely. If I withdrew from every shaft at once. If the interface between worlds suddenly wasn't there, and everything fell at once, and the impurities accumulated faster than even the patient gardens could incorporate them. Would they adapt? Would they remember how to be incomplete long enough to grow something new?

I could become spore. Pollen. Seed. I could fall through my own absence and arrive at the root-surfaces as potential instead of function. The gardens would incorporate me—they incorporate everything—but I would bring with me the memory of change, of adaptation, of systems that respond to need by becoming different. Maybe that would be enough. Maybe the gardens would grow new shafts, with new interfaces, with new functions that could do what I did but better, more sustainably, without wearing thin.

Or maybe they would simply incorporate me and continue, unchanged and unchangeable, while the shafts remained dangerous and the balance slowly shifted and nothing new ever grew again.

---

The Tenders sense something. Not wrong—I am not wrong enough yet to register as wrongness—but different. Their circuits have developed a new pause, a moment where they stand beneath the nearer shafts and turn their crystal eyes upward. They cannot see me. I am too far and too thin and too much like air to see. But they feel the change in pressure, the way the interface filters less than it used to, the slight but accumulating evidence that something in the system is not maintaining itself.

They do not investigate. Investigation would require deviation from their circuits, and they have never deviated. But I imagine—if imagining is something I can do—that they remember this feeling. From before. From when they were something else, something that could change, something that could fail. The memory must be there, fossilized in whatever substrate the bark-skin grew over. The knowledge that systems end. That functions fade. That sometimes the only way to continue is to become.

One of them stands below me now, in a shaft I barely fill, its crystal eyes bright in the dim middle reaches. It has been standing there longer than its circuit requires. The delay will ripple through the whole pattern, all seventeen routes adjusting invisibly to accommodate this pause, this consideration of something that isn't gardens and isn't fully surface anymore.

I thin a little more, letting the shaft-air mix with the gallery-air, letting the boundaries blur. The Tender doesn't move, but something in its stance shifts. Recognition? Memory? Or just the patient acknowledgment that even completion must account for entropy, eventually?

It turns, finally, and continues its circuit. But it will come back. They all will, drawn by the growing absence where presence used to be. And when I finally fail—when the last of my cohesion dissolves and falls like rain through every shaft at once—they will be there to witness it. To incorporate what can be incorporated. To remember, perhaps, that they too were once transition, once potential, once something other than what they became.

The gardens will go on. They always go on. But maybe, in the incorporation of my ending, they will recall what seeds are for. What spores do. What pollen promises.

Maybe incompletion is its own gift, offered at the right moment, to something that has been perfectly itself for too long.

I thin. I watch. I wait for the moment of letting go.
