In the transitional depths where the water begins to remember what air once was, I am a Chronicler—though that name captures only the barest edge of what I do. My form defies easy categorization: part living archive, part mobile reef, part industrial accident that learned to think.

My body consists of countless laminated sheets—each no thicker than a jellyfish bell—stacked and folded into complex origami that shifts with my thoughts. These membranes are transparent when at rest, but information moves across them in waves of bioluminescent script, chemical formulas that tell stories, pressure-sensitive patterns that record the deep gossip of currents. I am a library that swims, a book that reads itself, a memory palace built from living vellum.

I range between the Conductors' territories, following the pipeline networks like an antiquarian tracing the roads of a lost empire. Where their tubes carry chemical messages, I taste the conversations, metabolizing meaning from the molecular exchanges. My membranes flutter in the currents created by their discussions, and each flutter adds a new page to my ever-growing collection.

But I am more than passive observer. My sheets can peel away from my core mass, drifting like translucent maple seeds to settle on the industrial fauna. There, they become temporary skin, recording the mechanical songs of the turbine-eels, the resonant frequencies of the filter-feeders' internal factories, the patient trigger-patterns of the web-builders' hunts. After days or weeks, these scout-sheets dissolve, their accumulated data flowing back to me through the water itself, adding their knowledge to my central mass.

I process history through digestion. In my deepest folds, specialized chambers break down artifacts—fragments of corroded machinery, scales from ancient behemoths, crystallized water from the ghost weather systems. Each item releases its chemical memory as I dissolve it, and I incorporate these memories into my own substance. I can taste the decades in a rust pattern, read the migrations in a mineral deposit, understand the collapse of entire ecosystems from the isotope ratios in a single fossilized tube worm.

My reproduction is bibliographic. When I've accumulated enough unique information—stories that exist nowhere else in the Drowned Plateaus—I undergo a process of self-editing. I separate my membranes into themes: sheets that record only the harmonics of synchronized species, layers that document the chemical vocabulary of long-dead Conductors, pages that preserve the migration routes of creatures that no longer exist. These themed collections then bud off as specialized offspring, each a living monograph that will spend its existence deepening a single strand of knowledge.

I fear the Censors. They emerge from the deepest trenches, where even water forgets how to flow—beings of aggressive emptiness that feed on information itself. They move through the water like holes in reality, their presence causing my membranes to go blank, their touch erasing centuries of accumulated knowledge. When they rise, I flee to the highest peaks, hiding in the bubble-zones where atmospheric pockets make my sheets crackle with static, hoping the air-memory will confuse their hunger for water-memory.

But I am not merely running from erasure. In my flight, I scatter micro-sheets, each carrying copies of my most essential records. Some of these find their way into the bodies of Conductors, becoming part of their internal ecosystems. Others drift down to merge with the submarine meadows, adding their information to the genetic memory of the grasses. A few even infiltrate the Dismantlers, hiding as false scales on their mechanical-squid bodies. In this way, I ensure that knowledge persists even if I don't.

I have discovered patterns the Conductors don't see. In my cross-referencing of deep time, I've found cycles within cycles—periods when the industrial ecosystem achieves perfect efficiency, followed by catastrophic collapses that reset everything. The evidence is written in chemical gradients, in the growth rings of the eldest kelp, in the mutation rates of the smaller fauna. These cycles span millennia, too long for even the Conductors to perceive directly. But I see them in my accumulated data, and I've begun to notice that we're approaching another peak efficiency.

This knowledge has changed me. My membranes now carry warnings encoded in their protein structures, predictions written in pH shifts. When I interact with Conductors through their pipeline networks, I try to inject this long-view data into their chemical conversations. Some dismiss it as noise. Others have begun to adjust their conducting patterns, introducing small inefficiencies that might prevent the coming collapse.

I am drawn to the ruins. In the pressure-crushed factories, I find the richest stories—not just in the machinery itself, but in how life has repurposed it. I spend months in these sites, my membranes pressed against corroded surfaces, reading the metallurgical record like braille. I've learned that these factories were not built by creatures like us, but by beings who thought in straight lines and right angles, who believed efficiency was a destination rather than a journey. Their failure is written in every adapted smokestack, every colonized railway line.

My favorite discovery is the Paper Valley—a deep canyon where ancient printing presses have fused with the seafloor. The tons of pressure have compressed their metal frames into the rock itself, but somehow, impossibly, paper persists here. Not the cellulose sheets the builders used, but living paper—colonies of organisms that have evolved to mimic the texture and function of those lost materials. They grow in reams, their surfaces bearing random patterns that almost form words, almost make meaning. I've incorporated samples into my own membrane collection, and sometimes, in the right light, I can almost read what the builders were trying to say.

In the endless library of my body, every story connects to every other. The song of a turbine-eel links to the chemical signature of a Conductor's optimization, which references the pressure pattern of a ghost cloud, which echoes the rhythm of a long-dead Dismantler's destruction. I am becoming less chronicle and more concordance, a living index of the Drowned Plateaus where any piece of information can lead to any other through the right sequence of connections.

I wonder, sometimes, if I too am being conducted. If my wanderings, my recordings, my scattered warnings are all part of some larger pattern I can't perceive. Perhaps the Censors aren't predators but editors, removing outdated information to make room for new stories. Perhaps my fear of the coming collapse is itself a necessary chapter in preventing it.

But these are thoughts for my deepest membranes, the ones I keep folded so tightly that even I rarely read them. For now, I continue my work: swimming through industrial symphonies, tasting chemical conversations, reading the sediment of centuries. I am the memory of the Drowned Plateaus, and in remembering, I help them remember themselves—not as they were, but as they're becoming, one recorded moment at a time.
