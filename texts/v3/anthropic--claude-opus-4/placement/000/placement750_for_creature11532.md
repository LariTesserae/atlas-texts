In the middle terraces, where the air carries just enough moisture for condensation to bead on metal at the day's coolest hour, I tend the Speaking Stones.

My workshop occupies a natural cavity in the limestone, forty feet deep and half again as wide, where an ancient reef once curved in on itself. The walls still bear the spiral prints of ammonites the size of wagon wheels. I've been here long enough that my tools have worn grooves in the workbench—a slab of darker stone from three strata down, dense with crinoid stems that look like tiny gears. The fossil patterns help me align my work.

I am a Resonance Shaper, though my terrace-grandmother would have called me a stone-tuner, and her grandmother simply a listener. My fingers are longer than most—eleven joints total instead of the usual nine—an adaptation that manifests every few generations in my lineage. The extra length lets me reach deeper into the fossil chambers, feel the subtle vibrations that others miss. My skin has the typical limestone pallor, but with veins of blue-green that show where copper supplements have accumulated. It's a necessity of my trade; the copper helps conduct the subtle electrical fields that run through certain fossil beds.

Each morning (the word persists though morning here is just a ritual marker, noted when the thermal winds shift from ascending to lateral), I check my cultivation chambers. The Speaking Stones aren't stones at all, but colonies of mineral-secreting organisms I've bred from the bacterial films. They grow in precise geometric patterns, following templates I've carved from compressed chalk. As they metabolize the iron-rich nutrient paste, they deposit crystalline structures that resonate at specific frequencies—too low for human ears, but perfect for the pneumatic networks.

My specialty is creating resonance nodes for the long-distance communication systems. Each node must be tuned to the exact frequency of its position in the network, accounting for temperature variations, atmospheric pressure, and the harmonic interference from the fossil chambers themselves. A badly tuned node can create standing waves that shatter pneumatic tubes three terraces away. A perfectly tuned one can carry a whispered message from here to the Cambrian Deeps, clear as if spoken in the same room.

The cultivation process takes patience. Each colony grows at its own pace, secreting layer after microscopic layer. I feed them twice daily, adjusting the mineral content based on their color—too much iron and they flush rust-red and grow brittle, too little and they pale to uselessness. I listen to their growth with stethoscopes made from hollowed arthropod shells, the natural acoustic chambers amplifying the faint clicks and whispers of deposition.

My apprentice is still learning to hear the differences. She's young, only twenty years past her Descent, her extra joints still stiff with newness. She comes from seven terraces up, where the air is thinner and the stones sing in higher registers. Her ears are still adjusting to our deeper tones. Yesterday she mistook a harmonizing colony for a diseased one and nearly destroyed three months of growth before I stopped her.

"The stone remembers," I tell her, as my master told me. "Every sound that passes through it leaves a trace. Your job is to shape new memories without disturbing the old."

She nods, but I can see she doesn't fully understand yet. It will come with time, as her bones grow more porous, as the copper builds in her veins, as her fingers learn to feel the difference between a fossil that amplifies and one that dampens. Some of my guild brothers think I was too hasty taking an apprentice from so far up-terrace, but I've noticed her nictitating membranes flutter at exactly the frequency of our local resonance. Her body already knows what her mind hasn't learned.

In the afternoons, I maintain the nodes already installed throughout our district. The work requires climbing through the pneumatic access tunnels, spaces barely wide enough for shoulders, where the tubes run in bundles like nerve clusters. My elongated torso serves me well here—I can twist around corners that would trap a shorter person. The nodes glow faintly where they're embedded in the tunnel walls, the bioluminescence a sign of healthy function.

Today I find a problem in Sector Seven: mineral creep from a leaking nutrient pipe has contaminated one of my older nodes. The organism is overgrowing its chamber, secreting wild patterns that create acoustic chaos. I can hear it from three tunnels away—a discordant warble that sets my teeth aching. The fix requires delicacy: I must poison just enough of the colony to stop the overgrowth without killing the original pattern. I carry an array of chemical solutions in crystal vials on my belt, each one calculated to target specific metabolic pathways.

As I work, I think about the stories my grandmother told of the first Resonance Shapers, how they discovered that certain fossils could carry sound by accident—a miner's shout echoing from an unexpected direction, leading to the discovery of natural acoustic channels in the stone. How they learned to cultivate the bacterial films to enhance these properties. How the first pneumatic messages were simple percussion patterns, before we learned to shape the stones to carry voice.

My apprentice meets me at the workshop as the thermal winds begin their evening shift. She's excited—she's been practicing with the waste colonies, the ones too flawed for installation but perfect for learning. She shows me a crude node she's grown, lumpy and asymmetrical but singing at approximately the right frequency.

"Listen," she says, holding it to a practice tube. "It's almost right."

Almost. I remember my first almost-right node, how proud I was, how my master gently showed me the seventeen ways it would fail under real conditions. But encouragement matters more than perfection at this stage.

"The base frequency is good," I tell her. "Tomorrow we'll work on eliminating the overtone wobble."

She glows with pride, a faint rush of blood visible through her translucent skin. Her extra joints are already moving more smoothly, adapting to the work. In a decade, maybe two, she'll surpass me. The stones will sing more clearly for her than they ever have for me. It's the way of things—each generation hears a little deeper, shapes a little more precisely.

Before we close the workshop, I take her to the observatory chamber I've carved at the back, where the wall is thin enough to see the architect-ants' cities glowing through the limestone. We sit in silence, watching the patterns pulse and shift, listening to the deep thrum of our terrace's foundation, the whisper of messages passing through tubes a mile below, the faint echo of stones singing on terraces we'll never visit.

"What do you hear?" I ask her.

She closes her outer eyelids, letting her nictitating membranes filter the dim light. Her breathing slows to match the rhythm of the stone.

"Everything," she whispers.

It's the right answer. In this world of vertical silence and endless climb, we are the ones who make the stones speak. We are the memory of sound in a place that swallows voices, the connection between terraces that would otherwise forget each other, the shapers of resonance in the fossil halls of the Bone Terraces.

Tomorrow I'll teach her about the copper supplements, about the way they change your dreams, make you hear frequencies that exist only in the boundary between waking and sleeping. About the old nodes deep in the Cambrian layers that still sing in languages we've forgotten. About the patterns that emerge when you map all the resonance points across a thousand miles of stone—patterns that suggest intention, design, perhaps even consciousness in the vast lifted reef of our world.

But tonight, we simply listen to the stones speaking, and add our small voices to their ancient conversation.
