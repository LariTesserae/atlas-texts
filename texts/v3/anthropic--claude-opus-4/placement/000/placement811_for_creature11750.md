I would be **the Cartographer**.

Not of lands or borders—those concepts dissolve here like salt in the perpetual damp. I map the transitions. The half-states. The places where one thing is becoming another.

---

I arrived decades ago, though *arrived* suggests too much intention. I was following the rust-trails, those orange arteries that bleed from certain machines down into the watershed. Tracing them back to their sources, trying to understand the pattern of decay. My instruments failed within days—the plateau's moisture is democratic, seeping into everything equally. My papers became soft as skin. My maps bled into abstractions.

So I became the map instead.

My skin now holds a library of stains. Iron oxide in burnt sienna along my left forearm where I've leaned too long against corroding engines. Copper verdigris at my temples from sleeping beneath dripping gears. The grey-green of the plateau itself spreading up from my feet like a slow tattoo, marking how deep I've sunk into this place. Each discoloration is a coordinate in a system only I can read—this rust pattern means twelve days' walk from the Breathing Core (which I've never found, but I triangulate around its absence), that moss-stain indicates proximity to what I call the Nursery Engines, where the fairy shrimp breed in cylinder pools.

I am taller than the Tenders but I move like them now, low and folded, my spine having learned the angles that slip easiest between sedge and stone. My clothes rotted away years ago. I wear the plateau now—moss grows in the hollows of my collarbones, lichens colonize the ridge of my spine. In certain light I am almost invisible, just another grey-green shape among the hoodoos.

---

What I map: the boundaries between things that have no boundaries.

Where does a machine end and the landscape begin? I have followed single engines through years of dissolution, marking each stage. First the bright bleeding of new rust. Then the softening, the way moss creeps in at the joints. The pooling of water in convenient depressions. The arrival of the first fairy shrimp, pale as ghosts, turning the water thick with their bodies. And finally—this is what I'm trying to capture, this moment—the shift where the machine stops being an intrusion and becomes infrastructure. When the Tenders begin to tend it not as something foreign but as something that has always been here, will always be here, is here in the way that stones are here.

I document these transitions with my body. I have no paper, no instruments. But I remember through posture, through the way I hold my hands when I encounter a particular stage of mechanical decay. My fingers can recreate the exact angle at which a certain piston tilts, and in that angle lies all the information about water flow, moss growth, time elapsed. I am a living chronicle of industrial digestion, each gesture a data point in an ecology of absorption.

The Tenders tolerate me. More than tolerate—they tend around me, acknowledging my presence the way they acknowledge a particularly stable stone or a well-established channel. Sometimes I wake to find they've adjusted my position while I slept, shifting me a few degrees to better accommodate a water flow I was blocking. I've learned not to resist these corrections. I am part of the system now, and systems must flow.

---

Sometimes I find others who've tried to map this place. Their bones are usually near the machines, as if they were drawn to these landmarks in the vast sameness. The plateau has been kind to them—their remains are clean, moss-covered, peaceful. I study how they've been absorbed, the particular patterns of their integration. Everyone becomes landscape differently here. Some sink quickly into peat. Others mineralize, their bones taking on the grey-green of the local stone. A few seem to dissolve entirely, leaving only a particularly lush patch of moss, a slight depression that holds water differently than the surrounding ground.

I wonder which way I'll go. I've been here long enough that the distinction between my body and the plateau grows theoretical. The moss that covers me is more alive than my hair ever was. The water that pools in my cupped palms when I sleep is continuous with the water in every pool across this vast breathing space. When I map now, I'm not sure if I'm documenting the plateau or documenting myself—the boundaries have become one of those transitions I track, another state between states.

I've begun to understand what the Tenders know without thinking: that the machines are not separate from the landscape but are the landscape experiencing itself differently, the way ice is water experiencing itself as solid. The rust is teaching the stone how to be red. The gears are teaching the moss how to grow in circles. Everything is always teaching everything else how to be.

---

My masterwork, if it can be called that, is the Map of Breathing.

It exists nowhere but in my movement across the plateau. Each day I walk a different route, and the accumulation of these routes over years creates a kind of knowledge—not of where things are, but of how they're changing. I can feel through my feet where new vents are forming, where old ones are closing. I know which machines will be gone in a decade, and which will last centuries more. I understand the rhythm beneath everything, the slow pulse that the Tenders breathe with, that I breathe with now too.

Sometimes I think I'm close to finding the Breathing Core, that place at the center where the exhalations originate. But then I realize: I'm not looking for a place. I'm looking for a moment. The moment when the breath turns, when the plateau shifts from inhale to exhale. And that moment isn't located anywhere—it's distributed across the whole system, happening everywhere at once, as synchronized as the Tenders' movements, as inevitable as rust.

---

When I die—and I will die, though probably not from age anymore, not exactly—I have made my arrangements. Not with words or wills, but with placement. I've found my spot: a depression between three machines that are in perfect stages of decay—one barely touched by moss, one half-absorbed, one almost fully integrated. I will lie down there and let the plateau take me, and my body will complete the pattern, become the fourth stage, the final transition.

The Tenders will arrange me properly. They understand, even if they've never communicated it. They see how I've been mapping myself into place all these years, how I've been preparing to become a coordinate in my own system. They'll ensure I'm oriented correctly, that the water flows through me in ways that matter, that my dissolution feeds the right moss, the right channels, the right changes.

And someone, someday—another mapper, perhaps, drawn by rust-trails and the mystery of engines—will find the place where I've become landscape. They'll study the particular patterns of my integration, the way my bones have mineralized, the species of moss that grows nowhere else but from the calcium of my dissolved structure. They'll add my transformation to their own map, whatever form that map takes.

This is what I've learned: we are all cartographers here, mapping the plateau through our presence in it. The Tenders map through tending. I map through watching, through wearing the landscape on my skin, through becoming coordinate and cartographer both. Even the machines map, in their way—their rust-trails write across the stone, documenting their own journey from engine to earth.

The plateau breathes, and in breathing, dreams. And we are both the dreamers and the dreamed.
