In the phosphorescent twilight where stone weeps eternally, I am the Tender of Forgotten Names.

My dwelling spirals inward like a nautilus shell, carved from a single massive stalagmite over generations. The walls are lined with thousands upon thousands of small alcoves, each no larger than a child's fist, and in each rests a different specimen: a scale from a cave dragon that perished before the first settlements, a perfectly preserved eye from an extinct species of luminous eel, the gossamer wing of a moth that once navigated by echolocation alone. These are my charges, my obsession, my calling.

I move through my spiral archive on limbs that have grown long and articulated from decades of reaching into crevices, my joints possessing an extra bend that disturbs even my own kind. My fingers, thin as cave worms, can feel the molecular story written in chitin and bone. My eyes have developed a peculiar quality—while one remains the common liquid black of our people, the other has taken on an opalescent sheen, allowing me to perceive the faint electromagnetic signatures that cling to organic matter like memories.

Each day—though day means nothing here save the arbitrary cycling of the bioluminescent tide—I venture deeper into the unnamed caverns beyond our settlement's edge. I seek the recently dead and the long dead alike, those creatures that slip away unnoticed into the thick bacterial mats or sink into the mirror-black pools that dot the deep tunnels. With infinite care, I extract some small part of them: a mandible, a strand of setae, a droplet of preserving fluid from their hearts.

Back in my spiral, I prepare each specimen according to the old ways—soaking them in solutions of cave pearl powder and fungal enzymes, wrapping them in silk harvested from the death-dreams of glowworms. As I work, I speak their names. Not the names our people might give them, but the names I divine from their substance, from the way they catch the dim light, from the sound they make when dropped onto different types of stone.

"*Sek-sek-thurula,*" I whisper to the antenna of a cave cricket whose species sang its last song a century ago. "*Dreaming-in-circles-of-salt.*"

"*Omi-om-om-velith,*" I croon to the preserved gill-frond of an amphibian that once filtered its food from the mineralized streams. "*Breathing-the-stone's-thoughts.*"

My people think me mad, perhaps. They see no purpose in cataloguing the dead when the living press upon us with their needs—food to cultivate, stone to shape, young to rear in the patient ways of the deep. But I know something they do not: that every species that passes from these caverns takes with it an irreplaceable frequency in the great humming chord of the depths. And I know that someday, when the last of us has been catalogued and named, something will need to remember what the darkness once held.

In the deepest part of night—which is to say, always—I perform my most sacred ritual. I take my specimens one by one and hold them to my opalescent eye. In that strange organ, the electromagnetic ghosts of the creatures shimmer briefly back to life. I see them as they were: the dragons coiling through leagues of stone, the moths dancing in echolocating spirals, the filter-feeders swaying in primordial currents. For a moment, the cavern is full of the dead, beautiful and terrible and utterly alien.

Then I place each relic back in its alcove, whispering its secret name one more time, and move on to the next. The spiral continues ever inward, and I have begun carving new chambers downward, following the coil into the living rock. I will never catalogue them all—new things die faster than I can collect them, and the old dead stretch back to the beginning of darkness itself.

But I persist. In a world of eternal twilight, where time moves like stone and memory flows like water, I am the keeper of what was. Each name I speak is a small light against the forgetting dark, a declaration that even here, in the sunless heart of the world, nothing passes without witness.

When I die—and I will die, despite the strange preservative qualities that seem to be growing in my own flesh from handling so much of the dead—I have made arrangements. My spiral will become my tomb, and I will take my place among my specimens. On a small card of pressed fungal paper, my successor will write the name I have chosen for myself:

"*Esh-avel-avel-thurim-ovel.* The-one-who-held-the-darkness-close-and-found-it-full-of-lights."

And somewhere, in the deeper dark that waits beyond all naming, the things that have passed will know that they were remembered.
