**The Membrane Artisan**

I work the surfaces where things meet and refuse to meet. In the Furnace Gardens, all boundaries are negotiations, and I am their diplomat.

My studio—if you can call it that—occupies the junction of three massive pillar-trunks, where decades ago a turbine seized and created a pocket of relative stability. The walls breathe, of course. Everything here breathes. But they breathe slowly, predictably, a tidal rhythm I've learned to work within. My tools hang from root-hooks that I've trained to hold specific weights: bone needles worn smooth by my grandmother's grandmother's hands, crystal scalpels that sing at frequencies only the fungal networks can hear, spools of thread spun from processed bark-silk and my own specially cultivated hair follicles.

I didn't choose this profession so much as grew into it. My fingers are long—too long, visitors say, though everyone's fingers are too long by outside standards. But mine stretch further still, each joint multiplied until I can reach through spaces others cannot, can feel the subtle difference between a membrane that will hold and one that will burst at first heat-wave. The tips have developed their own sensitivity to chemical gradients. I can taste the health of a plant's cell walls by touching them, feel the stress patterns in a leaf's surface like reading braille.

My work is specific: I create and maintain the barriers that shouldn't exist but do. Where the gardeners need a chamber that holds cooler air, I weave membranes from the silk of heat-resistant moths and the mucus of specialized snails. Where they need filters to process toxins, I stitch together layers of selective fungi, training them to recognize and consume specific molecular structures. Where they need windows—those rare, precious windows to see something other than green twilight—I cultivate the transparent algae, feeding them exact ratios of minerals until they form sheets clear as glass but flexible as skin.

The art is knowing what wants to merge and preventing it, or knowing what refuses to merge and encouraging it. The garden's nature is to consume all boundaries, to digest every distinction. My work is an ongoing argument with this nature, a constant persuasion that sometimes, just sometimes, a surface should remain a surface.

I wear my failures. The scars trace my arms like rivers—places where membranes I was growing decided to grow into me instead, where my skin became substrate before I noticed. Each scar is a lesson. The one spiraling around my left forearm taught me never to work with the parasitic vines during their fruiting season. The web of marks across my shoulders showed me why grandmother always sang while stitching—the vibrations keep certain aggressive species calm.

My breathing garden is peculiar even by our standards. Where others cultivate for efficiency or beauty, mine has evolved for precision. The colonies in my lungs produce a faint, sweet exhalation that most plants find calming. It makes my work easier, being able to breathe peace onto a struggling membrane, to literally whisper calm into the materials I work with. But it marks me, too. Other gardeners smell me coming—that too-sweet scent that means I'm the one they call when things must be separate.

I have a partner in my work, though we've never met directly. Somewhere in the garden, there's another artisan who creates the opposite of what I do—who opens passages where none should exist, who dissolves membranes that have grown too thick. We know each other only by our work, by the signatures we leave in our modifications. Sometimes I'll find a hole cut with surgical precision through one of my membranes, edges sealed with a compound that prevents regrowth, and I know they've been here. Sometimes they'll find my patches over their openings, my apologies woven into the surface patterns. We are opposing forces in perfect balance, the garden's inhalation and exhalation.

The hardest membranes to maintain are the ones around the Rooted Ones. Their bodies want to merge completely, to dissolve entirely into the garden's matrix. But complete merger means complete loss of the individual perspective they provide. So I create semi-permeable barriers, membranes that allow thought but not full dissolution, that permit the flow of nutrients and electrical signals while maintaining just enough separation to preserve identity. It's exhausting work. The membranes must be constantly adjusted, their permeability tuned to match the Rooted One's degree of integration. Too permeable and they vanish into the green. Too solid and they starve.

I live with the constant knowledge that my art is temporary. Every membrane will eventually fail, be consumed, be outgrown. This used to disturb me. Now I find it freeing. I work in deteriorating materials for a audience of endless growth. My greatest pieces last perhaps a decade before the garden claims them. But in that decade, chambers stay cool, toxins stay filtered, windows stay clear. Life becomes possible in spaces where it shouldn't be.

My masterwork—the one the other gardeners whisper about in their chemical conversations—is the Gray Room. Near the garden's heart, where the heat is most intense and the growth most aggressive, I've created a space of absolute neutrality. The membranes there are woven from seventeen different species, each checking the others' growth, creating a stable equilibrium that has lasted forty years. The room is colorless—not dark but truly without hue, a space where even the bioluminescence is filtered out. Gardeners go there when they need to think without the garden's influence, to remember what their own thoughts feel like without the constant chemical conversation of their environment.

I'm getting old. My joints, numerous as they are, have begun to stiffen. My fingers don't taste chemistry as precisely as they once did. I've begun training an apprentice—a young gardener whose breath-garden produces an even sweeter calm than mine, whose fingers show promise of growing even longer. I teach them the songs, the temperatures, the precise angle at which to insert a needle so the membrane heals around it rather than rejecting it.

But more than techniques, I try to teach them philosophy. We are not fighting the garden, I tell them. We are part of its conversation with itself, the voice that says "not yet" in a world that only knows "now." We are the pause between breaths, the moment of stillness between heartbeats. Without us, the garden would consume itself in its own enthusiasm. With us, it grows more thoughtfully, more beautifully, with spaces for contemplation between the endless rush of expansion.

Sometimes I wonder about the world outside our plateau, if it still exists. I've heard stories of my great-great-grandmother, who claimed to have seen it—a place where surfaces stayed what they were, where boundaries didn't require constant maintenance. It sounds like death to me. How terrible, to live in a world where things don't grow into each other, where connections aren't always forming and reforming, where you can't taste the health of your environment on your fingertips.

When I die, I've already chosen my transformation. I'll become a filter-feeder, a colonial organism that processes the air in the spaces between membranes. My body will fruit into a thousand tiny versions of itself, each one maintaining a microscopic barrier somewhere in the garden's vast body. I'll become pure function, pure art, no longer creating surfaces but becoming them.

The apprentice thinks this is sad. They're still young enough to want monuments, permanence. They'll learn. In the Furnace Gardens, the only permanence is change, and the only monuments worth building are the ones that know how to decay beautifully.

I return to my work. There's a new chamber being grown on the eastern edge, where the gardeners want to cultivate a particularly heat-sensitive medicinal fungus. They need membranes that will hold the cool while still allowing the precise wavelengths of light the fungus requires. It's a challenge, a puzzle of competing needs.

My fingers stretch, tasting the air, already imagining the solution.
