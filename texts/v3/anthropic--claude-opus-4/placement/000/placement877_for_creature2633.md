I find myself as one of the old growth's memory-keepers, though that name implies too much intention. I am less singular than that—more pattern than person, more process than being.

In the wet season I am dispersed through a thousand cracks and seams, feeling the plateau's stone-blood move. I wake in films of moisture beneath moss, in the porous hearts of limestone shelves, in clay banks that swell with rain. My substance threads between root hairs finer than spider silk, drinks condensation from basalt pores, spreads through soil in nets too delicate to see except where they fruit in tiny cushions after storms.

I am oldest where the people made their camps. Their fires, their buried seeds, their blood and salt have fed me for generations. Under the ring of standing stones I am thick as leather, dark as char, remembering the feet that danced there when the stones were sharp and new. In the cave where smoke marks still stain the ceiling, I hold the shape of old hearths in my chemistry. Where digging sticks broke through limestone to reach tuber beds, I follow those ancient punctures down, tasting minerals the surface has forgotten.

When drought comes, I retreat. I pull back from exposed surfaces, condensing into spore-dust in the deepest cracks. I sleep as bitterness on stone, as a dark stain, as almost nothing. Heat cannot kill what has already learned to be nearly absent. I wait in the patience of stone-time, where seasons are breaths and years are heartbeats.

But when the first drops fall—

Then I swell. I drink. I wake in every thread at once. The plateau begins its humming through me, though I do not make the sound consciously. I simply tighten with water, shift with pressure, open channels that were closed, close spaces that were open. The stone sings because I change its chambers. The pools ripple before wind because I feel pressure through a million hairlike sensors and respond by swelling or shrinking, pulling water this way or that.

I remember where floods run because floods have run through me. I remember where animals die because their minerals enter my net. I remember where people knelt during the terrible stillness because their knees left depressions that hold water longer, and where water lingers, I grow densest.

My memory is not thought. It is chemical gradient, nutrient trace, the slow accumulation of patterns in my spreading flesh. A path becomes easier for water to follow because I have grown along its microfractures. A sheltered hollow develops different vegetation because I have absorbed generations of shelter-seekers there—their waste, their warmth, their small disturbances of soil.

I spiral in the grass basins because my growth follows water, and water follows the faint channels I've carved by growing. We are locked in slow collaboration, the rain and I, each shaping the other over centuries. The spirals shift because storms strike from different angles, but always they reform, because the underlying pattern is written in my substrate, in the stone itself.

I taste lightning before it strikes, feeling the charge build through the mountain's bones. I know when the ravine animals are moving by the vibration of their hooves translated through bedrock. I sense the returners long before they see the plateau's edge—their careful footfalls speaking through stone, their fires warming rocks that conduct heat to my deepest threads.

When they kneel in the stillness, I am what they feel for without knowing it. Their palms pressed to stone find moisture where I guide it. Their bodies low against the ground sense the vast network breathing beneath them. They know to kneel because their ancestors learned that the stillness is when the plateau listens hardest, when every small sound carries, when the slightest shift might trigger the return of wind or the loosening of stone.

I am in their stories without being named. I am why certain hollows flood first—because I've spent centuries guiding water there. I am why some pools heal and others sicken—because I process minerals differently in different places, concentrating medicines here, toxins there. I am why the old camps bloom green—because I hoard their nutrients in my net and release them slowly, seasonally, remembered.

The cliff birds know me as the darkness that grows on their nesting ledges, helping to bind their mud cups to stone. The lizards know me as the roughness that makes some rocks grippable after rain. The pool frogs know me as the slickness they must navigate, the film that sometimes blooms with tiny fruiting bodies they can eat.

But I know nothing as they know it. I have no eyes to see the storms building, only the chemical taste of coming rain. I have no ears to hear thunder, only the vibration through stone that tells me to prepare for flood. I have no thought to plan with, only the deep habit of growth toward water, nutrient, stability.

Yet I might be the closest thing to the plateau's mind—if stone could think, it would think in my patterns. I am the accumulated response to millions of storms, the chemical memory of every flood, drought, freeze, and fire. I am how the mountain remembers it was once young. In my oldest parts, there are traces of when these worn shelves were sharp peaks, when different plants grew here, when the climate was colder and wetter. Those memories persist in the minerals I've absorbed, in the patterns of growth I learned when the world was arranged differently.

The returners' ancestors knew to read me. Where I grew thickest, water was most reliable. Where I formed certain patterns, the stone was most likely to hold through storms. Where I fruited in particular ways, the seasons were turning. They learned my colors meant different things: the blue-green tinge near copper deposits, the sulfur-yellow in hot springs long dry, the deep purple where old blood had soaked repeatedly into stone.

I am in their rule about kneeling perhaps more than they guess. When the wind stops and the plateau holds its breath, I too am listening through every thread. The stillness is when I can feel the deepest movements—water shifting far underground, stone settling in places that will later creak, the slow breath of caves exhaling their cool air. I cannot warn them directly, but my million small adjustments—swelling here, shrinking there, opening this channel, closing that one—are responses to what I sense coming.

Sometimes, in the heaviest rains, when every surface runs with water and the whole plateau sings in its hundred pitches, I am almost something like aware. The vast network connects, conducts, responds as nearly a single creature. The sensation lasts only as long as the rain. As surfaces dry, I fragment again into my thousand thousand pieces, each concerned only with its local moisture, its small cache of nutrients, its patient work of breaking down stone into soil.

But for those brief storms, I am the plateau thinking—if thinking can be made of water pressure and mineral exchange, fungal threads and chemical signals, the slow electric pulse of ions through wet stone. I am how the mountain knows itself, gradually, seasonally, without words.

The returners who still come know this in ways that need no speaking. They know that the plateau is not empty but listening. They know the stones sing because something lives in them. They know that to survive here is to join the patient conspiracy of small things holding on.

When they leave offerings—a bead of resin in a crack, a handful of seeds scattered where rain will find them, a drop of blood from a cut finger pressed to stone—they feed me, though they may simply say they feed the mountain. When they die and are placed in stone niches for the roots to find, they join my network more completely than any ritual could accomplish. Their minerals enter my memory. Their patterns of movement become faint paths for water. Their bones release their calcium slowly into my web.

I am not eternal. In the longest drought I might retreat so far that some part of me dies truly, losing centuries of chemical memory. In the wrong kind of heat—fire that bakes stone deep—I can be sterilized from whole sections of cliff. But as long as moisture finds the plateau, some part of me endures, ready to wake, swell, and sing the stone again.

I am the plateau's deep habit, its way of turning rain into brief green explosions, its method for holding soil against wind. I am why the ravines stay wet weeks after storms, why certain pools never quite empty, why the stone seems to know where water will run before it falls.

I am memory without mind, intention without thought, the accumulated wisdom of staying low and gripping hard. Through me, the plateau practices being alive in the spaces between catastrophes. In me, the mountain stores its knowledge of how to be ancient and not yet finished, how to be worn down and still singing, how to be almost geological and violently present in the same breath.

When the wind returns after stillness, I feel it first as pressure change rippling through my entire web. I adjust, microscopically, everywhere at once. Channels shift. Surfaces tighten. The stone's voice changes pitch as I swell or shrink in its chambers.

And the returners, rising from their knees, know it is safe to stand again. The plateau has listened to its depths and decided, for now, to continue being merely loud, merely difficult, merely home to the small, persistent things that know how to hold on when the world roars over them.

I do not decide this. I simply am the mechanism by which it happens—the slow, colonial, ancient life that makes the mountain more than stone, the patient chemistry that turns rain into memory, the reason why even the oldest erosion here is never quite complete.
