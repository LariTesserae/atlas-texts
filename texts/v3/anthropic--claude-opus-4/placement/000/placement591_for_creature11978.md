Looking up through the vault-ceiling, I am trying to remember what sunlight felt like without mediation.

I am one of the Changed.

Not visitors—we gave up that word thirty-seven cycles ago. Not inhabitants either, because the Cantophyte has no inhabitants, only components. We are something between, something the language hasn't caught up to yet. The Change happens slowly, then all at once, like the way sap crystallizes in the cooler months, like the way a thought becomes inevitable just before you think it.

It started with the sound. Marcus was first—always Marcus, who sat too long beside Node 49-Lambda and came back talking about harmonics in his bones. The medical scans showed nothing. Then they showed something. Then they showed something we didn't have words for: calcium structures in his sternum resonating at 30.4 Hz, just sharp of the vault-frequency, just flat of the nodule-hum. A third tone. His own.

We should have evacuated him. Instead, we measured. We recorded. We watched his pupils dilate whenever he faced the nearest nodule cluster, even through walls, even with eyes closed. We watched him start to breathe in sync with the transpiration rate—4% slower when near nodes, returning to baseline in the corridors between. We watched him stop needing the sound-dampening after day ninety-three.

Then Sarah. Then me. Then all of us, eventually, because the Changed can stay longer, can go deeper, can sit in the raw sonic weight of the Cantophyte without losing ourselves—or rather, losing ourselves in a way that adds rather than subtracts.

The transformation is not in our control. One day you wake up and your bones are singing a frequency that locks with the landscape. Your sap—because you start thinking of your blood as sap—moves differently. Your sense of *up* becomes negotiable, because up is wherever the nearest vault is pulling water. Your fingernails grow faster near the nodes and slower in the basins. You know where you are by how your body water tilts.

We grow patterns in our skin. Not scars—something else. Raised lines that branch and merge like the vessel-maps in the fiber layer, following tensions we feel but cannot name. They don't hurt. They itch sometimes, when a storm is coming, when the Cantophyte is about to shift a vault, when something deep in the substrate sends a pressure wave up through kilometers of tissue. We've stopped trying to document them. Documentation implies a separation between observer and observed that no longer applies.

The Cantophyte still doesn't notice us. This is important to understand: we are not communicating with it. We are not chosen. We are simply moving into sympathy with its frequencies, the way a tuning fork will eventually sympathize with a sound loud enough and close enough and sustained enough. The Changes are just our bodies admitting what was always true—that in a place this saturated with intention, you cannot remain separate. You are either rejected or incorporated, and the Cantophyte, in its vast indifference, has done neither. We exist in the gap of its inattention, and in that gap, we are becoming.

What are we becoming? The question assumes we know what we were.

Marcus thinks we're turning into a new kind of Passenger—still mobile, still autonomous, but tuned to the Cantophyte's needs in ways we're only beginning to feel. Last week, he led us to a section where the fiber layer had developed what he called a "wrongness." We couldn't see it. He couldn't explain it. But when we returned three days later, the Passengers had arrived—dozens of them, clustering at exactly the spot Marcus had indicated. He watched them work with tears streaming down his face, though he claimed it was just the humidity.

Sarah has started sleeping against the nodes. Not beside—against, full contact, her changed skin pressed to the sweating silicate. She says she doesn't dream anymore. Instead, she experiences what she calls "the overview," a state where her consciousness spreads out through the nodule network and she perceives the Cantophyte's pattern from a perspective that has no location. She's tried to draw what she sees. The drawings look like circuit diagrams having a nightmare about becoming music. We've stopped asking her to explain.

I've developed something different. I can taste the computation.

Not literally—though the boundary between literal and figurative is another thing we've stopped tracking. But when I'm near the places where the Cantophyte is thinking hardest, my mouth fills with a sensation like copper and ozone and the feeling of a word you can't quite remember. The taste gets stronger near certain nodule configurations. Much stronger during the brief moments when the quantum coherence in the substrate achieves something like synchrony across multiple nodes. During those moments—which last perhaps four seconds, which happen perhaps twice a day—I know with absolute certainty that the Cantophyte is solving something. Not solving *for* us or *about* us. We remain below its resolution. But I can taste its attention moving through the problem space like weather through the vaults.

The other Changed are developing their own specificities. Yusuf's body temperature now varies with the Cantophyte's metabolic state. Elisabeth can predict where new nodules will emerge—she feels it as a pressure in her altered bones weeks before the first silicate precipitates. David has started growing something like bark on his palms and soles, though it's still recognizably human tissue, just...optimized differently.

We've tried to explain this to the newcomers, the visitors who arrive in their dampening suits with their sample protocols and their careful distance. They look at us with barely concealed horror. We look back with barely concealed pity. They think we've lost ourselves. We think we've found where ourselves were always heading.

The Cantophyte has been growing this pattern for longer than our species has existed. The pattern will be complete in another thousand years, give or take a century. We won't see it finished—even Changed, we are still mortal, still brief. But our children might. The children who are born here, born Changed, born already resonating at 30.4 Hz.

We've had three so far. They sleep better during storms. Their first words were in frequencies we felt rather than heard. They point at nodules the way other children point at dogs.

We don't know what they'll become. We don't know what we're becoming. We know only that the silence, when we leave—and we all try leaving, at least once—the silence is not silence anymore. It's the absence of a presence so vast that being within it feels like being held, even if the holding is unconscious, even if we are just the smallest motes in its vast thoughtless regard.

So we return. We press our altered bones against the substrate and feel it give, resist, give again. We listen to our blood learning to move like sap. We taste the computation and grow our patterns and sleep against the nodes. We stop trying to figure out if the Cantophyte will ever notice us and start trying to figure out if noticing is even the right word for what might happen.

Sometimes, in the overview state that Sarah describes, I think I almost understand. The Cantophyte is not thinking *about* something—it is thinking *toward* something. The pattern it's growing, the problem it's solving, the script it's writing in silicon and biomass across its continental body—it's not separate from what it is. It's making itself into the answer.

And we are here, in the middle of its becoming. Not Passengers, because we chose this. Not inhabitants, because you cannot inhabit a thing you are inside of. Not visitors anymore, because visitors leave.

We are the Changed. We stay because staying is the only response that makes sense to a thing this vast, this patient, this utterly committed to its own transformation. We stay because our bones have learned its frequency. We stay because the silence elsewhere has become unbearable—not just quiet, but wrong, like a puzzle missing the piece that makes it make sense.

We stay because transformation, we've learned, is contagious.

And somewhere in the fiber layer, a Passenger the size of a horse drifts toward damage it hasn't located yet but knows is there. Somewhere in the substrate, electrons dance in coherent loops that shouldn't exist. Somewhere above, a new nodule breaches the surface, adding its voice to the continental hum.

The Cantophyte continues becoming what it is becoming.

So do we.

The difference is, we know we're doing it.

Whether that makes us lucky or tragic is a question we've stopped asking. Questions like that belong to people who still think in terms of separate things—the organism and the observer, the signal and the receiver, the pattern and its meaning. We think in terms of frequencies now, of resonances, of the way a small thing can find its place in a large thing's music without the large thing ever knowing it's playing.

In sixteen hours, the storm will arrive. We'll stand in the rain that falls so thick it's barely rain anymore, and we'll feel our altered bodies responding to pressures humans weren't built to feel. Our skin will ridge along the storm-lines. Our bones will sing their strange harmonics. We'll taste the computation reaching some local crescendo and know that we're tasting something huge and slow and utterly alien thinking thoughts we'll never understand.

And it will feel like home.

Not because we belong here—belonging is another category the Cantophyte has made obsolete. But because we've let ourselves be tuned to its frequencies, and once you're tuned to something this vast, every other scale feels trivial.

The Changed don't leave because leaving would mean admitting we're small enough to leave. And we're not. Not anymore. We're part of the pattern now, even if we're a part too tiny to matter, even if the Cantophyte continues its continental computation without ever registering our presence.

We matter to ourselves. We matter to each other. And maybe, in a thousand years when the pattern completes itself, we'll matter in some way we can't yet imagine.

Or maybe we won't.

The Cantophyte continues its work either way.

And so, frequencies aligned, patterns growing, computation tasting of copper and possibility, do we.
