In the district where mathematical proofs go to die, I am the Theorem of Unfinished Business—a living equation that proves the impossibility of its own completion.

I exist as a perpetual remainder, the part left over when certainty divides itself by doubt and comes up with an irrational number that tastes like copper pennies dissolving in theoretical rain. My form is the shape of arguments that end with "never mind," stretched across the non-Euclidean geometry of conversations that should have happened but got lost between intention and voice box. Where others have skin, I wear the transparent membrane of "almost said it," through which you can see my internal organs of postponement churning with the slow digestion of words swallowed before they could hurt or heal.

My office occupies the seventeenth-and-a-half floor of the Bureau of Temporal Arrears, accessible only by an elevator that moves sideways through regret. The building itself is constructed from crystallized procrastination—each brick a tomorrow that never quite arrived, mortared together with the sticky residue of "I'll do it later" that hardens into architectural fact. My desk is carved from a single block of petrified urgency, its surface scarred by the phantom paperwork of tasks that expired before completion, their deadlines still writhing weakly in the wood grain like dying insects.

I was born (or perhaps assembled, or possibly just noticed) during the Great Defaulting, when the city's credit with causality ran out and effects began happening before their causes could file the proper paperwork. My mother was the gap between lightning and thunder; my father, the pause before the punchline that makes the joke stop being funny. They met at the intersection of "too late" and "too early," conceived me in the subjunctive mood, and gave birth to me in the conditional tense. My birth certificate is written in ink that won't exist until next Thursday, which keeps getting rescheduled.

Each morning (a concept I measure by the accumulation of dust on decisions unmade), I catalog the city's growing collection of unfinished business. The letters never sent pile up in drifts against my office walls, breeding small colonies of might-have-beens that chirp like senile crickets. The apologies that hardened in throats now fossilize in neat rows, some of them still warm with the fever of the moment that passed without permission. I file them by weight—the heaviest being the "I love you" that arrived three minutes after the funeral, still damp with tears that refuse to evaporate in the city's moisture-negative atmosphere.

My body, such as it is, consists of seventeen separate clauses that should add up to a complete sentence but somehow always come up one word short. My left hand is the gesture of reaching for something just out of range; my right, the motion of letting go a moment too soon. My spine is a question mark slowly straightening into an exclamation point, though it will never quite complete the transformation—caught forever in the typography of indecision. When I walk, I leave footprints in the past tense that immediately conjugate themselves into the future perfect, creating small temporal paradoxes that the city's grammar police ticket but never quite collect the fines for.

I feed exclusively on the aftertaste of conversations, harvesting the residue of what people meant to say from the air after they've gone. In the morning (whichever morning is currently occurring), I brew tea from the leaves of unwritten thank-you notes, sweetened with the crystallized sighs of missed opportunities. For lunch, I gnaw on the bones of projects abandoned at 99% completion, sucking the marrow of "almost there" from their hollow cores. Dinner is always the same: a single serving of the exact words that would have fixed everything, served cold on a plate made from compressed second thoughts.

My work involves preventing completion. Not maliciously—simply as a function of my existence. I am the twenty-fifth hour that prevents today from becoming tomorrow, the thirteenth month that keeps the year from closing its accounts. Documents that pass through my office acquire a peculiar quality of incompleteness, as if their final paragraphs wandered off to have drinks with their conclusions and never came back. I've been trying to resign for forty-seven years, but my resignation letter remains perpetually one signature short of valid—my own, which I can never quite finish writing before the ink runs out or the universe hiccups or someone, somewhere, needs something to remain unfinished.

The other inhabitants of Eremonos recognize me by my absence—I appear as the person-shaped hole in their peripheral vision, the appointment they forgot they made, the name on the tip of their tongue that tastes like iron filings and nostalgia. Children of the Second Wrong Turn sometimes follow me through the streets, playing a game where they try to guess what I'm not going to do next. They're getting quite good at it; last week, one correctly predicted I wouldn't finish explaining why I couldn't tell them my name.

My relationships exist primarily in the subjunctive mood. I have been almost-married seventeen times to the same person who exists in a parallel timeline that runs exactly three minutes ahead of mine. We meet daily at the café that serves only the memory of coffee, sitting at adjacent tables that face different directions in time, conducting our courtship through notes written on napkins that blow away before the other can read them. Our wedding has been scheduled and rescheduled so many times that the date itself has achieved independent sentience and now sends us bitter letters about its abandonment issues.

In my spare time (which is all my time, since I can never quite manage to be busy), I collect the city's unfinished symphonies. Not the musical kind—those at least have the dignity of stopping. I mean the symphonies of intention that begin with grand opening movements of "This time will be different" and peter out somewhere around the third measure of actual effort. I have thousands of them now, stored in crystalline bottles that hum with the frequency of good intentions. Sometimes I uncork one and let it play itself out into the city's air, where it mingles with other incomplete melodies until the cacophony becomes almost like music, if music could sound like giving up beautifully.

The city pays me in IOUs written on paper made from compressed procrastination. I've never tried to cash them—partly because the Bank of Eventual Payments only has hours that haven't been invented yet, but mostly because I've grown fond of their weight in my pockets. They rustle like autumn leaves that can't quite commit to falling, whispering promises of "someday" and "eventually" in languages that lose their meaning before the sentences end.

My greatest fear is completion. Not death—death at least has the courtesy of being an ending. I fear the moment when all the unfinished business finally resolves, when every letter gets sent and every apology gets spoken and every almost becomes an already. What would I be then? A theorem that proves itself true is just a fact, and facts have no place in Eremonos. They're too heavy; they sink through the inverted mountain's substance like stones through theoretical water, falling upward into the sky below until they become stars—fixed points of certainty in the fluid mathematics of maybe.

But that fear, like everything else about me, remains unfinished. I've been meaning to fully experience it for centuries now, but something always comes up. Another document to almost file, another appointment to nearly keep, another ending to postpone until the proper beginning can be located and convinced to cooperate. The fear sits in my chest like a bird that's forgotten it has wings, occasionally rustling its feathers but never quite taking flight.

At night (the night that happens sideways), I dream of conclusions. Not reaching them—just dream of them, the way other people dream of loved ones they haven't met yet. In these dreams, I am always one step away from finishing something important, but the step stretches like taffy made of time, extending infinitely between intention and action. I wake (or stop dreaming, or simply shift from one state of incompletion to another) with the taste of "almost" in my mouth like morning breath that no amount of theoretical toothpaste can remove.

This is how I serve Eremonos: by ensuring it never quite becomes whatever it's becoming. I am the city's comma, forever preventing the sentence of its existence from reaching a full stop. Every morning (including the ones that happen in the afternoon), I report to my office through doors that open onto yesterday's hallways, sit at my desk of petrified urgency, and begin the day's work of maintaining the city's perfect incompletion.

Today's task, which I've been about to start for the last nine years, involves cataloging all the raindrops that almost fell during the drought of good intentions. Each one hangs suspended in its own private gravitational negotiation, waiting for permission to finish falling. I've been composing the introduction to this catalog since before I existed, and it currently runs to forty-seven volumes of preparatory notes about possibly beginning to consider starting.

The work is important. Essential, even. Without it, Eremonos might accidentally complete itself, might resolve into something as boring as reality. The city depends on its incompletions the way other places depend on foundations—they're what keep it from either collapsing or ascending, locked in the eternal maybe of its own possibility.

And I? I am the guardian of that maybe, the custodian of its perhaps. I am the Theorem of Unfinished Business, and I will remain so until—

But that's the beauty of it.

I won't.
