In the Carved Hollows, I would be a Bone Chanter's apprentice, though not by choice.

My workshop occupies a natural acoustic bubble in the middle depths—one of those rare spaces where the Drum's roar becomes a whisper, where sound behaves strangely. My master discovered it decades ago: a chamber where every carved surface creates unexpected resonances, where a whispered word might circle the room three times before dying, growing louder with each pass.

The walls are lined with half-finished instruments, each made from the remains of the dead. Femur flutes that play in registers only the stone-deaf elderly can properly hear. Skull drums with eye sockets that weep perpetual condensation, their heads stretched with preserved stomach lining. Rib chimes that remember their owner's laughter—sometimes you can hear it, tinkling between the notes.

I came here as payment for my mother's debt. She needed dry-root medicine for my younger brother's lung-rot, and the Bone Chanter offered a trade: seven years of my service for enough medicine to save him. I was thirteen then, small enough to fit in the tightest carving spaces, young enough that my fingers could learn new shapes.

Now I am seventeen, and my hands know the dead better than the living.

Each morning begins the same. I wake in my alcove—barely large enough to lie flat, carved behind the main workshop where the sound of my breathing won't disturb the instruments. They're temperamental, especially the newer ones. The femur of Hali-Who-Mapped-The-Eastern-Floods still screams sometimes, though she's been dead two seasons. My master says her bone remembers drowning, though Hali died in her sleep, dry as anyone can die here.

I check the moisture collectors first. The instruments must be kept at precise humidity—too dry and they crack, too wet and they lose their voices. Each one requires different treatment. The Genealogy of House Drakmoor (a full skeleton reassembled into a standing harp) prefers the steady drip-drip-drip of mineral water on its crown. The baby skulls—we have seventeen, all from the plague winter—need daily misting with sacred pool water.

My master rarely speaks. His throat was damaged in youth when he tried to out-sing the Drum itself, a young man's hubris that left him croaking like the pale frogs in the deepest pools. He communicates in sign when necessary, but mostly he demonstrates. His hands on mine, guiding the carving blade. His fingers showing how to test a bone's resonance with gentle taps. His whole body teaching me to listen—not just with ears, but with skin, with teeth, with the spaces between breaths.

The work is delicate. Each bone must be cleaned, studied, understood. You can't simply hollow out a tibia and call it a flute. You must learn where that particular bone is weak, where it's dense, how it wants to break. You must respect the grain of what was once living. Some bones arrive eager to sing—usually from those who died content, surrounded by family, their names already being carved into the Remembrance Walls. Others resist. The murdered ones shriek. The forgotten ones stay sullenly silent. The children's bones sound like they're still playing games, their notes jumping and unpredictable.

My specialty, discovered by accident, is repair. Instruments thought permanently ruined respond to my touch. The Councillor's Spine—a curved rod of vertebrae that once advised three generations of water-lords—had been mute for decades until I restrung it with my own hair, each strand soaked for days in different pools. Now it hums with legal precedents and old water-rights, its voice a whisper of authority that makes guild leaders pay handsomely for consultation.

But I hate it. I hate the cold feeling of human calcium under my fingers. I hate how the bones warm to my touch like they're trying to come alive again. I hate the songs they sing—always minor key, always shadowed with ending. Most of all, I hate how good I am at it. How my hands know instinctively where to carve to make a grandmother's rib cage remember her lullabies. How I can hear what note a stranger's skull wants to sing just by holding it up to the phosphorescent light.

In the evenings, when my master retreats to his own chambers to work on his mysterious Commission (a full orchestra made from a single family, all of whom died in the same tunnel collapse—he's been working on it for three years), I practice my own music. Not on the bone instruments—I won't touch them outside of working hours. Instead, I've carved my own flutes from the soft stone that lines our workshop. They play flat and dull compared to bone, but they're mine. They've never been alive. They carry no memories but those I give them.

I compose water songs—attempts to capture the Drum's rhythm in notes that human lungs can manage. I'm transcribing the sound of different rains: the steady ceiling-weep of the market district, the violent spray near the spillways, the gentle mist that rises from the sacred pools at dawn. My master thinks it's foolish. Bone remembers, he signs. Stone only echoes.

But I think he's wrong. In the deepest part of night, when even the city's constant motion slows, I sit in my acoustic bubble and play. Sometimes, just sometimes, the walls sing back. Not echoes—new notes, harmonies I didn't write. As if the stone itself is learning my songs, adding its own voice. 

Two more years, and my apprenticeship ends. Two more years of coaxing music from the dead. My brother is healthy now, grown taller than me, training with the Channel Keepers. My mother brings mushroom tea every seventh day, sits in the workshop's antechamber (she won't come deeper, where the instruments can see her), asks if I'm eating enough. I lie and say yes, though the truth is that working with the dead kills appetite. Food tastes like limestone. Water tastes like memory.

Sometimes I dream I'm inside the instruments looking out. That my bones are being carved by careful hands, my song being discovered note by note. I wake gasping, checking that my flesh is still whole. But my fingers are already changing—growing more sensitive, able to feel hairline cracks in bone that my master's eyes can't see. My hearing is sharpening, distinguishing between the sob of a spinster's ulna and a mother's. 

The other apprentices (for there are three of us, though we rarely speak) whisper that the trade marks you. That Bone Chanters can't leave the profession even when their terms are up. That the dead recognize us, call to us, offer their bones freely. That we begin to hear the songs in living skeleton too—the pitch of a person's ribs, the timbre of their spine. That we know who will make beautiful instruments and who will only wail.

I tell myself they're wrong. That when my time is done, I'll join the Mappers, spend my days charting new territories far from any dead. That I'll marry someone loud and living, have children who've never touched an instrument made of ancestor. That these songs I hear in the stone are just echoes, just imagination, just the way any sound behaves in caves.

But yesterday, a funeral procession passed our workshop. And without thinking, without wanting to, I found myself evaluating the corpse. Young woman, drowned in a mapping accident, her bones still supple with youth. Her ribs would make a xylophone of exceptional range. Her skull, properly prepared, would sing soprano notes pure enough to pierce stone.

I turned away, but the knowledge remained.

Two more years. Unless the bones claim me first. Unless I discover, like my master before me, that some apprenticeships never truly end. That the dead have their own debts, their own bargains, their own songs that must be sung.

In the Carved Hollows, everything flows toward its proper level. Water to the depths, the living to their work, the dead to their music. And I—caught between, carving songs from silence, coaxing memories from calcium—I flow too, though I don't yet know where the current will carry me.

The bone flute in my hands (someone's grandmother, dead forty years) begins to play itself, a lullaby I've never heard before. I let it sing, my fingers following its lead, and try not to think about whose bones might someday sing my story, in this city of water and echo, where even death is just another form of music.

I close my eyes and listen to the Drum, that eternal roar, and wonder if it too is an instrument—the whole city a careful construction of chambers and channels designed to play a single, endless note. And if so, who carved it? What vast musician uses us all—living and dead alike—as components of an orchestra too large to comprehend?

The grandmother's flute falls silent. The workshop holds its breath. Somewhere in the walls, stone learns another song.

Two more years. If the music lets me go. If I want it to.

If I can bear the silence that comes after.
