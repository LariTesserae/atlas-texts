In the seep where water weeps perpetually down granite, where the stone face never quite dries even in the bleaching heat of August, I am a colony of nostoc.

I say "I," but this is already wrong. We are spheres within spheres, cells strung on mucilage, pearls of blue-green wrapped in a gelatin that shivers when the water runs fast and firms when it runs slow. From far away we look like dark jelly spread on stone. From close, we look like caviar made of storms — each bead its own depth of blue-black-green, shifting with the light.

We came here as dust. One cell, maybe two, blown up from some dried pond edge, some cracked earth where our previous incarnation had gambled on rain and lost. That's how it works with us: feast and famine, flood and desiccation, each colony a roll of the dice that sometimes comes up snake eyes. But here — here the seep never fails. Here we have won a strange lottery.

The granite wears a beard of us now. We hang in lobes and curtains where the water tracks, building our communes in three dimensions. Layer on layer, the old cells dying inward while the young cells reach outward, each generation standing on a cemetery of their ancestors. This is how we grow thick. This is how we come to drape.

In the morning when the sun strikes the seep, we begin our work. Each cell is a tiny factory of sugar and nitrogen. We breathe in what others cannot use — the pure nitrogen of air, the raw minerals of rain — and we breathe out what others need. We are chemists. We are philanthropists. We feed the moss that grows beside us, leaked sugars and fixed nitrogen seeping from our edges. The moss does not thank us. The moss does not know we exist except as substrate, as the thing to grip when reaching for light.

When clouds cover the sun, we shift our internal machinery. Different genes wake. We make different proteins, preparing for the lean hours. Our color deepens. What was blue-green becomes blue-black. We are solar panels that can retune themselves, though we were doing this a billion years before any engineer dreamed of silicon and cells.

Rain swells us. We become grape-fat, trembling. Too much rain and pieces of us tear loose — small rafts of colony washing down the rock face to lodge in new cracks, start new colonies. This is how we travel. This is how we colonize a cliff face: by coming apart at the seams and trusting physics to deliver us somewhere livable.

Drought shrinks us. We pull back into ourselves, concentrating our mucilage until we are more plastic than jelly. We can survive like this for years. Decades, even. The outer layers sacrificing themselves to shield the inner ones, dying into a kind of patent leather that cracks but does not crumble. When the water returns, we drink through our wounds.

The hummingbird knows us as a place to pluck spider silk for her nest. She hovers at our surface, delicate bill selecting the finest strands where sheet-web weavers have used us as scaffolding. We feel her wingbeats as earthquakes. Each stroke displaces millions of us, sets us resonating. By the time we still, she is gone.

The mayflies know us as nursery. They lay their eggs in the film of water that sheets across our surface. Their larvae live their first days among our beads, feeding on the diatoms that feed on us. We are the world entire for something smaller than a comma. We are the sky and the earth and the sea.

At night we switch to different work. The machinery that fixes nitrogen runs best in darkness — oxygen is poison to those particular enzymes. So we partition ourselves in time: photosynthesis by day, nitrogen fixation by night. We are shift workers in our own bodies.

The screech owl does not know we exist. The gray fox does not know we exist. Even the salamander that hunts in the splash zone below us, gorging on our fallen fragments, does not know we exist as anything but background, but wetness, but the dark shine on stone.

But the stone knows. We are eating it, slowly. Our acids and our grip. Our expansion when wet, our contraction when dry. We are carving microscopic valleys where we flow. In a thousand years, there will be grooves here. In ten thousand, channels. We are patient. The stone is patient. We are having the slowest conversation in the world.

Sometimes I dream (can a colony dream?) that we are not many but one. That all nostoc everywhere is the same nostoc, playing out endless variations on a single theme: survive, divide, endure. That the colony blown to dust in Mongolia and the colony drowning in a Canadian bog and we here on this granite seep are fingers of the same vast hand, feeling the shape of the world.

Sometimes in the deepest dark when even the bats are quiet, I feel my constituents considering rebellion. Each cell carries the memory of how to live alone. We could dissolve. We could become a billion single swimmers. But morning always comes before we decide, and with morning comes the work, and the work needs all of us, and so we stay ourselves, which is to say we stay together.

The seep whispers its endless whisper. Iron and calcium and the memory of rain. We taste it all. We archive it in our proteins. We are libraries of water chemistry, each colony a record of our particular place.

One day the seep will fail. The geology will shift, or the climate will change, or both. We will dry to leather, then to dust. Some few cells will blow away to try their luck elsewhere. Most will enter that long sleep that has no waking.

But not today. Today the seep runs strong. Today we are fat with water and light. Today we fix nitrogen and leak sugars and build our slow blue-green empire one cell at a time. Today we are nostoc, which is to say we are ancient, which is to say we are still here.

The sun climbs. We begin to shine.
