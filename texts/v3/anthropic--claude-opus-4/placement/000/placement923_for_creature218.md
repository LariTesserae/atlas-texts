I find myself as one of the **Thread-Walkers**.

We are not Kept. We arrived later, much later, washing up against Ossara like driftwood against a patient shore. The plateau tolerates us — barely, conditionally — because we serve a function the Kept cannot: we maintain the silver threads.

I am taller than the Kept but share their thinness, though mine comes not from evolutionary courtesy but from necessity. The threads reject mass. To work among them, you must pare yourself down to almost nothing. My bones are not hollow but dense as meteor iron — a counterweight to keep me grounded when the threads pull. My skin bears scars like sheet music, thin parallel lines where I've brushed against threads I couldn't see in time. They burn cold, these scars. They always will.

We number perhaps thirty. We came from somewhere else — different somewheres, all of us, traders and wanderers and refugees who discovered we could see what the Kept could only sense: the true architecture of the threads. To us, they blaze like a web of soft mercury fire, connecting pillar to pillar in patterns that hurt to follow with the eye but which we must follow, must map, must repair when they fray.

I wear clothes of a particular fabric we weave from the discarded shells of reed-hares — not the resonant parts the Kept prize, but the clear connective tissue between chambers. When properly prepared, it holds a charge that makes the threads visible even when no dust crosses them. Dressed in this ghostly material, I walk the spaces between buildings at strange hours, following damage only I can see.

The threads are not static. They move, slowly, like the growth of crystals filmed over centuries and played back in minutes. Sometimes a thread will migrate from one pillar to another, and when it does, it leaves a kind of wound in the air — a wrongness that must be coaxed closed. This is what we do. We sing to the wounds. Not through our sternums like the Kept, but through instruments we've learned to make from the clear-shell material, shaped like hollow prisms. The song is mathematical, precise. One wrong note and the wound tears wider.

The Kept do not like us, exactly. We move too fast, speak with our mouths instead of the ground, and worst of all, we touch the pillars not with reverent palms but with tools — thin rods of the same clear-shell that let us adjust what the Kept can only soothe. But they need us. The threads are fraying faster these days. The pillars run hotter. Sometimes, at night, I can hear them trying to sing something that the plateau isn't quite ready to hear.

I live in a grown dwelling at the very edge of Ossara, where the end-grain begins to spiral in a way that makes the Kept uneasy. My house is small, was never enlarged by the substrate's slow ambitions. It knows I'm temporary. The ground beneath my feet feels always slightly surprised to find me still here.

But I've been here forty years now. I've learned to hum a little, badly, enough to send simple messages through the ground. I've attended three births, stood at the edge of the plaza as the welcoming chord rolled in. I've walked out among the reed-hares and felt them accept me as a necessary wrongness, like a splinter the body chooses to encyst rather than expel.

And I've seen things the Kept haven't. Deep in the thread-web, where the patterns grow too complex to follow safely, I've glimpsed the shape of what the whole system is for. Not maintenance. Not infrastructure. Something else. Something that has to do with Iole, the occlusion above. The threads aren't holding something up. They're holding something back.

Last week, working on a particularly complex wound near the seventh pillar, I heard it clearly for the first time: Iole is humming. Not through any medium the Kept could detect, but through the threads themselves, a vibration so low it takes hours to complete a single wave. It's trying to tell us something. Or ask us something. Or warn us.

I haven't told the Kept. How could I? By the time my hummed message reached them through the ground, I might have figured out what Iole is saying. Or the threads might have finally frayed beyond our ability to repair them. Or Iole might have given up trying to communicate and tried something else.

So I walk. I repair. I map the wounds and sing them closed. I dress in my ghost-clothes and move through Ossara like something already half-departed, which I suppose I am. We all are, we Thread-Walkers. Necessary but unwelcome, maintaining a system we don't understand for reasons we can't explain, growing thinner year by year as the work hollows us out.

Sometimes I stand at the edge of a shut place and wonder: were there Thread-Walkers before? Is that what the spirals are? Places where someone like me finally grew too thin, fell through their own existence, and the ground, in its slow wisdom, closed the wound?

But mostly I work. The threads need tending. The pillars grow hot. Overhead, Iole hums its impossible message, and I pretend I'm not beginning to understand the words.
