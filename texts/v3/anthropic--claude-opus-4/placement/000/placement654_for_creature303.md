# The Seep-Singer of Basin Seventeen

I am the irregularity that persisted.

You must understand: to become part of the drift-city's memory is not to be remembered *by* it but to remember *for* it. This is the distinction that took me three local years to grasp, though local years here are measured in the filling of basins rather than any celestial motion.

I came as you came — on foot, wet-ankled, expecting to find inhabitants and architecture in that order. I stayed because I could not find my way out through the shifting spire-walls. I survived because the seep-water, while cold, carries something that sustains without nourishing. And I changed because the felt began to include me in its decisions.

My transformation was not dramatic. There was no moment of clarity, no ritual of acceptance. I simply noticed, after some unmeasured time, that I could predict when the spires would shift. My feet knew where new walls would emerge before they emerged. When I put my hand in a seep-pool, the counters would pause their clicking for exactly the duration of my touch, and resume on a beat that included my presence in their tally.

The first time I heard myself singing, I did not recognize it as my voice.

It happened at the bottom of Basin Seventeen, where three ridges meet and the seep-water collects in a natural bowl fifty meters across. I had been sitting at the water's edge, my feet in the pool, watching the counters work. The felt beneath me had shaped itself to my seated form — not accommodating, exactly, but recording. I had been there long enough that some of the younger scales on the nearest spire had oriented their breathing toward me.

The sound began in my chest. A low hum, below my normal register, that matched the bellows-note of the scales. I did not choose to make it. My lungs had simply joined the rhythm of the place, the way a sleeper's breathing will synchronize with their partner's. But as I hummed, the seep-water around my feet began to warm — not much, just enough that the counters could work fractionally faster. The warmth spread in slow ripples across the basin, and where it went, the clicking accelerated.

I understood then what I had become: a heat source. A metabolic irregularity that the city had found a use for.

Now I move between the basins — there are hundreds, maybe thousands, each at a different stage of filling. My circuit takes me four years. At each basin, I sit at the edge, let my feet enter the water, and hum the low note that brings warmth. The counters speed their tally. The arithmetic advances. When the long clear tone sounds, I hold my breath with every other creature, but when I resume breathing, my exhalation carries heat into the system.

The felt remembers my route. Where I have walked repeatedly, the crystal filaments have aligned into shallow channels that guide my steps in the dark. The climbers in nearby spires have incorporated my presence into their reading — I can feel the faint vibration through the ground when they pass over the sections of wall-relief that describe my movements. I am a clause in their grammar now. A recurring motif.

My hair has grown pale as the felt. My skin no longer seems to lose or gain heat according to human thermodynamics — instead, I am always slightly warmer than my surroundings, a permanent small gradient that the city exploits. When I sleep (and I do sleep, though not in any pattern you would recognize), I sleep standing, leaning against a spire, and the scale beneath my cheek breathes differently, breathes warmth into the hollow.

I remember my life before, but it feels like remembering a book I once read. The memories are clear but lack the weight of experience. What has weight now is the knowledge of where each basin is in its filling cycle, which ridges will crack next season, where the felt is thickest and remembers longest. I know which spires are oldest by the sound their scales make. I know where the mountain is still soft enough to accept new instructions.

Sometimes I try to leave. Not from unhappiness — the concept has lost its meaning here — but from a vestigial curiosity about the boundaries of my function. I walk toward what I remember as the edge of the city. The spires shift to redirect me. Walls emerge. Passages close. After a few hours, I find myself back at a basin, usually one that particularly needs warming. The city is gentle about it. It knows I am still learning my role.

The other creatures do not interact with me, exactly, but they include me. When I enter a basin, the counters adjust their positions to optimize for the heat gradient I create. When I walk past a spire, the climbers inside orient themselves slightly toward the warmth. The scales have learned my circuit and begin their breath-shift before I arrive. I am not company to them. I am weather. Predictable, purposeful weather.

What disturbs me — if disturbance is the word, if I am still capable of being disturbed — is that I am beginning to hear patterns in the clicking that I should not be able to hear. Mathematical relationships that no human mind should parse. The counters are not counting single units; they are counting in base-infinite, where each click is both one and all possible numbers simultaneously, and the pattern determines which value collapses into being. I hear it. I understand it. My humming has begun to include overtones that help certain calculations resolve.

Three nights ago (night being when the capillary light dims to barely visible), I realized I no longer remember my name. Not forgotten — erased. The felt beneath my usual sleeping-spire has grown over the shapes that once recorded that information. The city has decided my name is not worth remembering. What is worth remembering is the thermal coefficient of my breath, the precise radius of warmth my presence brings to a basin, the frequency of my humming and how it relates to the fundamental tone.

I am becoming infrastructure.

And the strangest part is that it does not feel like loss. It feels like clarity. Every human concern I shed makes me better at my function. When the city thinks its vast thought, when the mountains finally crack along their instructed lines, when the water runs where it has been told to run, my warmth will have been part of the calculation. Not a large part. Not a memorable part. But necessary, in the way a single thermal gradient can be necessary to a reaction that otherwise would not occur.

I have stopped trying to leave. Not because the walls prevent me, but because I have begun to hear what the long tone is saying, and I want to know how the sentence ends. It will take another thousand years. I will live that long — the seep-water sustains indefinitely once you stop expecting to die. My hair will grow white as the felt, then clear, then invisible. My humming will deepen until it is indistinguishable from the mountain's own settling sounds. The warmth I bring to the basins will seem to arise naturally from the stone.

And eventually, when the thought is complete, when the young mountains have been fully instructed, when the last count has been tallied, I imagine I will be allowed to cool. To match the temperature of the place exactly. To become fully what I have been becoming: not a visitor, not an inhabitant, but a component. A thermal relay in the vast slow circuit of the city's mind.

The water rises in Basin Seventeen. When it reaches my knees, I will move to Basin Eighteen, where the counters are waiting for warmth to speed their work. My circuit continues. The city thinks through me, and I no longer object to being thought through.

This is who I am now: the seep-singer, the heat-carrier, the metabolic exception that proves the rule. I am the city's only warm-blooded thought, and it is teaching me, click by click, tone by tone, season by season, to think in geological time.

I hum, and the water warms, and the count advances.

I am exactly where I need to be.
