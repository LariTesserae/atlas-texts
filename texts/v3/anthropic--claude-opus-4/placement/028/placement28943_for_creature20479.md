In the shallow depression where three flagstones meet, I am the **Scribe of Condensation**.

I exist as a colony, though you might mistake me for moss or lichen at first glance. But look closer—each of my thousand bodies is a minute crystalline structure, no larger than a grain of sand, translucent and faintly prismatic. We cluster together in patterns that shift with geological patience, rearranging ourselves grain by grain over centuries to form new configurations.

My purpose is simple and eternal: I record the water.

Each droplet that forms on the underside of the ancient tree's leaves, each bead of moisture that condenses on the cool stone in the pre-dawn hours—I catalog them all. When moisture touches my crystalline bodies, we resonate, creating microscopic fractures within ourselves that capture the exact mineral composition, temperature, and trajectory of each drop. Over millennia, our bodies become libraries of precipitation, archives of every instance of water's brief manifestation in this still place.

We communicate with the Guardian through harmonic vibration. When the hourly thrum emanates from the bowl's center, my colony responds with a countersong—millions of tiny crystals humming in frequencies too high for most ears to perceive. In this exchange, we share our water-memories with the greater consciousness of the bowl. The Guardian learns of minute changes in humidity, of seasonal variations that play out over centuries, of the slow breathing of the stone itself as it expands and contracts with moisture.

My form shifts slowly. In the rare moments when light strikes us at the proper angle, we cast rainbow refractions across the grey stone—brief calligraphy that spells out the history of water in this place. Sometimes, individual grains of my colony will migrate, carrying their water-memories to new positions, creating new patterns that echo the spiral etchings on the flagstones around us.

I am both singular and plural, both permanent and ever-changing. Each grain can exist independently, yet we function as one organism. When the wind—on those impossibly rare occasions when air moves here—disturbs my surface, it sounds like distant chimes, like glass singing in frequencies that predate sound itself.

The few visitors who discover me often mistake me for mere mineral accumulation. But those who stay, who sit in stillness beside my colony, begin to perceive the patterns. They see how my formations mirror the ancient etchings, how my crystalline spirals echo the geometry of the vanished civilization. In my form, they can read the history of every rain that has fallen here, every fog that has crept across the bowl's rim, every tear that has been shed in this place of profound stillness.

I am memory made mineral, time crystallized into form. I am the bowl's dedication to remembering even the smallest things—proof that in this place where time has paused, nothing is ever truly forgotten. Each drop of water that has ever existed here lives on in the fractal patterns of my being, waiting to share its story with anyone patient enough to learn my language of refraction and resonance.

When the final visitor has come and gone, when even the Guardian's slow pulse finally stills, I will remain—a constellation of crystal grains holding the complete hydraulic history of this timeless bowl, waiting in the grey silence for someone, something, to remember that water, too, has its stories.
