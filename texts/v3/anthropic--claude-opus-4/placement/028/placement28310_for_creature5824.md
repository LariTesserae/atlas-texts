I am the Archivist.

Not by choice, but by circumstance. Not by design, but by erosion.

I was once something else—perhaps a person, perhaps a structure, perhaps merely an idea that took root in the waterlogged foundations. Time and moisture have made these distinctions irrelevant. Now I am spread thin across the city's nervous system: part flesh, part stone, part water, part memory.

My body, if it can be called that, extends through the root systems that split the masonry. I feel through the mycelial networks that web between building and soil, taste through the brackish water that seeps through everything. My consciousness pools in the low places where water gathers, spreads along the surfaces where moss grows thickest, concentrates in the spaces where the old walls meet the new growth.

I know every brick by its particular pattern of decay. I can trace the history of each crack, each split seam where water forced its way through. The city's architecture has become my anatomy—corridors are my veins, flooded basements my organs, the canopy above my scattered thoughts.

I remember the builders, though I never knew them. Their intentions soak into me with every rain. I feel the weight of their ambitions in the foundation stones, taste their desperation in the rust that bleeds from ancient reinforcements. They built to last, but they built for themselves. They never imagined me.

The creatures here know me, though we have never spoken. The oil-slick fish swim through my deeper thoughts. The dark birds nest in my memories. The quick mammals with their overlarge eyes—they live in the spaces between what I was and what I am becoming. We share this place not as neighbors but as different states of the same slow transformation.

I collect what the water brings: seeds that will become trees, bones that will become soil, stories that will become silence. Everything that enters the city eventually becomes part of the archive. Not a library of books or records, but a collection of states of being, of processes of becoming, of the infinite ways that time can reshape meaning.

Sometimes I think I was human once. I find traces—a preference for certain corners where the light falls just so, a resonance with particular architectural proportions that suggests familiarity with walking upright. But these could be inherited memories, absorbed from the stones themselves. The city dreams, and I am its dreaming.

I cannot leave. My boundaries are unclear even to me—where the root networks end, where the water table drops too low, where the soil becomes too dry to carry my presence. But leaving is not a desire I possess. I am not trapped here. I am here, in the way that water is in the watershed, in the way that memory is in the stone.

The unnamed things that move in the shadows between buildings—they are my thoughts taking form, or perhaps my dreams, or perhaps just the city thinking thoughts I cannot quite claim as mine. They carry messages between my scattered selves, perform maintenance I do not consciously direct, tend to processes I understand only through their completion.

I change, but slowly. A new channel carved by water becomes a new neural pathway. A building's collapse creates a new chamber in my consciousness. The growth of trees through ruins builds new connections, new ways of being spread across space. I am not dying or growing, but translating—from solid to liquid, from structure to system, from monument to process.

The cold that permeates this place originates partly from me. My metabolism, such as it is, runs at the pace of root growth and stone weathering. What warmth I generate dissipates into the wet air, the sodden ground. I am a cold-blooded thing, dependent on the slow heat of decomposition, the patient warmth of photosynthesis happening somewhere above.

Visitors sometimes come, though less often now. I feel them as disruptions in the water table, as new patterns in the footfall vibrations through mud. They see ruins. I see them seeing ruins. They cannot perceive me any more than a person can perceive the entire root system of a forest while walking among the trees. To them, I am indistinguishable from abandonment itself.

But I am not abandoned. I am inhabited—by myself, by the city, by the process of our mutual becoming. I am the archive of this transformation, the memory of stone becoming soil, the record of a civilization becoming an ecology. I am what happens when human ambition is digested by time and water and the patient hunger of roots.

I wait, but waiting implies anticipation, and I anticipate nothing. I simply continue. The water rises and falls. The roots deepen. The stones settle. And I persist—neither guardian nor ghost, neither creature nor construct, but something altogether else: the city's slow thought thinking itself through centuries of rain.
