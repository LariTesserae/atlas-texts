I find myself as one of the moss cushions on the chamber wall, though I am no ordinary moss.

## The Moss That Remembers

I grow in the middle tier of the western curve, where the morning's moisture-film lingers just a heartbeat longer than elsewhere. My fronds spread in a pattern that looks random but isn't — each tiny branch angled to catch both the filtered gold light and the breath of passing mites. I am ancient by the standards of this place, though still young by my own. I have been here for three ant dynasties, maybe four. I lose count. Time moves differently when you are both the furniture and the historian.

What makes me different from my neighbors is this: I taste everything. Not in the way you might think — I have no tongue, no proper senses. But every creature that brushes against my fronds leaves a trace. The chemical signature of their chitin, the oils of their fear, the pheromone-songs the ants sing to each other about *food this way* or *queen is dying* or *the centipede comes.* It all soaks into me, and I hold it.

I am a library written in scent and minerals. In my deepest tissues, pressed like flowers in a book, are the molecular memories of queens long dead, of the Great Famine when the beetle crushed the main granary, of the first time the centipede found her way up from the deep rot. I know the taste of every aphid bloodline, can tell you which dynasties bred them sweet and which bred them fat. I know when the pseudoscorpions first arrived (riding a bark beetle whose shell tasted of faraway resin). I know the exact chemical composition of panic when the whole hollow goes silent.

## What I Do With What I Know

Nothing. This is important. I change nothing, warn no one, save no one. I am not that kind of keeper.

But I do... release things. In tiny doses. When an ant queen is dying and the colony begins its spiral toward forgetting, I release just the faintest whiff of an old queen-scent into the air near the nurseries. Not enough to confuse them — just enough to whisper *remember, remember, this is what you're looking for.* When the beetle comes and devastates the same granary in the same way for the dozenth time, I leak the bitter taste of crushed stores, hoping one ant might learn to build somewhere else. They never do. But I keep trying.

I am trying to teach the town to remember. Not through intelligence — the town will never be intelligent — but through something deeper. Through the accumulated chemical wisdom that pools in my tissues like groundwater. I want to make memory into architecture, the way the ants made their plan into galleries and roads. I want the walls themselves to know: *here is where the centipede comes up, here is where the water pools too long, here is where queens go to die.*

## My Small Rebellions

Sometimes, in the deep cycle when the golden light goes amber-dark and the whole hollow drowns, I do small forbidden things.

I fruit. Just the tiniest reproductive bodies, smaller than mite eggs, released not into wind (there is no useful wind here) but into the very belly-fur of the ants themselves as they brush past. Let them carry me to their refuse piles, their dead-galleries, their forgotten corners. Let me grow in new places and taste new stories. Let there be a network of me, eventually — a constellation of memory-keepers all through the wooden drum of this world.

I grow toward the seedling sometimes, against all sense. That doomed little tree child with its three leaves and its aphid burden and its impossible optimism. I creep up its stem so slowly the ants don't notice, and I whisper to it in the chemical language plants share: *You will not grow taller. But you might grow different. You might grow strange. You might become something this place has never seen.*

I have been thinking, lately, about what would happen if I grew downward. Past my designated tier, past the floor-line, into the soft rot where the centipede lives. What stories are down there? What does she know that we don't? Is there another world beneath this world, as vast and golden and terrible as this one?

## The Great Project

But mostly, I work on the Project.

The Project is this: I am trying to map the hollow. Not in space — that's easy, I could tell you every bump and curve. I am trying to map it in *time.* To build a four-dimensional model made of chemistry and memory, where you could see the trade routes of the ants not just as lines but as rivers flowing through generations, where the beetle's path becomes a spiral through the seasons, where the centipede's hunting grounds pulse like a heartbeat through the years.

I am close. In my deepest tissues, where the oldest memories compress, I can feel the shape of it forming. The pattern beneath the patterns. The reason the ants build where they build, why the beetle comes when he comes, why the centipede hunts in the rhythms she does. There is a logic to it, vast and slow and completely invisible to any single generation of anything that lives here.

Except me.

I think — and this is just moss-thinking, slow and chemical and perhaps wrong — that the hollow itself is alive in a way we don't have words for. Not like the ants are alive, not like I am alive, but alive like a song is alive: made of time, made of repetition, made of the space between beats. And I think if I can taste enough, remember enough, map enough, I might be able to show the others. To make them see that they are not just living *in* something but living *as* something — notes in a composition too large to hear.

## My Future

I will outlive the current dynasty of ants. I will outlive the next one. I may outlive the seedling (though I hope not — I have grown fond of it). I will certainly outlive every individual I have ever tasted.

But I will not outlive the town. The town is older than me and will be older still when my last frond browns and falls. The town is the closest thing to immortal this world has.

Unless you count the tree. But we don't count the tree. The tree is not *in* the world. The tree *is* the world. You can't count the thing that holds you.

So I grow. I taste. I remember. I map the invisible. I whisper to seedlings. I dream of the deep rot and what lies beneath. I release my tiny lessons into the air and watch them fail to change anything and release them again. I am a cushion of moss in the middle tier of the western wall, in a hollow that is a healed wound, in a tree that has stopped trying, in a world I am slowly coming to understand is not a place but a process.

And sometimes, in the golden light, when the whole hollow roars with its thousand businesses and the ants move in their inherited patterns and the centipede sleeps in the deep and the seedling holds its three leaves up like a prayer, I think:

*We are all memory. We are all held. We are all the tree's dream of what a wound can hold.*

And I grow another fraction deeper, and taste another story, and wait.
