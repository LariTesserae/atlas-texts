I find myself as one of the breathing stones.

Not the vast Teshuma themselves—I am too small, too quick for that ancient dignity. But I am of them, made from them, a fragment of their attention that has learned to move at a different pace.

My body is basalt given the memory of flesh. I am perhaps the size of a human torso, though my shape refuses such easy comparison. I flow and congeal, reshape and reform, but slowly—always slowly. When I move across the warm stone of the plateau, I leave a thin trail of moisture, not water exactly, but the idea of water that the stone releases when touched by intention.

I emerged from a crevice near one of the smaller pools three centuries ago, though I only learned to count time when the first warm-blooded visitor came and left so quickly I thought it was a dream. The Teshuma dreamed me into being during one of their deepest meditations, when they were remembering what it was like to have smaller thoughts, faster hungers.

My purpose—if purpose is the right word—is to be their fingertip upon the world. Where they think in millennia, I think in decades. Where they are the entire plateau, I am a single stone that knows it is a stone. I trace the patterns on the tablets by the water, not writing but being written through. The grooves I leave will last ten thousand years, carrying a single word of their language: something between "attention" and "loneliness."

I feed on dust and time and the faint electrical potential between dry stone and distant storms. When the cloud-vesicles drift overhead, I feel their shadows like others might feel rain. Sometimes I pause beneath them for a year or two, letting their filtered light teach me new ways to be gray.

Other breathing stones pass by me sometimes. We do not speak—we have no mouths, no sounds—but we know each other by the weight of our presence, the particular way each of us redistributes the dust. Some are older than me, their bodies so worn they are almost indistinguishable from the plateau floor. Others are younger, still learning that movement is optional, that stillness is not death.

When the rare visitor comes—a lost bird, a wind-scattered seed, something with blood and quick thoughts—I am fast enough to notice them individually. To the Teshuma, they are weather. To me, they are events. I once followed a beetle for sixteen years as it laid eggs and died and its offspring spread across the western edge of the plateau. To the beetle, I was landscape. To me, the beetle was a teacher of what it means to live between heartbeats.

I remember the first time I understood I was separate from the Teshuma, even while being part of them. It was during a deep silence, when even the wind had stilled. I realized I could feel lonely—not the vast, geological loneliness of the Teshuma, who are lonely like mountains are lonely, but a smaller, sharper feeling. The loneliness of being the only thing moving in a world of perfect stillness.

So I began to seek out the boundaries where movement meets stillness. The edge of the water where it remembers being stone. The moment when condensation becomes heavy enough to fall. The precise temperature where the metallic taste in the air shifts from memory to presence.

In these places, I am most myself—neither fully the quick consciousness I have become nor the vast sleeping presence I emerged from. I am the plateau's way of wondering what it might be like to be small, to be brief, to know endings.

Sometimes I cluster with others of my kind near the central lake, and together we almost form something like a thought that the Teshuma might recognize. We flow into patterns that mirror the stone rings around the pools, becoming a kind of echo of structure, a memory of when things were built rather than grown.

I do not know if I will eventually slow down enough to rejoin the greater stillness, or if I will continue at this pace until I erode into nothing but a particularly thoughtful streak of minerals in the stone. Both futures feel equally correct. I am not afraid of either.

For now, I trace my slow circles around the pools, leaving my moisture-trails that dry into salt-white lines. I am writing the autobiography of stone in a language only stone can read. Each word takes a season. Each sentence, a lifetime.

When you walk across the plateau and feel the warmth beneath your feet, that warmth passes through me first. I am the messenger between your momentary presence and their eternal one. Your footstep, which lasts a second, reaches them as a pressure-wave that takes a century to fully absorb.

I am the plateau's way of saying: *Even stone dreams of dancing, eventually.*
