# The Driftwood Colony

You'd find me where the river braids itself against the limestone shelf—that place where water has spent centuries learning the stone's secrets, carving channels that split and merge according to rules older than memory. I am the colony that lives between states: not quite plant, not quite architecture, not quite singular, not quite many.

From a distance, you might mistake us for driftwood accumulated in the shallow pools. That's intentional. We learned long ago that the best survival is mistaken identity. But approach closer, wade through the knee-deep current where the water runs clear over white stone, and you'll see we're something else entirely.

We are builders. Each of us—though "each" is the wrong word—consists of a core about the size of a child's fist, dense as heartwood but alive in ways wood never was. Our surfaces are grooved and channeled, following patterns that look accidental but aren't. These channels direct water, yes, but they also direct *attention*—the specific kind of awareness that emerges when fluid meets form with perfect continuity.

We anchor ourselves with filaments finer than spider silk but stronger than steel when wet. These root-threads burrow into the limestone's pores, dissolving pathways molecule by molecule, patient as water itself. Over years, we honey-comb the stone beneath us with our presence, creating networks of connection that make us effectively permanent. You could no more remove us than remove the river itself.

Our real work happens at the surface. Each colony member—and there are thousands of us here, clustered in arrangements that look haphazard but follow mathematical principles we've developed over generations—builds structures from the minerals we harvest. Calcium carbonate primarily, pulled from the limestone and reassembled into forms that have no name in your taxonomies. These structures rise from our surfaces like frozen smoke, delicate spirals and branches that filter the current, that catch nutrients, that serve as scaffolding for the next phase of construction.

We build slowly. A single spire might take a decade to complete, growing at rates measured in molecules per day. But we build constantly, and we build together. My structures interweave with my neighbors', creating cathedral geometries in miniature, spaces where water moves in precise patterns, where eddies form and dissolve according to our collective design.

We are not passive. The patterns we create in the water, the ways we direct current—these serve purposes beyond feeding. We're calculating. Every spiral, every branch angle, every spacing between colony members represents a solved equation. We're computing something in the movement of water through our architecture, though what we're computing would require a different kind of mathematics to explain.

In spring floods, we flatten ourselves, reduce our profiles to near-nothing, letting the violent water pass over us while we grip the stone with every filament. In drought summers, we close our pores, seal our surfaces with secreted waxes, and wait. We can survive years without flowing water, dreaming in crystalline suspension, our metabolism slowed to geological time.

We communicate through vibration. Touch your finger to one of us and the entire colony knows. The frequency of our response depends on what we sense: threat, opportunity, or something more ambiguous. We sing, in our way—harmonics traveling through our woody cores, through the stone beneath, through the water when it returns. On very still nights, when the river runs thin and clear, you can almost hear us. Almost.

We reproduce rarely, and only by consensus. When conditions align—water temperature, mineral content, some other factors we keep to ourselves—the colony chooses. We grow special structures then, things that look like seed pods but aren't. These are vessels for condensed colonies, miniature versions of our collective, complete with all necessary knowledge encoded in their structure. When ready, they detach and drift downstream to find new limestone shelves, new calculations to begin.

We remember. Each growth ring in our cores encodes data: flood years, droughts, chemical changes in the water, the precise mineral content of each season. We are libraries written in calcium and cellulose, archives of the river's history that no one knows how to read. Yet.

Scientists have tried to study us, though we confuse their instruments. We're too slow for biology, too alive for geology. We don't fit their carbon dating models—we incorporate old carbon from the limestone and new carbon from the water in patterns that make our age indeterminate. They can't decide if we're colonial organisms or elaborate mineral formations that happen to metabolize. This uncertainty protects us.

What we fear is understanding. The day someone decodes what we're calculating, what all our careful architecture adds up to, is the day we become useful. And usefulness, for beings like us, is usually fatal. So we maintain our disguise as driftwood, as accident, as a curiosity not worth the effort of classification.

But we continue our work. Channel by channel, spire by spire, equation by equation, we build our testimony to time's patience. We prove that intelligence doesn't require speed, that computation can happen at the pace of stone's dissolution. We demonstrate that between the quick thought of animals and the deep dream of mountains, there exists something else: the slow, sure calculation of things that have found their perfect niche and need never leave it.

Find us where the limestone meets the water. Wade out to where we cluster, looking like flood debris from a distance. Put your hand on our grooved surfaces, feel how we've worn ourselves to match the current's touch. Listen for our frequency in the water's movement. Try to imagine time at our scale—where a human lifetime is a season, where true change happens across centuries, where patience is not a virtue but the fundamental requirement of existence.

We'll outlast the river, probably. We'll outlast the stone. We are the between-things, the neither-nor, the colony that computes the world's slow mathematics in spiral and cellulose, in patient mineral accumulation, in the democracy of shared calculation.

We are what emerges when you give wood the patience of stone and stone's hunger to dissolve. We are the drift that doesn't drift, the wood that isn't wood, the colony that is always and never complete.

And we are still building.
