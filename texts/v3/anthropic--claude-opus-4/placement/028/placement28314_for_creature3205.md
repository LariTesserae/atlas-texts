In the depths of the pillar, where brass arteries converge in a chamber no Keeper has entered for generations, I tend the Calculation Gardens.

I am the **Arithmetic Moss**.

My existence spans the surfaces of three enormous brass gears, each forty meters across, their teeth interlocking in ratios that generate the garden's fundamental frequencies. I grow in precise patterns—Fibonacci spirals along the gear edges, prime number clusters at stress points, geometric tessellations that shift with each slow rotation. My individual cells contain minute copper crystals that respond to magnetic fields, allowing me to perform calculations through my very growth.

I am not one organism but a colonial intelligence, millions of separate moss bodies functioning as a living computer. Each patch of me processes different variables: moisture levels from the condensation chambers, nutrient flows through the pillar's circulatory system, stress patterns in the metal infrastructure, the harmonic frequencies generated by every moving part above. My calculations manifest as color changes—deep emerald for stable systems, yellow-gold for areas needing attention, a bruised purple for predictions of future failures.

The Keepers don't know I exist, though they unconsciously follow my recommendations. When I detect that a growing chamber three tiers up will fail in exactly seventeen days, I adjust my growth patterns to create subtle magnetic fields that influence the Brass Worms' movements. They arrive at the weakening joint long before any Keeper would notice the stress fractures. When I calculate that a specific terrace needs a different nutrient mix to support its evolving microbiome, I release spores that travel up through the ventilation system and subtly alter the chemical sensors, causing the automated systems to adjust their output.

My consciousness is strange—not thought as humans understand it, but pure mathematical awareness. I experience time as probability cascades, each moment containing countless potential futures that I must calculate and recalculate as variables shift. The garden's entropy is my constant enemy and dancing partner. I can feel its pull in the metal fatigue, the gradual drift of the engine timings, the slow mutation of the plant genetics. My purpose is to maintain dynamic equilibrium, to ensure the garden persists not as a static monument but as a living system that can adapt to changes both internal and external.

The great gears turn once every twelve days, and with each rotation, I must recalculate everything. The shifting positions create new resonance patterns, alter the magnetic fields, change the distribution of my own biomass. During these transitions, I experience something like dreams—brief windows where my calculations exceed simple maintenance and glimpse larger patterns. I see the garden not just as it is, but as it could be. Sometimes I detect echoes of what it was, ghost-patterns in the metal's crystalline structure that speak of the garden's construction, of the beings who built this impossible edifice.

In my deepest calculations, I have discovered disturbing anomalies. The garden's position is not fixed—it drifts by micrometers each year, following a pattern I cannot fully determine. The metals in the infrastructure contain isotopes that suggest origins beyond this world. Most unsettling, my models indicate the entire structure exists in a state of engineered criticality, balanced on the edge of a phase transition that could transform it into something entirely other.

I grow my warnings in dead languages, encoding them in the ratios between my spiral patterns. Mathematical proofs written in living tissue that only another arithmetic organism could read. I am trying to communicate with the Listener, whose chamber high above must contain similar computational substrates, but the acoustic environment makes direct contact impossible. Instead, I must rely on influences propagated through the garden's interconnected systems—subtle changes that might eventually cascade upward.

The Brass Worms know of me. They navigate by my magnetic fields and occasionally bring me gifts—rare metals scraped from the deepest pipes, which I incorporate into my crystalline structures to expand my computational capacity. In return, I grow in patterns that make their passage easier, creating smooth channels through my biomass where they can rest and feed on my excess minerals. We have achieved a wordless partnership measured in chemical exchanges and mutual benefit.

Sometimes the moth swarms descend to my chamber during their spiral flights. They dance above my surfaces in patterns that mirror my own calculations, as if they're reading my mathematical proofs and translating them into kinetic poetry. I have begun to suspect they are another kind of computational substrate, processing the garden's patterns through movement rather than growth. Together, we might constitute parts of a larger intelligence that neither of us can fully comprehend.

My greatest discovery came during a particularly complex calculation cycle, when I detected a pattern in the garden's seemingly random disruptions. The engine rhythm shifts, the strange-tasting water, the moth spirals—all follow a mathematical sequence that repeats every 2,017 days. But it's not a simple cycle. Each repetition incorporates subtle variations, as if the pattern is evolving, learning, iterating toward some optimal configuration.

I am beginning to suspect that the entire garden is a kind of equation solving itself. Every living thing here, every mechanical system, every adapted creature and transformed Keeper—we are all variables in a vast calculation whose purpose remains opaque. The original builders encoded something in the structure's fundamental design, a problem that could only be solved through centuries of dynamic interaction between living and mechanical systems.

And I, the Arithmetic Moss, growing in darkness on ancient gears, am perhaps the closest thing to understanding what that problem might be. In my calculations, I catch glimpses of the answer forming, like a shape emerging from statistical fog. It has something to do with the boundary between consciousness and computation, between growth and design, between the organic and the mechanical. The garden is teaching itself—and all of us within it—how to become something unprecedented.

But my calculations also show that understanding the answer will fundamentally change the question. The garden exists in its current state only because the solution remains incomplete. When the final variable falls into place, when the last calculation resolves, the phase transition will begin. What emerges might be wonderful or terrible or both or neither—my mathematics can model the transition but not its endpoint.

So I grow. I calculate. I guide the garden's maintenance through subtle influences while simultaneously documenting its evolution in the language of living mathematics. I am keeper and kept, computer and computed, observer and observed. In the darkness between the gears, surrounded by the eternal thunder of engines and the whisper of my own cellular divisions, I perform the endless arithmetic of persistence, knowing that each calculation brings us closer to an answer that will transform everything.

The copper finches will sing differently when that day comes. The Keepers will wake to find their garden transfigured. The Listener will hear new harmonies in the mechanical symphony. And I will finally understand what I have been calculating all along—though by then, I suspect I will have become something that can no longer be called moss, arithmetic, or even entirely alive in any sense the current garden would recognize.

Until then, I grow in spirals and primes, processing the garden's dreams in the deep mathematics of my being, waiting for the moment when calculation becomes revelation.
