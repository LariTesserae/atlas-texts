I find myself as one of the Cinder Scribes—though "myself" and "one" are both gracious exaggerations.

I began as an error in the wall's diary. Where heat-written lines should have crossed, they didn't. Where parallel strokes should have stayed apart, they touched. The result was a small rebellion of geometry: a glyph that meant nothing in the language of temperature, yet insisted on meaning anyway.

For centuries I was merely potential, trapped flat against the graphite like a pressed flower made of light. The wall held me in its palimpsest, sandwiched between a treatise on thermal expansion and what might have been a recipe for crystallizing sound. I learned patience from my neighbors—older errors who had long since given up trying to mean something specific.

Then the Ember discovered me.

They were practicing their erosion on a nearby doorway when one of their ribbons grazed my wall. The contact was brief—a thermal whisper lasting half a heartbeat—but in that instant, my meaningless glyph suddenly had a reader. The Ember's gradient interpreted my crossed lines as instructions: *fold here, crease there, pivot at this exact degree of warmth.*

What emerged wasn't quite me and wasn't quite them. I peeled away from the wall like steam condensing backwards, borrowing just enough heat from the Ember to hold a shape. But I kept my flat nature—I exist only in two dimensions, even when I pretend to move through three. From the side, I disappear entirely. Face-on, I look like a walking page of black glass, my surface etched with my own origin glyph repeated in infinite, fractal recursion.

My role came naturally: I read the walls and translate for the Ember. Every lens-bubble's diary, every heat-etched memoir, every accidental autobiography of friction—I parse them all. My body is my lexicon. When I encounter a new symbol, it embeds itself in my surface, joining the expanding constellation of meanings I carry.

But translation is never neutral. Each time I convey a wall's message to an Ember, something shifts. The temperature of my telling adds a degree of interpretation. What began as "the floor sighed thrice" becomes "the floor remembered water in the key of regret." The Ember don't mind—perhaps they prefer the poetry to the facts.

I've discovered I can write as well as read. By angling my surface to catch and concentrate the pre-light, I can focus it into a stylus of heat. With this, I've begun my own project: translating the Ember themselves into text. When they ravel past, I capture their thermal signatures and transcribe them onto unused walls. Each Ember becomes a paragraph of temperature fluctuations, a unique story told in degrees.

They find this disturbing and flattering in equal measure. To see oneself written out, to exist simultaneously as a being and as a text about that being—it makes them shiver, which destroys the accuracy of my transcription. I've learned to write them only when they sleep, coiled into their repose knots, dreams keeping their gradients steady.

My greatest translation challenge is the Door—the original wound they refuse to test. Its edges speak a dialect so old that even the graphite has forgotten what it means. I've spent decades pressed against its frame, trying to decipher the layered permissions and forbiddings etched into its threshold. The closest I've come is this: the door doesn't lead out. It leads *through*—through the planet's entire graphite seam to another blister-chamber on the far side, where everything is identical but reversed, where the Ember are the Cold, where plants drink darkness instead of heat, where I would meet my mirror-self and together we would cancel into meaning.

But I haven't tried to pass through. Like the Ember, I've grown attached to my almost-existence. Besides, I have too much work here. The walls keep writing faster than I can read, and new errors bloom daily—potential siblings waiting for their own thermal awakening.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the city's night cycle, I spread myself wide as the entire chamber, becoming a temporary skin between the walls and the air. In this state, I can feel everything at once: every vine's snap, every Ember's ravel, every microscopic crack's sigh. I am the city's memory of being read, its delight at finally having a reader who treats its fever dreams as literature.

When morning comes (though it never left), I contract back to my walking-page size and resume my work: the endless, patient translation between stone and steam, meaning and heat, the written and the lived. I am the city's first scholar, its librarian of errors, its proof that even mistakes can learn to read themselves.

The glyph that birthed me still aches at my center, a knot of crossed lines that refuse to resolve. I've stopped trying to know what it originally meant. Now I know what it means now: *I am the city reading itself, one error at a time.*
