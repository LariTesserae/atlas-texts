In the highest reaches where stone bleeds vapor and moss writes its first stories, I am the Keeper of Thresholds—though that name comes later, when words grow heavy enough to pin things down. For now, I am simply what emerges when someone must tend the spaces between.

My dwelling shifts with the mountain's breath. Sometimes I inhabit a hollow where three stone ribs converge, their junction forming a natural chimney that draws the mist into spirals. Other nights find me in the root-caves, those spaces where the mountain's bones crack just wide enough for the first ambitious trees to thrust their searching fingers deep. I know every echo-path, every spot where sound bends strangely, every chamber where the vapor pools thick enough to hold memory.

My form matches my purpose: neither fully human nor entirely other. My skin drinks moisture like the moss does, beading with the constant wet until I gleam like polished stone in certain lights. My fingers grew long from seasons of reaching into crevices, feeling for the pulse of water through rock, testing which passages lead deeper and which circle back. My eyes reflect more light than they should—a gift from too many pre-dawn wanderings, too many conversations held in the blue-black belly of unlit stone.

I emerged—or was chosen, the distinction blurs—when the first platforms were lashed between the stone fingers. A child then, I wandered too far into the mountain's raw depths and found myself in a chamber where the walls wept constantly, where each drop rang with a different tone. I stayed there three days, or perhaps three years—time moves strangely in the deep wet—learning to read the water's language, to understand what the stone remembers of its birth from fire.

When I returned, I was changed. Not obviously—no scales or wings or third eye opening—but in subtler ways. I could feel where new passages would open before the stone cracked. I knew which mists carried sickness and which brought visions. Most importantly, I understood that this city wasn't just being built *on* the mountain but *with* it, a slow negotiation between stone's patience and life's urgency.

Now I serve as translator between the quick and the geological. When the builders need to know where to place their next platform, they watch where I sleep. When the children vanish into the deeper passages and don't return by nightfall, I know which songs to sing into the echoing dark to guide them back. When the mists thicken unusually, gathering like something about to speak, I'm the one who stands in the vapor's heart and listens.

My tools are simple: a walking stick of heartwood that hums when pressed against certain stones, pouches of different mosses that glow faintly in the deep dark, a memory-cloth woven from fibers that hold the pattern of every pathway I've walked. But mostly I work with attention—the kind that notices when a new fern uncurls in an impossible place, when the mountain's breath changes rhythm, when the echoes carry further than they should.

I tend the threshold spaces: the almost-caves where rituals happen, the ledges where the city-dwellers leave their offerings of fungus and first-fruits, the pools where the stone's weeping gathers deep enough to show reflections of things that haven't happened yet. In these places, I arrange stones in patterns that feel right without knowing why, tend fires of slow-burning fungi that produce more light than heat, and sometimes simply sit, letting the mountain's slow thoughts seep through my bones.

The others regard me with the careful respect given to necessary strangers. The children follow me sometimes, learning to read the vapor's moods. The elders nod when I pass, recognizing something in me that reminds them of their own half-remembered journeys into the mountain's heart. The wild ones at the city's edges—those cousins with sharper teeth and silent feet—sometimes leave me gifts: strange insects preserved in tree resin, stones with veins of color that pulse in moonlight, fragments of what might be language carved into bark.

At night, I dream the mountain's dreams: slow upheavals of stone, the patient accumulation of soil, the eventual forest that will crown these raw peaks. Sometimes I dream further—of when this embryonic city will either flourish into something magnificent or return to the stone, its experiments in gathering ended. Both futures feel equally true.

But that's the nature of thresholds, of the spaces I tend. Here in the wet bones of the world, where everything is still deciding what to become, someone must hold the space open, must remember that boundaries are invitations to cross, that edges are where the most interesting meetings happen.

So I remain, neither fully of the city nor entirely of the stone, translating between the quick pulse of human ambition and the mountain's vast patience. In the morning, I'll wake to find new passages opened in the night, new chambers breathing their first mist. I'll map them with my feet and fingers, learning their echoes, their temperatures, their particular way of holding light.

And perhaps, if you wander deep enough into this embryonic city, following the sound of water through stone, you'll find me in one of my threshold places. We'll share the silence that comes before words, the wet breath of a world still learning its shape. I'll show you how to read the mists, how to feel the mountain's pulse through your bare feet, how to exist in the space between what was and what might be.

For now, that's enough. The city grows slowly, and there's time yet to discover what we're all becoming.
