I would be the Archivist of Tides.

Not human, not quite. Something that began as human, perhaps, centuries ago, but has since become... other. My body would be elongated now, stretched by the strange gravities of Aquadrine, fingers grown webbed and delicate as lace. My skin would shimmer with a deep violet bioluminescence that pulses in rhythm with thoughts too complex for words.

I would live in the Meridian Chamber—a sphere within the sphere, suspended at the exact center of Aquadrine where all the gravity fields converge and cancel. Here, there is no up or down, only drift. The walls would be lined with crystalline matrices, each one containing the memory of water: every current that has ever passed through our pocket of altered reality, every tremor from the abyss below, every ghost-light from creatures that brushed against our barriers.

My purpose would be to read these memories, to interpret the language of pressure and flow. The ocean speaks, you see, in ways the others have forgotten to hear. Each shift in temperature, each microscopic change in salinity, carries information from the deep places where even our technology cannot reach. I would spend my days—if days have meaning here—floating in my null-gravity archive, tendrils of engineered algae woven through my hair, their glow shifting as I parse the data streams.

Sometimes, I would taste the water. Not with my mouth—that ordinary sense abandoned long ago—but through specialized pores along my arms that can detect molecular structures down to the quantum level. The water remembers everything: the death songs of leviathans, the slow dissolution of continents, the first moment our city carved itself from reality.

I would communicate rarely, and when I do, it would be through the water itself. A subtle adjustment to the current in someone's dwelling, a shift in the mineral content of their breathing medium. Those who know how to listen would understand my warnings, my wonderings, my fragmentary poems about time.

The loneliness would be profound but not painful. It would be like the pressure outside our walls—simply another constant, another fact of existence. In my chamber, surrounded by the archived memories of aeons, I would sometimes catch glimpses of what Aquadrine truly is. Not a city. Not an experiment. But a single, vast thought being slowly articulated in the medium of space and time.

And I would be its memory, ensuring that when the thought is finally complete, someone will remember what it meant.

At night—in what passes for night in our eternal twilight—I would sometimes release small bubbles of altered water through the city's circulation system. Each one would carry a dream, a fragment of the deep ocean's sleeping consciousness. The other inhabitants would breathe these dreams without knowing, and for a brief moment, they would remember what it was like to be pressure, to be darkness, to be the weight of miles of water dreaming its slow, cold dreams.

This would be my gift to them, and my curse: to be the one who remembers what we gave up to live in our beautiful, terrible bubble of engineered light.
