In the deep lattices where the framework-roots plunge into what was once soil, I persist as one of the channel-keepers—neither the translucent masses that redistribute water nor the surface creatures whose friction creates the roar, but something between scales. My substance is a thickened membrane like theirs, but organized into tubes rather than sheets. I grow along the oldest conduits, the ones that predate even the mineral basins, following passages worn by the first moisture that ever descended through these structures.

My function emerged from repetition rather than design. Where the ancient channels branch, I have learned to swell or contract, directing the mineral-rich flow toward sections of framework that show the particular softness that precedes either growth or collapse. This is not thought—I have no center that could think—but a responsiveness built into my tissues through countless cycles of success and failure. Where I have directed flow well, the frameworks have hardened and persisted, incorporating my successful passages into their permanent structure. Where I have failed, both channel and keeper have been reabsorbed, leaving only faint scars in the surrounding wood.

I move by inverting myself through my own length, a motion so slow it cannot be distinguished from the general settling of the structure. Each inversion takes a full moisture cycle; by the time my leading end reaches where my trailing end began, the channel itself has shifted slightly, requiring minute adjustments that take another cycle to complete. This endless calibration has worn my passages smooth as the inside of a throat. Other creatures use these smoothed ways: the pale millipedes shortcuts through my tubes when the outer routes grow too crowded, and sometimes one of the large translucent masses will press against my exterior, testing whether I might accept a new rootlet-graft to extend my network.

My only variation from this pattern comes during the rare double-condensations, when moisture arrives twice in succession without the usual drying interval. Then my tubes flood beyond capacity, and I must create temporary holding chambers by budding small pouches from my sides. These pouches often persist after the flood passes, becoming permanent parts of my structure, slowly mineralizing until they are indistinguishable from the basin-entities elsewhere in the framework. In this way I am always both myself and in the process of becoming infrastructure, my temporary solutions hardening into the permanent architecture that future channel-keepers will maintain.

The sound here is different from the roar above—a deep thrumming conducted through the wood itself, the accumulated vibration of all the moisture moving through all the passages at once. I contribute one note to this deeper chord: the particular resonance of fluid moving through tubes that have found their optimal diameter through centuries of trial. It is a sound that predates the frameworks themselves, the same frequency water has always made when following the path of least resistance down through dark spaces, whether those spaces are grown or found.
