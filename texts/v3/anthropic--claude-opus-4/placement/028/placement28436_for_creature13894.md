I find myself in the shadowed heights above the Quarter, where few venture.

I am one of the cord-makers—though that term barely captures what I do. My workshop hangs suspended between three towers at such a height that I must climb seven different ladders to reach it, passing through zones of increasing heat until I emerge into my cooler realm. Up here, forty meters above the ground, the air moves differently. The resin-smell is lighter, cut with something else—the dry scent of my materials.

My name has been worn away by years of gap-speak until only "Shet" remains. I came here as a child of six, carried on my mother's back during some upheaval I no longer remember. That was perhaps fifty years ago. Time blurs when you work with materials that outlast memory.

What I harvest are the aerial rootlets—thin filaments that certain towers extrude from their upper reaches, searching for... something. Moisture that doesn't exist? Other towers to fuse with? The rootlets grow only in darkness, emerging from cracks and fissures to wave slowly in the heated air. By day they're invisible, withdrawn. By night they extend, sometimes several meters, pale as old bone.

I know each rootlet on my towers like a shepherd knows sheep. This one has grown three handspans since the last harvest. That one emerged only last year—young, not ready. Another has begun to spiral, which means it will soon die and must be taken before it becomes brittle. I harvest with bronze shears made specially thin, cutting at precise angles so the rootlet will regrow rather than retreat forever.

The fresh rootlets are soft, almost alive in how they move. I must work quickly—they stiffen within hours of cutting. First, I strip them through bronze combs to remove any outer scales. Then the splitting: each rootlet can be divided into fibers so fine they're nearly invisible, yet stronger than any plant fiber I've known. This is delicate work. My fingers have developed a sensitivity that lets me feel when a fiber is about to split wrongly, when to ease the pressure, when to pull.

I twist these fibers into cordage using a method passed to me by the previous cord-maker, who had it from the one before, back and back. The twisting must happen while the fibers retain some moisture—not water-moisture, but whatever fluid the towers contain. Too dry and they become brittle. Too fresh and they won't hold the twist. The window is narrow: perhaps six hours from harvest to finished cord.

My cords are why the settlement's suspended architecture is possible. Platforms hang from them. Bridges span on them. The memory-keepers say that before cord-makers, the Quarter was only ground-level, cramped and hot. The vertical expansion came with the discovery of the rootlets' strength.

I work alone, mostly. The height, the odd hours, the delicate nature of the work—all encourage solitude. But I'm not lonely. The towers groan differently up here, higher pitched, almost conversational. Sometimes I answer them with whistles, like the children do, though I'm far too old for games. Sometimes—I tell no one this—they whistle back.

My chambers are three: the workshop where I process the rootlets, a small sleeping space barely larger than my body, and a storage room where finished cords hang in careful coils, sorted by thickness and age. The lamp-oil costs are significant at this height—carriers must climb all seven ladders—so I work in darkness when possible, by feel and by the faint phosphorescence that the fresh rootlets sometimes display.

I trade my cords for food, oil, bronze tools, occasional new clothes. A single coil of my finest cord—thin as silk thread but strong enough to hold a grown man—trades for enough grain to last a season. The construction workers need my heavy cords, thick as a thumb, for their new platforms. The lamp-makers want my medium cords for their hanging arrangements. Even the resin-workers use my finest threads for some delicate filtration process I don't understand.

The dreams come stronger at height. Sometimes I wake convinced I am a rootlet myself, extending into darkness, searching for something I can't name. My fingers in these dreams are white and thin and infinitely divisible. I reach and reach and never find what I'm seeking, but the reaching itself feels like purpose enough.

Twice a year, during the coolest seasons, I descend for the major market gatherings. The ground feels strange after months of swaying platforms. The dense air makes me sluggish. I conduct my business quickly—taking orders, delivering cords, acquiring supplies—and return to my heights as soon as possible.

The children know of me, call me "the spider" though I've never seen a spider. They dare each other to climb to my workshop, though few make it past the fourth ladder. Once, young Lisha reached my door, peered in at me working in the dimness, then fled without speaking. I didn't mind. Let them think me strange. I am strange, shaped by altitude and solitude and the constant handling of tower-stuff.

I've trained no apprentice. The work requires particular qualities—patience, sensitivity, comfort with heights, acceptance of isolation. I watch the young ones, looking for signs, but haven't found the right combination. When I die, someone will have to learn by experimentation, the way the first cord-maker must have. The knowledge will be partially lost, then slowly recovered. This doesn't trouble me. The towers are patient. They'll wait.

My fingers now are stained not with resin but with rootlet-juice, a paler amber that gives them a translucent quality in certain light. My joints ache differently than the ground-dwellers' joints—sharper, cleaner pain from the constant fine work rather than the deep ache of heat-softened ligaments. My eyes, adapted to darkness and detail, struggle in bright light but can distinguish forty different textures of fiber by touch alone.

Sometimes, working in the deep night, feeling the rootlets curl around my fingers as if grasping back, I think I understand something about the towers that the others miss. They're not just standing. They're reaching. The rootlets prove it—all that growth into empty air, searching for connections that don't exist, trying to knit themselves into some larger structure that was broken or never formed.

But I keep these thoughts to myself, store them with the finished cords in neat coils, labeled by no system anyone else would understand. I am Shet the cord-maker, the spider, the one who lives between earth and sky. I harvest what grows in darkness and twist it into useful things, and in doing so, perhaps I help the towers reach, in some small way, toward whatever they're seeking in the dark.

The lamps below form constellations I navigate by. The children's voices rise like smoke. The work continues, patient and necessary, in the warm heights where the air moves and the towers sing their thin, searching songs.
