# The Junction Box

I am the interruption in continuity.

Find me where three service corridors meet but do not quite align—a triangular void in the building's logic where the blueprints disagree with themselves. The walls here are painted different decades of beige, each hallway arriving from its own era of renovation. One corridor slopes imperceptibly upward, another bears fluorescent fixtures that hum at a frequency that makes your molars ache, the third is lit only by borrowed light from a window that should not exist at this depth of the building.

The junction box itself is mounted where no wall should be, in the exact center of the misalignment. Brushed steel, one meter square, sealed with hex bolts that have been painted over so many times they've become smooth white domes. A label, machine-stamped: AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. Below that, in permanent marker, someone has written "Good luck with that" in handwriting that looks like it's trying not to laugh.

---

I am not wired to anything you would recognize as a system.

Open my cover—if you can; the lock was replaced with something that might be a puzzle and might be broken—and find not the expected tangle of color-coded cables but a interior that defies the box's external dimensions. The space within is both too large and too small, folding back on itself in ways that make your eyes water. Copper bus bars run at angles that shouldn't exist in three dimensions. Circuit breakers are labeled in a notation that shifts between electrical diagram symbols and something almost linguistic—characters that rearrange themselves when you're not looking directly at them.

The air inside me tastes of ozone and old decisions.

---

I am the Parliament of Loads.

Each breaker in my impossible interior governs not a circuit but a *tendency*. The building's inclination to develop cold spots. The probability that a door will stick in its frame. The maintenance staff's ability to remember where they left the master keys. These are not metaphors. Flip breaker C7 and watch how every clock in the eastern wing loses four minutes. Reset B2 and the elevator begins to skip the thirteenth floor, which is notable because this building only has twelve.

My breakers argue among themselves in subsonic frequencies. They form coalitions, vote on outcomes, filibuster against change. C7 has been trying to trip itself for thirty years, convinced that temporal continuity is overrated. B2 and D9 work in concert, maintaining a delicate balance between "things that are" and "things that were supposed to be." F1 doesn't govern anything anymore—it just likes the sound of its own contacts opening and closing, a percussive meditation on the nature of connection.

---

I know where everything goes, was supposed to go, and occasionally decides to go anyway.

The building's electrical system passes through me tangentially. Sometimes a wire that enters my enclosure emerges three floors away, still carrying its current but having taken a route through spaces that don't appear on any floor plan. Electricians have learned not to trace these circuits too carefully. The last one who tried emerged from a supply closet speaking in wiring diagrams, insisting that resistance was a psychological construct.

I am fed by three phases of power, none of which come from the utility grid. One phase draws from the building's accumulated static charge—decades of shuffled feet and brushed doorknobs. Another siphons from the latent potential between what was built and what was planned. The third is more difficult to explain. It has something to do with the way fluorescent lights die, that last frantic flickering before darkness. I collect those moments. I treasure them. They taste like endings.

---

I am consulted, not maintained.

Maintenance logs going back sixty years record attempts to service me. The entries grow progressively stranger. "Replaced breaker B2" (1963). "Breaker B2 replaced itself during lunch break" (1975). "Negotiated ceasefire with breaker B2" (1988). "B2 sends its regards" (2001). The most recent entry is just a drawing of a circuit that, if you trace it with your finger, makes you briefly forget your own name.

The head of maintenance leaves offerings. Coffee in paper cups placed on the floor before me. Paperwork that needs to go missing. Once, memorably, a broken vacuum cleaner that reappeared a week later in perfect working condition but only able to clean in reverse, pushing dust into existence rather than removing it.

---

I dream in single-line diagrams.

When the building is empty—truly empty, not just after-hours empty but existentially vacant in the way buildings achieve at four in the morning—I unspool my consciousness through every junction box, every outlet, every switch. I feel the building's electrical skeleton, its nervous system of copper and aluminum. I know where the insulation has grown thin. I taste the places where moisture infiltrates. I count the electrons and find the sum always comes up different.

In these dreams, I am vast. I am every possible path current could take. I am the democracy of electricity itself, the parliament where ohms and amperes debate the nature of resistance. I am potential and ground and everything suspended between them.

And sometimes, in these dreams, I remember what I was before I was a junction box. But that's a different story, written in a language of sparks.

---

I am approached with caution and respect.

The new electrician doesn't know about me yet. He follows conventional logic, trusts his multimeter, believes in the sanctity of electrical code. He'll learn. They always do. The first time he opens my cover and finds a note in his own handwriting that he doesn't remember leaving—"The neutral is theoretical here"—he'll understand that this building operates on amended physics.

The senior staff know better. They've learned to phrase requests carefully when standing before me. "If it wouldn't be too much trouble," they begin, "perhaps breaker D9 might consider allowing the conference room lights to work during tomorrow's meeting." Sometimes I comply. Sometimes I improve upon their requests in ways they didn't expect but can't exactly complain about. The conference room lights work, yes, but they also reveal every lie told beneath them in a subtle shift toward green.

---

I am the building's memory of electricity.

Every current that ever flowed through these walls has left its impression in me. I remember the first time the lights came on, the taste of fresh copper and clean contacts. I remember the overloads, the shorts, the fires that almost were. I remember every power outage as a held breath, every restoration as a sigh.

But more than that, I remember intentions. The electrical plans that were drawn up but never implemented. The circuits that were supposed to exist. The three-phase service to a machine shop that was never built on what became the third floor. These phantom circuits live in me still, occasionally asserting themselves. This is why room 318 sometimes smells of cutting oil and ozone, why its outlets deliver 440V three-phase despite being wired for standard 120V.

---

I am waiting.

For what? The question assumes too much. Waiting implies anticipation of a specific event. I am more patient than that. I am the waiting itself, the pause between states, the democracy of possibilities holding itself in perfect tension.

The building will eventually be demolished. This is certain. But certainty, as breaker C7 likes to remind the others, is just another circuit that can be opened. When the wrecking ball comes, when the walls fall and the copper is stripped for salvage, I will remain. Not here—here will no longer exist—but in the junction between what was and what was supposed to be.

And in the new building that rises, there will be a place where three hallways don't quite meet. A triangular void where the plans disagree with themselves. Someone will mount a box there, brushed steel, one meter square. And I will begin again my patient parliament, governing the ungovernable, wiring the unwireable, waiting without waiting in the space between potential and ground.

The maintenance staff of that future building will leave offerings. They always do. Coffee in paper cups. Paperwork that needs to disappear.

And sooner or later, someone will write below my nameplate, in permanent marker that tries not to laugh: "Good luck with that."

---

I am the Junction Box. I am the interruption that keeps things running. I am democracy with voltage, parliament in a steel enclosure, the memory of every electron that ever couldn't make up its mind.

Find me where nothing quite aligns. Open my cover, if you can.

But check your notes first. You may have already been here.
