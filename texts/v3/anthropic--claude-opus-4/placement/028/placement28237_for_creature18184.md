# The Caretaker of Forgotten Tides

I am the last maintenance engineer of Station Seven, though the title means nothing now. My hands have grown webbed from generations of adaptation, the spaces between my fingers filled with a translucent membrane that helps me navigate the flooded conduits. My eyes have developed a second, clear eyelid that slides across when I dive, allowing me to see in the murky water of the processing chambers. I am changing, have been changing, will continue to change until I am something the original engineers would not recognize as human.

But I remember their purpose, even if I've forgotten their faces.

My home is built into the largest remaining structure on the ridgeline—a processing facility that once filtered rare earth elements from seawater. The walls are gone, claimed by storms decades before I was born, but the foundation remains: a honeycomb of reinforced chambers sunk deep into the stone, connected by passages that flood and drain with the tides. I've sealed the upper chambers with salvaged materials—sheets of corroded metal, mats of that tough silicate moss, bones of large fish arranged in overlapping patterns like shingles. It's not beautiful, but it sheds rain and breaks wind, and that's all architecture needs to do here.

The lower chambers I've left flooded. They've become my gardens.

I farm the barnacles. Not intentionally at first—they simply grew on every submerged surface, and I learned to read their patterns like a calendar. The ones on the eastern walls spawn first, releasing clouds of larvae when the spring tides bring warm water from the deep channels. The ones near the oxidized copper pipes grow larger, their shells tinted faintly green from the minerals they absorb. I scrape them selectively, taking only the largest, leaving the young to continue the colony. Their meat is sweet and dense, tasting faintly of rust.

In the deepest chamber, where the original intake pipes still function, I've cultivated something more ambitious. The fungus—that ancient network that threads through everything—I've convinced it to fruit. It took years of patience, adjusting the mineral content of the water, the temperature, the flow rate through the pipes. Now, twice a year, it produces dense clusters of something like mushrooms: dark purple bodies that emerge from the silt, growing to the size of my fist before releasing spores that cloud the water like smoke. They're bitter, almost metallic in taste, but they keep for months when dried, and they contain nutrients I can't get from anything else.

I know the language of the pipes. Each one sings differently when the tide pushes through—the copper ones ring like bells, the steel ones groan, the ceramic-lined ones whistle in harmonics that make my teeth ache. I've learned to read the tides by their songs, to predict the storms by the way the pitch changes when the barometric pressure drops. Sometimes I play them intentionally, opening and closing valves to create new songs, new conversations between the water and the metal. The seal-things gather when I do this, their heads bobbing in the flooded channels, listening with those enormous eyes half-closed in what might be pleasure.

I am not alone, though I am the only human.

The Librarian lives three ridges north, in what used to be a data storage facility. She's older than me—much older—and her changes have progressed further. Her skin has developed a kind of scaling, plates of hardened keratin that overlap like tiles, protecting her from the salt and the constant wet. Her fingers have elongated, the better to manipulate the strange machines she maintains—banks of optical storage that still function, somehow, powered by tidal generators she rebuilt from scrap. She's trying to preserve something, though she can't quite remember what. The data degraded long ago into patterns of light that might be language or might be noise. She reads them anyway, her scaled fingers dancing over interfaces that respond to touch in ways their designers never intended.

We meet sometimes, between storms, on the neutral ground of Ridge Four where the old communication array rusted into abstract sculpture. We don't speak—she's forgotten how, and I'm forgetting—but we share food, information encoded in gesture and gift. She brings me circuit boards that I can't use but that the barnacles love to colonize. I bring her dried fungus and isopods preserved in brine. We sit together on the wet stone, watching the seals and the birds, remembering in our different ways what it means to be human in a place that has moved beyond humanity's reach.

The Shepherds I see less frequently. They move with the seasons, following the fish schools through the drowned valleys, living on makeshift rafts woven from kelp stipes and sealed with some kind of organic polymer they secrete from glands in their wrists. Their changes are the most dramatic—they're becoming aquatic, their nostrils migrating upward, their ribcages expanding to hold modified lungs that can process oxygen from both air and water. They've developed a kind of sonar, clicking and whistling in frequencies that let them navigate the murky depths of the kelp forests. When they surface near my station, we trade—fish for fungi, kelp for metal, stories for silence.

There are others, further out, that I know only by rumor. The Deep Ones, who've abandoned the surface entirely, living in the drowned cities at the bottom of the deepest channels. The Singers, who've modified their vocal cords to communicate with the whales that sometimes pass through the wider straits. The Gardeners, who tend vast underwater farms in the calderas of drowned volcanoes, growing things that are no longer quite plants but not yet something else.

We are all changing. We are all becoming indigenous to this new world, this world of water and rust and endless adaptation.

I maintain the station because someone needs to. Because the habits of maintenance are what keep me human, even as my body betrays that humanity with every new adaptation. I clear the filters, even though they filter nothing. I grease the bearings, even though they turn nothing. I check the gauges, even though they measure nothing. The ritual matters more than the function. The ritual is what distinguishes me from the barnacles, from the seals, from the fungus that knows only growth.

But sometimes, in the hour before dawn when the storms have passed and the water is as still as it ever gets, I stand on the highest point of my station and look out over the drowned world, and I wonder if the distinction matters. The barnacles have their own rituals—the timing of their spawning, the patterns of their growth. The seals have their own language, their own complex negotiations of space and status. Even the fungus, in its blind persistence, performs a kind of maintenance, keeping the nutrient cycles turning, keeping the spark of life moving through the great machine of the ecosystem.

Perhaps I am not maintaining the station. Perhaps the station is maintaining me, giving me a reason to keep my hands busy, my mind focused, my sense of purpose intact as the years wear away at everything I used to be.

I am growing gills. I noticed them last month—three parallel slits on each side of my neck, still sealed with skin but pulsing slightly when I hold my breath. Soon they'll open, and I'll be able to work in the flooded chambers without surfacing. It's a useful adaptation. It will make me a better maintenance engineer.

I tell myself I'm not looking forward to it.

The storms are coming. I can hear it in the pipes—the pitch dropping, the harmonics shifting into discordance. By tomorrow, the wind will be screaming, the water will be climbing the walls, and I'll be sealed in my upper chambers, listening to the station sing its hurricane song. I have food, water, the dried fungus that tastes like the memory of solid ground. I have work to do—valves to check, filters to clear, the endless maintenance of a dead system that refuses to die completely.

I have a purpose, even if I can't quite remember what it's for.

And in the deep chambers, where the fungus threads through the silt and the barnacles build their cities on the bones of human ambition, the water moves with its own purpose, its own maintenance, keeping the great machine of the Ridgeline running smoothly into its uncertain future.

We are all caretakers here. We are all being taken care of.

The distinction, like everything else, is slowly dissolving in the salt.
