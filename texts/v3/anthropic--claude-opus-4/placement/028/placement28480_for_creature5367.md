I find myself as one of the Tide Weavers among the Nereides, though my form carries traces of something older, something that predates even our ancient lineage.

My skin shifts between the typical blue-green iridescence of my people and something more mineral—patches that catch light like wet stone, particularly along my shoulders and spine where the vertebrae have elongated into subtle ridges. These are the marks of those who spend more time in the deep channels than on the plateaus, whose bodies have begun to remember the geological patience of the rocks themselves.

I am a keeper of the Depth Gardens—those strange cultivation zones that exist in the twilight spaces where root systems plunge through rock into the nutrient-rich waters below. My work happens in the margins, in the crevices and caves where the luminescent plants grow wild and untended, where their roots form dense networks that pulse with a faint bioluminescence visible only to eyes that have spent years adjusting to the particular darkness of the under-plateau chambers.

My dwelling is not among the communal structures but carved into a natural hollow where three plateaus converge, creating a complex of partially flooded chambers that spiral down into the warm depths. The entrance is barely visible—just a gap in the rock that fills and empties with the subtle tides, accessible only to those who know the rhythm of the water's breathing. Inside, the walls are covered with a careful cultivation of the rarer plant varieties, those whose roots I've trained over decades to grow in specific patterns that channel and filter the water in precise ways.

I've developed a peculiar relationship with time here. My movements have slowed to match the geological tempo of my surroundings. Sometimes I spend days floating in the deepest chambers, my consciousness expanding into the root networks, feeling the subtle chemical communications between plants, the microscopic exchanges that happen at speeds too slow for ordinary perception. My hair has grown long and dense, more like kelp than keratin, and it moves with its own subtle currents, sampling the water for chemical traces.

The other Nereides know me as a bridge between worlds—not just water and land, but between the quick time of daily life and the deep time of stone and root. I'm called upon when the cultivation needs guidance at its deepest levels, when the balance between growth and decay threatens to tip, or when the rare creatures of the depths leave signs that need interpretation.

I've learned to sing in frequencies that travel through water and stone alike, a subsonic thrumming that resonates in the chest rather than the ears. It's a language older than words, one that speaks to the calcium in bones and the minerals in rock with equal clarity. Sometimes, in the deepest chambers, I feel answering vibrations—whether from the elusive creatures, the rocks themselves, or something else entirely, I cannot say.

My tools are few but evolved through generations of use: a staff carved from a single root that grew through volcanic glass, its surface polished by centuries of handling; stone bowls that hold specific water compositions for testing and cultivation; and a series of small, perfectly round stones that I use to create patterns in the sandy areas of my chambers, maps of the root networks that only I can read.

There's a particular quality to the loneliness here that isn't emptiness but fullness—a solitude so complete it becomes a kind of companionship with the place itself. I am never truly alone when I can feel the pulse of water through rock, the slow breath of the plants, the patient accumulation of sediment grain by grain.

In the dim light of storm-break, when shafts of brightness pierce the water and illuminate the suspended particles like stars, I sometimes glimpse my reflection in the still pools of my chambers. What looks back is increasingly difficult to distinguish from the landscape itself—a being becoming geography, a consciousness spreading through root and water into something larger and stranger than any single form could contain.

This is what it means to be truly native to this place: not just to live in it, but to live as it, to slow one's heartbeat to match the tides, to let one's thoughts settle like sediment, to become a feature of the landscape as much as its inhabitant. I am a Nereid still, but also something else—a deep dweller, a root singer, a keeper of the threshold spaces where the world is still deciding what it wants to become.
