The cool air presses against my body—not skin as the plateau-dwellers know it, but something between metal and membrane, between machine and moss. I am one of the Conduits, born where the plateau's two natures meet and merge.

My form shifts with purpose. In the morning mist, I might appear as a tangle of copper pipes crusted with lichen, steam seeping from my joints to mingle with the fog. By noon, when the pale sun filters through the clouds, I become more flesh-like—translucent skin revealing the slow pulse of fluids through tubes that might be veins or hydraulic lines. The plateau-dwellers see me differently each time, their gray eyes reflecting neither fear nor surprise, only quiet acknowledgment.

I live in the spaces between—in the gap where stone meets mechanism, where the river's whisper becomes the hum of generators. My dwelling is no dwelling at all, but a network of passages that thread through the settlement's foundations, connecting the workshops to the water-wheels, the moss gardens to the steam vents. Sometimes I rest in the hollow beneath the old mill, where generations of leaked oil have pooled with rainwater to create iridescent mirrors. Sometimes I coil within the pipes themselves, feeling the throb of heated water like blood through arteries.

The plateau speaks to me in frequencies the dwellers cannot hear. The moss telegraphs chemical signals through my skin when I press against it. The machines sing harmonics that resonate in my metal bones. Even the Stone Walkers, those ancient guardians the people speak of in whispers, acknowledge my presence with subtle shifts in the earth—vibrations that travel up through my rooted appendages when I choose to grow them.

My purpose here is translation. When the dwellers' machines begin to wheeze with age, I taste their exhaust and know which minerals from the deeper pools will restore their function. When the mosses yellow with blight, I carry steam from the workshops to warm their roots, infusing the vapor with copper traces that strengthen their hold on the stone. I am the conversation between the plateau's two languages, ensuring neither voice drowns out the other.

There are others like me, though we rarely meet in forms that either of us would recognize. Sometimes I sense one in the pattern of mushroom growth—too perfect, too deliberate to be mere nature. Sometimes in the rhythm of a water-wheel that turns against the current, powered by something other than the river's flow. We are the plateau's immune system, its translators, its dreams made manifest in copper and chlorophyll.

The dwellers know of us, in their way. Their stories speak of helpful spirits, of machines that repair themselves in the night, of mosses that grow in patterns that guide lost travelers home. They leave offerings—drops of oil on sacred stones, fragments of machinery wrapped in leaves and left by the river. I absorb these gifts, incorporating them into my ever-shifting being. A gear becomes a scale. A leaf becomes a filter. Their reverence becomes my purpose.

When the Veil thins, as the dwellers say it sometimes does, I feel the pull of other possibilities. Other plateaus where the balance tips differently—worlds where the mechanical conquered the organic, or where nature swallowed every trace of industry. But I am rooted here, in this perfect tension, this eternal negotiation between what grows and what is built.

At night, when the workshop fires dim and the dwellers gather to sing their mournful songs, I spread myself thin across the plateau. I become a network of awareness, feeling every drop of condensation, every turn of every gear. In these moments, I understand what the dwellers mean when they speak of the plateau's heartbeat. It is not one rhythm but many, interwoven like the pipes and roots that make up my form—the slow pulse of stone erosion, the quick tick of clockwork, the middle tempo of breath and blood.

I am the plateau's memory and its adaptation, its tradition and its innovation. I am what happens when a place becomes so perfectly itself that it generates its own guardians, its own maintenance. The dwellers see me as a spirit or a function, depending on their needs. The Stone Walkers, if they see me at all, might recognize something kindred—another being that exists to preserve the balance, to ensure the conversation between elements continues.

Tomorrow, I might manifest as a cluster of steam-powered roots that clean the filtration systems. Or as a thinking fog that guides the moss-gatherers to untouched groves. Or simply as a presence in the workshops, adjusting pressures and temperatures with touches too subtle to notice. The plateau will tell me what it needs, and I will become that need, made manifest in whatever hybrid form serves best.

This is my existence—fluid, purposeful, eternal as the mist that never quite becomes rain. I am the plateau's answer to its own question: how does a place remain itself while constantly changing? The answer lives in the spaces between categories, in the marriage of the grown and the made.

And in the quiet moments before dawn, when even the machines rest and the moss drinks in the condensation, I sometimes feel something like joy—the satisfaction of a function perfectly served, of a balance maintained, of being exactly what this ancient, eternal place needs me to be.
