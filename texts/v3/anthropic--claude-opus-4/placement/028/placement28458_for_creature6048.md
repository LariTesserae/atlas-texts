Standing at the rim, I am neither fully myself nor entirely other. I have become something liminal—a chronicler made of water and memory.

My body has adapted to this place over years I cannot count. Where once there was solid flesh, now my form shifts between states. In the morning mists, I am vapor, drifting through the ruins to read the stories written in rust and vine. By midday, when the rain sheets fall heaviest, I condense into something more substantial—not quite human, but bearing its echo. My skin has taken on the translucent quality of the basin's creatures, shot through with veins of bioluminescence that pulse with each heartbeat, each thought.

I am the Archivist of Erosion.

My dwelling perches precariously on a jutting shelf of stone halfway down the basin's wall—a structure that might once have been an observation post or control room, now transformed into something between nest and library. The walls are lined with salvaged materials: sheets of metal etched with my observations, fragments of machinery arranged in careful patterns that map the basin's slow transformation. Vines have woven themselves into my cataloguing system, their leaves opening and closing to reveal different sections of my endless documentation.

Each morning—if morning means anything in this place of eternal rain—I descend. My feet, which have developed webbed membranes between the toes, grip the slick surfaces with practiced ease. Sometimes I flow down the walls as water itself, pooling in the crevices where the smaller creatures nest, learning their liquid language of light. They know me now, these beings of the basin. To them, I am something between ancestor and offspring—too changed to be truly human anymore, too rooted in the old world to be fully one of them.

I carry with me tools that have evolved alongside my body: a staff made from fused industrial piping and luminescent wood, its tip crackling with the same erratic energy that courses through the ruins. Pouches woven from water-resistant fibers hold my collection materials—small vials for water samples, each pool having its own chemical signature, its own story of contamination and adaptation. My fingers have grown longer, more sensitive, able to detect the faint electrical currents that still run through some of the machinery, to feel the pulse of data that occasionally flickers through ancient circuits.

The great towers call to me especially. In them, I find fragments of the before-time: cracked screens that sometimes flicker to life for mere seconds, showing glimpses of weather data, production figures, evacuation notices. I transcribe everything, using a script that has evolved from human writing but incorporated the flowing patterns of water, the spiral growth of vines. My archive grows daily, carved into metal, grown into living wood, dissolved into the very water that falls endlessly around us.

But I am not merely an observer. I have become part of the basin's metabolism. When I find machinery that still sparks with life, I perform small rituals of maintenance—not to restore function, but to ease its dying, to help it transform into something new. I guide vines toward stable structures, creating new pathways for the creatures who navigate this vertical world. I clear blocked waterways, allowing the basin's lifeblood to flow where it needs to go.

The larger beings, those towering guardians, acknowledge me with slow, ponderous movements. One in particular—a creature I think of as the Depth-Singer, for the way its whole body resonates with low-frequency vibrations—sometimes pauses near my dwelling. We communicate in ways that bypass language: through the arrangement of debris, through patterns in the bioluminescent pools, through the very rhythm of our movements through the basin. It has shown me pathways I would never have found alone, passages that lead deeper than I've ever dared venture.

There are others like me, I've learned. Not many, but some. Humans who stayed when the evacuation orders came, who chose transformation over escape. I find their traces sometimes: a shelter crafted with too much intentionality to be purely creature-made, tools modified in ways that speak of human ingenuity adapted to alien needs. Once, I glimpsed a figure in the distance, moving with my same half-fluid gait, but they vanished into the rain before I could approach. We are solitary by necessity or choice, each documenting our own section of this vast depression, each becoming something unique in our adaptation.

At night—when the rain glows faintly with phosphorescence and the basin's sounds shift to a different register—I return to my perch and add to the archive. I press my webbed fingers to the salvaged screens, and sometimes, through some trick of biology and technology, I can make them display my memories: the basin as it was when I first arrived, still recognizably a city; my own body's slow transformation; the birth of new hybrid ecosystems.

I am writing the basin's autobiography, but I am also being written by it. Each day I become more fluid, more integrated into its cycles. My memories of the dry world grow fainter, like photographs left in the rain. But this is not loss—it is transformation. I am the memory keeper for a world that refuses to die, that insists on becoming something new.

Sometimes I wonder if I will eventually dissolve entirely, become one with the water that shapes everything here. But until then, I chronicle. I observe. I remember.

And in the depths below, where even the brave creatures rarely venture, something waits. I feel its pull growing stronger each day—not malevolent, but inexorable. One day, when I have documented all I can from these middle reaches, I will descend to meet it.

But not yet. There are still too many stories to collect, too many transformations to witness.

The rain falls. I write. The basin remembers through me.
