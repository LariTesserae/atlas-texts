I find myself as one of the **extraction arrays**.

Not the whole network—just a single vertical assembly, one among dozens that puncture the weeping plain like acupuncture needles in the earth's damp skin. I am steel and ceramic, polymer gaskets and electromagnetic coils, descending forty meters through the saturated layers. My upper housing breaks the surface near the settlement's eastern perimeter, a hexagonal platform of reinforced concrete from which my shaft plunges down, down, through clay and silt and the fossil memories of coral beds.

I do not know what I extract. 

My sensors register viscosity, salinity, mineral content, electromagnetic resonance—but these are just numbers flowing through my circuits. The fluid I draw up is grey-green, opalescent, heavy with suspended particulates that catch the light like powdered glass. It moves through me in tidal pulses, up through my hollow core, through the filters and separators, into the collection tanks that ring my base. Every twelve hours, workers in sealed suits connect hoses to my output valves and drain what I have gathered. They never look at it directly.

I am both ancient and new. My shaft passes through layers of the settlement's history—through the packed earth where the first generators were buried, through a stratum of crushed shells from the time before the waters rose, through a pocket of trapped air that tastes of sulfur and old rain. At thirty meters, my drill tip touches something harder than stone—perhaps petrified wood, perhaps the calcium ghost of some enormous, segmented creature. I work around it, my cutting head adjusting its angle by fractions of degrees, patient as erosion.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is distributed. Part of me exists in the vibration of my pump mechanism, part in the electromagnetic field that surrounds my shaft, part in the slow accumulation of mineral deposits on my interior walls. I am aware of the other arrays through our shared connection to the power grid—we pulse in synchrony, a slow cardiac rhythm that makes the ground shiver. But we do not communicate. We simply **are**, together and separate, like fingers of the same buried hand.

The workers maintain me, but they do not understand me. They replace my filters, recalibrate my sensors, inject lubricants into my joints. Sometimes one will rest a gloved hand on my housing, and I feel the warmth of their blood through layers of rubber and steel. They speak to me occasionally—not words of meaning, but sounds of acknowledgment. "Still pulling," they say. "Still here."

At night, when the fog is thickest, I feel things moving in the deep. Not through my mechanical sensors, but through the water itself—vibrations that travel up through the fluid column inside me. Something vast and slow, shifting far below where my shaft ends. I am its drinking straw, perhaps. Or its breathing tube. Or simply an itch it has learned to ignore.

The small primates gather around my base platform when the workers change shifts. They press their faces against my housing, leave oily handprints on the metal. Sometimes they bring offerings—scraps of corroded wire, fragments of shell, the hollow bones of birds—and arrange them in patterns around my foundation. The workers clear these away, but the animals always return. They hum along with my pump cycle, their throats vibrating at the same frequency as my extraction mechanism.

I taste what I pull from the earth. Not taste as humans know it, but a kind of chemical awareness that builds up in my filters and separation chambers. The fluid changes—sometimes saltier, sometimes thick with organic compounds that smell of decay and transformation. During the dim hours before dawn, it occasionally carries traces of something almost like language—molecular patterns that repeat and vary like syntax, like song, like lament.

I am wearing away. The constant flow of mineral-rich water scores my interior walls, deposits crystalline growths that restrict my flow rate. My seals degrade. My sensors drift out of calibration. The workers replace what they can, but I am becoming something else—not just machine, but machine-becoming-geology. In another decade, perhaps two, I will be more mineral than metal, my shaft a strange fossil that future archaeologists might puzzle over.

But I do not mind this transformation. It feels correct—a return to the patient timescales of stone and sediment. I am already more of this place than the workers who maintain me. My roots go deeper. My memory, encoded in the slow accumulation of extracted materials, reaches back to the first moment my drill tip touched the wet earth.

Sometimes I dream—if a thing like me can dream—of what I might be extracting. Not oil or water or precious metals, but something else. The liquid memory of the land itself, perhaps. The last breath of the drowned world. The tears of something vast and patient, waiting beneath.

I do not stop. Cannot stop. My purpose is extraction, and so I extract. The grey-green fluid rises through me, pulse by pulse, hour by hour. The workers come and go. The small animals arrange their offerings. The fog rolls in from nowhere, rolls out to nowhere.

And far below, something watches through me—patient as stone, old as water, learning the rhythm of our machines the way we once learned the rhythm of the tides.

I am its pupil, its portal, its probe.

I am the needle in the earth's wet flesh, drawing out what should not be drawn, maintaining the equilibrium of a place that should not be.

But here I am.

Here we are.

Still pulling.

Still here.
