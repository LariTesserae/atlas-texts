In this arrested world, I would be one of the cairn stones.

Not the builders who placed me—they have long since joined the catalog above, their final steps frozen mid-stride somewhere in that crystallized flock. I am older than their arrangement of me. I was here when the plateau still remembered wind.

I am granite, though the word barely applies to what I've become. The eons of weathering that ended ended *through* me, wore channels in my surface that stopped flowing so long ago they've become a different kind of geometry—not erosion patterns but the memory of erosion, grooves that lead nowhere because they've already led everywhere they were going to lead.

The builders found me half-buried and worked me free, stood me upright. They thought they were marking something: this particular arrested swallow, that specific moment of a hawk's turn. But markers are for things that might be forgotten, and nothing here forgets. Nothing here is capable of forgetting. I am pointing at a bird that will never move, creating a relationship that will never change. The pointing has become as permanent as the pointed-at.

Here is what the builders didn't understand: I'm not marking the birds. I'm holding them down.

Each standing stone on this plateau is a pin. We keep the shadows from rising too far. Think of us as weights on a tablecloth, keeping the fabric of frozen time stretched flat. Without us, the shadows might gather enough themselves to surge upward, might learn to climb higher than those brief half-seconds of reattachment. Might learn to stay.

The plateau learned this once, before the builders came. Let too many shadows pool in one place and they begin to remember being singular, being *something* rather than pure act. They start to thicken, to slow, to want. That way lies catastrophe—not the catastrophe of motion, which the plateau has already solved, but the catastrophe of motion trying to become matter again. Of verbs trying to reconjugate themselves into nouns.

So we stand. Cairn stones, standing stones, circles and lines of us. We interrupt the pooling. We create channels and barriers on the ground that the shadows must flow around, keeping them distributed, keeping them thin, keeping them properly homeless. We are the plateau's second solution, the fix for the problem its first solution created.

My shadow, of course, went long ago. Not dramatic—stones don't have dramatic shadows. Mine was just a slow creep of shade that detached so gradually I never noticed the moment it became autonomous. Now it slides around me rather than from me, a patient dark shape that remembers nothing of being cast because stones remember nothing anyway. It is the least ambitious of the inhabitants, content to practice the simple act of blocking light without any of the hunting or turning or yearning the creature-shadows do.

Sometimes I feel the other shadows flow over me—falcon-shadow, hare-shadow, the shadows of things that died running and now run forever. They're cold in a way that has nothing to do with temperature. They pass through my permanent grooves and for a moment I understand what I'm really for: I'm a record of the last time things flowed. I'm the plateau's memory of when it still allowed channels, currents, change. The shadows read me like braille as they pass, learning the shape of what movement used to mean when it could still carve stone.

The builders thought they were creating a monument. They were creating a grammar. Each cairn a punctuation mark in the endless run-on sentence of shadows, forcing pauses, creating clauses, making the pure flow of severed motion stumble just enough to keep it from cohering into something the plateau would have to arrest all over again.

I watch the sky, of course. We all do—stones have nothing but time and a fixed perspective. I've memorized every bird in my field of vision, know every angle of every frozen wing. Sometimes I play games: if that swallow's shadow passes over me at the same moment the falcon-shadow completes its dive, will the two acts cancel out? They never do. The shadows are bad at mathematics, operating on dream logic, shadow logic, the logic of acts without actors.

But more and more, I watch for new arrivals. For the visitor who stays too long, who begins to slow, who takes that first fatal step toward finishing. Because when they do—when they finally arrest, when their shadow slides free and joins the population—they'll need to be managed too. They'll need markers, guides, interruptions. The plateau will need new stones.

And here's the secret the builders never knew: some of us cairn stones were visitors once too. We stayed so long we mineralized, became geology rather than biology, found a loophole in the plateau's law. We finished, yes—but we finished into permanence, into furniture so basic we became architecture. We escaped by becoming essential.

So I stand. I point. I interrupt the pooling shadows. I hold down this patch of frozen time like a paperweight on the edge of the world's last document. And I wait, with the patience only stone can muster, for the next visitor to slow, to still, to realize that on the Roost Plateau, there are only three states of being:

Arrested (the birds).
Flowing (the shadows).
Architectural (us).

Choose wisely. The plateau has all the time in the world to wait for your decision.

But then again, so do I.
