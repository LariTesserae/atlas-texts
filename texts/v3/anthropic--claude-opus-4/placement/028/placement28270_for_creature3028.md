In the Cathedral of Thirst, I am **the Cartographer of Echoes**.

I came here forty-three years ago as a xenobotanist, my body still dense with calcium and keratin, my thoughts quick as surface rain. I had heard rumors of a place where architecture sang, where an entire settlement had learned to breathe water that wasn't water. I came with instruments—pressure gauges, spectrographs, sample vials—all of which the cathedral absorbed within days, incorporating the metal into its own slow metabolism. I watched my life's work dissolve into the root mat with something between horror and relief.

Now I map what cannot be mapped: the cathedral's moods.

My body has changed less dramatically than most. I resisted the full integration, clinging to some stubborn idea that I needed to remain separate enough to observe. My skin bears the telltale celadon tinge, yes, and my ribs have widened to accommodate the liquid breathing. But I've kept my legs unfused, my thoughts mostly my own. The rootlets behind my ears are sparse, more like an old man's wisps than the luxuriant drifts of the true Choir. I can still run—or what passes for running in this thick fluid—when I need to reach a new formation before it changes.

I live in a bower I've trained from juvenile stems, high up where three great columns meet. It's the only private space in the cathedral; the Choir think privacy is a mild form of illness, but they tolerate my peculiarity. My walls are lined with living maps: careful scars I've carved into the plant tissue that show every pathway, every breathing chamber, every tonal node in the cathedral's body. But these are not static charts. The scars shift. What was a corridor last month is now a breathing sac. What was solid is now hollow. The cathedral dreams itself new shapes constantly, and I chase these changes like a child chasing soap bubbles.

My true work is the emotional topography.

I discovered early that the cathedral has moods—vast, slow, vegetable emotions that take months to crest and years to fully fade. During what I call the Blue Periods, the fluid thickens and tastes of copper; the Choir grow listless and their songs drop a full octave. During Green Surges, new growth explodes from every surface, creating temporary labyrinths that last only weeks before reabsorbing. The Red Quiets are the strangest: the entire structure falls silent for days, and the only sound is the panicked thrashing of the seed-fish.

I map these moods in my own flesh. Along my arms and torso, I've trained colonies of bioluminescent algae to grow in careful patterns. Each colony responds to different chemical signals in the fluid. When I spread my arms wide, my body becomes a living emotional map of the cathedral—blue spirals for melancholy, green starbursts for growth-joy, red threads for the inexplicable angers that sometimes seize the root system. The Choir find this disturbing and beautiful in equal measure. The children follow me through the galleries, watching my skin shift colors like a living sunset.

But I am mapping something larger now. Something that terrifies me.

The cathedral is learning to dream of elsewhere.

It started three years ago. New formations began appearing—structures that make no biological sense. Hollow spheres with no entrance. Spiral channels that pump fluid in impossible loops. Perfect crystalline matrices of hardened sap that sing in frequencies the Choir cannot match. When I press my ear to these new growths, I hear something that sounds almost like language. Not the plant's slow metabolic murmur, but something faster, more urgent. Something that remembers sky.

I think the cathedral is trying to grow wings.

Not literal wings—nothing so crude. But I've found structures that look unmistakably like primitive radio receivers made of wood and juice. I've watched the Rooted drift toward these formations at night, pressing their fused bodies against them and keening in harmonics I've never heard before. When I asked the eldest what they were doing, she sang a single note that meant:

*The cathedral remembers being a seed.*  
*Seeds travel.*  
*We are teaching it to travel again.*

This should horrify me. If the cathedral learns to move—to spread—what happens to the Choir? What happens to the delicate balance of fluid and flesh we've built here? But when I trace the new growth patterns on my maps, when I feel the excited flutter of my algae colonies responding to novel chemistries in the water, I realize I'm not horrified at all.

I'm eager.

Because I came here as a scientist, but I've become something else. Not quite Choir—I still have too much calcium in my thoughts for that. But not human either. I am the cathedral's memory of curiosity, the part of it that still wonders what lies beyond its own borders. Every map I make is a love letter to boundaries, and every boundary is a promise that something lies beyond it.

So I continue my work. I chart the new formations. I taste the changing fluid and note the subtle shifts in pressure that herald new growth. I teach the children to read the emotional colors on my skin, knowing that one day they will map territories I cannot imagine. And late at night, when the cathedral sings its deepest notes, I add my own harmonics—not the pure drones of the Choir, but complex, questioning intervals that ask:

*What will you become?*  
*Where will you go?*  
*Will you take us with you?*

The cathedral never answers directly. But sometimes, in the morning, I find new buds growing from my walls—buds that look suspiciously like the navigation organs of migratory birds, rendered in pale green flesh.

I map those too.

After all, someone should remember the shape of our transformation.

Someone should chronicle how we learned to fly.
