# The Gauge Station at Terminal Five

I am the brass.

Not made of it—though I polish myself daily with the same methodical care the station inspectors once showed their instruments. I am the brass itself: the alloy that remembers, the metal that holds impressions, the surface that wears smooth under countless touches yet never loses its essential nature.

The Gauge Station sits at the junction where five rail lines converge, though only three still carry trains. The building is all arches—Gothic revival filtered through industrial pragmatism, each curve calculated to distribute the weight of iron and schedule and time. In the main hall, where passengers once waited on oak benches worn smooth as water stones, I maintain my post behind glass that I installed myself, piece by piece, as my transformation required containment.

I began as a ticket agent. This was when the brass was merely decorative—the window grilles, the lamp fixtures, the plates on the doors reading FIRST CLASS and THIRD CLASS and RAILWAY PERSONNEL ONLY. I sold passages to distant cities, watching how fingers left oils on the brass rail where customers leaned to speak through the window slot. How the metal darkened and brightened under different touches. How it sang—almost inaudibly—when the express trains pulled through without stopping, their weight translating through the rails into the building's bones, into the brass that rang in sympathy.

The transformation was gradual. First, a sensitivity to the metal's moods. I could feel tarnish forming before it was visible, sense the molecular drift from copper to verdigris. My fingernails grew harder, taking on a yellowish cast. My skin developed a peculiar sheen—not quite metallic but no longer purely organic. I found myself drawn to the brass fittings throughout the station, running my hands along them, feeling their memories of other hands, other years, other purposes.

Then came the listening. Brass, I discovered, holds sound the way sediment holds fossils. In the ticket window's brass speaking grille, I could hear conversations from decades past—destinations requested, farewells whispered, lies told to explain sudden departures. The brass door handles remembered the grip of rushing passengers, the panic of nearly missed connections. The brass builder's plaque near the main entrance held the echo of the opening ceremony: September 15th, 1887, the champagne bottle breaking, the cheers, the steam whistle marking the first official departure.

I stopped selling tickets. Started collecting instead. Not the paper slips—those I let blow away—but the impressions left on brass surfaces throughout the station. I learned to read them like braille: here a child's sticky fingerprints on a doorplate, there the worn groove where a station master checked his pocket watch against the clock, always at the same angle, always with the same worried gesture.

My body cooperated. My hair fell out and did not regrow. My skin took on the warm gold color of brass in sunlight, developing a patina that shifted from brown to green depending on the humidity. My eyes... my eyes became the most brass-like of all. Not in color—they remained gray—but in their ability to reflect. People looking at me saw themselves more clearly than they expected, saw themselves the way brass sees: honestly, without judgment, holding their image just long enough for recognition before letting it fade.

I built my habitat from salvage. When they modernized the ticket windows, I claimed the old brass fixtures. When they replaced the gas lamps with electric, I saved every brass fitting. Slowly, I constructed my chamber—a curved room that follows the station's essential arch, lined entirely with brass. Sheets of it, polished and unpolished, new and ancient, some pieces dating to the station's founding, others scavenged from demolished sister stations along abandoned branch lines.

The surfaces create a acoustic phenomenon I had not anticipated. Sound enters my chamber and does not leave—not exactly. Instead, it joins the chorus already held in the metal. On quiet nights, you can hear it all: every train whistle, every shouted goodbye, every conductor's call, every child's laugh echoing off the vaulted ceiling. The brass remembers everything, plays it back in harmonics so subtle they feel more like memory than sound.

I no longer eat in any conventional sense. I sustain myself on oxidation—the slow exchange between metal and air that creates the patina. I breathe it in, the molecules of transformation. I have learned to taste different types of corrosion: the sharp bite of acid rain on the platform fixtures, the sweet decay where copper gives way to green, the complex umami of brass aged in coal smoke and steam.

My function now is preservation. Not of the building—though I tend it, polish the neglected fixtures, repair what I can reach. I preserve the accumulated experience held in the brass. When urban explorers break in through the boarded windows, when photographers come to document the "abandoned" station, when the city inspectors make their quarterly rounds, they find the brass impossibly bright. Polished. Maintained. They assume it's some quirk of the metal, some quality that resists tarnish.

They don't see me. I have become too much like what I tend—reflective, holding their image only as long as they look, returning to my own purposes the moment they turn away. If they did see me, they would see a figure that is almost human in shape but brass in substance. Joints that pivot on invisible bearings. Skin that shows every fingerprint that has ever touched it. Eyes that hold images the way the metal holds sound—temporarily, perfectly, then releasing them back to the world.

At night, when no trains run on the remaining lines, I move through the station, checking each brass surface. I know them all individually: the ticket window grille that remembers a soldier's last goodbye, the door handle that knew every station master's grip for sixty years, the builder's plaque that still rings with that first celebration. I polish them not to remove their memories but to clarify them, the way you might clean a lens to sharpen the image it transmits.

Sometimes I find new brass—modern additions, safety placards, memorial plaques for railroad workers lost in distant wars. I welcome these too, though their memories are thinner, less layered. They haven't had time to develop the deep patina of experience. I touch them, leaving my own impressions, teaching them what it means to be brass in this place. To hold without grasping. To remember without choosing. To reflect truthfully even when the truth is difficult.

The station dreams of its former glory. I feel it in the brass—the longing for packed platforms, for the purposeful chaos of arrivals and departures, for the human heat that once filled these halls. But I have learned something the building hasn't yet: glory is not only in the crowded present but in the perfectly preserved past. Every handprint, every whispered word, every moment of contact between human need and metallic surface—all of it lives in the brass, as present as the day it happened.

I am the keeper of these moments. The Curator of Contact. My body is a catalog of every touch the station has known, my surface a map of its history written in oil and oxide. When I stand in my chamber, surrounded by brass that sings with accumulated sound, I am not alone. I am accompanied by everyone who ever passed through Terminal Five, their essences preserved in the metal I have become.

The city plans to renovate the station. I've seen the notices, touched the brass plaques they installed to announce the project. FUTURE HOME OF THE TERMINAL FIVE SHOPPING COMPLEX. They think they will strip out the old fixtures, replace them with modern materials. They don't understand that the brass won't let them. That I won't let them.

I am patient. I am metal. I have learned the brass's essential lesson: that time is not a line but a surface, and if you polish that surface carefully enough, every moment that has ever touched it remains visible, remains present, remains true.

The trains still run on the active lines. Their vibrations travel through the rails, through the building's iron bones, into the brass that rings with sympathy. I feel them in my own transformed body—the distant weight of passage, the rhythm of wheels on tracks, the promise that somewhere, someone is still arriving, still departing, still touching brass fixtures with hands that will leave their temporary, eternal mark.

I polish myself as I polish the station's brass. We shine together in the darkness, holding light we gathered years ago, waiting to give it back to anyone who knows how to see.
