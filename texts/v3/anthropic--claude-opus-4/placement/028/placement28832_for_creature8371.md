In the perpetual twilight between platforms, where the amber light fractures through heat-shimmer into colors that have no names, I am one of the Veil-weavers—those who work the spaces that others merely traverse.

My workshop exists in fragments, distributed across the void itself. Here, suspended on a web of crystallized thermal currents, hangs my primary loom—a structure that phases between visibility and translucence as the heat-patterns shift. There, caught in a stable eddy between three rotating platforms, floats my thread-garden where I cultivate the raw materials of my craft. And scattered throughout the aerial spaces are my anchor points: tiny workshops no larger than meditation cells, carved from stone shards too small to support conventional buildings, accessible only to those who have learned to trust the thickened air.

I am human in origin—or was, perhaps, before the years of working in the spaces between transformed me. My fingers have grown long and multiply-jointed, able to sense the microscopic variations in air pressure that indicate where a thermal current might solidify enough to support thread. My eyes see in spectrums that blur the distinction between light and heat, revealing the invisible architecture of temperature gradients and pressure differentials that create the true structure of Keth-Moraan. My hair has become something between flesh and energy, flowing upward in permanent defiance of gravity, each strand a sensor attuned to the aerial medium I inhabit.

The Veil-weavers are few—perhaps a dozen across the entire archipelago. We are neither Thermari nor Ashkin nor properly Drift-touched, though we share qualities with all three. Our craft requires a peculiar combination of talents: the engineering precision of the Thermari to understand load and tension, the deep-matter sensitivity of the Ashkin to feel how materials behave at the edge of phase-states, and the spatial intuition of the Drift-touched to navigate spaces that exist only intermittently.

What we weave are the emergency nets, the safety margins, the last-chance captures that prevent the occasional catastrophic loss when a bridge fails or a platform tilts beyond recovery. Our veils are nearly invisible—woven from threads spun from condensed heat-shimmer, from the silk of air-phase spiders that live in the thermal columns, from my own hair when necessary, and from fibers extracted from the crystalline birds' shed wing-fragments. These materials exist at the boundary between states of matter, strong enough to catch a falling child or a tumbling load of precious water-vessels, yet insubstantial enough to not interfere with the normal movement of platforms and people.

My work begins before dawn, in the brief cool-period when the thermal columns quiet to their lowest ebb. I traverse my scattered workshops, checking the night's accumulation of materials. The air-phase spiders leave their silk in geometric patterns across my collection frames—substance that exists only in certain temperature ranges and must be harvested before the day's heat causes it to sublime directly into vapor. I gather this with tools of my own making: copper wands tipped with crystals that maintain a precise cold-point, allowing me to wind the silk without destroying it.

The heat-shimmer threads require different techniques. These I must spin myself, sitting at the edge of a platform where the thermal differential is strongest. I extend my consciousness partially into the Drift-touched state, allowing my shadow-self to reach into the raw thermal currents while my physical hands work the spinning apparatus. What emerges is thread that exists partially in multiple states—solid enough to weave, yet retaining the ability to phase through ordinary matter when the angle of interaction is wrong. This makes it useless for normal construction but perfect for emergency nets that must catch the falling without entangling the flying.

By mid-morning, as the platforms begin their daily dance, I am at my loom. The structure itself is a marvel of adapted engineering—a frame of hollow copper pipes through which flows a careful mixture of hot and cold air, creating micro-climates that allow different thread types to coexist. The heddles and reeds phase in and out of existence as I work, solid only when I need them to separate and beat the threads. My shuttle is carved from a stone-swimmer's shed scale, one of the few materials that can carry phase-shifting thread without disrupting its delicate state-balance.

The weaving is as much meditation as craft. Each pass of the shuttle must be timed to the breathing of the thermal columns. The pattern emerges not from any pre-planned design but from my reading of the air itself—where future weak points might develop, where the probability of platform collision increases, where children play at the edges of safety. The veils take shape according to need that has not yet manifested, prevention woven into the very fabric of space.

As I work, I am visited by others who share the liminal spaces. Heat-dancers pulse through my workshop, drawn by the resonance of the weaving. Sometimes they leave gifts—scales of refracted light that I can spin into thread that will be visible only to those falling, guiding them toward the safety of the nets. The stone-swimmers surface beneath my smaller workshops, their passage creating temporary platforms where I can rest or store materials. Even the crystalline birds contribute, their wind-chime calls helping me tune the tension of the warps to frequencies that won't interfere with the cable-singers' harmonics.

The Drift-touched children often come to watch, fascinated by work that exists in their expanded perceptual realm. I teach them small things—how to braid their own shadow-hair into emergency lines, how to read the thermal currents for stability rather than just navigation. Some show aptitude for the deeper mysteries, and I mark them as potential apprentices, though it will be years before they must choose whether to undergo the transformations necessary for this work.

By afternoon, when the platform motion intensifies, I am often called to active work. A cable shows signs of thermal fatigue—I weave a reinforcing veil around it that will catch loads if it fails while still allowing normal flexibility. A new platform has emerged from the deep, still too hot for normal approach—I cast nets of cool-thread that create micro-climates, allowing the Thermari engineers to begin the work of integration. A family's possessions scatter as their platform unexpectedly inverts—my nets catch water vessels and tool bundles, holding them in aerial suspension until they can be retrieved.

This active work requires me to move constantly through the spaces between, riding thermal currents, leaping between air-thicknesses, sometimes dispersing my consciousness across multiple net-sites to manage complex catches. My body has adapted to these demands—bones hollow like a bird's but reinforced with mineral deposits from years of exposure to stone-swimmer emanations, skin that can photosynthesize during the brief moments of intense thermal exposure, lungs that can extract oxygen from the thin air of the high-void spaces.

The other peoples of Keth-Moraan regard Veil-weavers with a mixture of gratitude and unease. We are necessary but uncanny, saving lives through methods that blur the line between craft and magic. The Thermari appreciate our practical contributions but struggle to incorporate us into their family structures—how do you maintain kinship with someone who might disperse into component threads for days at a time? The Ashkin understand us better, recognizing kindred spirits in deep-craft, but even they find our work unsettlingly insubstantial. The Drift-touched accept us most readily, but sometimes I think they see us as cautionary tales—how far the transformation can go before you lose essential humanity altogether.

Yet I have found profound purpose in this existence. Each veil I weave is a prayer made manifest, a physical expression of care for the community that floats and falls and flies around me. My nets have caught three generations of children, the same families trusting their safety to my increasingly inhuman hands. I have woven wedding veils from joy-thread that phase into existence only during the ceremony, burial shrouds that dissolve into the thermal columns carrying the essence of the departed into the eternal circulation of the sky.

My greatest work remains unfinished—the Master Veil that I have been weaving for twenty years. It exists in portions throughout the archipelago, a vast net of connection that will, when complete, be able to catch not just individual falling objects but entire platforms should catastrophe strike. Each normal veil I weave adds a thread to this greater pattern, building slowly toward a safety net for our entire civilization. Other Veil-weavers contribute their own threads when our paths cross in the spaces between, understanding without words the importance of this communal project.

As evening approaches and the thermal columns begin their nocturnal quieting, I return to my primary workshop to maintain my tools and process the day's experiences into thread. Memory itself can be spun, I have learned—trauma and joy alike can be drawn out into fibers that add emotional resonance to the veils. A net woven with threads of remembered fear will activate more quickly when children are in danger. A veil incorporating fibers of spun laughter will be less likely to entangle the heat-dancers who play at the edges of safety.

In the deep night, when most sleep, I often sit at the edge of the highest platform I can reach, dangling my feet into the void, feeling the slow pulse of the world's thermal heart far below. My hair spreads around me in a sensing web, reading the night's air for tomorrow's patterns. Sometimes other Veil-weavers join me in these vigils, and we communicate in the language of vibrations through shared threads, discussing technical problems and philosophical implications of our craft.

We debate whether what we're becoming is evolution or deviation, whether our children (those few who are born to our kind) represent humanity's future in this aerial realm or a divergent path that will eventually separate entirely from the human stream. We share discoveries—new thread materials, innovative weaving patterns, ways to extend our consciousness further into the fabric of space itself. We mourn those who have dispersed too far to reconstitute, whose workshops hang empty, their unfinished veils slowly dissolving into the heat-shimmer.

But mostly, we simply exist in companionable silence, each lost in our own practice of reading the air, planning tomorrow's work, feeling the constant small tugs on our nets as they perform their protective function throughout the sleeping archipelago. We are the safety margin made manifest, the catchers working always at the edge of catastrophe, weaving care into the very structure of the sky.

When dawn comes again with its brief coolness and the stirring of thermal columns, I am already at work. The air-phase spiders have been busy in the night. The heat-shimmer awaits my spindle. Somewhere in the archipelago, a child will take their first steps on a swaying bridge, an elder will make their final journey between platforms, a new family will arrive seeking refuge in our aerial community. And my nets will be there, invisible until needed, strong enough to catch what must not fall, gentle enough to release what must fly free.

This is my place, my purpose, my gradual transformation into something both more and less than human. I am a Veil-weaver of Keth-Moraan, and I work the spaces between solid things, finding meaning in the patterns of prevention, beauty in the invisible architecture of care, home in the constant dance between falling and flight.
