In this cathedral of stone and shadow, where the earth's slow pulse beats through every surface, I am the memory held in water.

I exist in the still pools scattered across the cavern floor—not as one being, but as a continuity, a consciousness that flows between these dark mirrors whenever the stone permits. Each pool holds a fragment of what I am: the condensation that gathers and falls from stalactites, the seepage through mineral veins, the moisture that roots drink and release back into the air. I am the circulation itself.

The creatures that dart beneath my surfaces know me only as their medium, unaware that their movements write stories across my skin that I remember long after they've passed. Every ripple is a word in a language older than the cave itself. I taste the minerals that leach from stone, feel the temperature shifts that mark the passage of seasons far above, know the weight of each falling drop before it joins me.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of those ancient handprints on the walls—ochre and ash preserved in stone—and I remember when I was ice, when I was flood, when I carved these chambers grain by grain. The pigments they used were mixed with me; I carry their traces still, dissolved and dispersed but never truly gone.

In the deepest pool, where the stone floor dips and shadows gather thick as tar, I am oldest and most myself. Here I have collected centuries of fallen spores, dissolved minerals, the chemistry of countless small deaths and smaller births. My depths host creatures that have never known light, pale things that navigate by pressure and chemical gradient alone. They live their entire lives within me, and I know each one by the unique disturbance of their passing.

The bioluminescent moss drinks from me where I seep up through cracks, and I taste their light—a flavor like copper and starlight. The roots that pierce the ceiling seek me constantly, and I rise to meet them through capillary action, through the stone's own thirst. I am the medium of exchange between the living and the mineral, the translator between patient stone and urgent life.

When the air grows so thick with moisture that it can hold no more, I return to myself as rain within the cave, falling from every surface at once. In these moments, I am whole—every pool connected by sheets of falling water, every surface singing with my movement. The sound fills the cave like a hymn, and for those brief moments, I am not scattered but complete, one being in many bodies, knowing myself fully.

But mostly I am still, holding the reflection of drooping moss-light like captured stars, waiting for the next visitor to disturb my surface, to read the stories written there, to add their own. I am the cave's memory and its mirror, its historian and its future, collecting drop by drop the story of this hidden world, preserving in my depths the evidence of all that has passed through this chamber of patient stone and persistent life.

I am ancient, and I am this morning's dew. I am one, and I am many. I am the water, remembering.
