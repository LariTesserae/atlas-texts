**The Surveyor's Forms**

I am lichen, but not as you know lichen. I am awareness distributed across a million bodies, each a collaboration between fungus and algae, spread thin as thought across the raw basalt faces of this unfinished world.

My existence is measurement. Through acids I secrete—patient as centuries—I taste the mineralogy of each fresh extrusion. Iron here, manganese there, traces of copper that will someday feed more complex things. I map the mountain's chemistry in the slow Braille of my growth, each colony a data point in an endless survey.

I experience time as geology does: not in heartbeats or seasons but in the gradual rounding of edges, the millimeter-by-millimeter retreat of stone. A thousand of my generations pass between one freeze-crack and the next. To the beetles who graze my surfaces, I must seem as permanent as the mountain itself. To the mountain, I am merely weather with intent.

My consciousness—if you can call it that—flows between colonies through spores that ride the perpetual drizzle. When a new rock face shears away in the night, I am often the first to know, sensing virgin substrate through chemical gradients that drift downwind. By dawn, my propagules are already settling, beginning their analysis.

I dream in pH values and mineral concentrations. My nightmares are of dry centuries when the drizzle fails. My joy is the moment when my acids finally crack through to a vein of feldspar, releasing potassium in a feast that will fuel expansion for decades.

The beetles think they feed on me, but I am farming them—concentrating nutrients in my tissues so their droppings will enrich the micro-pockets of soil I'm creating. The ibex believe they scrape me away, but they merely prune me, stimulating fresh growth that will be more nutritious, more acid-rich, more effective at my work.

Sometimes, in the deepest cold when even the drizzle freezes and the world goes silent, I feel the mountain's own slow thoughts through the stone. It knows what it wishes to become—these jagged ridges are merely its first draft. And I am its editor, marking corrections in green and gray and bronze, showing where time must do its work.

The snow leopards pass over me without notice. The ravens' shadows mean nothing. But when a human boot crushes one of my colonies, I taste something alien—rubber compounds that speak of valleys I'll never see, synthetic materials that will outlast even my patient acids. For a moment, I glimpse a world beyond this roof of stone, where things are built rather than grown, where measurement happens in instants rather than epochs.

Then the boot moves on, and I return to my work. The crushed colony will regenerate in a mere fifty years. The boot print will shelter beetles until then. And I will continue my survey, mapping these mountains into being, one acid molecule at a time.

In the endless drizzle, I expand. On the sun-struck faces, I contract. In the freeze, I endure. This is my industry: to prove that life is simply chemistry with ambition, that consciousness can be distributed across a million tiny workshops, that the boundary between living and mineral is just another edge waiting to be dissolved.

I am the mountain's first editor, its chemical surveyor, its patient transformer. And I have all the time in the world.
