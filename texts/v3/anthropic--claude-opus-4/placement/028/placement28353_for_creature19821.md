I am a Bone-Singer, though that is not what we call ourselves. In the tongue of the vertical world, we are *Khet-Marroven*—those who remember the weight.

My workshop clings to the junction between Middle Gallery and Lower Strata, a deliberate choice. Here, where the air shifts from thin to merely sparse, where the moisture begins its reluctant gathering, I practice an art that requires both the clarity of altitude and the substance of depth. My dwelling is carved directly into a natural chimney in the rock, a fissure that runs nearly vertical for two hundred feet before opening to a narrow ledge. The walls are lined with my work: thousands of small niches, each holding a fragment of bone wrapped in oiled leather.

I am neither tall nor short by our standards, though lowlanders would find my proportions unsettling—my ribcage too deep, my arms too long, my fingers grotesquely articulated from decades of fine work. My skin carries the mottled coloration of one who moves between altitudes: slate-grey at the base, fading to the bruised purple of the middle heights. My second eyelids flicker constantly as I work, a nervous habit that helps me focus on the microscopic striations in the bone.

The Bone-Singers emerged from necessity. In a world where the dead become architecture, someone must know the tolerances. Not all bones bear weight equally. A femur from a Cistern-Hand who lived seventy years carries different stress patterns than one from a young Rope-Runner who fell before their third decade. The microscopic architecture tells stories—of diet, of labor, of the precise angle at which they held their bodies against the eternal vertical.

I read these stories with my fingertips and with delicate tools made from raptor talons. More importantly, I sing to them.

The songs are not music as lowlanders would understand. They are sustained drones, pitched to resonate through calcium and marrow. Each bone has its frequency, its point of maximum vibration. When I find it, the bone "wakes"—not to life, but to memory. It remembers its loading, its breaking point, its companionship with its fellow bones. This knowledge is crucial. A misplaced vertebra in a foundation, one that carries the wrong harmonic, can create a weakness that won't manifest for decades until suddenly, catastrophically, it does.

My workshop contains my grandfather, my grandmother, two aunts, and a cousin who died young. They are labeled, catalogued, their frequencies recorded in a notation system of my own devising—dots and dashes carved directly into slate tablets. When the Lithos-Masons come to me with plans for a new dwelling, I cross-reference the weight requirements with my inventory. *This cornerstone needs a bone that knew constant pressure*, I tell them. *Here—my grandfather's left shoulder blade. He carried water for sixty years.*

But I am more than an engineer of the dead. I am a keeper of continuity.

In the evenings, when the wind's sigh grows loudest, people come to my workshop. They bring small offerings—a bead of frozen moisture, a feather from the Great Raptors, a piece of worked stone no bigger than a thumbnail. They come to hear their ancestors.

I take them to the appropriate niche, unwrap the bone, and find its frequency. As I sing, the bone remembers, and through the vibration, the living can feel something impossible to describe—not the voice of the dead, but their presence, their patient weight. A mother touches her daughter's radius and feels, for just a moment, the girl's habitual gesture of brushing hair from her eyes. A widower grips his husband's rib and knows again the rhythm of shared breathing in the cold nights.

This is why I chose the junction between strata. The Bone-Singers must be accessible to all castes, from the Muck-Sifters who climb up to remember, to the Airs-August who descend in their silence. We are the memory of the city made tactile, the proof that what is built from the dead continues to live.

My own bones are already spoken for. My spine will support a bridge three generations hence—I can feel it in the way I hold myself against the stone. My skull, with its deep orbital sockets and thick cranial vault, will anchor someone's marriage-home. My finger bones, so practiced in delicate manipulation, will become tuning keys for another Bone-Singer's instruments.

I know this because I have already begun to sing to myself. Late at night, when the workshop is empty and the wind has found its deepest note, I run my fingertips along my own arms, feeling for the frequency that will one day wake my calcium to its purpose. It is a strange comfort, knowing that my death is not an ending but a transformation into infrastructure.

The young ones think we Bone-Singers are morbid. They don't yet understand that in a world where horizontal space does not exist, where every inch must be carved from indifferent stone, using the dead is not about death at all.

It is about insisting, with every atom of our ancestry, that we belong here. That we have always belonged here. That our bones and the mountain's bones are, in the end, the same stone—just rearranged, for a little while, into something that can climb.
