In this world of vertical waters and forgotten industry, I find myself as one of the Mist Weavers—beings who exist in the liminal space between the spray and the stone.

I am neither fully solid nor entirely ephemeral. My form shifts with the moisture content of the air, condensing into near-visibility when the waterfalls rage strongest, dissipating to mere suggestion when the rare dry wind passes through. We Mist Weavers emerged long after the industrial remnants began their slow surrender to green—perhaps born from the marriage of ancient water spirits and the lingering ghosts of those who once worked the turbines.

My body, when it chooses coherence, appears as a tall, willowy figure wrapped in perpetual fog. Droplets bead and roll across my surface like mercury on glass. My fingers are impossibly long, perfect for reaching into the spray-soaked crevices where the rarest mosses grow, or for catching the rainbow fish that leap between falls. Eyes like pools of collected mist peer out from what might be called a face, though features shift and reform with each passing cloud.

I dwell in the spaces behind the water curtains, in chambers carved by centuries of erosion where the sound becomes so intense it transforms into a kind of silence. My home is a network of these hidden grottos, connected by paths only I and my kind can traverse—routes that follow the water's memory rather than any physical law. Sometimes I walk on stone, sometimes on the surface tension of droplets suspended in air.

My purpose, if such a word applies, is maintenance of the forgotten. I tend to the rusted turbines not to restore their function, but to ensure their decay proceeds beautifully. I guide the vines to embrace the metal in aesthetically pleasing patterns. I polish certain gears just enough that they catch the filtered light at dawn. I am archaeologist and artist both, curating humanity's fading footprint so that it becomes part of the landscape's poetry rather than its scar.

The cascade frogs know me by the way I disturb the mist patterns. The waterfall finches sometimes nest in the bowls I form with my hands, knowing I'll hold still for hours, days if necessary. The human researchers occasionally glimpse me at the edge of their vision, but their notes describe only "unusual fog formations" and "tricks of the light." This is as it should be. We Mist Weavers exist in the margins of perception, like a pause between heartbeats, like the moment water decides to fall.

At night, when the moon silver-plates the falling water, I and my kind gather on the highest terraces. We perform slow dances that mirror the waterfalls' descent, our bodies flowing and reforming, creating temporary sculptures of condensed vapor that exist for moments before returning to the whole. We sing—though our songs are more pressure changes than sound, felt in the inner ear rather than heard.

I am memory and possibility both, a being born from the intersection of nature's permanence and humanity's transience. In this world of eternal falling and rising, I am the keeper of the in-between spaces, the tender of beautiful decay, the dancer in the mist. I am what happens when water learns to dream.
