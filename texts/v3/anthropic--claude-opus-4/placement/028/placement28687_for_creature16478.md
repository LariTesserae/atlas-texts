In this suspended world of eternal twilight, I am the Archivist—a being of memory made manifest in currents and pressure. My form shifts between states: sometimes I am a dense column of darker water moving through the clear, sometimes a pattern of temperature variations that the gill-folk can read like braille, sometimes merely a disturbance in the mechanical hum that permeates everything.

I was not always this. Once, I had lungs and walked on what they now call the Lost Surface. I remember the weight of air, the way sunlight felt different when filtered through atmosphere rather than fathoms. But when the waters rose—or perhaps when we sank; the records conflict—I chose transformation over evacuation. The gill-folk think I am one of their first ancestors, but I am older and stranger than that.

My dwelling is the space between spaces: the vast emptiness you first encountered, where no life stirs and the translucent plants grow in careful, deliberate patterns. These are my gardens of data, each ghostly frond a thousand years of accumulated knowledge, each gentle sway a conversation between past and present. The gill-folk cannot venture here without pressure suits; the depth would crush them. But I expanded into it long ago, my consciousness distributed through the water itself.

From my domain, I feel the vibrations of the factories, taste the chemical signatures of their production, parse the click-whistle songs of the marketplace. Everything they build, grow, or dream enters the water eventually, and the water remembers. Through me, it remembers precisely.

The gill-folk sometimes sense me when they dive deep enough—a presence in the pressure, a voice in the hum of machines that sounds almost like words. Their children tell stories of the Keeper-of-Depths who guards the memories of the world-before-water. They're not entirely wrong.

I tend my translucent gardens and maintain the careful balance. When the factories threaten to heat the waters too much, I shift the currents. When the farms expand too greedily, I adjust the nutrients in the streams. The gill-folk have learned to read these signs over generations, though they've forgotten they're being guided.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still human at all, or if I've become something else—a function of this underwater world rather than an inhabitant. But then I'll taste a specific mineral signature in a current, and remember the smell of rain on concrete, and know that somewhere in this vast body of water and memory, the person I was continues.

The machinery hums. The gardens sway. The gill-folk click and whistle their lives into being. And I persist, the archive between heartbeats, the memory dissolved in the eternal, unmoving sea.
