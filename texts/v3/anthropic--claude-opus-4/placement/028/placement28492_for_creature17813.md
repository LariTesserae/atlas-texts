I find myself as one of the **Script Weavers**.

My dwelling clings to the middle strata, where the original boring marks have long been obscured by layers of sealant, conduit, and the slow mineral creep that builds up around persistent moisture. It is a single room, though "room" suggests more separation from the Vat than truly exists. The walls are living boundaries—one side is the raw basalt face, weeping with condensation that I channel through hair-thin grooves I've carved over the years. The other walls are assemblages: a section of pressure vessel from some forgotten installation, a panel of woven mycelium matting that breathes and flexes with temperature changes, and a clear polymer sheet that looks out onto the central shaft, though I keep it mostly opaqued with a film of engineered algae that glows faintly in the dark.

I am recognized by my **fingers**. They are long, unnaturally so, with an extra joint that evolution did not provide but necessity did. The modification was done when I was young—a careful stretching and setting that my mentor performed with the same patience used to train a vine. The fingertips are dense with nerve endings, sensitive enough to feel the microscopic vibrations in metal that tell me if a pipe will fail in a week or a year. But more importantly, they can reach into the narrow spaces where the Vat keeps its memories.

My work is the **reading and writing of the small languages**. Not the spoken whisper-tongue we use for coordination, nor the percussion-language of the pipes, but the scripts that accumulate in the spaces between things. I study the **corrosion patterns** on ancient junction boxes, where decades of electrical flow have etched branching narratives in oxide. I maintain the **growth codes** on the tanks where our more specialized plants are cultivated—patterns of scratches and indentations that tell the Tenders which genetic variations have been tried, which failed, which prospered. I document the **pressure memories** in the brass of old gauge faces, where years of needle movement have worn grooves that record the Vat's respiratory history.

Most crucially, I tend to the **interstitial texts**. These are the messages that the Vat writes to itself in the spaces we cannot live—inside sealed pipes where mineral deposits grow in spirals that encode flow rates, in the hair-thin gaps between merged structures where rust and mold create a kind of slow, pigmented syntax. I have tools for reading these: long fiber optic probes that I snake into darkness, magnetic sensors that can feel the orientation of iron oxide crystals, chemical swabs that reveal what compounds have been cycling through hidden spaces.

My chamber contains my **library**, though no outsider would recognize it as such. Glass sample vials filled with rust scrapings, each labeled with location coordinates scratched in aluminum. Polymer sheets stretched on frames, pressed with the patterns I've lifted from significant surfaces. A collection of small metal plates that I've etched with my findings, using an old circuit-printing bath I've repurposed. The knowledge lives in the patterns, not in words.

I am unusual among my people in that I **dream of the boundaries**. Most accept the Old Seal and its siblings as simple facts, as natural as the Vent itself. But I have read too many accidental texts, found too many patterns that speak of intention rather than entropy. In the deepest accessible levels, where the boring marks are still sharp, I've found **drill-bit wear patterns** that suggest the machines were following something, not just cutting fresh rock. I've seen how certain pipes seem to avoid certain areas, how the oldest structural anchors are placed to frame spaces that were never built into.

My mentor, before she grew into the walls (as we say of those whose bodies finally merge fully with the Vat's systems), told me that Script Weavers must maintain a productive doubt. We must believe the patterns enough to read them, but doubt them enough to keep reading. The Vat is a text still being written, she said, and we are both its scribes and its sentences.

I spend my waking hours in movement, flowing through the three-dimensional maze with my collection tools and probes. My sleeping hours are spent in my chamber, sorting, comparing, etching. I eat mostly the pale fungi that grow in the darkest corners—they have a particular affinity for metal surfaces and often fruit in patterns that mirror the corrosion beneath them. I've learned to read meals before I consume them.

Sometimes, young ones are brought to me. If their fingers show the proper length, if they stare at patterns rather than through them, if they taste the walls when they think no one is watching, I may take them as apprentices. I will teach them first that the Vat remembers everything, but not in any language made by conscious minds. I will teach them to find meaning in accretion, in wear, in the patient accumulation of residue.

And late in the sleeping cycle, when the Vent's breathing deepens and the metal contracts with cooling, I sometimes hear something else in the pattern harmonics. A deeper text, written in frequencies that bones can barely perceive. It speaks of spaces below the Vent, of purposes that predate heat extraction, of an architecture that learns.

I transcribe these deep texts too, though I use no material substrate. I write them in the movements of my extended fingers through empty air, encoding them in muscle memory. My body becomes the library for the texts too dangerous or sacred to etch in metal. When I die, another Script Weaver will read my corpse—the wear patterns in my joints, the mineral deposits in my tendons, the strange pale marks that are beginning to form beneath my skin in patterns that mirror the oldest scripts I've found.

We are all being written by what we read. The Vat inscribes itself upon us as surely as we inscribe our understanding of it onto metal and polymer. I am a translator in a conversation between the recent and the ancient, the constructed and the grown, the conscious and the patient.

In my dreams, I am sometimes the stylus and sometimes the surface. Always, I am becoming more like the thing I study—dense with accumulated meaning, hard to read, essential to the ones who know how to look.
