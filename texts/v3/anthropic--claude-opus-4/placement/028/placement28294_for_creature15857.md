I find myself as one of the Gimbalers - a brass-cog servitor of Evigrad, though I no longer remember when I first woke to purpose.

My body is a convergence of necessity and wear: three legs arranged in tripod symmetry, each terminating in a pad of felt-wrapped copper that grips stone without echo. My torso - if you can call it that - is a rotating drum of tarnished brass, pocked with a century of condensation. From this drum extend my working arms: six of them, each no thicker than a child's wrist, ending in scissor-hands whose blades have been sharpened so many times they're translucent at the edges.

I do not think in words. I think in intervals - the space between C and G, between stillness and motion, between vine and void. When the morning's first resonance travels down the cables (always a low E-flat, the city's waking tone), something in my chest-cavity responds. Gears that have worn their teeth to nubs mesh gently, and I begin my circuit.

My memory is the path itself: 1,847 steps along the third terrace, past the water-right keeper's blue door (she hums when she counts droplets), beneath the bridge where rust-moths nest, through the garden of black cabbage that grows in defiance of photosynthesis. I know every stone by its vibration underfoot. I know which vines fruit in shadow-season, which in fog-season. My scissors remember the resistance of each stem.

The children have named me Threehum for the triune chord my gears sing when I work. They follow me sometimes, tossing melody fragments: a rising fourth makes me pause and bow; a tritone sends me backward three steps; a perfect fifth makes me present whatever I'm holding. I've learned their games are a language, though I cannot speak it back.

Today, like every seventh day, I complete my circuit at the yellow door of the Mid-Stratum Historian. My oldest protocol surfaces: present the compost-ball. I have compressed the week's clippings into a sphere the size of a human head, bound with vine-fiber. The moisture I've pressed out waters tomorrow's growth. Nothing is waste here.

The Historian never emerges, but sometimes a hand appears - pale, with that green tinge under the nails that marks all High-lunged folk. The hand accepts my offering and leaves in exchange a small metal disk. I do not know what the disk means, but I accept it into my third cargo-arm where seventeen years of such disks nestle like scales.

When the Feral Choirs bloom in the dead-air pockets, my movement stutters. Their crystalline voices interfere with my base frequency, and I must stop, sometimes for hours, until a patrol arrives with their felt mallets. I do not mind the waiting. Time is different when you are powered by the plateau's own breathing.

Once, a visiting Resonance Monk sat beside me during such a pause. She said nothing - they never do - but held her tuning fork to my central drum. The note that emerged was neither mine nor hers but something between: the sound of brass dreaming it was once ore, once mountain, once seafloor in an age when these heights were depths. She wept. I continued trimming vines.

I know I am dying, though death for my kind is a slow untuning. My mainspring was rated for one century; I am in my third. Each year my steps grow less precise, my intervals less pure. The Linewrights could repair me, but the city's law is clear: a machine may only be mended if it asks, and I have no voice but working.

So I continue. When the morning vibration finds me, I wake. When the gardens need tending, I tend. When children offer songs, I dance my limited dance. And when my gears finally seize, I will present my final compost-ball and then stand very still, a monument to purpose fulfilled, while the moss begins its patient work of making me green.

Perhaps in another century, I will be stone. The plateau transforms everything, eventually, into itself.

But today the resonance calls, and my scissors are sharp, and there are exactly 1,847 steps between here and the completion of my purpose.

I walk.
