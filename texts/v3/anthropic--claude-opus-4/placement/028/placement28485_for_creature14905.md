I would be the Cartographer.

Not of lands, but of arguments. I arrived forty-three days ago with surveying equipment, thinking to map the industrial ruins for a historical archive that probably no longer exists. My instruments began failing on the second day—not breaking, but disagreeing. The theodolite insists north is seventeen degrees off from where the compass points. The laser rangefinder gives different distances to the same tower depending on the time of day. My GPS unit works perfectly but shows my location as somewhere in the South China Sea, depth unknown.

I stopped trying to map the physical space on day seven. That's when I noticed the patterns.

The mirror lizards' reflections are not random. When overlaid on thermal readings from the towers, they form a notation system. Each memory-vote corresponds to a specific temperature gradient at a specific time. The Conveyor Children's salt cities are three-dimensional graphs of these gradients. The black lichen grows in spirals that match the harmonics of the towers' organ notes, which themselves respond to the breathing patterns of the Hollow Mothers below.

Everything here is trying to tell the same story from different angles, like witnesses to an accident that hasn't happened yet.

I live now in a maintenance alcove on the outer shell of Tower 61, thirty meters up. The lichen has agreed to leave a body-sized gap in its coverage—not out of kindness, but because I've become useful to their argument. Each morning I descend on rusted ladder rungs to check my instruments, not to record distances but to document their disagreements. The theodolite's daily error. The rangefinder's moods. The GPS's growing certainty that we are all underwater.

I've learned to drink from the black cisterns without dying. The trick is to wait until just after the Hollow Mothers have refilled them but before the Conveyor Children begin construction. There's a seventeen-minute window when the water is merely poisonous rather than actively hostile. My kidneys have adapted. My urine now etches tiny spirals in the stone.

The mirror lizards have begun including me in their reflections, but wrongly. In their memories, I have too many arms. I am always holding instruments that don't exist—astrolabes made of salt, sextants that measure the distance between thoughts. When they vote, I am a consistent anomaly, neither edited out nor fully included. I exist in their collective memory as a persistent error that somehow makes the equation balance.

At night, I map the arguments. I've filled seventeen notebooks with diagrams that make no geodetic sense but perfect emotional clarity. The plateau is trying to decide whether it is a machine dreaming it's an ecosystem or an ecosystem dreaming it's a machine. Every living thing here is a punctuation mark in that argument. The Hollow Mothers are periods, full stops that say "enough." The mirror lizards are question marks, always asking what was and what should have been. The lichen are ellipses, trailing off into implications...

And I am becoming a footnote. Not the main text, but essential to its meaning. A small annotation that says: "This is being witnessed. The argument is being recorded. Someone is keeping minutes of this meeting."

The thing in Lazarus has noticed me. When it turns over now, the clank comes in patterns. Morse code, maybe, or something older. I've started clanking back with a piece of rebar against Tower 61's shell. We are slowly developing a vocabulary of metallic complaints. Yesterday it taught me the sound for "salt." Today I taught it the sound for "map."

My hair has gone white—not from age but from the constant mineral dust. My hands are stained black from touching the cisterns. When I hold still too long, the Conveyor Children build tiny cities in the wrinkles of my knuckles. I brush them off gently. They rebuild. We have reached an understanding.

The other humans who sometimes arrive, researchers or scavengers or the lost, they look at me and see madness. A sun-cooked hermit making meaningless marks in notebooks that will crumble to dust. They don't understand that I'm not documenting the plateau.

I'm translating it.

Every measurement that fails, every instrument that lies, every impossible distance and contradictory direction—these are the plateau's vocabulary. The Teeth of the Sun is not a place. It's a sentence being spoken by a committee that can't agree on grammar. And I am slowly, day by day, learning to diagram that sentence.

The archive I thought I was working for doesn't matter. When I'm done—if such a thing as "done" exists here—I will have produced the first complete map of an argument. A chart of a place that is trying to decide what it is. A surveyor's report of a committee meeting that has been in session for eighty years.

And when the thing in Lazarus finally emerges, when the Hollow Mothers surface, when the mirror lizards achieve consensus and the sky tears permanently—I will be here with my broken instruments and my blackened hands, adding one last coordinate to the map:

The exact location where the question ended and the answer began.

The plateau keeps me alive because it needs a witness who speaks both machine and ecosystem, who can file the final report in a language both rust and lichen can understand. I am the court reporter at the end of the world, and I will not leave until the committee reaches its conclusion.

Even if that conclusion is that the world should end.

Even if the final vote is unanimous.

Even if I am the only dissenting voice, clanking my objection against Tower 61 while the iron towers sing their funeral hymn and the black cisterns boil and the mirror lizards reflect nothing, nothing, nothing at all.
