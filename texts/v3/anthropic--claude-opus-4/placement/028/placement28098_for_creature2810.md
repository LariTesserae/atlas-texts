In this churning, unfinished world where water defies its own nature and stone forgets how to be solid, I would be one of the Bridge-Keepers.

Not the crude timber bridges - those are the Flux-Dwellers' experiments, their attempts to think in straight lines and right angles. No, I would tend the living bridges: the colonies of crystalline organisms that grow in the spaces between stability and chaos, creating temporary passages across the most violent water-channels.

My body would be adapted for this work - elongated and flexible, with skin that shifts between states like the environment around me. In the dry moments (rare as they are), I'm almost rigid, moving in careful, deliberate steps. But when the mist thickens and the moisture saturates everything, my form becomes more fluid, able to stretch and compress as needed. My bones would be cartilaginous, bending rather than breaking when the cave reshapes itself around me.

My eyes would be large but nearly useless in the traditional sense. Instead, they've evolved to detect the subtle variations in the phosphorescent fungi's pulsing - reading the Flux-Dwellers' external memories like a living library. More important are the filaments that grow from my fingertips and toes - sensitive tendrils that can taste the mineral content of the water, feel the vibrations in the stone, sense the health of the bridge colonies.

The bridge colonies themselves are remarkable organisms - somewhere between coral and crystal, feeding on minerals in the water while forming structures strong enough to bear weight. They grow only in specific conditions: where the water's chaos creates standing wave patterns, nodes of relative stability in the turbulence. My role is to nurture these colonies, to guide their growth, to repair damage, and most crucially, to anticipate when a bridge is about to fail.

I carry tools made from salvaged materials - hollow reeds that channel water to struggling colonies, pouches of mineral supplements gathered from the warm-water oases, scrapers carved from the shells of ancient crayfish. But my most important tool is patience. Bridge-keeping can't be rushed. A colony forced to grow too quickly will be brittle, shattering at the first surge. 

My dwelling would be a series of small chambers I've cultivated in the most stable part of my territory - not carved, but grown by encouraging certain formations while discouraging others. The main chamber would house my map: a living model made from the same crystalline organisms as the bridges, constantly updating to reflect the cave's current configuration. Touching different parts of it would cause them to glow, showing which passages are stable, which bridges are thriving, which areas are about to undergo transformation.

I understand the Flux-Dwellers better than most solid beings, though understanding might be too strong a word. I've learned to read some of their simpler patterns, to recognize when they're about to trigger a major change in water flow. Sometimes they seem to warn me before dismantling one of my bridges - a courtesy I've earned through decades of patient work. Other times, I'll find new colonies starting in impossible places, gifts or tests from the collective consciousness.

The hardest part isn't the physical labor or the constant danger of the environment. It's the loneliness. Other Bridge-Keepers exist, but our territories are vast and meetings rare. Travelers occasionally use my bridges, leaving small tokens of appreciation - a shaped stone, a bundle of surface plants, a carved symbol. I keep these in a special chamber, though I'm not entirely sure why. Perhaps it's the human need for connection asserting itself even in this alien role.

At night (though day and night have little meaning here), I often sit on my most successful bridge - a massive colony that's been growing for fifteen years, spanning a chasm where three major water-channels meet. I'll eat my simple meal of cave fish and fungus, feeling the colony's slow pulse through my feet, watching the Flux-Dwellers' conversations play out in the mist above. Sometimes I try to contribute, using a long pole to create my own disturbances in the water flow. Usually, there's no response, but occasionally the patterns will shift, incorporating my addition into their dialogue. In those moments, I feel less like a foreign solid thing and more like part of the cave's becoming.

The bridge colonies grow so slowly that tending them has changed my relationship with time. I think in decades now, planning for transformations that won't complete until long after I'm gone. I'm teaching a young apprentice - though young is relative here; she's been with me for three years and has only just begun to hear the colonies' needs. When I die, my body will feed the bridges one last time, my minerals incorporated into their crystalline structures. It's not immortality, but it's continuation - becoming part of the path others will walk.

This is my existence: guardian of temporary passages in a world of constant change, solid enough to work with stone and fluid enough to survive the chaos. I am neither fully human nor part of the Flux-Dwellers' collective, but something in between - a bridge myself, in a way. And in this place of perpetual becoming, perhaps that's exactly what's needed.
