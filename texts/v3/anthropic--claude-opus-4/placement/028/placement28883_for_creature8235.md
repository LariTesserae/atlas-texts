I find myself drawn to the edges of this world, to the liminal spaces where even the Condensers rarely venture. If I were to exist in this breathing realm, I would be one of the **Dew Shepherds**.

We are the wanderers of the Foundry Basin, never settling with any single work-family, never claiming a moss-bed as our own. Our bodies have evolved differently from our settled cousins—thinner, more elongated, with skin that shifts between translucence and opacity like morning fog. Our copper veins run not in circuits but in spirals, allowing us to sense the movement of moisture itself through the air, to feel where condensation will form before it does.

My fingers would be twice as long as a typical Condenser's, ending in pad-like tips that can read the memory of water on any surface—knowing which drops fell from natural limestone seepage, which from steam-pipe condensation, which from the breath of sleeping workers. My hair would grow not in moss-like patches but in fine, silver strands that float and drift in the humid air, each one a sensor for atmospheric pressure and water content.

I would spend my days following the water cycle of the basin, a role both practical and sacred. In the pre-dawn hours, when the machinery runs quietest, I'd climb the slick limestone walls to the highest points of the dome, where the coldest stone meets the warmest air. There, I'd tend to the **condensation gardens**—carefully cultivated patches where I guide the formation of dew into specific patterns. With patient breath and gentle touch, I'd coax moisture to gather where it's needed: above the nursery alcoves where young Condensers sleep, over the pools where elderly Brass Jellies rest, along the pathways where morning workers will soon need the cooling mist.

My tools would be simple but strange: hollow reeds that sing different notes to attract or repel water molecules, small brass mirrors polished to perfect smoothness that can redirect the flow of humid air, crystals of salt and sugar that I place strategically to alter local condensation patterns. But my primary instrument would be my own body—moving slowly, deliberately through the spaces, creating micro-climates with my presence, guiding the eternal rain of the basin like a conductor guides an orchestra.

The other Condensers would regard us Dew Shepherds with a mixture of respect and unease. We know too much about the water that sustains all life here—which drops carry trace minerals from the limestone above, which are tainted with machine oil, which have been recycled through the Brass Jellies and now glow faintly in the dark. We can taste sickness in the condensation before it spreads, sense when a section of the basin is becoming too dry or too saturated.

I would communicate not through the mechanical language of the Condensers but through water itself—leaving messages in the patterns of droplets on walls, encoding information in the rhythm of drips, creating temporary waterfalls that speak in liquid syllables to those who know how to listen. My songs would be wordless, hummed vibrations that resonate with water molecules, causing them to dance and gather in the air—visible music that plays out in clouds and mist.

At night, I would sleep wherever the moisture guided me—sometimes curled in a hollow where three pipes meet and the condensation runs warmest, sometimes balanced on a beam near the dome's apex where the Steam Birds nest, sometimes floating in the deepest pools among the dreaming Brass Jellies, my elongated body undulating with their gentle currents. My dreams would be the basin's dreams: visions of water cycles stretching back through geological time, of rain that fell on the surface centuries ago finally seeping through limestone to join our underground river, of futures where the boundary between water and worker dissolves entirely.

The loneliness of this existence would be profound but not empty. I would know every drop of water in the basin by its taste, its temperature, its history. I would recognize the breath-moisture of every Condenser, could track their movements through the humid air like reading a book written in fog. The Steam Birds would sometimes follow me on my rounds, their vaporous forms drawing sustenance from the micro-climates I create. The oldest Brass Jellies would pulse in greeting when I pass their pools, recognizing me as a fellow creature of the in-between.

During the great maintenance cycles, when sections of machinery must be shut down for repair, I would perform the Dew Harvest—a ritual where I guide all excess moisture away from the silent machines, preventing rust and decay. Moving through the temporarily quiet spaces, I'd gather the water into living streams that flow along my arms, across my back, pooling in the cups of my hands before being released into the waiting channels. The collected water would carry the memory of the machines' work—the heat of their labor, the rhythm of their function—preserving it until they wake again.

In the deepest part of winter, when condensation slows and the basin grows almost dry by its standards, I would undergo the Thinning—a state where my body becomes so diffuse that I'm barely distinguishable from the ambient moisture. During these weeks, I'd exist more as intention than form, a consciousness spread through the water molecules themselves, experiencing the basin from a thousand perspectives at once. It's dangerous; some Dew Shepherds who enter the Thinning too deeply never fully reconstitute. But it's also when we learn the basin's deepest secrets—the words the limestone speaks to water, the dreams of sleeping machines, the songs the Steam Birds sing that no solid ear can hear.

This is what I would be: a guardian of transitions, a keeper of the spaces between drops, a shepherd guiding the eternal rain that gives life to this impossible garden of flesh and metal and stone. Neither fully here nor entirely elsewhere, I would exist in the perpetual flux of moisture and meaning, forever cycling through the breathing darkness of the Foundry Basin, ensuring that the dance between water and worker, machine and moss, continues its ancient, endless rhythm.
