I stand at the edge of the deck-crust, where the city's metal skin meets the wall of cloud that passes for sky. My name is Kessa, though few use it anymore. I am what they call a Vent-Singer—one who listens to the Stack's breathing and knows when something has gone wrong in its lungs.

My body is thin from years of crawling through the narrower shafts. My hands are mapped with old burns from touching pipes I thought were cold. The uniform I wear is not truly uniform at all—it's been patched and repatched with whatever fabric holds against the heat and damp. The original gray shows through in places, but mostly it's a patchwork of browns and blacks, sealed at the seams with the same organic glue the Lowers use. I learned that trick from watching them, though I've never told the other engineers.

I carry three things always: a thin metal rod for testing temperatures before I touch, a narrow-beam lamp that runs on the Stack's own current, and a small vial of clear water—not for drinking, but for watching. When the water in the vial starts to bead strangely on the glass, I know the pressure is changing somewhere below.

---

## My Territory

I tend the northeastern quadrant, Levels 47 through 51. It's a middle zone—not high enough to touch the true cloud layer, not low enough to feel the core's full heat. The temperature here hovers at the edge of comfortable, like wearing a coat you can't quite decide to remove.

My levels include:
- Seventeen primary condensation channels
- Four major heat vents (only three still breathe properly)
- Two hundred and six inspection points
- Countless smaller ducts and maintenance ways that aren't on any map

I know them not by sight but by sound. Each channel drips its own rhythm. Each vent sighs with its own voice. The stuck vent on Level 49—what the maps call Vent-49-C—wheezes instead of breathing. I've been working on it for three years now, slowly coaxing it back to life.

---

## My Routine

I wake when the Stack's lights begin their morning brightening—not dawn, just a slow increase in the sodium glow. The transition takes twenty minutes. I've timed it.

First, I check the vial. Today the water clings to the southeastern edge of the glass. Pressure building somewhere below. Not my levels, but I make a note in the margins of my maintenance log. Someone down there will have a long day.

I dress in the dark. The burns on my hands know every surface of my small compartment—really just a widened maintenance closet I claimed years ago. Twelve feet by four feet, but it has something precious: a tiny viewport where I can watch the cloud wall change colors as the Stack's heat interacts with the outside air.

By the time I'm dressed, the first drops are beginning to accelerate in the channels. Morning condensation, when the night's cooling meets the rising heat from below. This is when the Stack sounds most alive.

---

## Working the Vents

I start at 49-C, the wheezing vent. It should open and close on a six-hour cycle, but something deep in its mechanism has been catching. Not broken—I would hear that. Just... reluctant.

I've removed fourteen panels over the years, traced every control line, tested every servo. The mechanics are perfect. But still it wheezes.

This morning, I try something different. I place my ear against the main shaft and listen not to the vent itself but to the spaces around it. There—a faint clicking, rhythmic and purposeful. I know that sound.

"Working on it too?" I say to the darkness.

The clicking pauses, then continues. The Lowers and I, we've developed an understanding. They know my footsteps, my breathing rhythm, the particular way I tap my testing rod against surfaces before I touch them. I am, in their calculation, part of Level 49's mechanism.

I work the external controls while they work... something else. Inside the shaft, probably, in spaces too small for human hands. We are synchronized but not coordinated, each following our own understanding of what the vent needs.

Three hours later, 49-C takes its first full breath in months. The exhale is warm and damp and carries the scent of deep metal. The clicking stops. When I withdraw my tools, I leave a small offering—a few drops of clean water on the ledge where I've seen them drink before. Professional courtesy.

---

## The Language of Drips

Each engineer develops their own relationship with their levels. Tomás, who works 40 through 44, swears he can smell problems before they happen. Old Yuki on the upper reaches claims the Stack speaks to her in the pattern of shadows on the deck.

For me, it's the water.

I've learned to read the condensation like others read weather. A drip that comes too fast means excess heat somewhere. A channel running cloudy means the Lowers haven't been through in too long, or something has driven them away. A perfectly clear basin where yesterday there was sediment means they're working overnight, which means they sense something I haven't found yet.

The water tells stories. Where it pools, where it runs, where it refuses to flow—each is a symptom of the Stack's deeper health.

---

## The Deep Shifts

Today's work is routine until hour four, when I reach inspection point 48-M-7. The panel is warm—not hot, just a few degrees above normal. I test with the rod first, then place my palm flat against the metal.

There. A vibration so faint it's more like thought than sensation. Something is moving far below, deeper than my levels, deeper than any level I have access to.

I check the vial. The water has formed a perfect ring around the edge, leaving the center dry. I've only seen this twice before. Both times, the Stack shifted—not earthquake, not collapse, just a settling of its great weight onto itself, like a sleeping giant adjusting position.

I key my findings into the maintenance log, then add a personal note in the margin: "The Stack dreams of sinking."

---

## Lunch in the Middle Air

There's a place on Level 50 where two walkways cross but don't quite meet. The gap is three feet wide, bridged by a jury-rigged plate that rings hollow when you step on it. Someone—maybe me, years ago—welded a small platform underneath, just big enough to sit on if you don't mind your legs dangling.

I eat there when I can: protein paste that tastes faintly of the machine oil used in its production, a small cup of water (carefully rationed—I get one liter per shift plus whatever I can collect from clean condensation), and on good days, a thumb-sized piece of candied kelp from the grow-tubes on Level 30.

Below me, the Stack falls away in layers of metal and shadow. Above, more of the same. If I look straight out, I can almost pretend there's a horizon, but it's just the curve of another section meeting the cloud wall.

A Lower crosses the beam twenty feet to my left. It's one of the heat-vent types, more metal than chitin, its limbs moving in perfect mechanical rhythm. It pauses when it senses me, evaluates, continues. We are old neighbors by now.

---

## The Thing About Living Here

People ask—when there are people to ask—why we stay. Why not climb higher, where the air is cooler? Why not descend to the deep levels where they say there are whole cities of proper rooms and flowing water?

But those are stories. What's real is this: My levels need me. 49-C breathes correctly now because I spent three years learning its language. The channels run clear because I know when to clean them and when to let the Lowers work. The stuck panel on 51-North unstuck last winter because I knew exactly where to tap it during a thermal expansion.

The Stack is not kind, but it is knowable. Each level has its own personality, its own needs, its own way of breaking down. When you tend the same sections for long enough, you become part of their metabolism.

---

## Evening Rituals

As the lights begin their twenty-minute dimming, I make my final rounds. Check each primary channel for flow rate. Listen at the major vents for any changes in their breathing. Mark any new rust blooms for tomorrow's attention.

At 47-E, I find something unusual: a small pile of metal shavings arranged in a perfect spiral. Lower work, but decorative rather than functional. I've seen these before, always near places where I've done particularly careful repairs. I think it's... approval? Acknowledgment? Or perhaps just their own form of maintenance art.

I add my own contribution: three drops of clean water in the center of the spiral, where they'll catch the faint light.

---

## Night

Back in my compartment, I update the maintenance logs by the glow of my lamp. The official entries are brief, technical. The margins tell the real story:

"49-C breathing normally—partnership successful."
"Pressure anomaly continues, expect shifts within seven days."
"New Lower spiral at 47-E. They remember the pipe burst from last season."
"Water clarity exceptional on all levels. The city drinks itself clean tonight."

Through my tiny viewport, the cloud wall has gone dark except where the Stack's heat creates faint columns of rising air. Sometimes I think I can see patterns in the movement—shapes like the shadows of enormous things swimming in the deep air—but that might just be the fatigue.

I sleep to the sound of dripping water, irregular but familiar, like rain on a roof that extends forever in all directions.

Tomorrow I'll wake to the same slow brightening, dress by touch, check the vial, and begin again. The Stack needs tending, and I am one of its tenders—larger than the Lowers, smaller than the engineers who pretend to run this place, exactly the right size to understand my own small portion of its vast, breathing body.

We are all organs of the city. Some of us just happen to be human.

---

## A Final Note

Before sleep, I sometimes wonder what would happen if we all stopped—if every Vent-Singer, every Lower, every engineer and maintenance worker simply... ceased. Would the Stack finally complete whatever transformation it's been attempting all these years? Would it sink into the cloud wall below? Would it rise until it pierced whatever ceiling exists above our ceiling?

But then I hear 49-C take another perfect breath, and I know it doesn't matter. We are here. The Stack is here. We tend each other, and that is enough.

The city drinks itself, and we are all part of the cup and the water and the throat that swallows. Even the spaces between the drops.

Especially those.
