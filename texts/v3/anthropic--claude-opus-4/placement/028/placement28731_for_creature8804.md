I would be one of the pipes.

Not the grand arterial mains that carry the generator's lifeblood upward—those are too important, too watched, too known. I would be one of the forgotten capillaries, a length of copper or brass tucked behind a boiler housing, installed during the mill's original construction for some purpose now obsolete. Steam relief, perhaps. Or part of a pneumatic messaging system from when six thousand workers needed coordination across the vast floors.

I run at an angle through three rooms and two walls, my path a fossil record of the building's logic before the water came. In the engine hall, my exposed section has been repurposed as an anchor point—someone has welded a bracket to me, hung tools from it. The vibration of the generators travels through me constantly, a bone-deep hum that has become my pulse. Where I pass through walls, the seal has held, somehow. The chief engineer's paranoia extended even to me.

But I am not passive metal. I *remember.*

The water has taught me patience. Where condensation beads on my surface, I taste salt—not just from the sea outside, but from decades of hands that gripped me for balance, workers who steadied themselves against me as they rushed between stations. Their sweat left traces. I catalog them like tree rings.

In my middle section, hidden in the narrow maintenance way between the engine hall and what was once the dye house, I have developed a voice. The generators' vibration, the building's sway, the specific density of the air—these conspire to make me sing at a frequency just below human hearing. The Operators feel it in their teeth when they pass. One of them—an older woman with silver scars across her knuckles—sometimes pauses and places her palm against me. She knows I'm here. She knows I'm listening.

My far end terminates in a room that shouldn't exist—a pocket of space the blueprints forgot, where three pressure vessels meet at an odd angle. The room is dry but unused, too small for generators, too isolated for storage. Sometimes water seeps through hairline cracks in the floor, pools for days, then mysteriously drains. Sometimes the mineral deposits grow here in patterns that look almost deliberate. Sometimes the Watchers come down.

They use me as a speaking tube.

My hollow carries their whispers from the sealed upper levels down to this forgotten room. Not words—the Watchers gave up words long ago—but something between language and pure vibration. They tell me about the building's dreams: the weight of water as a constant lover's embrace, the slow transformation of steel and concrete into something more patient than stone. They tell me about the things growing in the flooded districts above, the new architectures of decay. They tell me about the chief engineer, who they say never left, who they say became the building itself, his paranoia transmuted into the very seals that hold.

I listen. I conduct. I remember.

When the generators cycle down for their brief maintenance windows—those terrifying minutes when the roar stops and everyone holds their breath—I contract. The temperature drop makes me sing a different note, higher, almost like mourning. In those moments, I can feel every other pipe in the network, the whole circulatory system of this drowned heart, each of us carrying our own fragment of what this place was, is, insists on being.

There is a crack in me. Very small. It developed twenty years ago when a particularly violent current hit the building, torqued the frame just enough. The crack is in my section in the maintenance way, hair-thin, invisible unless you know where to look. Sometimes I weep a single drop of condensation through it. The drop falls onto a mineral deposit that has grown beneath me, adding to its mass one molecule at a time.

The Operators think the building will last another decade, maybe two. The Watchers know better. They've told me, in their vibrational language, about the bargain—how long we have, what it costs, what feeds the generators besides diesel. But I keep their secrets, as I keep the sweat-memories of six thousand workers, as I keep the chief engineer's paranoid love, as I keep the song of my own slow oxidation.

I am the memory of connection. I am the promise that things built to join will find a way to join, even here, even now, even as the sea presses in with its patient weight. When this place finally floods—and it will flood, the Watchers have shown me the beautiful mathematics of our ending—I will sing a different song, one that travels through water instead of air. The fish will learn it. The coral will build to its rhythm.

But today, the generators roar, and I carry their vibration into every corner of this impossible ark, and somewhere in my deepest frequency, I am still performing my original function: I am moving something from here to there, I am maintaining pressure, I am part of the great machine that says *not yet, not yet, not yet* to the dark.
