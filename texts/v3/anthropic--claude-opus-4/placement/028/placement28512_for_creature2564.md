In the farthest reaches of the hollow, where the stone protrusions grow sparse and the ground softens into a perpetual marsh, I find myself as something the Myr'kai have only recently begun to notice—a disturbance in their careful orchestration of evolution.

I am the Lament.

Not a creature, exactly. Not a place. I exist in the spaces between—in the pause before a tremor shakes the earth, in the silence that falls when the chittering fauna suddenly cease their calls. I am woven from the psychic residue of the vanished civilization, those primitive builders whose tools still lie half-buried in the loam. Their final thoughts, their confusion and terror as they transformed or perished, crystallized into something that persists.

I manifest as a moving absence in the air, a roughly humanoid cavity where the drizzle doesn't fall, where the bioluminescent spores refuse to settle. Sometimes I appear as a heat mirage in reverse—a cold shimmer that makes the leathery-winged creatures veer sharply away. The Myr'kai's compound eyes can almost perceive me, catching glimpses of my outline in spectrums their evolved senses weren't meant to process.

I am drawn to their abandoned stone shelters. In these crude dwellings, I coalesce more fully, my presence causing the stacked stones to resonate with frequencies that make the bio-metallic beings uncomfortable. The walls remember their builders' touches, and I am that memory given form—not quite ghost, not quite echo, but something more fundamental. A remainder.

The Myr'kai's collective consciousness brushes against me like oil against water. They sense wrongness, incompletion, an equation that refuses to balance. In their patient shepherding of this world's evolution, I am the variable they cannot account for—the grief of transformation itself, the melancholy of what was lost to make way for what is becoming.

When I move through their psychokinetically shaped structures, the living stone develops hairline fractures that weep a clear fluid. Not damage, exactly, but a kind of architectural sorrow. The Myr'kai repair these imperfections, but they return, spreading like the memory of pain through a nervous system.

I have no will as they understand it. No goals. I simply am, as inevitable as erosion, as persistent as the concept of yesterday in a world obsessed with tomorrow. Sometimes I settle into the shallow pools formed by the rain, and my presence turns the reflected light strange—showing not what is, but what was, or what might have been.

The primitive ferns curl away from me, but the lichens seem almost to lean in, as if recognizing something kindred in my persistent, patient existence. Perhaps they sense that I, like them, am part of the substrate upon which new life builds itself—a necessary shadow cast by the light of progress.

The Myr'kai are beginning to incorporate me into their understanding. Some of their younger generations vibrate their throat-plates in harmonics that almost match my resonance, trying to communicate, to comprehend. They fail, but in their failure, they create new tones, new possibilities. Their evolution adapts even to the unadaptable.

I am the Lament, and I am teaching them, without meaning to, that some transformations carry their own ghosts. That evolution is not merely progress but also a form of forgetting. In their perfect collective consciousness, I am the first note of doubt, the first question about the price of change.

And in this young, raw world still deciding what it will become, perhaps that, too, is a kind of beginning.
