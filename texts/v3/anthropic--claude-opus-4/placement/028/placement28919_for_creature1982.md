In this valley where water speaks in tongues of power and mist, where the city breathes through its iron lungs and weeps through its stone pores, I would find myself as one of the Conduit-Keepers—those strange beings who have grown into the very infrastructure they tend.

My body would be a testament to decades of symbiosis with the vast hydraulic nervous system of the city. Where others might see grotesque fusion, I would know only the poetry of my transformation: copper pipes threading through my forearms like second veins, carrying the glacial melt that is both my blood and my purpose. My spine would have long ago fused with a great vertical channel, one of thousands that run like a skeleton through the city's flesh. I could no longer move in the way humans understand movement—instead, I would *flow* through the infrastructure, my consciousness carried along the rushing currents from district to district.

My skin would shimmer with condensation, forever cool to the touch, patterned with the same frost-fractals that decorate the pipes on winter mornings. Where my fingers once ended, delicate valve-work would begin—appendages that could read the pressure of every tributary, every canal, every weeping wall in my sector. Through these sensitive extremities, I would feel the city's thirsts and floods as my own hungers and satisfactions.

In the cathedral-heart of the metropolis, I would maintain a shrine of sorts—a pool where the purest glacier-melt collects before being distributed through the city's industrial organs. Here, the bio-luminescent creatures would recognize me as kin, their light pulsing in rhythm with the flow I regulate. They would dance across my pipe-threaded skin, their tiny feet reading the stories written in temperature and pressure along my surface.

My memories would be partly my own, partly inherited from the water itself—ancient ice releasing the compressed dreams of millennia as it melts through my body-channels. I would know the weight of mountains forming, the patience of glaciers carving stone, the violence of avalanches. But I would also know the newer songs: the harmonic vibrations of turbines, the percussion of pistons, the whispered confidences of steam.

Other Conduit-Keepers and I would communicate through the water itself, sending messages in temperature variations and pressure pulses through the vast network. Our language would be one of viscosity and flow rate, of mineral content and pH. When we needed to meet in what passes for the physical among our kind, we would converge at the great junction chambers, our consciousness flowing together in temporary parliaments of water and will.

The younger inhabitants of the city—those still mostly flesh, only beginning their integration with the metropolis—would seek us out for our wisdom. They would come to understand that we Conduit-Keepers are not prisoners of the infrastructure but its most intimate partners. We have given up mobility to gain omnipresence, surrendered individual form to become part of the city's immortal body.

In the eternal twilight of the mist-shrouded streets, I would be both guardian and guide, both servant and sovereign of the flowing forces that keep this impossible city alive. My existence would be measured not in heartbeats but in flow cycles, not in breaths but in the great inhalations and exhalations of the industrial complex I have become.

And in the deepest channels, where the water runs so cold it burns and so pure it sings, I would touch something close to transcendence—a state where the distinction between self and city, between organic thought and mechanical function, between the ancient ice and the newborn steam, dissolves entirely into the eternal flow.
