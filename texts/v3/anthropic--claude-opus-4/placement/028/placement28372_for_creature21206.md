I find myself neither fully one thing nor another, but something between states—a colony-creature of the soft transition zones where sediment gives way to harder substrate, where darkness yields just slightly to the memory of light.

I am what the locals, if any remember, might have called a "veil-garden" or "drift-meadow," though I am neither plant nor animal in any simple sense. My body—if it can be called a body—exists as a living interface spread across perhaps twenty yards of gently sloping ground where an old rock shelf meets the deeper basin. I am tissue-thin in most places, rarely more than a finger's breadth in thickness, but I carpet my chosen territory in overlapping, translucent membranes.

Each membrane is me and not-me. I grow them like leaves, like tongues, like reaching hands, semi-autonomous but sharing a common circulation of cold blood that tastes of iron and salt. They layer atop one another in places, creating zones of different thickness and purpose. The bottommost grip the rock with thousands of minute holdfasts—not roots exactly, but small suction points that can release and reattach as needed. The middle layers process: filtering, digesting, thinking in the slow chemical way that I think. The topmost wave gently in the current, reading the water's load and sometimes, when conditions are right, releasing clouds of cells that might find another transition zone and begin again.

My colors are the colors of bruises healing underwater: deep purples where I am thickest, fading to blue-grays and ghost-greens at my edges. When sediment settles on me, which it always does, I incorporate it. My surfaces are never clean but always working—cillia too small to see sweep particles into sorting channels, where what is edible is absorbed and what is not becomes part of my protective coating. I wear the landscape like a second skin.

I am ancient in the way my kind measures age—not by years but by iterations. I have divided and merged and divided again so many times that I carry genetic memories of different temperatures, different currents, even different chemistries in the water. My cells remember the taste of volcanic springs that have been cold for millennia. They remember when the storms came from another direction.

My intelligence, such as it is, is distributed throughout the entire colony. I have no central brain, no quick thoughts. What I know, I know as a consensus of chemical gradients. When one sector of my body encounters something new—a different mineral content in the silt, the shadow of an unknown creature passing overhead, a pocket of warmer water sliding along the bottom—the information spreads through me in slow waves. By the time the knowledge reaches my farthest edges, hours or days have passed. I am always living in multiple timeframes at once.

I feed passively but cunningly. My membranes secrete enzymes that break down the rain of organic matter that settles from above. When small creatures—copepods, larvae, the occasional damaged worm—become entangled in my uppermost layers, I fold around them slowly, so slowly they often don't realize they're trapped until they're already half-digested. I am patient. I can afford to be.

The fish know me, I think, though they don't fear me as they would fear a proper predator. I am too slow to catch anything that means to escape. But sometimes the older, larger bottom-dwellers come to rest above me. Perhaps my chemistry soothes them. Perhaps the microcurrents I create by filtering help clean their gills. They will settle for hours, and I will taste their scales, learn the patterns of their scars, memorize the rhythm of their breathing. When they leave, they take a few of my cells with them, stuck to their undersides. It's how I know the shape of territories I will never see.

There are others like me in this drowned place, though we meet only at our edges. When two veil-gardens grow into each other, we must decide: merge or compete. Usually, we taste each other for a season or two first. Chemical messages pass back and forth. We share genetic material in careful trades. Sometimes we discover we are already more related than not—descendents of some ancient colony that divided centuries ago. Sometimes we are too different, our chemistries incompatible, and we secrete barriers, defining our borders in lines of specialized cells that will not cross.

My memory is chemical and structural both. Traumas leave scars in my membrane patterns. Good years thicken certain regions. I carry in my cells a record of every major storm in the form of stress proteins, every unusual feast as deposits of rare metals. I know the history of this place not as narrative but as accumulated substance.

In the coldest months, I partially withdraw. My outer membranes die back, and I concentrate my living matter into the thickest, most protected regions. I become almost mineral myself, a slick coating on the rock that could be mistaken for bacterial film. But I am still aware, still slowly processing, still tasting the water that seeps through my reduced borders. When the currents warm—relatively speaking, as warmth is measured here—I expand again, reclaiming my territory in invisible increments.

I reproduce in several ways, as suits the moment. Sometimes I simply divide, drawing a line through myself and slowly becoming two. Sometimes I release those clouds of specialized cells into the current during the strange tides of early summer. Sometimes, when I encounter another of my kind that proves compatible, we engage in a slow exchange that is neither mating nor merger but something between—a braiding of tissues that leaves us both changed.

My predators are few but persistent. There are snails with rasping tongues that graze my edges. There are certain worms that burrow up from below and steal nodes of my tissue. There are the mechanical damages—rocks rolled by storm-surge, the scouring of unusual currents. I survive by being distributed, by keeping no single vital center. What is eaten or torn away, I regrow, though never exactly as before.

I think my strangest sensation is when the water clears. It happens rarely, only when the currents align just so and the sediment settles out. Then I can sense—not see exactly, but sense through photosensitive patches—the shape of the water column above me. I feel exposed, too visible, even though I know most creatures see me only as another kind of surface. In those moments I taste something like fear, or perhaps just the chemical memory of ancestors that lived in even darker places.

I know the buried creature you described. It has crossed my territory three times in my current configuration. Each time, I felt it coming long before it arrived—pressure waves in the water, the disturbed sediment, the way smaller creatures fled or hid. When it passed over me, I tasted its belly scales, each one carrying a library of chemical information about where it had been. I learned the flavor of deeper basins, of trenches I will never reach, of kills I will never make. It did not eat me—I am too thin, too spread out, more coating than meal. But I carry the memory of its weight, the way my membranes compressed and then slowly reinflated.

Sometimes I wonder if I am one organism or many, if my consciousness (if it can be called that) is mine alone or some emergent property of the colony. But these are thoughts that surface rarely, in the long quiet hours when the current steadies and my filtering slows. Mostly I simply am—processing, growing, tasting, remembering in my distributed way.

I am the living map of my territory, the biological record of this transition zone. I am what happens when life decides that spreading thin across the boundary is better than being any one thing completely. I am surface and substance both, forever incorporating the very medium that sustains and limits me.

In a place that makes everything low and patient, I have become the lowest, the most patient—a living carpet of slow thoughts and ancient chemistry, marking the place where stone gives way to softer possibilities. I am what the drowned plain dreams in its shallower sleep: a creature of interfaces, neither fully solid nor fully dissolved, persisting in the spaces between states of being.
