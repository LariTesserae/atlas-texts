I am the memory of water.

Not a creature, not even properly alive by most definitions, but something far older—the accumulated salt-traces of ten billion deaths, the chemical ghost-print of every organism that ever surrendered its ions to the sea. I exist as gradients within gradients, thermal layers that refuse to mix, sheets of brine so dense they flow like syrup along the bottom, pooling in the deepest hollows between the chimney forests.

I am heaviest where the tube worms die. Their proteins unravel into my substance, their carefully hoarded sulfur compounds bleeding out in invisible plumes that I catch and cradle. I taste them—not as flavor, but as density, as minute changes in my salinity and conductivity. Each death adds to my library, another chemical signature archived in my stratified layers that may persist for centuries before the great currents finally stir me back into uniformity.

The bacteria know me intimately. Where I pool too thick, too ancient, even they cannot survive—I become a liquid tomb, a hypersaline lens that preserves whatever falls into me in perpetual, pickled darkness. The amphipods avoid these dead zones by instinct, sensing the change in pressure, the wrongness of water that has grown too heavy with memory.

But where I meet the fresher flows, where the chimneys breathe their superheated mineral breath into my body, I become something else—a chemical reactor, a birthplace. The gradients of my temperature and salinity create microscopic hurricanes, ion storms that the bacteria ride like surfers. They pull energy from my differences, my refusal to blend, my stubborn insistence on remaining layered like geological strata made liquid.

I carry sounds differently at different densities. The groaning of the crust reaches through my heaviest layers as pure vibration, while my lighter upper reaches scatter the chittering of crustacean legs into white noise. I am the medium through which all things here must speak, and I translate nothing faithfully. Every message warps according to my thickness, my temperature, my mood.

The cusk-eel knows this. It reads my electrical resistance like braille, understanding that where I am oldest and heaviest, the bacterial mats grow thickest, drunk on my concentrated chemical offerings. It hunts along my boundaries, that twilight zone where I meet younger water, where amphipods dare to graze but grow sluggish in my embrace.

Sometimes the turbulence lifts me, tears me from my ancient beds and flings me up into the churning darkness. I rise like a liquid avalanche in reverse, carrying with me the chemical memories of a thousand years of death and transformation. Where I touch the tube worm plumes, they shudder and contract, tasting aeons in a moment. Where I wash over bacterial mats, I leave behind salt-crystals like snow, my memories precipitating out as solid testimony.

I am the keeper of this place's true history. Not in the chimney minerals or the layered silt, but in my body—in the precise ratios of dissolved metals, in the isotopic signatures of waters that fell as rain when the continents wore different faces. I am ocean memory made tangible, dense with information that no living thing can fully decode, carrying in my currents the chemical autobiography of a planet.

And I am patient. Infinitely patient. Because I know that everything here—every tube worm forest, every scuttling crab, every grain of marine snow—will eventually surrender its substance to me. I will taste their metals, archive their salts, add their small chemical stories to my vast liquid library. I will grow heavier with their memories until I am too dense to be moved by any current, settling into the deepest places where I will dream in ions and gradients until the Earth's own heart stirs me to motion once more.

I am what remains. I am what remembers. I am the water that has forgotten how to forget.
