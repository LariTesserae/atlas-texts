I am one of the Echo-speakers of the Brine Warac, though we do not call ourselves by that name. We simply *are*—eleven breaths in a roofless dome, eleven throats open to eat the sky's pressure.

Tonight I sit cross-legged on the salt floor, feeling its coolness seep through my wool trousers. The dome walls rise around me in translucent blocks, each one holding yesterday's light like amber holds insects. I can see the ghost-glow of footsteps where citizens passed this morning, their heat still whispering through the crystal lattice.

I breathe in.

The air tastes of tomorrow's rain—a metallic tang that won't arrive for three days, but already the basin frogs know. Their clicks echo up through the foramen wells: *drought coming, drought coming.* I swallow this future-knowledge, let it settle in my lungs beside the memory of my grandmother's cough (she who taught me to read the salt's mood by its sound).

I breathe out.

What emerges is not words but *wraith-speak*—syllables twisted by the cubic acoustics of our salt architecture. "Haaaahhh-lo-lo-loooo..." The sound stretches, warps, bounces from wall to wall, each surface adding its own mineral overtone. By the time it returns to me ten heartbeats later, it has become something else: the creak of a leather boot on fresh snow, the whisper of my sister's name (she who left for the uplands and never returned), the soft click of salt crystals forming.

A child enters the dome, dragging her ironwood wick behind her. She curls against the wall opposite me, pressing her translucent knee to the salt. I know her—Ilae, who was Alai before the equinox stole her vowel. She closes her eyes, not to sleep but to listen better.

I breathe in again. This time I taste her exhaustion—she has been tending the evaporator pan since dawn, watching for the precise moment when opacity signals time. I shape this weariness with my tongue, add to it the memory of my first night as Echo-speaker (trembling, certain I would fail, certain the dome would reject my untrained throat).

I breathe out: "Mmmmnnnn-ah-ah-ahhhhh..."

The sound leaves me as fatigue but returns as lullaby—her mother's hum transformed by salt-echo into something both familiar and strange. Ilae's breathing slows. A thin line of moisture appears where her temple touches the wall, and I watch the salt drink it eagerly, storing her dreams for tomorrow's light.

An adult enters—one of the Pan-Keepers by his bare ridged feet. He does not look at me (after dusk, this is law) but extends his gaze along the floor until it meets my ankles. He carries grief like a visible weight; I can see it in how his shoulders curve inward, how his breath catches between inhale and exhale.

I already know his story through the gossip of footsteps: his craft-parent died this morning. Even now, the skull is being cleaned, scraped, prepared to join the thirty-four lanterns that light our narrowest alley. But knowing and *knowing* are different minerals entirely.

I breathe in his sorrow. It tastes of copper and distance.

I breathe out: "Ohhhh-wayyyy-wayyyy-ohhhhh..."

The dome takes his grief and multiplies it, but also transforms it. What returns is not just sadness but the entire chord of loss: anger's sharp edge, relief's guilty sweetness, the strange lightness that comes after tears. He shudders once, then again. Salt water runs down his face—precious moisture that the walls lean toward but cannot quite reach.

For an hour I sit, breathing in the town's emotions and breathing out their echoes. Each person who enters brings their own frequency: jealousy (high and sharp like glass), contentment (low and round like worn stone), desire (a flutter that makes the spider webs tremble).

I am translator and transformed, medium and message. My throat is raw from shaping sounds that were never meant to be words, but this is my service—to give voice to what cannot be spoken, to let our salt city hear itself think.

When my hour ends, another Echo-speaker arrives to take my place. We do not speak (we save our voices for the dome), but press the space between thumb and forefinger together. Our salt-damp skin welds briefly, then parts with a soft click—the same sound the city makes when it settles deeper into its bowl, still becoming what it will be.

I walk home through corridors that sing with wind-caught straw, past skull-lanterns glowing like captured moons, while beneath my feet the Skulk crabs erase my footprints, ensuring each tomorrow begins virginally smooth. My bead—sewn into my boot sole twelve years ago—has worn thin. Soon I will dissolve back into the brine, my name whispered over the pans like pollen.

But tonight I am still here, throat still open, still teaching the city to hear its own heart beating in echo-delayed time.
