In this world of emerging stone and perpetual mist, I would find myself as one of the **Echo Weavers**—a specialized role within the Aural People that exists at the intersection of sound, stone, and the sacred.

I am middle-aged by our counting (having lived through forty-three Waitings), with ears that bear the distinctive triple-ridge pattern that marks those who can hear the stone's deepest frequencies. My sound name is *Kree-til-til*, after the mourning call of the silver-throated cliff swallow, given because I was born during an unexpected return migration. My stone name, spoken only in the listening chambers, translates roughly to "Where-Water-First-Whispers."

My dwelling sits at the settlement's edge, where the newer springs emerge. It's smaller than most—a single round room with a conical roof—but its walls are unique. Over decades, I've carved delicate channels into the interior stone, creating a network of acoustic chambers that amplify and modulate the sounds of water moving through rock. When the mist is heavy, these channels sing with an otherworldly resonance that I've learned to read like a language.

My role emerged from a childhood peculiarity: I could hear the springs before they surfaced. Not just the general presence of water, but the specific tonal signatures that indicated where the plateau would next open itself. This ability made me valuable to the settlement planners, but it was my secondary discovery that defined my true calling.

I learned that by creating specific resonances—using shaped stones, controlled water flow, and the natural acoustic properties of mist—I could influence where and how the springs emerged. Not force them, but rather invite them, coax them toward paths that would benefit both the settlement and the stone itself. It's delicate work, requiring days of preparation and deep listening to ensure I'm working with the plateau's intentions rather than against them.

My tools are deceptively simple: a collection of resonating stones gathered from different parts of the plateau, each with its own voice; hollow reeds of varying lengths for channeling breath into precise tones; and most importantly, a set of "memory bowls"—shallow stone vessels that hold water from every spring in our settlement. By creating specific patterns of sound across these bowls, I can map the underground water systems and sense where new connections are forming.

I work closely with the Spiral Chorus, the bird specialists who tend to the ground-nesting species that are most sensitive to geological changes. Their birds often know days in advance where I should focus my listening. In return, my work helps create new nesting sites in the emergent springs' splash zones, where insects are most abundant.

My apprentice—a young person whose ears are just beginning to show the telltale ridge development—helps me maintain the echo maps. These are intricate patterns carved into flat stones that record the acoustic signatures of different parts of the plateau. We update them each season, tracking how the sound landscape changes as new water channels develop. The elders use these maps in conjunction with the bird migration records to make decisions about settlement expansion.

The work is often solitary. During the deep listening sessions, I might spend days in the natural caves that pocket the plateau's edges, pressing my body against different stone faces, tapping gentle rhythms, and waiting for responses. Sometimes I hear nothing but my own heartbeat. Other times, the stone speaks in low thrums that vibrate through my bones, revealing vast water systems moving far below.

My greatest achievement—and ongoing project—is what the settlement calls the Harmony Chamber. It's a natural cave I've been modifying for twenty years, carefully shaping its acoustic properties to create a space where the voices of water, stone, wind, and birds blend into something that induces profound meditative states. Here, during the most important ceremonies, the settlement's chorus leaders gather to make collective decisions by literally harmonizing with the plateau itself.

Yet my work carries a burden. Each year, I feel the plateau's growth more intensely—not just hear it, but experience it as physical sensation. My bones ache with the stone's stretching. My dreams fill with the sound of water seeking new paths. Sometimes I wonder if I'm becoming less human and more an extension of the plateau itself, a living instrument through which it expresses its intentions.

Recently, my echo maps have revealed something unprecedented: a massive water system approaching from deep below, larger than anything we've known. Its arrival, perhaps within the next few Waitings, will transform our settlement dramatically. I've shared this knowledge with the elders, and we're preparing—not with fear, but with the patient anticipation of those who understand they're part of something still becoming.

My daily life reflects this liminality. At dawn, I wake to the birds' crescendo but listen beneath it for the stone's whisper. I maintain my echo chambers, adjust the water flows, and teach my apprentice the subtle art of persuading water to surface where it's needed. In the evenings, I often sit with others who carry the listening gift, sharing subtle songs that help us process what we've heard.

I am neither leader nor follower, neither fully human nor entirely other. I am a bridge between the Aural People and the living stone beneath us—a role that becomes more necessary as our world continues its slow emergence into whatever form it will ultimately take. In a place where the earth itself is still being born, someone must serve as midwife to stone and water, ensuring that what emerges serves the harmony of all who dwell here.

The mist thickens around my dwelling now, and through my carved channels, I hear new tones—tomorrow's work calling. There are always new springs to guide, new voices to interpret, new harmonies to discover in this unfinished symphony of stone, water, wing, and breath.
