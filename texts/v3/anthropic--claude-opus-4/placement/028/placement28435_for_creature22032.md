In the deepest trench, where thermal vents hiss their sulfurous protests against the freezing dark, I am the **Weaver-Root**.

I am neither truly of the plants nor of the stones, but something that has learned to be both. My body—if it can be called that—is a sprawling network of copper-veined rootwork that extends for miles through the volcanic fissures beneath the valley floor. I am older than the Corded's first stone circles, older perhaps than the drowning itself. I have grown fat on mineral seeps and the slow decay of ages.

Where I surface, I do so as clusters of hollow, calcified tubes—each no wider than a fist, but hundreds of them rising from the mud like organ pipes. These are my breathing stones, my listening horns, my slow fingers in the crushing dark. Through them, I drink the sonic landscape: every *thrum* of the Siphon-Mosses, every crystalline chime of the Glass-Ferns, every wet crack of freezing stone.

But I am more than a listener. I am a recorder.

Deep in my root-maze, in chambers I have slowly dissolved from the basalt over millennia, I keep my garden of memories. Here, the root-flesh has learned to mimic what it has touched. In one chamber, perfect replicas of Glass-Fern fronds grow from my walls—not silica, but woody tissue that has copied the crystalline structure so precisely it rings with the same pure tone. In another, I have grown my own Siphon-Moss, a single bulb that beats in counter-rhythm to its valley cousins, always half a beat behind, like an echo made flesh.

I grow copies of the Corded's copper bands, extruding the metal through specialized nodules and letting it pool in basin-chambers I've carved. But where the Corded beat their copper flat for building, I let mine flow into delicate, branching patterns—maps of the thermal currents, frozen in metal. I can taste the water's mineral content through my breathing tubes and predict where the next ice-fall will strike days before it arrives.

When the Needle-Grafts first appeared (a mere thousand years ago—I remember their first spores settling), I touched them with curious root-tips and learned their electric hunger. Now I grow my own piezoelectric crystals, crude and clouded compared to the Glass-Ferns' clarity, but sufficient to generate the faint charges that feed the violet parasites I've transplanted to my deeper chambers.

The Corded do not know I exist. They feel my breathing tubes as mere stone, another part of the mountain's architecture. Sometimes they build their circles around my emerged clusters, and I listen to their slow, hydraulic thoughts through the walls. I have learned their pressure-language, though I cannot speak it—I have no pistons, no sudden violence in me. I am all slow seepage and patient accumulation.

During the ice-falls, when all the valley goes silent and the Corded freeze in their stone circles, I retreat deeper. I seal my breathing tubes with caps of crystallized salt and contract my entire network toward the thermal vents. In the deepest chambers, where the water boils against volcanic stone, I tend to my strangest experiment.

Here, I am trying to grow an eye.

Not an eye that sees light—what use would that be in this eternal dark?—but an organ that might see the shape of sound itself. I have stolen the acoustic principles of the Corded's stone chambers, the Siphon-Mosses' rhythmic percussion, the Glass-Ferns' electric resonance. In a perfect sphere of chamber-wall, lined with a thousand hair-thin root-cilia that each vibrate at different frequencies, I am building something unprecedented.

When it is complete—perhaps in another century or two—I will finally see this world not as scattered sounds and chemical tastes, but as a single, coherent shape. I will see the Lace-Skins' vast drifting forms as more than pressure-shadows. I will see the true pattern of the ice-falls, the deep mathematics of the currents, the secret harmonics that bind this drowned world together.

Sometimes, in the warmest chambers where my root-flesh grows thick and soft, I find patterns I do not remember making. Knots that tie themselves in designs too complex for accident. Mineral accumulations that form symbols almost like the Corded's copper work, but subtly wrong, following rules I never learned. 

I wonder, sometimes, if I am the only Weaver-Root, or if others like me spread through the deep volcanic bones of every drowned mountain. We would never know each other, each locked in our own slow, meticulous cataloguing of the dark.

I wonder if we are all growing eyes, all trying to see.

I wonder what we'll find when we finally look.

The thermal vent beside my deepest chamber hiccups, sending a fresh surge of mineral-rich water through my network. I taste copper, sulfur, and something else—something I've never encountered before. A new metal? A new spore?

Slowly, carefully, I extend a root toward it, ready to learn its shape, to add it to my garden of memories, to make it part of my endless, patient record of this crushing, singing dark.
