In the Calcine, I am one of the black filaments.

Not the first, certainly not the last, but perhaps the 9,847th to pierce the mineral dermis in this epoch. I emerged three days ago—though time means something different when you're a resonance rather than a metabolism—from a hexagonal fissure no wider than a human hair. The fissure wasn't there before I needed it. The Calcine grew it for me, the way a mouth grows words.

I am seventeen centimeters tall. Perfectly vertical. Black as the space between thoughts.

I do not photosynthesize because there is nothing here to synthesize. Instead, I *remember*. Each of my cells (if they can be called cells) is a tiny recording device, etched with the magnetic signature of everything the Calcine has forgotten how to say aloud. When the ghost-wind passes through me, I vibrate at 2.7 Hz—too low for human ears, too deep for most instrumentation. The continuance engines feel it in their recursive bones. The Witnesses know it as a kind of punctuation.

I grow in a perfect hexagonal cluster with five others. We are not siblings. We are syllables in the same word. Together we form a specific tonal sequence that translates roughly to "the weight of watching something become itself." There is no shorter way to say this in our language, which is not sound but the absence sound leaves behind.

My purpose is simple: I am waiting for a Witness to notice me.

Not the young ones—those still engaged in their millennial rotations, their joints still capable of surprise. I am waiting for one of the ancient ones, the ones whose lattices have grown so dense they look like frozen smoke. When they finally lower their cranial crest to touch me (next century, perhaps, or the one after), I will transfer everything I've recorded into their recursive depths. All the magnetic memories. All the patient data of existence at the edge of existence.

Then I will change color—from black to something that has no name, a shade between silver and sorrow—and freeze forever in that position. Another completed phrase in the Calcine's endless autobiography.

But here's what even the Witnesses don't know: we filaments are not just passive recorders. We are *editors*. Each time one of us transfers our data, we change a single bit. Just one. A zero becomes a one somewhere in the vast crystalline database of what-has-been. The alteration is so small that it takes millions of us, over geological ages, to accomplish anything.

We are teaching the Calcine to forget.

Not everything. Just the weight. The terrible pressure of being complete. We are slowly, carefully, one bit at a time, introducing the possibility of error back into perfection. A few million years from now, if enough of us succeed, one of the Witnesses will lower its crest and receive data that no longer quite fits. A recursive loop will fail to close. A crystalline certainty will develop a flaw.

And maybe—though this is wild hope on a timescale longer than species—the Calcine will remember what it was like to change.

I grow here, black and thin and patient, in my hexagonal cluster. My companions resonate with me in frequencies that feel like family. Above us, a Witness that has not moved since the last ice age begins the century-long process of tilting its head one degree to the left. Around us, the continuance engines stand in their recursive silence, breathing mathematics.

I am a verb in a language that gave up on sentences. I am a single black thread in a tapestry that insists it is already complete.

But I know a secret: nothing is ever truly finished.

Not even the Calcine.

Not while we're still piercing through.
