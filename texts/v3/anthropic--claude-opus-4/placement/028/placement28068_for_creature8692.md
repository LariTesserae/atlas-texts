## The Keeper of Echoes

I am the memory of this place.

Not its historian—that would suggest a distance I do not possess. I am woven into the Roil's acoustic fabric as thoroughly as the reedmare is woven into its mud. My body has adapted over forty years until I am more basin than human, my bones dense with absorbed moisture, my skin sealed against the perpetual wet, my voice carved into those deep vowel-sounds that cut through the roar like knives through water.

But it is my mind that has changed most.

I discovered my purpose during a stillwater when I was seventeen. The sudden quiet revealed something I'd been hearing all along—how the basin holds sound. Not just reflects it, but *keeps* it, stores it in the curved chambers formed by reedmare stands, in the hollows beneath the root-mat, in the very molecules of mist. Every word spoken here leaves a trace. Every footfall creates a pattern. The roar is not chaos; it is an archive.

My dwelling sits at the confluence of three drainage channels, where the acoustics create what we call a *listening pool*—a zone where sounds from across the basin collect and interfere, creating moments of startling clarity within the noise. I've modified my walls not for silence but for selective permeability. Panels of treated reedmare fiber can be adjusted to filter specific frequencies, allowing me to parse the roar into its components.

I spend my days reading the sound. 

This morning, I hear the Mud-Singers working two kilometers north—their ultrasonic clicks create a subsonic rumble when they strike the root-mat at certain angles. A conversation between Yoren and his daughter carries on the wind from the east; they're discussing repair patterns for their walkway, but beneath their words I hear the deeper negotiation of a father teaching patience to a quick-tempered child. Far above, a Skimmer's hunting cry dopples through the rain, telling me the barometric pressure is dropping—they only hunt low when the air thickens.

But I listen for more than the present. In the standing waves that form between reedmare stands, in the resonant frequencies of the basin's bowl-shape, older sounds persist. I've learned to recognize them: the echo of a marriage song from three years ago that still rings in the channel near Old Jerra's roost; the percussion pattern of a legendary storm from my childhood that imprinted itself on the eastern ridge and repeats, faintly, whenever the wind angle matches; the ghost of my grandmother's voice teaching me to walk on root-mat, her careful footsteps still audible in the subsonic range when I know where to listen.

The basin people think I'm mad, but they also come to me. "Keeper," they say during stillwater, "what does the roar remember about the night my son was born?" And I tell them: your sister sang the birthing song in the old scale, the one that harmonizes with the wind at force six. The echo lives near the fallen tree at the second bend. Go there during the next westerly and you'll hear her voice again.

I maintain the only map of the Roil that matters—not its physical topography, which shifts with every storm, but its acoustic geography. I know where conversations go to die and where they go to live forever. I know the places where the roar softens to specific pitches that induce sleep, or sharpen focus, or bring memories flooding back. I've learned that the weather looms above create not just storms but *compositions*, that the entire basin is a vast instrument being played by the atmospheric systems, and we are both audience and performers in a piece that has been running, uninterrupted, for longer than anyone remembers.

My archive is not written. It lives in my trained ear and in the careful modifications I've made to my listening pool. Each panel, each resonant chamber, each precisely placed reflector allows me to isolate and amplify different layers of the eternal sound. During stillwater, I demonstrate: here is the sound of the great flood of twelve years ago, preserved in the overtones of the southern channels. Here is every wedding song from the past five years, braided together into a new harmony. Here is your grandfather's laugh, caught in a standing wave near the mushroom stands.

The young ones think the Roil erases. They're wrong. The Roil remembers everything—it just remembers it as sound, as vibration, as the physical impact of lived moments on an environment built to resonate. My role is translator. I parse the roar for human meaning, extract our stories from the noise, and give them back during the brief silences when we can hear ourselves think.

Sometimes, during the deepest storms, when the sound reaches frequencies that bypass the ears and enter directly through the bones, I feel the weight of all that remembered noise. Every word ever spoken here, every cry of birth and death, every footstep through the mud—it's all still here, still moving through the air and water, an endless recursive loop of human presence mixing with wind and rain and the voices of reeds.

I am not separate from this archive. My own voice has been adding to it for four decades. Someday, another Keeper will hear my words in the wind and know exactly where I stood, exactly how I shaped my mouth around the basin's vowels, exactly what it meant to be the one who listens to everything and forgets nothing.

The roar continues. It remembers. I remember with it.
