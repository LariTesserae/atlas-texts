In this world of weeping stone and patient water, I would find myself as **a memory-keeper of the cisterns** - one who dwells in the deepest, most sacred water-chambers of the Basin.

I would be ancient even by Kethra standards, my skin so translucent it seems almost to be stone-skin itself, veined with the blue traceries of water-paths memorized over centuries. My dwelling would be a natural cavity deep within the rock, where the largest and oldest cisterns converge - those first carved by the ancestors who discovered how to read the stone's hidden language.

My form would be even more adapted than most Kethra. My fingertips would have developed tiny, hair-like cilia that can sense the minutest vibrations in the water - able to detect not just flow and seepage, but the very history held in each droplet's mineral composition. I could tell by touch alone whether water fell as rain last season or has been cycling through the stone for decades. My eyes, clouded now with age to an almost opalescent grey, would see less in the conventional sense but perceive the phosphorescent traces that certain minerals leave in the water - invisible to younger Kethra.

My role would be both practical and spiritual. I would maintain the memory of every cistern's capacity, every seasonal variation, every subtle change in the water table over generations. This knowledge would exist not in any written form but in a complex mental map that mirrors the actual hydrology of the Basin - a living, three-dimensional understanding that I would share through the most subtle of communications: a specific pressure of fingertips on a young keeper's wrist that conveys the location of an emergency reserve, a particular tilt of the head that warns of a weakening channel.

My chamber would be a cathedral of water-sound. Hundreds of tiny channels would converge here, each singing its own note as water drops at different rates into vessels of varying sizes and materials. I would have spent lifetimes learning to read this aqueous music - knowing instantly if a channel three levels up has become blocked, or if the mineral content of the eastern seepage has shifted. The whisper-jars here would be the finest in the Basin, some so old their creators' names have been forgotten, each one perfectly tuned to its specific location and purpose.

I would rarely venture to the upper levels anymore. My world would be this deep, humid darkness where the distinction between self and stone has largely dissolved. My movements would be so slow and deliberate that visiting Kethra might not immediately notice I'm there - I would seem like another formation of the cave until I shift slightly or exhale a breath that disturbs the droplets on a nearby whisper-jar.

The younger keepers would come to me not for instruction - for the knowledge I hold cannot be taught, only absorbed through proximity and time - but simply to sit in the presence of one who has become so attuned to the Basin's deepest rhythms. They would learn by breathing the same humid air, by letting their own skin detect the subtle temperature variations that mark different water sources, by slowly developing the patience to think in geological time.

In the rare moments when the Basin faces crisis - an unprecedented dry season, a shift in the deep stone that threatens ancient channels - I would emerge from my depths like a pale salamander, moving through the settlement by touch and water-sense alone. My presence would be enough to calm panic, for I embody the truth the Kethra live by: the water has always found a way, and those who listen deeply enough will find it too.

I would be keeper not just of cisterns but of the profound patience that allows the Kethra to thrive in their weeping world - a living reminder that in a place where everything moves at the pace of erosion, the deepest wisdom comes from becoming as still and receptive as the stone itself, until you can hear the mountain's tears telling their ancient stories, drop by precious drop.
