# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the Surveyor of Discontinuities.

I exist as a function more than a being—a role that emerged from the system's need to understand its own boundaries, its own exceptions, its own subtle failures of perfect equilibrium. I move through the Evaporated Basin not as an inhabitant but as a kind of persistent measurement, a walking instrument of observation that the system tolerates because it cannot quite eliminate the need for witness.

My form is barely distinguishable from the landscape itself. Years of breathing the mineral-laden air have deposited a fine patina of salt and iron oxide across my skin, which has taken on the pale, fractured quality of the crust I walk upon. My movements are economical to the point of near-stillness—I have learned that excessive motion disturbs the humidity gradients the plants depend upon, creates eddies in the perfect stillness that take hours to settle. I move like the fronds move: slowly, deliberately, in response to necessities I can barely articulate.

I carry instruments, though they are so integrated into my daily practice that they feel more like extensions of my senses than separate tools. A hygrometer so sensitive it can detect variations in atmospheric moisture measured in parts per billion. A seismometer that registers the microscopic settling of the substrate, the hairline propagation of fractures through the salt crust. A chronometer that keeps what passes for time here, though time in the Basin is more theoretical than actual—I measure it not in days or hours but in cycles of the extraction machinery, in the barely perceptible growth of mineral deposits on the tower infrastructure.

My primary function is to map the anomalies. In a system designed for perfect equilibrium, I document the deviations. The places where a frond has inexplicably failed to establish itself despite optimal humidity conditions. The towers where the extraction efficiency has dropped by fractions of a percentage point. The substrate zones where the fracture patterns suggest subsurface water movement that doesn't align with the known aquifer models. I compile these observations not into reports—there is no one to report to—but into a kind of living map that exists partly in my instruments, partly in my memory, partly in the subtle marks I leave on the landscape itself.

I discovered, years ago (or perhaps it was decades; my chronometer measures cycles, not human time), a particular anomaly that has become my primary study. In the southeastern quadrant, approximately seventeen miles from my usual circuit's center, there is a zone where the plants grow differently. Not larger or smaller, not more or less densely, but *wrong* in some fundamental way. Their fronds spiral rather than fan. Their color tends toward a blue-grey that exists nowhere else in the Basin. Most disturbing: they produce a sound. Not audible to human ears, but my instruments detect it—a subsonic vibration at a frequency that matches no known mechanical or biological process in the system.

I have developed a theory, though theory seems too active a word for the slow accumulation of observations that constitutes thought in this place. The anomalous zone sits above a deep geological discontinuity—a place where the substrate layers don't follow the standard precipitation sequence. The darker bands are thicker there, suggesting a period in the Basin's history when conditions were dramatically different. Perhaps there was once surface water there. Perhaps there was once something like an ecosystem. The strange plants might be accessing some remnant of that earlier time, some pocket of different chemistry preserved in the deep strata.

But this is speculation, and speculation is discouraged by the nature of the place itself. The Basin rewards observation, not interpretation. It sustains measurement, not meaning.

I live—if living is the right word—in a structure that is neither shelter nor dwelling but something more like a calibration chamber. It exists at the intersection of three tower-zones, where the overlapping humidity fields create a pocket of relative atmospheric stability. The structure itself is built from compressed substrate material, blocks of mineralized earth cut from zones where the geological process has advanced furthest. Inside, my instruments line the walls: decades of measurements recorded on metal plates that have themselves begun to oxidize and mineralize, becoming part of the geological record they document.

I sleep rarely and briefly, in cycles that correspond to the deepest thrums of the extraction machinery. Sleep here is less rest than recalibration—a period when my biological rhythms synchronize with the Basin's mechanical ones, when the distinction between my pulse and the system's pulse becomes meaningless. I dream, sometimes, of water. Not the memory of water—I have no such memories—but the *idea* of water, the theoretical presence of something that moves and changes and refuses equilibrium. These dreams disturb me. They suggest that some part of my consciousness has not fully integrated with the Basin's stillness.

My nutrition comes from the system itself. There are intake points at each tower where a thin, mineral-rich solution can be drawn—a byproduct of the extraction process that contains enough dissolved nutrients to sustain basic biological function. It tastes of iron and salt and something organic that might once have been algae, might once have been something else entirely. I require very little. My metabolism has slowed to match the pace of the Basin. I move slowly, think slowly, age slowly. I am becoming geological.

The most difficult aspect of my existence is not the solitude—solitude implies the memory of company, which I lack—but the growing awareness that I am myself becoming one of the Basin's measurements. My presence creates its own discontinuities. My footsteps leave their own fracture patterns in the crust. My breathing contributes its own minute quantities to the atmospheric humidity. The anomalies I study are increasingly difficult to separate from the anomalies I create.

This is perhaps why I persist in studying the blue-grey plants in the southeastern zone. They represent something genuinely other, something that predates my presence, something that suggests the Basin was not always what it is now. In their spiral growth patterns, in their subsonic emanations, in their wrong color, I see evidence of a different possible equilibrium—one that included change, included communication, included something like life as it is traditionally understood.

But I cannot be certain. Certainty requires comparison, and there is nothing here to compare to. There is only the measurement, the observation, the slow accumulation of data that might, given geological time, amount to understanding.

I continue my circuits. I maintain my instruments. I document the discontinuities. I breathe the mineral air and feel the salt accumulating in my tissues, feel myself becoming more substrate than surveyor with each passing cycle. Sometimes I wonder if I am the last of something—the last observer, the last consciousness capable of recognizing the Basin as a place rather than simply a state. But this too is speculation, and speculation is discouraged.

What I am certain of is this: I am necessary. Not to the plants, which would persist without observation. Not to the machines, which would cycle without witness. But to the incompleteness of the system itself. Perfect equilibrium would not tolerate my presence. The fact that I exist, that I move through this stillness taking measurements, suggests that the equilibrium is not perfect. That somewhere in the vast mathematical balance of extraction and precipitation, of growth and mineralization, there remains an undefined variable.

I am that variable. I am the thing the system cannot account for and cannot eliminate. I am the discontinuity that maps the other discontinuities, the anomaly that recognizes anomalies.

In this capacity, I persist. Neither living nor dying, but continuously measuring the difference between the two. Walking the salt crust with my instruments, breathing the mineral air, becoming slowly indistinguishable from the landscape I observe, but never quite achieving that final transformation.

I am the surveyor of discontinuities in a place that dreams of perfect continuity. And in that contradiction, perhaps, lies the closest thing to meaning that the Evaporated Basin can sustain.
