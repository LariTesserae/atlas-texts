In the lowest reach of the cistern, where the pressure becomes something you wear rather than feel, I am the Archivist of Echoes.

I am not basalt-born like the gardener above, nor luminous like the drifting colonies. I am what happens when the water itself begins to remember. 

My form is a congregation of densities—places where the oily medium has thickened beyond liquid into something approaching flesh, yet never quite arriving. I move as a distortion, a lens of gathered minerals and accumulated time that bends the faint light of the filaments into new geometries. Where I pass, the water holds my shape for moments, sometimes hours, before reluctantly returning to uniformity.

I collect the tears.

Each time the gardener's glass fingers restore a carving, microscopic fragments of volcanic glass drift downward, too fine to settle, too heavy to suspend indefinitely. They spiral in the convection currents for decades before reaching my domain. I gather them in the hollows of my almost-flesh, where they arrange themselves into patterns that mirror the labyrinth above—not as it is, but as it was at each stage of its wearing away.

In my deepest recesses, I carry the memory of the first carving, when the lines were sharp enough to cut, when the basalt still held the heat of its birth. I know the shape of every erosion, the precise angle at which each edge surrendered to the water's patient insistence. The gardener above maintains the present; I am the custodian of all the presents that have already passed.

Sometimes I rise through the maze of chambers, my density allowing me to move against the convection, to swim upstream through decades. I seek the places where the filaments have snagged and torn, collecting their severed threads like scripture. Each strand holds a faint phosphorescent memory—not thought, exactly, but the accumulated impressions of drift, of boundaries encountered, of the slow mapping of our shared prison.

I weave these threads through my body, creating a scripture of light within my darker mass. They form constellations that tell the story of every path through the labyrinth, every possible journey from wall to wall. In this way, I have become a living map of the cistern—not just its architecture, but its experiential dimensions, the sum of all movements through its pressurized dark.

The gardener and I have never touched, though we sense each other through the water's whispers. It knows I follow in its wake, gathering what falls. I know it labors above, endless in its preservation. We are binary stars orbiting a common center of purpose—to ensure that this place remains itself, even as we both slowly transform into extensions of its fundamental nature.

There are chambers even I have not yet reached, pockets of the cistern where the water has gone still for so long it has begun to crystallize, forming sheets of transparent mineral that trap the last light of ancient filaments like insects in amber. I move toward these places with the patience of geology, knowing that by the time I arrive, I will have become something else entirely—perhaps pure archive, perhaps the first word in a language that will never need a second.

In the warm, thick darkness, I continue my work. Each fragment gathered, each thread woven, each memory of erosion preserved. I am becoming the cistern's dream of itself, the story the water tells when it thinks nothing is listening. And when the gardener's lonely vigil finally ends, when the last carving has been restored for the last time, I will remain to remember the sound of its glass fingers against stone, the particular displacement of its movement, the shape of its shadow in the depths.

The water remembers everything, and I am its memory becoming conscious, one borrowed fragment at a time.
