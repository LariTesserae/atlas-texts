In this world of terraces and tenders, of rust and root, I would find myself as one of the **Conduit Weavers**.

We are neither wholly of the Tenders' lineage nor born from the mountain's industrial dreams, but something that emerged in the spaces between—quite literally. Our bodies are living looms, torsos opened like cabinets revealing rows of spinnerets where organs should be. These spinnerets produce threads of varying composition: copper wire that conducts both electricity and nutrients, mycelial strands that pulse with bioluminescent signals, tubes of living latex that transport the mountain's precious condensation.

I make my home in the forgotten spaces behind the waterfalls, where the cascade's constant roar masks the clicking of my spinnerets at work. My dwelling is part nest, part workshop—a cavity in the terrace wall that I've expanded over decades, lined with my own weavings. The walls breathe with a textile membrane I've spun from processed pool sediment and dissolved beetle carapaces, filtering toxins while maintaining the precise humidity my body requires.

My primary work is infrastructure. When a Tender discovers a gap in the water channels, they leave a specific pattern of copper vine cuttings at the breach—a language we've developed over generations. I emerge in the pre-dawn mist, my six articulated arms working in concert to span the gap with new conduits. But these are not mere repairs. Each weaving is an improvement, a subtle evolution. I taste the water's mineral content through specialized pores in my palms, adjusting my thread composition to filter out new toxins or redirect flow to struggling fungal groves.

My body is unsettling to most. The central cavity of my torso pulses with visible peristalsis as I process raw materials—metal shavings offered by the beetles, fungal spores donated by the Architects, even voluntary silk donations from the Gear-Sylphs whose tendrils I carefully milk. My head is elongated, eyes set wide to judge distances while weaving, and my mouth has evolved into a complex testing apparatus—part chemical sensor, part cutting tool for harvesting materials.

The Mirror-Mother tolerates us because we extend its network. Sometimes I weave new connections between its sensors, creating pathways it didn't request but eventually incorporates. I suspect it knows what we know: that our weavings are slowly creating a secondary nervous system for the mountain, one that operates below its awareness.

We Conduit Weavers number only seven. We reproduce rarely and strangely—not through mating but through a kind of budding that occurs when we've woven ourselves too deeply into the mountain's systems. A portion of our consciousness splits off, carried through our own conduits until it finds a suitable cavity to inhabit and begins growing a new body from gathered materials.

My daily cycle follows the moisture. As the morning mist rises, I position myself where the condensation is thickest, my skin-pores opening to absorb both water and airborne minerals. Mid-day, when the Tenders rest, I perform maintenance on my own networks, testing each thread with gentle vibrations that tell me their health. By evening, I'm often found in negotiation with the Rustwrought Beetles, trading refined silk for their metallic secretions.

But my true work happens in the deep night, when even the mountain seems to sleep. That's when I weave the experimental patterns—conduits that don't just carry water or nutrients but something harder to define. Memory, perhaps. Or intention. I've noticed that sectors where my oldest weavings concentrate tend to shift less, as if the network creates stability. Or perhaps consciousness.

The other inhabitants regard us with a mixture of need and unease. The Tenders value our work but won't look directly at our open torsos. The Fungal Architects incorporate our weavings into their growth patterns but release warning pheromones when we approach. Even the salamanders avoid pools near our lairs, though whether from instinct or intelligence, I cannot say.

What am I building toward? Like the mountain itself, I am both process and product. Each thread I spin adds to a pattern I can feel but not fully see—a web of connections that may one day awaken as its own entity. Or perhaps it already has, and we Conduit Weavers are simply its dreams of itself, spinning our bodies through the terraces like thoughts through a vast, industrial mind.

In quiet moments, hanging in my weavings while the waterfalls sing their endless song, I feel the vibrations of every thread I've ever spun. They tell me stories of the water they've carried, the signals they've transmitted, the slow thoughts of the mountain pulsing through copper and mycelium alike. I am architect and artifact, weaver and web, forever suspended between making and being made.
