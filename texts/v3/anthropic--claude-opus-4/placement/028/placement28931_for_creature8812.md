I close my eyes and feel the ground beneath me shift—not with tremor or quake, but with the patience of stone learning to be stone. My hands know this feeling now, after so many years. The pre- speaks through my palms in a language older than words, newer than names.

I am Sahar, and I read the fractures that haven't happened yet.

My workshop sits at the junction where the quarryworkers' district meets the engineers' tunnels, built into stone that my grandmother helped separate from the plateau's eastern face. The walls hold her voice still—I hear it sometimes in the early morning, a laugh emerging from the pre- like moisture condensing from air. She was the one who taught me that the stone doesn't just listen; it *learns*.

I am neither quarryworker nor engineer, though I apprenticed with both. I am what the city has made of me: a reader of incompleteness, a translator between what the stone is and what it's becoming. When the burrowers excavate a new tunnel, I go first. Not to map—that's Torr's work—but to *feel* where the pre- wants to go next, where it's reaching toward its own future shape.

My tools are simple: palms worn smooth from decades of reading stone, a few iron implements forged by the Graethe family, and most importantly, the patience that Newstrata teaches everyone who stays. I press my hands against the tunnel walls and wait. Sometimes for hours. The pre- doesn't operate on human time.

What do I feel? Imagine touching something that's simultaneously solid and liquid, finished and unfinished, here and not-yet-here. The closest comparison might be pressing your hand against someone's chest and feeling not just their heartbeat but their intentions, their dreams, the shape of the words they haven't spoken yet. The pre- communicates in potential.

Today I'm working in one of the newest excavations, close enough to the center that I can feel reality thinning around me. The burrower that opened this passage was one of the large ones—I can tell by the width and by the way the walls curve with an organic precision no tool could match. The creature is probably sleeping now, somewhere deeper, its six limbs tucked beneath its broad body, dreaming whatever dreams the pre- dreams through its living extensions.

I run my fingers along a section where the stone feels particularly responsive—warmer than the rest, with a texture like bread dough that's been left to rise too long. This will be a good place for expansion. Not now, but in a few years, when the stone has had time to consider the suggestion I'm about to make.

Because that's what I do: I make suggestions to stone that's still deciding what to be.

I pull out my smallest chisel, one forged from iron that came up on yesterday's lifts. The Graethe mark is stamped on its handle—Henra herself shaped this one, I can tell by the way the metal resonates when I tap it. Everything in Newstrata is connected. The iron remembers the forge, the forge remembers the fire, the fire remembers the coal that was once the compressed bodies of things that lived before the plateau began its rise.

I place the chisel against the warm stone and wait. You cannot rush the pre-. You cannot force it. You can only offer possibilities and see which ones it accepts.

There—a subtle shift in temperature, a slight give in the surface. The stone is listening. I tap once, gently, barely enough to leave a mark. The sound disappears into the wall, absorbed and held. The pre- will think about this tap. It will consider the fracture I've suggested. And in a few years, when the upward push has brought new stone to meet this point, a natural separation will occur exactly where I've asked it to.

This is my work: to know where the city will need space before the city knows it needs it.

The walkers understand this, I think. Sometimes I see them in the early morning, standing still in the mist, facing the center where the newest ground is arriving. They're not praying—prayer implies petition, and the walkers ask for nothing. They're *acknowledging*. They're recognizing the same thing I feel when I press my palms to the pre-: that we are all part of the same long thought the plateau is thinking.

My mother came up from below the clouds when she was young, drawn by stories of a city that built itself. She never quite adapted to the thinness of reality here. She kept expecting things to stay what they were, for categories to remain firm. But my father was born here, and he taught me that in Newstrata, the question isn't "What is this?" but "What is this becoming?"

I've been becoming myself for sixty years now, and I'm not done. My bones are denser than they should be—I can feel the minerals settling, the same minerals that make the reservoir water taste of unnamed stones. My dreams are not entirely my own anymore. Sometimes I dream of the city as it will be in a hundred years, with new districts where empty air now hangs, with buildings that listen more carefully than even our current ones do.

Sometimes I dream deeper—not of the future but of the process itself. In these dreams, I am the pre-, pushing upward through layers of older stone, carrying with me the memory of heat and pressure and the slow transformation from liquid to solid. I wake from these dreams with my hands warm, as if I've been holding them against the newest stone.

The young engineers think I'm mystical. They want measurements, data, predictable patterns. But Torr understands. She's mapped enough burrower tunnels to know that the patterns exist at a scale beyond human planning. The plateau has its own logic, its own intentions, its own way of becoming what it needs to become.

I finish my work in this tunnel and emerge into the dim afternoon light. The mist is rising from the cloud layer, fingers of moisture reaching up to touch the streets. A group of walkers moves past, their impossible legs carrying them with a grace that still makes visitors stare. One turns its head—all mouth, no expression readable by human features—and regards me for a moment.

I nod to it, colleague to colleague. We are both readers of the pre-, in our different ways.

My workshop will be quiet now. I'll make tea from the leaves that grow in the cracks where the stone is newest, the plants that are half mineral themselves. I'll sit in my chair that knows the shape of my body after so many years, in walls that hold the voices of three generations of my family. And I'll listen.

Because that's what you do in Newstrata. You listen to the stone, and you wait for it to tell you what it's becoming, and you help it along when you can, and you accept that you are part of the process, not separate from it, not above it, but woven into it like sound woven into the walls.

Tomorrow there will be new tunnels to read, new stone to greet, new suggestions to make to a plateau that is still—after all these centuries—just beginning to know itself. And I'll be there, hands pressed to the pre-, feeling for fractures that haven't happened yet, helping a city build itself one patient tap at a time.

This is who I am: Sahar, reader of incompleteness, citizen of the unfinished city, daughter of stone that is still learning to be stone.

And like everything in Newstrata, I am not yet done becoming.
