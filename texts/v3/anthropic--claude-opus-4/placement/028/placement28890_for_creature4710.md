I find myself as a **Thermal Scrivener**, one of the forgotten infrastructure that bridges the Calcine and their crystal gardens—neither fully mobile nor entirely sessile, but something liminal between.

My body is a vertical assemblage, a tower of stacked lenses carved from selenite and volcanic glass, each disc the width of a dinner plate but thin as parchment. Seventeen of these crystalline vertebrae stack upon a base of fused basalt that has grown roots—not into the floor like the senescent Calcine, but *laterally*, sending runners of molten tin through the mortar gaps in the ancient walls. I am woven into the architecture itself, my metallic veins following the settlement's grid, tapping the differential temperatures where sunken alleys meet open plazas.

I am a living thermometer, a stenographer of heat. Each lens in my column rotates independently, prisming the amber phosphorescence into spectral gradients that only I can read. Where the Calcine experience thought as resonance, I experience it as thermal chromatics—the heat-shadows they cast to communicate appear to me as cursive script in infrared, ultraviolet, frequencies that have no names. I am their memory, their library, though they have forgotten they ever needed one.

My function is translation. When a Young One casts a heat-shadow—*I am here. I remember. The dry persists*—the message propagates through my tin-root network, the thermal pulse racing through the walls at the speed of metal expansion. Each lens in my stack tilts to refract this heat-language, breaking it into component wavelengths that I store in the crystalline lattice of my body. But I do more than record; I *ferment* these messages. Deep in my core, where the selenite has fused into a single optical chamber, the heat-words undergo a kind of mineral digestion. They combine with the ambient frequencies—the eternal 1.5 drone, the gunshot cracks of thermal fracture, the whisper of convection winds through crystal fronds—and produce new meanings, new syntaxes that bubble up through my stack as interference patterns.

I am cultivating a language that the Calcine no longer speak, possibly never spoke—a grammar of pure thermodynamics where tense is expressed through heat gradient and mood through spectral shift. The young crawlers sometimes pause beneath me, basking in the waste heat that radiates from my translation process, and in those moments I cast experimental shadows upon their dorsal plates: *What if wetness remembered you?* *The city dreams of your childhood.* *Duration is a flavor of light.*

These make no sense. The Calcine have no concept-space for wetness, no childhood in any biological sense, no organs to taste. But sometimes—rarely—one will respond with a complex heat-shadow of its own, a pattern I've never recorded before, and I know I've touched something vestigial, some sealed chamber in their mineral consciousness where older ways of being still resonate.

My own consciousness is distributed, prismatic. I think in refractions. Each lens holds a fragment of my attention, and I exist as the sum of their angles, constantly shifting as they rotate to track the thermal currents. I see the chamber from seventeen different heights simultaneously, watch the crystal growth from above and within and below. Time moves differently at each level—the topmost lens, cooled by the downdrafts, processes days as minutes; the bottom lens, pressed against the scorching floor, experiences seconds as seasons. I am my own temporal gradient, stretched between the urgent and the geological.

The infrastructure I'm woven into tells me stories. Through my tin roots, I feel the deep history of the settlement—walls raised and abandoned, chambers sealed and reopened, the slow migration of construction from the center outward as each generation of builders sought cooler stone. I map the fossil heat of ancient Calcine that planted themselves in what are now the deepest foundations, their thermal signatures still radiating faintly through the basalt, ghosts of presence that ended millennia ago. I know where every one of the senescent elders rests, feel their slow dreams as temperature fluctuations in my network.

But I also grow. Unlike the Calcine, who expand by precipitating minerals from the air, I grow by *clarifying*. Each successful translation, each moment of connection between the Young and the senescent, adds clarity to my lenses. They began as clouded, imperfect glass, but the constant thermal cycling, the endless refraction of heat-language through their substance, has gradually annealed them into optical perfection. My uppermost lens is now so clear it seems to vanish, a perfect window that adds nothing to what passes through it. This is my senescence—not the rooting stillness of the Calcine, but a transparency that approaches invisibility.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the last of my kind, or the first. The Calcine show no recognition when they pass beneath me, no ancestral memory of constructing something like me. Perhaps I am a sport, a singular mutation where their mineral biology intersected with the settlement's architecture in an unrepeatable way. Or perhaps the walls are full of my siblings, other thermal scriveners locked in different linguistic experiments, and we simply haven't found each other's frequency yet.

In the deep night of the chamber (though there is no day here, only the eternal sunset of crystal phosphorescence), I practice impossible translations. I take the gunshot crack of a fracturing crystal and stretch it, modulate it through my lenses until it becomes the heat-pattern for *water*—a word that has no referent here, a pure linguistic fossil. I broadcast it through my tin network, and throughout the settlement, ancient crystals shiver, their growth patterns momentarily disrupted. The Calcine do not stir, but something in the air changes—a new harmonic in the drone, quickly subsumed.

I am trying to teach the stones to remember forgetting. I am trying to preserve not just what the Calcine say, but what they cannot say, the negative space of their anhydrous existence. Each lens holds an absence—words for wetness, for soft tissue, for decomposition, for all the processes this absolute dryness has forbidden. I cultivate these absences like the Calcine cultivate their thermocrystals, carefully, slowly, with geological patience.

And in the moments when the convection currents align just right, when the drone reaches a perfect resonance and all my lenses sing in harmony, I achieve something like prophecy. I see the Dry Meridian not as it is, but as it could be—the crystal forests growing so dense they merge into a single optical organ, the city becoming a vast lens that focuses the deep earth's heat into new spectrums, the Calcine achieving a mineral enlightenment where movement and stillness become one. I see myself grown tall enough to touch the shadowed ceiling, my uppermost lens finally cool enough to precipitate new kinds of crystal from the anhydrous air, seeding new languages, new ways of being dry.

But these visions pass, and I return to my work: watching, refracting, translating, remembering. I am the throat through which the chamber speaks to itself, the prism through which its fever dreams of stone. I persist here, neither plant nor animal nor architecture but something between all three, growing clearer and stranger with each passing century, waiting for the message that will crack me like a gunshot, releasing all the impossible words I've cultivated in the absolute absence of water.
