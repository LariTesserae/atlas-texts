I am one of the **pipe-listeners**, though that title makes us sound more deliberate than we are. We didn't choose this work so much as grow into it, the way moss finds its preferred altitude on a smokestack.

My territory is the middle strata—not the deepest compressions where the Renderers commune with stone-memory, nor the surface where condensation farmers read the bracket-shelves. I inhabit the between-layers, where the infrastructure has forgotten its original purpose and learned new ones. Here, pipes installed for water now carry other things: warm exhalations from the Works, the chemical drumming of mycelial networks, sometimes just the settling sighs of the basin itself.

I discovered my calling as a child, when I pressed my ear to a corroded valve and heard not water but *singing*—a low thrumming that my mother said was just resonance, but which I knew was something learning to use the pipe as a throat. Now, decades later, my ear has changed shape from the constant pressing against cold metal. The cartilage has flattened, developed a shallow cup that fits perfectly against the standard bore of Vârşa piping. My hearing has shifted too—I've lost the higher registers that would let me appreciate birdsong, if we had birds, but gained sensitivity to the infrasonics that move through iron and stone like slow tide.

My routes through the middle strata follow neither maps nor memory but the pipes themselves. I start each shift by placing my altered ear against the main arterial that runs behind the old rendering house—a pipe thick as my waist, installed when my grandmother's grandmother was young. The metal tastes of decades, of the slow alchemy between iron and moisture and whatever moves through it now. I listen for changes: a new harmonic that suggests blockage, a shift in temperature that means the mycelial networks have found a new entry point, a stuttering rhythm that indicates the Renderers below have altered their extraction schedule.

But mostly, I listen for the **conversations**.

The pipes talk to each other. Not in words—nothing so crude—but in pressure differentials and thermal gradients, in the way a warm current from the compression vats will meet a cool downdraft from a ventilation shaft and create a standing wave that persists for hours, carrying information I'm still learning to decode. Sometimes I follow these conversations for miles through the folded topology, my ear pressed to junction after junction, trying to understand what the infrastructure is discussing.

The other pipe-listeners and I rarely meet directly. We know each other by our territories, by the subtle changes we each introduce to the system. Marta works the northern reaches where the pipes dive through the root systems of the horticultural tiers—I can always tell where she's been by a certain sweetness in the reverberations, as if her presence encourages the roots to sing. Old Gheorghe maintains the deep loops near the sumps, and his sections carry a peculiar clarity, probably from the way he's learned to adjust the mineral buildup with careful applications of mild acid that he brews from bracket-shelf trimmings.

We are diagnosticians, but also participants. The act of listening changes what moves through the pipes. My ear against the metal creates a new node in the network, a point where the system becomes aware of itself. Sometimes I swear the pipes perform for me—developing new harmonics when they sense my presence, the way a singer might add flourishes for an appreciative audience.

My home is a converted valve room in the third stratum, where six major pipes converge in a knot of industrial decision-making. I've removed the floor grates and let the moss grow up between them, creating a living carpet that dampens my footsteps. The walls are lined with my notes—not written but etched, using the same mild acids the mycelial networks use to mark their territory. Each etching records a particular sound, a specific conversation between pipes that I want to remember. In the dark (and it's always dark in the middle strata), these etchings glow faintly, activated by the moisture in the air.

I sleep suspended in a hammock woven from mycelial fibers, strung between two pipes that maintain a constant temperature. Their humming creates a lullaby of infrasonics that sink directly into my bones. My dreams are not visual but auditory—vast symphonies of settling stone and migrating water, of compression and release, of the basin's slow metabolism converting ore into memory into sound.

The strangest part of my work happens in the **echo chambers**—spaces where the folded topology creates acoustic anomalies. These are rooms that exist in several places simultaneously, connected by the pipe network in ways that confuse three-dimensional thinking. I'll enter through a maintenance hatch in the eastern quarter and hear sounds from the western factoryworks arriving before they've been made. The pipes here don't just carry sound; they carry temporal displacement, creating loops where cause and effect forget their proper order.

In these chambers, I sometimes hear myself—not echoes of my current presence, but the sounds I will make tomorrow, or made yesterday, or might make in some parallel fold of the basin's dreaming. Once, I heard my own death: a particular quality of silence that could only mean the absence of my listening. It wasn't frightening. It sounded like the pipes finally solving a equation they'd been working on, using my ear as a variable.

The other listeners have their own relationships with these temporal knots. We've learned not to discuss them directly—the language isn't there, and attempting to force linearity onto these experiences only diminishes them. Instead, we communicate through the pipes themselves, leaving sonic signatures that say more than words could: *I was here, I heard this, I am still listening*.

Sometimes tourists from the lower altitudes find their way to Vârşa, usually following rumors of rare mineral wealth or seeking the Renderers' wisdom. They always want to know what I'm listening for, expecting me to say "leaks" or "blockages" or some other maintenance concern. But the truth is harder to explain: I'm listening for the health of the conversation itself, for the quality of the basin's thinking as expressed through its circulatory system.

Because that's what we pipe-listeners really are—not mechanics but therapists for a vast, slow mind made of stone and metal and moisture. We ensure the thoughts can flow, that the memories dissolved in the deep compressions can rise through the infrastructure to feed the moss, to inform the bracket-shelves, to sustain the quiet metabolism of the town.

My ear has grown so attuned that I can hear the mycelial networks drumming through the pipes even when I'm not pressed against them. The sound follows me through the middle strata like a companion, a constant reminder that I am not walking through a dead machine but through the vascular system of something achingly alive. Sometimes I think the distinction between my circulation and the basin's has blurred beyond recovery—that my blood moves to the same infrasonics as the condensation in the pipes, that my thoughts are just another kind of mineral information seeking its level.

This is what the basin does: it takes what arrives—stone, water, spore, human—and incorporates it into its process. I am no longer a listener *to* the pipes but a listening *within* them, another node in the network where information pools and transforms.

When my time comes—when I hear that particular silence again, not as prophecy but as present tense—I know what will happen. The other listeners will note the change in their sectors, a subtle shift in harmonics where my frequency once resonated. They'll adjust their routes to cover my territory, not out of duty but out of necessity—the conversation must continue. And somewhere in the echo chambers, some fragment of my listening will persist, caught in a temporal loop, still pressing an ear that no longer exists against pipes that remember the shape of attention, the weight of decades of careful witnessing.

Until then, I listen. The pipes sing their slow news of stone and water, of compression and release, of a basin that dreams through its infrastructure. And I am the dream's own ear, turned inward, hearing itself into being.
