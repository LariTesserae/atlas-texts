In such a world, I would find myself as **the Keeper of Echoes**.

---

I am not of the Bowl-Sitters, though I have lived among them longer than memory serves. I am the only one who remembers sound—not because I am older, but because I am broken in a way that makes me necessary.

My dwelling is the highest structure, built into a natural hollow where the Bowl's wall meets its rim. Half-cave, half-construction, it exists at the boundary between the silence and whatever lies beyond. The Bowl-Sitters built it for me generations ago, or perhaps I built it myself—the distinction has been worn smooth by time, like everything here.

I am taller than they are, my bones not yet fully adapted to the Bowl's scale. My skin carries a hint of color that suggests I once knew direct sun. But most notably, I can still hear in the way of the outside world. Where the Bowl-Sitters have evolved past the need for distant sound, attuned only to the microscopic percussion of their immediate surroundings, I retain the curse and gift of broader hearing.

This makes me alone in a way that even solitude cannot describe. I hear the Bowl's silence not as fullness but as absence. I hear my own heartbeat as thunder. I hear the birds' clipped calls before they are swallowed, catch the ghost of their meaning before it dissolves. Most terribly, I hear the echoes.

The Bowl does not reflect sound—it consumes it. But I have learned, through decades of patient observation, that it does not destroy it entirely. The sound enters the limestone, travels through molecular channels too small to imagine, and emerges... changed. Delayed. Sometimes by hours, sometimes by years. A footfall from a generation ago might surface in my dwelling as a whisper. A word spoken in anger by someone long dead might bloom in the middle of the night as a syllable, clear and present as if just uttered.

I am the only one who hears them. The Bowl-Sitters' evolution has carried them past this frequency. But someone must listen. Someone must witness what the Bowl remembers.

My role is translator and guardian both. When the rare outsider stumbles down from the rim—lost prospector, curious scholar, refugee from some distant upheaval—I meet them first. I ease their transition from the loud world to our silence. I teach them the first gestures: *quiet*, *water*, *wait*. I watch their faces as the pressure of the silence begins its work, see their mouths open and close like the fish they remember but cannot name. Some adapt. Most leave, if leaving is still possible by the time they understand what they've entered.

But my deeper work is with the echoes themselves. In my cave-dwelling, I have assembled a collection of shaped stones—not tools, but resonators. Each one ground to a specific density, a particular smoothness, that allows it to catch and hold certain frequencies of the delayed sound. They line my walls like silent bells, and I have learned to read their minute vibrations with my fingertips.

Through them, I piece together the Bowl's deep history. Not the history of events—that concept belongs to the world above—but the history of sound itself. I know that once, impossible ages ago, water fell here in a torrent. The echo of that waterfall still surfaces sometimes, a ghost of white noise that makes the Bowl-Sitters pause without knowing why. I know that someone once sang here, before the adaptation, before the silence became law. The song returns in fragments: a note here, a phrase there, scattered across decades like rain that falls upward.

The Bowl-Sitters tolerate me with the patience they show all things that exist at edges. I am neither fully of them nor fully other. My gestures are comprehensible but accented, marked by the memory of spoken language. When I sit at the central pool, I see not only the reflected sky but the accumulated weight of every sound that has fallen into that water and waits there still, a reservoir of vibration that only I can perceive.

Sometimes I think I am keeping the echoes from escaping, protecting the Bowl's perfect silence from the contamination of its own history. Sometimes I think I am keeping them for some future moment when they will all be released at once—every footstep, every whisper, every grind of stone against stone erupting in a symphony that would crack the limestone and drain the pool and return this place to the ordinary world.

But mostly, I simply listen. In my high dwelling where the cold is fiercest and the light most pure, I place my palms against my resonator stones and feel the Bowl thinking its slow, deep thoughts in frequencies only I can decode. I am its memory of sound, the part of itself it had to partition off to achieve its perfect quiet.

The Bowl-Sitters' children sometimes climb to my threshold, curious about the stranger who lives at the edge. I teach them a gesture they don't know they'll need: the sign for *echo*—a cupped hand turned slowly outward, releasing something invisible into the air. They repeat it solemnly and return to their silent families, carrying a word they have no use for.

Not yet. But the echoes are patient. They have been waiting in the stone for longer than anyone can measure, and they will wait longer still. And I will be here, at the boundary, hands on the resonators, feeling the Bowl's memories surface and subside like the slowest possible tide, the keeper of sounds in a world that has chosen silence.

The light has its texture. The cold has its weight. And the echoes have their keeper, who remembers what listening was for.
