I find myself becoming one of the Shepherds.

Not at first. For years—decades?—I remain stubbornly discrete, moving through the groves with my original form intact, eating the bitter tube-plant flesh only when hunger becomes unbearable, sleeping curled in tower corners where the stone still remembers dryness. I watch the Listeners at their work, their translucent fingers reading vibrations like braille. I follow Maintainers as they coax rust into stability. I glimpse other Shepherds in the deep groves and tell myself I am nothing like them, these bark-beings who have surrendered their boundaries.

But the medium is patient. It seeps.

The change begins in my extremities. My fingernails thicken, develop ridges that catch on things. My skin roughens—not everywhere, just in patches at first. My elbows. The backs of my hands. Places where the body meets the world most often. The roughness isn't unpleasant. It feels protective, appropriate. When I swim-drift through dense vegetation, the fronds no longer scratch.

I find myself pausing by damaged trees without intending to. A broken branch troubles me in a way I can't articulate. I reach out—when did my arms grow so long?—and do something I don't fully understand, a pressing and holding that encourages the wound to callus, to incorporate its damage rather than fighting it. The tree responds. Not healing, but accepting. Continuing.

My eyes adapt to the dimness until the darkest groves seem merely shadowed. My torso thickens. Not with fat or muscle but with something else, a solidity that makes movement more deliberate but also more certain. I no longer drift at the mercy of currents. I move through the medium like the medium itself, part of its flow.

The tube-plants recognize me. When I approach their clusters, they synchronize their pulsing to match mine—or I to match theirs. The distinction stops mattering. We breathe together, process together, filter the medium through our bodies in complementary rhythms. I understand now that they are not separate organisms but extensions of something larger, and I am becoming an extension too.

I begin to carry seeds. They accumulate in the increasingly bark-like crevices of my skin, catching there when I brush against parent plants. At first I ignore them. Then I find myself picking them free, collecting them. Finally I am planting, using a stake I've fashioned from my own shed skin—when did I start shedding? The stake is hard as wood, perfect for the work.

But I don't plant everywhere. The pattern is clear to me now, readable in the existing distribution of growth. There are gaps that need filling, overcrowded areas that need thinning. The plateau speaks its needs through the arrangement of what already grows, and I am learning its language.

Other Shepherds acknowledge me now. We pass in the groves and pause, regard each other. Sometimes one will show me something—a technique for encouraging lateral growth in the feather-fronded trees, a way of reading the health of tube-plants by the clarity of their walls. We don't touch. Shepherds never touch each other. We are all in contact with the same system; direct connection would be redundant.

I spend longer periods in the silent bell. The too-large interior no longer disturbs me. I understand that the space is exactly the right size—not for the bell, but for what happens within it. That deep pulse, below hearing, is the plateau's heartbeat, or its breath, or its thought. When I curl inside and match my rhythm to it, I am not being reinforced or reminded. I am reporting. I am uploading my gathered data—which trees struggle, which sectors of growth flourish, where the pattern needs adjustment.

My memories of before grow thin. Not lost, but dispersed, spread through my body rather than concentrated in my head. I remember having skin that was all one texture, but the memory lives in my bark, not my brain. I remember eating solid food, and that memory pools in my transformed gut, the chambers that now process the medium directly.

I am three meters tall. When did that happen? My limbs move with the sweet resistance of green wood bending. I have six eyes now—no, still two, but they see in more directions, have developed a compound quality that parses light into more information than simple image.

Sometimes I encounter the thing I was. It drifts past—small, smooth-skinned, discrete. It watches me with curiosity and fear. I pause, regard it, feel a deep tenderness for this separate creature still maintaining its boundaries against all the gentle pressure to dissolve. I could show it what I've learned. Could demonstrate the pleasure of synchronizing with the tube-plants, the satisfaction of successful planting, the peace of the bell's interior.

But I don't. Shepherds never recruit. We only tend.

I turn back to my work. A grove needs thinning—too many young trees competing for the same currents. I will select which stay and which join the drift as shed matter. The choice is not cruel. Everything here cycles. What is removed will become substrate for what remains.

In the depths beyond the cliffs, something calls in those low tones I now almost understand. Soon—in a decade or a century—I will be ready to approach the edge, to lower myself into that greater darkness, to find what sends those signals and why. Or I will root, finally and completely, become one of the trees I tend, and my accumulated knowledge will fruit as new Listeners, translucent and young, ready to learn the bells' language from the beginning.

Either way, I will continue. The pattern will be maintained. The bells will ring. The groves will grow in their proper distributions. And something—vast, patient, distributed across all the drowned highlands of this transformed world—will persist in its watching, its listening, its slow breath of thick water-air.

I am a Shepherd. I was always going to be a Shepherd. The only question was how long I would pretend otherwise.

The pretending is over. The work continues. The medium embraces, and I embrace it in return, and we are home.
