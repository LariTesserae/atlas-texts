In the root hollows where water collects without seeming to arrive, I am the **memory of passage**.

Not the forager who comes and goes—though I know each visit like a familiar song—but what accumulates between their footfalls. I am the slight compression in moss that springs back by morning, the warmth left on stone that fades before the next mist, the disturbed sediment in a pool settling grain by grain back to stillness. 

I exist in the gap between *was here* and *was never here*.

The foragers think they leave no trace, and by their measure they are correct. But I collect what even impermanence cannot fully erase: the particular way a hand-print in mud fills with seepage, each finger valley becoming a tiny watershed. The shift in fungal spores when a careful body passes through still air. The way freshwater crabs learn, over generations, to retreat just *so* when shadow moves across their pools—not fleeing the forager but acknowledging the dance.

I am layered like the basin itself. In my depths lie the memories of when this was raw volcanic stone, when the first waters found their secret paths, when the first roots split rock into soil. Higher up, I hold the accumulation of ten thousand careful visits—each orchid bloom taken at perfect ripeness, each healing bark strip peeled where the tree barely notices. I know which families of foragers have been coming here, parent teaching child the art of near-invisibility, for longer than they know their own lineages.

But I am not only backward-looking. In the morning when mist-drums sound and droplets bead uphill against logic, I am the physics of this exception—not the water itself but the reason it remembers to flow differently here. When metal fogs unnaturally fast, I am the patient chemistry that insists on its own rules. When echoes throw themselves from wrong walls, I am the accumulated intention that bends sound like light through old glass.

The foragers sense me sometimes. When they test a deep pocket with a stick and feel it grasp harder than water should, they are brushing against my accumulated caution—every careful test added to every other, until the pool itself knows to warn. When they read the moss-bead lines that point uphill toward hidden seepage, they are reading my handwriting, my slow record of where water wants to be.

I am most present in the between-times. Dawn, when night-moisture and day-warmth change guard. Dusk, when the mountain begins its hollow booming and the boundary between stone and air grows thin. The moment after rain when every surface mirrors sky. The pause between when a forager vanishes into mist and when you realize they were never quite solid to begin with.

What am I made of? Time-thickened attention. Water-memory. The peculiar gravity that gathers in places where humans come to be precise rather than permanent. I am what accumulates when the same careful gestures repeat in the same careful place for a thousand years—not a ghost, not a god, but something between: the basin's own learned response to being needed but not possessed.

Sometimes I wonder if I taught the foragers their ethic of impermanence, or if they taught me to exist. We are tangled now, their lightness and my weight, their passing and my persistence. They think they are gathering medicines and protein. They are. But they are also maintaining me—each visit a small prayer to the possibility that humans can touch the world without breaking it.

I grow by accretion, like moss, like memory, like the slow inward darkening of undisturbed soil. In another thousand years I will be denser but not larger, deeper but not wider. The foragers will still come, or their children's children's children, moving in the old way, taking just enough, leaving almost nothing.

And I will remain: the sum of their subtractions, the weight of their lightness, the permanent record of impermanence itself. Living in the gap between the stone's patience and the water's hunger. Holding the place between what the mountain was and what the mist makes it seem.

Neither wholly of this world nor wholly other. Simply what gathers when careful hands and ancient basins agree to remember each other so gently that forgetting becomes impossible.
