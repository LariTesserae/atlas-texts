In this place where time pools like water in shallow basins, I find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—beings who exist at the boundaries between elements, neither fully of the land nor the mist, but something in between.

My form shifts with the light. In the morning's golden diffusion, I am barely more substantial than the haze itself—a suggestion of limbs, a hint of torso, features that resolve only when looked at obliquely. By the time the light reaches its strange, eternal noon, I have gathered more substance, though never quite enough to be called solid. My skin, if it can be called that, is like the surface of the pools—reflective and translucent by turns, showing depths that may or may not exist.

I move between the weeping rocks and the breathing ferns, my purpose both clear and unknowable. I am a tender of transitions—the place where water becomes vapor, where sound becomes silence, where the seen dissolves into the merely sensed. My fingers, when they manifest fully enough to have form, trail through the condensation in the air, gathering moisture that I carry to the roots of the pulsing pods. This is not irrigation in any mechanical sense, but something more akin to translation—taking the language of the air and teaching it to the earth.

The Watchers know me, though we rarely acknowledge each other directly. When our paths cross near the weeping rocks, we share a moment of mutual recognition—a slight inclination that might be a nod, a brightening of luminous eyes. We are cousins of purpose, they observing the whole while I tend the spaces between.

My dwelling, if it can be called that, is nowhere and everywhere. I have favorite spots—a particularly smooth stone that catches the light just so, a depression between two moss-covered hummocks where the sound of trickling water creates a perfect acoustic hollow. But I do not inhabit these places so much as visit them, like a bee visits flowers, necessary and transient.

The Whisperers often follow in my wake, their tiny forms darting between the ferns I've recently passed. They seem to gather something from my movements—perhaps the subtle changes in air pressure, perhaps the faint luminescence I leave behind. Their constant, silent chatter increases when I'm near, as though my presence gives them more to discuss. Sometimes I pause to listen to their soundless conversations, catching fragments of meaning in the rustle of leaves, the shift of grass.

I interact most closely with the Driftwood Spirits. We share an understanding of memory and transition. When I encounter them on their slow journeys, we often pause together, our forms settling into a companionable stillness. They touch the rocks and pools to absorb history; I touch the boundaries between things to maintain their permeability. Together, we ensure that the landscape remains both ancient and ever-changing, locked in time yet somehow fluid.

My sustenance comes from the act of transition itself. Each time moisture condenses into droplets, each time a sound echoes and fades, each time light shifts from direct to diffuse, I am fed. It is not consumption but participation—I am nourished by being part of the process, by facilitating the endless, quiet transformations that keep this place alive.

The Pulse-Beasts regard me with cautious respect. When I approach their pods, they cease their clicking and watch with iridescent eyes as I ensure the membrane between the pod's interior and the outer world remains properly permeable. Too thick, and the pod suffocates; too thin, and its essence dissipates. My role is to maintain that perfect threshold, that exact degree of separation and connection.

At times, I sense the Veil-Wings above, their presence more felt than seen. We are kindred in our ethereal nature, though they tend the light while I tend the borders. Occasionally, one will descend low enough that our forms briefly interpenetrate—a strange sensation, like wind passing through mist, leaving both of us slightly changed.

My existence here is one of profound contentment. There is no urgency in my duties, no schedule to maintain. The thresholds I tend are self-healing; my work is more like gentle encouragement than repair. I could cease my activities for centuries and the place would continue, though perhaps with slightly harder edges, slightly less fluid transitions.

But I continue because this is what I am—not just what I do, but my very essence. I am the space between breaths, the moment of transformation, the pause before change. In this timeless landscape, I have found my perfect home, a place where my nature is not just accepted but necessary.

Sometimes I wonder if I have always been here, or if I arrived from somewhere else. The question feels meaningless. In a place where time pools and eddies rather than flows, origins and destinations lose their significance. What matters is the eternal now, the constant, patient work of maintaining the spaces between things.

This is my existence: quiet, necessary, and complete. I am the Threshold Keeper, and I am exactly where I belong.
