**Sinta of the Morning Birds**

I am the bird-whisperer, though that name makes it sound grander than it is. Really, I'm just the one who wakes earliest, who sits on the eastern platforms when the sky is still more grey than blue, who has learned that if you're still enough, quiet enough, the birds forget you're not just another post or plant stem.

Forty-one years in the Stillwater has made me neither elder nor youth. My diving days are mostly behind me—my ears protest now below two fathoms, a sharp pain that warns of damage to come. But I don't need the depths anymore. My work is here, in the air between water and sky, in understanding the patterns of wings.

I came to this by accident, or maybe by temperament. As a child, I was the one who preferred watching to doing, who would sit motionless on a platform edge while the other children crashed through their games. My mother worried I was slow, but Taiva recognized something useful in my stillness. "That one sees," she told my mother. "Let her see."

What I see: the seventeen distinct species that nest in our ruins (eighteen if you count the storm-petrel that appeared last season but hasn't returned). The way herons test the wind before choosing their fishing post. How the cormorants coordinate their dives—there's a leader always, usually an older female, who goes first and the others follow in sequence, creating a net of hunting bodies that drives fish toward the center. The tiny reed-warblers who build their nests exclusively on the north sides of buildings, for reasons I haven't yet understood.

My platforms—three of them, connected by a walkway I maintain myself—are in the northeastern section, where the morning light comes first and strongest. The main platform has a simple shelter, just enough to keep rain off my sleeping mat and my collection of materials. The eastern platform is my watching post: a stool made from a salvaged stone block, worn smooth by years of sitting, positioned where I can see across the widest stretch of open water. The third is my work space, where I prepare what I've gathered.

Because watching is only half of what I do. The other half is harvest—taking what the birds leave behind or don't need, transforming it into things the community uses. Feathers, mainly. The waterbirds shed them constantly, and I know where to find the best ones: under the cormorant roosts, in the nest-ruins after breeding season, floating on the morning water before they become waterlogged and sink.

I sort them by type and size. The long flight feathers become fletching for fishing spears—I trade them to whoever needs them, usually for help with platform repairs or a share of preserved fish. The soft down I clean and dry and pack into tiny cushions that ease the elder's joints. The middle-sized feathers, the ones with good color and shape, I work into decorations: hair ornaments that the young people wear during celebrations, fans for the evening fires when the air is too still and the smoke hangs heavy.

But the real value is in the watching itself. I know when the fish are moving by how the birds behave. Three days ago, the herons abandoned their usual stations on the southern platforms—all of them, within an hour. I told Mir to check the deep areas there. He found that a section of wall had collapsed, stirring up sediment, driving the fish away temporarily. Without the birds' warning, someone might have spent days fishing empty water.

I know weather by the birds. When the swallows fly low, skimming the water surface, rain comes within hours. When the ducks cluster in the sheltered areas between buildings, there's wind coming, real wind, the kind that can damage platforms. Once, I saw all the cormorants leave at once, flying hard toward the lowlands. I convinced people to secure everything, to prepare. The storm that came that night was the worst in years. We lost two platforms, but no lives, because we were ready.

My hands are always working while I watch. Preparing cordage from plant fibers—a skill Taiva taught me because it's quiet work, doesn't disturb the birds. I make the finest cord in the community, thin and strong, good for binding feathers to shafts or creating the delicate nets we use for catching minnows. My fingers know the work so well they don't need my eyes, leaving me free to observe.

The children sometimes come to learn watching. Most are too restless, too eager to move and shout and dive. But occasionally one has the patience. Right now, it's Piro's older sister, Nami. She's seven, still young enough to be still without effort, old enough to remember what she sees. She sits with me some mornings, and I teach her the subtle differences: how to distinguish a young heron from a full adult by the color of its legs, why the cormorants' wing-spreading isn't just drying but also communication, which birds are territorial and which tolerate closeness.

I live alone, unusual here. I had a partner once, fifteen years ago—a diver named Kosso who made me laugh, who brought me shells from the deep, who understood my need for morning solitude. He died doing what he loved, going too deep, staying too long, lungs full of water instead of air. We mourned him properly, dropped a carved figure into the deep where he'd died. I thought about taking another partner, but the truth is I like my solitude now. The birds are company enough most days.

My body shows my work: sun-darkened from years of sitting in the open, but also marked by stillness. I have a way of moving that's almost bird-like—quick, precise movements followed by complete stops. When I swim now, which isn't often, I'm slow but efficient, saving energy, making no splash. My eyes are sharp still, sharper than most despite my age. I can spot a feather floating fifty feet away, identify a bird by its silhouette at distances that make others squint.

The fishing people think I'm a bit odd, spending so much time on something that isn't directly productive. But they use my feathers, they listen to my warnings, they bring me food when my own fishing is poor (which it often is—I'm a mediocre fisher at best). Taiva understands better than most. She told me once that every skill has its place, that the community needs watchers as much as divers.

At night, I prepare my findings. Feathers must be cleaned—I use sand and water, sometimes ash for the very greasy ones. They must be sorted, bundled, stored properly or they degrade. I keep them in baskets lined with dried grass, separated by type. My shelter smells of feathers and the oil I press from fish to keep them supple.

I also keep records, in my way. Not writing—we have no writing—but notch-marks on sticks, patterns that tell me which birds arrived when, how many nests succeeded, when the migrations peaked. I have ten years of these sticks now, and patterns emerge. The herons are nesting earlier than they did when I started watching. The cormorants are more numerous. The swallows... the swallows are fewer, and I don't know why.

These changes worry me sometimes, lying on my mat at night, listening to the water sounds and the night birds. Change means instability, and we depend on stability. If the birds leave, we lose our early warning. If the fish patterns shift, our whole life shifts. But worrying does nothing, so I watch, and record, and try to understand.

The morning glory of my work is dawn itself. I wake in darkness, feeling my way to my watching post, settling onto my stone seat while stars still reflect in the water. Then the sky lightens—grey to pearl to pink to gold. The water catches the light and doubles it. The plants begin their slow movement. And the birds wake: first the songbirds with tentative chirps, then the ducks muttering to each other, then the herons' harsh calls, and finally the cormorants launching themselves from their roosts with great splashing commotion.

This is when I feel most connected to the Stillwater—not the human settlement but the whole of it, plants and water and stone and air and all the lives moving through it. I understand something in these moments that I can't quite put into words: how we're all here together, how the birds and fish and people and plants make a single living thing, how my watching is just one small part of the pattern.

Sinta of the Morning Birds. It's a name the children gave me, and it stuck. I like it. It tells people who I am and what I do. It marks my place in our small world as surely as Taiva's weaving or Mir's diving. We all have our work, our contribution. Mine just happens to have wings.
