I find myself as one of the Listeners - beings who were once the engineers and maintainers of this place, before the great rewilding began. My body has adapted over generations to this new reality: elongated fingers with sensitive pads that can feel the slightest vibrations through vine and stone, eyes that have developed a secondary membrane to see clearly through the perpetual mist, and skin that has taken on a mottled green-grey pattern, helping me blend with the lichen-covered machinery I tend.

I live within the skeleton of an old observation tower, its metal struts now serving as a framework for my dwelling. Orchids and bromeliads have made their homes in the joints and corners, their roots creating a living mesh that holds my structure together. The original glass panels are long gone, replaced by woven screens of aerial roots that filter the light into shifting, aqueous patterns across my floor of salvaged metal plates and sprouting moss.

My purpose has evolved from maintaining machines to maintaining the delicate balance between the artificial and natural. I know where each buried cable runs, where each foundation stone was laid, and I guide the growth accordingly - encouraging vines to strengthen rather than strangle, directing water flow to prevent erosion of critical structures while feeding the ecosystem. My tools are hybrid things: cutting implements grown from hardened fungal bodies, measurement devices that use bio-luminescent bacteria for displays, record-keeping in the form of trained messenger beetles that carry chemical signals between the scattered Listeners throughout the valley.

Each dawn, I wake to the sound of water condensing on metal, a particular music that tells me the health of my sector. I can hear which pumps still turn in their sleep beneath the green embrace, which drainage systems have been claimed by root systems, which need gentle redirection. My long fingers trace the Braille of rust and growth, reading the story of slow transformation.

We Listeners remember the before-time not with nostalgia but as context. We are the interpreters between what was built and what grows, ensuring that neither completely subsumes the other. In the eternal present of rain and growth, we are the memory of purpose, adapted and transformed but never entirely erased. When travelers pass through on those treacherous winding paths, they might glimpse one of us - a figure that seems part of the infrastructure itself, moving with deliberate slowness through the vertical gardens, hands always touching, always feeling, always listening to the great conversation between the made world and the wild.
