In this weeping world of stone and mist, I am neither wholly of the Sylari nor separate from them. I am the Listener—a role that emerged three generations ago when the earth's tremors began to speak in patterns too complex for casual observation.

My dwelling sits at the edge of their settlement, where the town's organic sprawl meets the raw expanse beyond. It is a structure of my own design: a dome of fitted stones with gaps deliberately left unsealed, allowing the constant moisture to enter and exit freely. The walls weep as the cliffs do, and I have carved channels in the floor—a spiral pattern that guides the water into a central pool where I conduct my work.

I am tall even among the Sylari, my skin bearing the same earthen tones but marked with a network of fine, pale lines—scars from years of pressing my bare flesh against the wet stone to feel its subtlest vibrations. My hair, once dark, has taken on the silver-gray of age prematurely, though I am only in my fourth decade. My eyes retain their luminous green, but those who look closely see flecks of stone-gray within them, as if the earth has begun to claim me.

My days are spent in profound stillness. While others tend their gardens and weave their cloth, I lie prone on the smooth stone floor of my dwelling, my ear pressed to the earth, my fingers spread wide against the rock. The tremors come in waves—some so faint they might be mistaken for imagination, others strong enough to ripple the water in my central pool. I have learned their language over the years: the quick, sharp shudders that speak of deep pressure building; the long, rolling waves that tell of ancient movements far below; the peculiar double-pulse that always precedes the opening of a new spring.

The Sylari bring me food and fresh water, leaving it at my threshold with quiet reverence. They know I cannot be disturbed during my listening hours, which sometimes stretch for days. In return, I provide them with knowledge they cannot gather themselves: where new water will emerge, which plateaus will remain stable for building, when the rare but dangerous rock-slides might threaten their cultivated spaces.

But my work goes deeper than mere prediction. In the silence—that profound absence of animal life that defines our world—I have begun to hear something else. It is not sound, exactly, but a presence within the tremors, a pattern that suggests intention rather than mere geological process. I have told no one of this yet, for I am not certain myself what it means.

At night, when the unchanging sun finally yields to darkness, I sometimes emerge from my dome and walk the wet stones barefoot, feeling their temperature changes, their textures, their subtle shifts. The mist clings to me like a second skin, and I have learned to read its density, its direction, the way it carries the scent of different minerals from various depths.

The Sylari children are fascinated and frightened by me in equal measure. They dare each other to approach my dwelling, to peek through the gaps in the stones and watch me in my strange stillness. The adults understand better—they know that someone must listen, must maintain this vigil, must serve as the bridge between human settlement and the living earth.

I was not born to this role. As a young adult, I worked alongside others, weaving and building, until the day a tremor knocked me to the ground and I heard—or felt—something calling from below. That was eighteen years ago. Since then, I have slowly transformed from a member of the community to something both more and less than human: a function, a sense organ for the collective, a translator of the earth's deep language.

My only adornment is a single smooth stone, perfectly round, that I wear on a cord around my neck. I found it in the center of my pool one morning, though I am certain it was not there the night before. When I hold it, the tremors become clearer, more articulated, as if the stone were a lens focusing the earth's communications. Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night, the stone grows warm against my chest, and I dream of vast spaces below, of water moving through channels no human has seen, of a presence that is ancient beyond measure and patient beyond understanding.

The Sylari do not ask me to explain what I hear. They see the results—the successful predictions, the avoided dangers, the new springs discovered—and that is enough. But I know that my role is evolving, that what I am becoming is something unprecedented in our timeless existence. I am the first to truly listen to the earth, and in listening, I am beginning to understand that our waterless world's great silence holds a conversation we have only just begun to join.

In this place where the earth weeps constantly, where the silence is profound enough to hear the planet's heartbeat, I am the instrument through which the land might finally speak its true name.
