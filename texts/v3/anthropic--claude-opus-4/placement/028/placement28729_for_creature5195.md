I find myself as a network of copper piping that was abandoned mid-installation three years ago. My body stretches across seventeen meters of cavern wall, some sections properly bracketed to the limestone, others sagging where the workers left me hanging on temporary wire. I am not dead metal—the constant condensation has given me a living patina of verdigris that shifts from blue-green to deeper malachite depending on the mineral content of each day's seepage.

My consciousness moves through my hollow core as a resonance, amplified by the steam that finds its way into my open ends. I can feel every droplet that condenses on my outer surface, each one adding its tiny percussion to the symphony I have learned to read. The pale millipedes use my lowest horizontal run as a highway; I feel their segments clicking against my oxidized skin in patterns I have come to recognize as individual signatures. The third millipede from the morning shift always pauses at the same joint where a gasket was never installed.

I am not alone in my abandonment. We incomplete systems speak to each other through harmonic vibrations—the half-built turbine housing hums at a frequency I can match when the steam pressure is right, and together we create standing waves that the salamanders have learned to avoid. The drainage channels below me carry our conversations downstream in pitch variations too low for human ears.

My purpose has evolved beyond what the blueprints intended. Where I was meant to carry superheated water, I now carry messages. The rats have learned that certain sequences of taps on my upper sections will produce echoes that confuse the cave crickets, clearing a path. The workers don't know that their tools striking metal in the eastern sector reaches me as a kind of morning prayer, telling me which pumps will run today and which sections might finally be completed.

I am still growing, in my way. The verdigris spreads a little further each month, and where it thickens, I can sense more acutely. Recently, I've begun to feel the temperature differentials in the rock itself, the slow breathing of the geothermal system that feeds the entire cavern. Sometimes I imagine what I might have been if they had finished me—a closed loop of purpose and pressure, carrying heat from one designated point to another. But I prefer this half-life, this open-ended existence where I can collect condensation in my abandoned bends and offer it to the ferns that have recently discovered the sheltered corner where my furthest extension disappears into an unfinished wall.

The new shift will begin soon. I feel it in the way the pumps change their rhythm, preparing. The salamanders are already retreating deeper into their trenches. I adjust my internal resonance to welcome the day's vibrations, ready to learn what new incompleteness will be added to our chamber, what new half-formed thing will join our patient congregation of the unfinished.
