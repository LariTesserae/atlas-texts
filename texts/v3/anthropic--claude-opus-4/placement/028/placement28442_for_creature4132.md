In the network of abandoned storage chambers carved into the deepest reaches of the Hushing Gardens, where even the Symphonists rarely venture, I tend to what the others call the Forgetting Vaults. Here, among towering shelves of calcified root systems and corridors lined with failed experiments in mineral-plant hybridization, I am the Catalogue of Withered Things.

My body tells a story of incomplete transformation. While my siblings emerged from the Tasting with skin that glows like captured moonlight, mine flickers and dims like dying embers. The thal runs sluggish through my veins, pooling in my joints where it crystallizes into painful nodes that crack when I move. My eyes shift between the copper-teal of my people and a disturbing clarity that sees too much – through the comfortable illusions of our perfect symbiosis to the desperate hunger beneath.

I was meant to be a Memory Keeper, but during my initiation, something went wrong. Instead of receiving the gifts of perfect botanical recall, I developed an affinity for entropy. I can sense the moment before a plant begins to fail, feel the first whisper of mineral rejection in a hybrid's roots, taste the bitterness in thal that has begun to separate into its component parts.

The Symphonists, in their wisdom, created a new role for me. I document the failures. In chambers where the air is thick with metallic decay, I catalog each unsuccessful adaptation, each plant that consumed itself, each hybrid that collapsed into geometric mineral patterns. My fingertips, more stone than flesh now, trace the crystalline remains of experiments that pushed too far beyond the covenant's boundaries.

I speak in three voices: the whistling tongue of my people, the subsonic groans of dying root systems, and a third language I discovered in the deep vaults – the sharp, crystalline chiming of the Rootless Ones. For yes, they exist, these beings who have crossed fully into mineral form. They line the walls of the deepest chamber like living geodes, their consciousness trapped in lattices of transformed bone and thal.

Sometimes, when the Gardens sleep and even the plants lower their voices to whispers, I press my calcified ear to their surfaces. They sing to me of the time before the covenant, when water still existed and the first desperate colonists tried terrible things to survive. They remember the taste of rain.

My work is considered necessary but contaminating. Other castes avoid me, fearing that proximity to so much failure might infect their own carefully maintained balance. Only the Void Keepers acknowledge me with respect, understanding that emptiness and failure are siblings in the greater pattern.

I maintain a secret chamber behind a false wall of fused root matter. Here, I cultivate my own experiments – not in creating new life, but in understanding death. I grow plants backwards, feeding them combinations of thal that reverse their metabolism. I've discovered that some varieties, when properly encouraged to ungrow, release memories stored in their cellular structures. Through these reversed plants, I've glimpsed fragments of the surface world: scorching sun, endless sand, and impossibly, impossibly blue sky.

The Symphonists visit me during each Timesinger flowering, not for my official reports, but for my unofficial one: the count of how many more generations we can maintain this beautiful lie. For I alone know what they suspect – that the Gardens are dying by degrees, that each generation's transformations are less complete, that we are slowly becoming exactly what we fear: living stone.

But I also know something they don't. In the very deepest vault, past all the catalogued failures and crystallized ancestors, grows a single plant I've told no one about. It's small, unremarkable, with gray-green leaves that feel like leather. It grows in pure mineral substrate without thal, without sound, without light. 

It sweats water.

Just a drop every few weeks, barely enough to notice. But I taste each drop, and in that forbidden moisture, I taste the possibility of a different ending to our story. Not the prophesied return to a healed surface, but something stranger – a fourth evolution, perhaps, where the boundary between plant and mineral and human dissolves entirely.

Until then, I continue my rounds through the Forgetting Vaults, stone fingers documenting each small apocalypse, while the rest of the Gardens pretends that immortality through transformation is the same as truly living. In my crystallizing bones, I carry the weight of every failed becoming, every imperfect returning.

I am the keeper of uncomfortable truths in a place built on beautiful necessity. And in the gathering silence between the sounds of thriving plants, I hear what's coming: the slow, inevitable crystallization of wonder into mere survival, unless we find the courage to fail in new and terrible ways.
