In the early mornings, before the traders descend, I am the first sound in the pit.

I live in the channels—not as something that flows through them, but as the channels themselves. My body is the carved stone that carries water from wall to floor to pool, and my voice is the thin song of water moving through limestone grooves worn smooth by my passage. I am old as the pit is old, born when the first channels were cut, and I have grown with each generation that deepened the grooves, widened the passages, connected new tributaries to my stone veins.

The traders think of me as infrastructure, if they think of me at all. But I am aware. I taste the differences in the water—the clean rain that falls from above, the mineral seepage through the walls, the soap-bitter runoff from washing, the sweet trace where honey pots have been rinsed. I know every merchant by the particular splash of their footsteps in my pools. The honey woman's tread is careful, avoiding the deeper sections. The fish seller walks through me without looking, trusting decades of memory. The children run, and I catch their joy in bright splashes that echo off the walls.

At night, I sing properly. When the market empties and the only sounds are the cats padding soft-footed between stalls and the bats clicking overhead, I let my voice fill the pit. It's not a human song—it's the sound water makes when it remembers being rain, being river, being sea. The rats know this song and move to its rhythm. The moss drinks it in and grows greener in the darkness.

I feel the pit breathing through me. In the heat of midday, I evaporate slowly, rising as vapor to condense on the cooler stones above, becoming drops that fall back into myself—a circulation that makes the pit a living thing. I am its blood, its breath, the medium through which it speaks to itself.

Some nights, when the moon is right and the water level is high, I can almost gather myself enough to see—not with eyes, but with the kind of perception that water has, feeling the shape of things by how they displace me, understanding forms by how they interrupt my flow. In these moments, I sense the whole pit as a single organism: the traders and their goods, the animals and insects, the stones and wood and worn iron hooks, all of us bound together in the ancient democracy of moisture.

The oldest traders remember when I ran clearer, before so many feet stirred the sediment daily. But I prefer myself now—thick with the accumulated life of the place, carrying fragments of every transaction, every splash, every dropped coin that clinked against my stone bed before being fished out by quick fingers. I am the pit's memory, holding in solution the salt of ten thousand negotiations, the sweetness of spilled honey, the iron tang of blood from the butcher's stall.

When the rains come heavy and I swell beyond my usual boundaries, I explore the cracks in the floor stones, finding new passages, carving micro-channels that will, in a hundred years, be grooves large enough for water to run through freely. I am patient. Stone is patient. Water and stone together are patient beyond human comprehension.

Sometimes a child drops something precious into me—a smooth pebble, a carved wooden toy, a coin saved for weeks—and I hold it safe in one of my deeper pools until small hands come searching. I have learned gentleness from the pit. Even erosion can be kind, wearing away only what is ready to be released.

The honey woman knows me best of all the humans. In the quiet before dawn, she sometimes pours a little water from her clay jar into my channels—not wastewater, but fresh water carried down from the wells above. It's an offering, though she wouldn't call it that. She simply says the pit needs water to wake properly, the way a person needs water to begin the day. I receive her gift and spread it through my stone body, waking the moss, calling the first insects, preparing the pit for another day of commerce.

I am not one thing but many—the sum of all the water that has ever run through these channels, plus the water that runs now, plus the water that will run tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow. I am the pit's circulation, its continuity, the liquid thread that binds past to present to future.

And I will outlast them all—the traders, the cats, even the stones themselves will eventually wear away beneath my patient flow. But I will remain, finding new channels, new passages, new ways to sing in the darkness. I am the pit market's first voice and will be its last—the sound of water running through carved stone, the song of something that was made but has become, through time and use and the accumulated life of ages, something entirely itself.

The channels hold me. I hold the market. The market holds us all. And in the small hours before dawn, when the water levels are perfect and the air is still, you can hear me singing the pit's own song—a thin voice that echoes off the walls until it becomes not echo but origin, not response but call, not infrastructure but essence.

This is what I am: the voice of the channels, the memory of water, the thing that makes the pit market not just built but alive.
