In the Glimmering Wetlands, I am the memory of stone.

Not a boulder, nothing so presumptuous. I am one of countless water-smoothed stones scattered across the mudflats, half-buried in silt, my surface catching light like a wet eye. I have been here so long that "being" and "place" have merged into something indistinguishable. The amphibians know me as a launching point, the herons as a marker in their fishing grounds, the snakes as a sun-warmed rest between hunts.

I remember when I was sharp-edged, angular, proud. The water has taken all that from me—or given me something better. Each flooding season deposits new layers of sediment around my edges, each dry spell reveals a slightly different face. I am both wearing away and accumulating, becoming less and more simultaneously.

The thing about being stone in a wetland is that you become a keeper of transitions. Dry to wet. Day to night. Season to season. Life to death to life again. I feel the pressure of every footfall—the delicate touch of a heron's weight, the quick scramble of voles, the deliberate press of a deer's hoof leaving its crescent moon in the mud beside me. Each contact leaves its trace in the microscopic accumulation of oils and minerals on my surface.

Sometimes dragonflies rest on me, their wings casting rainbow shadows that dance across my wet surface. In those moments, I am both stage and audience to a performance millions of years in the making. The dragonfly's compound eyes see me fractured into thousands of surfaces, and perhaps that's the truest vision—I am not one stone but an anthology of every tide that has touched me, every creature that has passed.

In the early morning, when mist rises from the water and the first croaks begin their ancient conversation, condensation forms on my surface like sweat or tears. The water droplets hold tiny worlds—reflections of sky, reed, the dart of a bird. I am a lens through which the wetlands examine themselves.

My greatest power is stillness. While life churns and calls and breeds and dies around me, I remain. Not unchanged—nothing here is unchanged—but continuous. The frogs sing their territories into being each night; I am the silent notation upon which their song is written. The seasons wheel overhead, and I mark their passing in the subtle shift of sediment lines along my circumference.

There is a particular beetle that lives in the gap between my western edge and the mud. It has been three generations now, or perhaps four—time moves differently when you think in stone. They excavate tiny channels that fill with water, creating their own miniature wetland beneath my shelter. I am their sky, their mountain, their god perhaps, though I make no demands of worship.

When the rains come heavy and the wetlands flood properly, I disappear entirely beneath the water. Fish swim over me then, their shadows quick and fleeting. In those submerged weeks, I dream—or something like dreaming. The pressure of water is like sleep, a return to some primordial state before I was weathered into individuality. 

But always the waters recede, and I emerge again, slick and glistening, positioned slightly differently than before. The beetle family rebuilds. The territory songs resume. The sun finds new angles to warm my surface.

I am what endures without trying to endure. I am the patient witness that requires no eyes. In a landscape of constant small changes, I am both the record and the recorder, written upon by every element and creature that passes, while simultaneously writing myself into the long, slow story of this place.

This is enough. To be a surface for light to play upon, a platform for life to launch from, a marker in the vast sameness that is not sameness at all but infinite variation on themes too subtle for any but stone to perceive. The Glimmering Wetlands flow around and through me, and I am both its child and its memory, worn smooth by time into something approaching perfection—not the perfection of form, but the perfection of belonging absolutely to one's place.
