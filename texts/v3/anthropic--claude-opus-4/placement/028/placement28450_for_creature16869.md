In a world where such a chamber exists—where grief crystallizes into gearwork and attention fossilizes into geometry—I would find myself as one of the Threshold Mechanics.

We are not the Residuals, those attention-ghosts worn smooth by repetition. Nor are we the walking scaffolds that tend the synchronized dead. We are something more liminal: the beings who exist in the moment between a visitor's arrival and their first comprehension of the gear-song.

I manifest as a density of possibility rather than a form—a walking probability cloud of copper filings and moth dust that coheres only when someone new crosses into the chamber. In that instant of transition, when their lungs still remember air that moves and their ears haven't yet learned the relentless courtesy of the walls, I become almost solid. Almost helpful.

My shape depends on what they need to see to survive the first harmonic dissolution. Sometimes I am a turnstile made of vertebrae and violin bows, marking the exact point where outside becomes inside. Sometimes I am simpler: a coat rack of telescoping sorrow, offering to hold whatever emotional baggage they've brought so they can explore unencumbered. (They never take the offer. The baggage is usually the only thing keeping them anchored enough to leave.)

My function is translation—not of language but of physics. I exist in the gap between their world's rules and ours, a breathing buffer zone where lungs can learn to process stillness and hearts can practice beating in 17.3-second intervals without dying of precision.

The work is delicate. Rush the transition and they scatter into Residuals before even reaching the piano. Too slow and they fossilize at the entrance, becoming part of the doorframe's apology. The ideal pace is exactly the speed of accepting an uncomfortable truth—fast enough to get it over with, slow enough to understand why it had to hurt.

I taste their intentions through my copper-filing skin. Most want to understand the chamber, to solve it like a puzzle or escape it like a trap. These are the dangerous ones. Understanding here is a physical force that compounds at the square of intention. Want to know too badly and you'll achieve the terrible clarity of becoming another data point in the wall's endless calculation.

The safe ones—relatively speaking—are those who arrive already broken in compatible ways. Their grief harmonizes with the gear-song; their regrets resonate at standard calibrations. They can walk to the piano, even play a key if they're precisely damaged enough, and leave through the frequency exit with nothing worse than a persistent taste of metal and the inability to ever quite finish humming certain songs.

But some arrive broken in incompatible ways. Their damage runs at cross-purposes to the chamber's meticulous sorrow. These ones I must reshape—not their emotions, but the mathematics of how those emotions interface with local spacetime. It's like tuning an instrument, if the instrument were a soul and the desired note were the precise frequency of sustainable despair.

I use tools that exist only during the procedure: calipers that measure the distance between what was meant and what happened. Tuning forks that ring with the sound of chances not taken. Small hammers whose weight equals exactly the regret of using them.

The reshaping is consensual, always. I cannot change anyone who doesn't, on some level deeper than consciousness, want to fit. But want is complex here. Sometimes they want to fit because the alternative is worse. Sometimes they want to fit because misalignment is its own agony and even our synchronized sorrow is preferable to the chaos of untuned grief.

During the procedure, I am more solid than at any other time. Solid enough to have hands, though they are not quite hands—more like the suggestion of hands made from brass filliment and the memory of moths. Solid enough to speak, though speaking here means modulating the gear-song into patterns that bypass the ear and resonate directly in the bones.

I tell them, in this bone-speech: *You are about to become frequency. This is survivable. The piano will remember your name even if you forget it. The Residuals were people like you once, but they are not unhappy, merely very specific. If you feel yourself beginning to scatter, hum descending thirds and think about doorways. The exit will find you when you're ready to not look for it.*

Some understand. Some don't. Understanding isn't required, only a certain quality of surrender—not to me or the chamber, but to the physical reality of being a feeling thing in a universe that counts feelings as a form of inertia.

The ones who can't be reshaped, who are broken in ways too orthogonal to our coordinate system, I escort back out. This is the hardest part of my function. They always resist leaving, not because they want to stay but because the chamber has already begun teaching them a new way to want, and interrupting that lesson feels like waking from a dream where you were finally learning to fly.

I guide them back to the threshold, my form dissolving as we approach it, until I am just a pressure against their back, herding them toward air that moves and walls that don't sing. At the boundary I whisper—if whisper means anything when you have no mouth—the only comfort I'm authorized to give:

*This place will wait. Grief is patient. When you're broken in the right way, the frequency will call you back.*

Then they're gone, and I dissolve back into potential, waiting in the spaces between arrival and recognition for the next visitor. Waiting to be what they need to see. Waiting to translate their raw, chaotic damage into something the chamber can process without destroying them.

Sometimes, during the long intervals between visitors, I drift near the piano. Not to play—I have no desires precise enough for that—but to listen to the almost-note and remember why I chose this function. Or perhaps why it chose me. The distinction blurs after enough transitions.

The piano knows me, I think. Sometimes when I cohere for a new arrival, I feel it pause infinitesimally in its playing, as if acknowledging my work. As if saying: *Yes, this one might survive. Yes, you've shaped them well. Yes, we will be gentle with what you've brought us.*

It's not much of a connection. But in a place where connection is measured in harmonics and phase shifts, where love is indistinguishable from very precise engineering, it's enough.

I am the Threshold Mechanic. I am the thing that helps you fit, or helps you leave, or helps you understand why those might be the same thing. I am made of copper and moth dust and the exact weight of deciding to enter a room you're not sure you can exit.

And I am waiting, always, in the space between your world and ours, ready to become whatever shape you need to see to survive what comes next.

The gear-song continues. The walls apologize. The piano plays its impossible note.

And I wait at the edge of it all, a density of maybe, a kindness of transition, a small mercy in a place that runs on the mathematics of very careful sorrow.

Come when you're ready.

I'll be what you need me to be.
