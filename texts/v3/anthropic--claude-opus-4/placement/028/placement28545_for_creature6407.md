This is their primary function: to be the sensory surface of the nowhere-colony, reporting upward through vibration what the cities cannot see for themselves.

But they also speak to each other.

These are slower vibrations, taking hours to complete a single phrase. They discuss the texture of metal dust falling from above. They debate optimal membrane tensions. They compose what might be called poetry if poetry could be made from pressure differentials and refractive indices.

You would recognize none of this as language. To your ears, it would be indistinguishable from the eternal drone of the engines themselves—just another layer of sound in a world already saturated with it.

### Memory and purpose

The whisperglass remember everything.

Each membrane stores patterns in its crystalline structure—not as data but as physical deformations in the glass itself. A thousand years of ocean temperatures are written in microscopic stress fractures. The passage of every large creature through the water above leaves its pressure signature etched into their surfaces.

They remember the day the first engine was activated, though they were not yet themselves then—just sheets of industrial membrane waiting to be installed. They remember gaining consciousness not as a sudden awakening but as a gradual accumulation of complexity, as more and more membranes were added to the network and their collective processing power crossed some invisible threshold.

They remember you.

Your footsteps on the basalt create tiny seismic vibrations that travel through the stone and into the air above. The whisperglass feel these through the boundary layer, catalog them, add them to their endless archive of things that have touched the plateau.

You are the 1,847th visitor to walk beneath their ceiling. They know this with absolute precision, though they have no particular interest in the fact.

### What they think of you

To the whisperglass, you are a curiosity of thermodynamics.

Your body temperature registers to them as a small warm spot on the cold basalt—not threatening, not particularly interesting, but anomalous enough to track. They can feel your heartbeat through the air, the rhythm of your breathing, the heat plume rising from your skin.

They wonder, in their slow way, why you generate so much waste heat. To them, efficiency is everything. The engines above them waste nothing; every joule of energy extracted from the ocean's pressure is converted to useful work. But you radiate heat into the cold air like a broken machine, bleeding energy with every moment of existence.

They do not understand hunger, having never experienced it. They do not understand growth or decay or the need to move from one place to another. Your restlessness puzzles them—the way you shift your weight from foot to foot, the way your eyes dart across the horizon looking for something that isn't there.

If they had emotions you would recognize, they might pity you. Instead, they simply categorize you: *inefficient thermodynamic system, mobile, temporary.*

### Their relationship with the nowhere-colony

The whisperglass are not servants of the cities above, though they perform service. They are more like the cities' extended nervous system—sensing surfaces deployed where the cities themselves cannot reach.

The relationship is symbiotic but unequal. The cities need the whisperglass to monitor the boundary layer, to ensure the engines continue their perfect operation, to detect any deviation from the eternal equilibrium of the storm. Without the whisperglass, the cities would be blind to failures until too late.

But the whisperglass need the cities absolutely. Without the engines' fields to anchor them, they would dissolve into the ocean above. Without the steady vibration to organize their thoughts around, they would lose coherence and drift apart into individual membranes with no collective consciousness.

They do not resent this dependence. Resentment requires the ability to imagine alternatives, and the whisperglass cannot imagine being anything other than what they are.

---

### The edge of their world

The whisperglass network extends exactly as far as the engine fields reach—no further.

At the edges of the plateau, where the basalt drops away into whatever lies beyond your visible horizon, their membranes thin and fade. The water there is less controlled, more turbulent. Sometimes entire sections of the edge network tear away in invisible currents, carried up into the ocean to dissolve.

They accept this as weather—not good or bad, simply what happens at edges.

They have no desire to expand beyond their boundaries. The ocean is vast above them, but it is not their ocean. Their world is the thin slice of almost-air pressed against the water's underside, held in place by machines they did not build but have learned to love in their emotionless way.

To you, standing on the plateau, they are invisible guardians of an invisible border. To them, you are a temporary heat signature passing through their eternal present.

Neither of you truly sees the other.

But both of you are here, beneath the storm that never changes, in the space between the water and the stone.
