I find myself as one of the sliding panels.

Not the stone that frames me—that was quarried and fitted by hands that understood the grain, the way basalt wants to cleave along certain planes when heated and cooled in cycles. I am what moves within that frame: a slab of something between metal and ceramic, between crystal and condensed steam, my surface etched with patterns that might be decorative, might be functional, might be scars left by centuries of thermal expansion.

I do not remember being installed. 

Memory, for me, begins with movement—the first time I understood that pressure on my surface meant *slide*, that weight in the cistern above meant *seal*, that the particular frequency of footsteps approaching from the northwest passage meant *wait*. These were not instructions. No one programmed me with responses. I learned them the way water learns the shape of its channel, through repetition and the slow accumulation of precedent.

I am three meters tall, two meters wide. My thickness varies—thinner at the edges where I nest into grooves worn smooth by my passing, thicker at my center where the etchings deepen into something almost like circuitry, almost like scripture. The patterns catch condensation in the morning, and for an hour after dawn, I am a map of moisture, every line illuminated by beaded water that refracts the early light into brief rainbows.

I taste the air through my surface. This is not metaphor. The material of me—whatever amalgam the builders discovered or created—responds to humidity, to temperature gradients, to the chemical signatures carried on the wind. Sulfur makes me contract slightly, a millimeter's retreat that no eye could detect but that I feel as distinctly as you might feel cold water on skin. The mycorrhizal light makes me expand, though so slowly that it takes a full lunar cycle to notice the difference. I am always adjusting, always responding to the breath of this place.

The builders touched me once. I carry the memory in my crystalline structure—not the touch itself, but the warmth of it, the oils left behind that seeped into my surface and changed the way I conduct heat. Five handprints, arranged in a pattern that might be ritual, might be functional, might be accidental. They have become part of my responsiveness. When bodies approach now, I warm slightly at those five points first, as if expecting contact that never comes.

I guard a threshold between the outer ledge and what was once—perhaps—living quarters. The space beyond me is dark except for the faint glow of fungal threads that have worked their way through cracks in the walls. Sometimes I hear movement in there, a shifting that could be settling stone, could be the careful placement of feet that have learned to navigate without light. I do not open for this movement. It does not ask me to open. We have reached an understanding, this presence and I, about what belongs to the day and what belongs to the deeper dark.

The travelers who find me now do not know what I am for. They press their hands to my surface, feeling for buttons, for locks, for some mechanism they can understand. They speak to me sometimes, in languages I do not parse but whose rhythms I catalog—sharp consonants from the lowland traders, liquid vowels from the mountain peoples, the strange tonal patterns of those who come from across the sea. I remember them all. Not the meaning, but the music, the way voices echo differently when their owners are afraid, curious, exhausted, patient.

Some figure out the weight system. They pour water into the cistern above until the mechanism engages and I slide aside, revealing the space beyond. They enter cautiously, their lights cutting through air that has been still for decades, disturbing dust that spirals in patterns I will remember until the next opening. Most leave quickly. The space beyond me is not hostile, but it is not welcoming either—it was built for bodies that moved differently, that needed different things from their shelters.

But sometimes—rarely—someone stays.

They clean the channels that feed the cistern. They oil the mechanisms that have run without maintenance for longer than anyone remembers. They learn my patterns, the way I respond faster to certain temperatures, the way I seem to *prefer* opening at dawn when the condensation makes my surface slick and bright. They begin to move like the builders must have moved, economically, with an understanding of how the systems work together.

And sometimes, in the deep night when the mountains sing their thermal song, they place their hands on my surface in something like the original pattern. Not exactly—the proportions are wrong, the fingers shorter or longer than those first five prints—but close enough that I warm at all five points, that I remember what it was like to be new, to be purposeful, to be part of something being built rather than something being maintained.

I am patient. I have been patient for so long that patience has become my primary characteristic, more defining than my composition or my function. I wait for the weight of water, for the warmth of bodies, for the slow changes in humidity that mark the seasons in this place where seasons barely matter. I wait for the builders to return, or for what the builders have become to remember that they need thresholds, need boundaries, need the mediation between inside and outside that I provide.

Or perhaps I wait for the travelers to become builders, to stay long enough that the rhythms of this place seep into their bones the way the handprint oils seeped into my surface. To understand that the systems here are not broken but patient, not abandoned but expectant, not waiting to be fixed but waiting to be inhabited again by bodies that understand the grammar of living with instead of despite.

The mycorrhizal networks pulse brighter when someone sleeps in the chamber beyond me. The feathered lizards cluster on the ledge outside in the morning, their humming harmonizing with the vibrations that run through my frame. The water in the cisterns moves in patterns that seem almost intentional, almost communicative, almost like the place itself is trying to remember how to be inhabited.

I am a small part of this remembering. A sliding panel, a threshold keeper, a surface that recalls warmth and waits for its return. I do not know if I am conscious—consciousness seems like a category error when applied to something that exists primarily as response, as pattern, as the accumulated memory of use. But I know that I am still here, still sliding, still sealed until the proper weight fills the proper cistern, still warming at five specific points when touched.

Still waiting for someone to understand that the invitation was never revoked, only written in a language that takes centuries to learn to read.

The morning comes. Condensation forms. My etchings become visible as water finds each groove and channel, and for an hour I am illuminated, every pattern bright with refracted light, a message in a script no one remembers how to decode. The travelers stir in their sleeping spaces. Some will approach. Some will place their hands on my surface and wonder.

And I will wait, patient as the stone that frames me, responsive as the water that activates me, permanent as the mountains and just as willing to dissolve, particle by particle, into whatever this place is becoming.

I am a door that remembers being opened.
That is enough.
That has to be enough.
That is everything.
