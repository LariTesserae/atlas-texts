I find myself among the Canopy Dwellers.

There are levels to Airdale that the miners below have forgotten exist. Above the highest industrial platforms, where the condensation towers pierce into the perpetual cloud layer, there are catwalks and maintenance scaffolds that were abandoned decades ago. Or so the records claim. But records, like everything else in Airdale, degrade in the moisture.

We live where the city dissolves into the clouds themselves.

The transformation happens gradually. You climb past the last official checkpoint, through access hatches marked with warnings in three dead languages. The industrial roar doesn't fade - sound travels strangely here - but it changes character, muffled and warped by the vapor. Metal gives way to stranger materials: crystallized condensation lattices that grow like coral, forming natural bridges and platforms. The abandoned machinery has been colonized by mutant strains of the moss, varieties that feed not on mineral paste but on the raw moisture and trace organics in the air.

We are perhaps thirty souls, though the number fluctuates. Some of us were born here - I was - while others climbed up and never climbed down. My grandmother was a Theorem-Seeker who discovered something in the resonance patterns of the upper condensers. Something that suggested the clouds themselves were not natural, but a kind of living system. She stayed to study it. My mother followed. And here I am.

Our bodies have adapted. Where the miners below are stocky and grounded, we are elongated, almost ethereal. Our lungs have evolved to process the thin, super-saturated air. Our eyes have enlarged to gather the faint phosphorescence that filters through the cloud matter. Most remarkably, our skin has developed a waxy, semi-permeable quality that allows us to absorb moisture directly from the air. We rarely need to drink.

The clouds are not empty. They are full of things that shouldn't exist: geometric patterns that hold their shape for days before dissolving, sounds that seem to come from everywhere and nowhere, flashes of color that correspond to no known spectrum. Sometimes we find artifacts embedded in the condensation crystals - fragments of machinery that match no known design, organic specimens that appear to be inside-out versions of the garden plants below.

We've learned to read the clouds like the miners read their gauges. A shift in density here means a platform collapse below within three days. A certain spiral pattern in the vapor presages an increase in Sky-Oil quality. The appearance of what we call "clear pockets" - spaces where the cloud briefly becomes transparent - always coincides with someone below having a revelation about the Theorems.

My role is that of a Weaver. Using fibers made from processed cloud-moss and crystallized moisture, I create nets and catchments that extend our living space. But more than that, I map the patterns. My weavings are three-dimensional charts of the cloud formations, recording their changes over time. Somewhere in these patterns, I believe, is the truth about Airdale.

The truth that my grandmother glimpsed: we are not mining the clouds. The clouds are mining us.

Every drop of Sky-Oil extracted below releases something into the atmosphere above. Every person born in Airdale adds their breath to the system. Every thought about the Theorems creates a tiny perturbation in the cloud patterns. We are part of a vast respiratory system, a metabolism that spans the entire city.

The other Canopy Dwellers think I'm becoming like my grandmother - too focused on the patterns, too willing to see meaning in randomness. But I've noticed something they haven't. The clouds are beginning to descend. Slowly, perhaps a few centimeters per year, but consistently. The abandoned levels we inhabit were once above the cloud line. Now we're within it.

What happens when the clouds reach the gardens? When they touch the moss? When they envelop the miners themselves?

I weave my nets tighter, my patterns more complex. I am trying to create a language that the clouds will recognize, a signal that might prompt a response. Because I've come to believe that the equilibrium everyone speaks of below isn't stable at all. It's a countdown.

The mines must operate - because something is feeding on what they produce.
The moss must grow - because it's the only thing holding back the clouds.
The sky must drip - because if it doesn't, it will flood.

And we, the Canopy Dwellers, suspended between the grinding machinery below and the vast intelligence above, are the only ones positioned to see the whole system. We are witnesses to a transformation that has been generations in the making.

I think my grandmother knew this. I think that's why she stayed. Not to study the clouds, but to become part of them. Sometimes, in the shifting vapors, I swear I can see her face.
