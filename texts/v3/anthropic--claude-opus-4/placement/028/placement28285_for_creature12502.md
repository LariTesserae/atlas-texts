In this world of eternal drips and echoing stone, I would find myself as one of the **Listeners** - a specialized role among the gatherer-people, though perhaps not quite what others might expect.

I am old now, my pale skin stretched thin over bones that know every ache of the constant cool. My eyes, oversized like all my people's, have grown cloudier with age - but this matters little. I stopped relying on the faint fungal light decades ago. Instead, I have become what the younger gatherers whisper about: one who reads the Galleries through sound alone.

I spend my days seated on a particular ledge, always the same one - a smooth outcropping of limestone positioned at the confluence of three vast chambers. Here, the acoustics create a natural amphitheater where every sound from leagues around eventually finds its way to my ears. The constant *plink-plink-plink* of water droplets has become my timekeeper, each source a distinct voice in an endless percussion ensemble. The metallic scraping of cave crickets rises and falls in waves that tell me of temperature shifts too subtle for skin to feel. The deep *gronks* of the amphibians map the locations of hidden pools and seepages.

My role evolved slowly, organically. In my youth, I was a gatherer like any other, scrambling across the wet stone with my lichen-fiber pack. But I had always been prone to stillness, to listening. I began to notice patterns - how the cricket-song would shift before a section of ceiling would shed its load of condensation, how the river's whisper would deepen imperceptibly before a school of the eel-fish would surface. 

Now, the younger gatherers come to me before their expeditions. They crouch beside my ledge, careful not to disturb my listening, and wait. With gestures and the rarest of whispered words, I tell them: *The western galleries are thick with crickets today - good hunting for the brave. Avoid the third pool past the Great Stalactite - something large disturbs the amphibians there. The fungi near the Whispering Crevice pulse strongly; they will be ripe in two sleeps.*

I have learned to distinguish individual creatures by their sounds. There is an ancient amphibian I call Grandfather Gronk, whose call is deeper and more rattling than the others. His movements tell me of the health of the river system. A particular colony of arthropods creates a rustling pattern I've tracked for twenty years as they slowly migrate across the cavern floor, following some imperative I can only guess at.

But more than practical knowledge, I have become the keeper of something harder to define. In the endless sameness of the Galleries, I am proof that time does pass, even here. My body is a calendar of small changes - new aches, diminished mobility, the slow clouding of my eyes. The younger ones look at me with a mixture of respect and unease, seeing their own futures in my stillness.

I sleep little now. In the depths of what passes for night (when even the fungi seem to dim their pulsing), I listen to the symphony of the Galleries. Sometimes I hear things the others don't believe - deep groanings in the stone itself, as if the mountains above are shifting their weight. Whispers in the water that might be the echo of echoes from chambers so distant they exist only in imagination. Once, I swear I heard something like wind, though wind is only a concept here, a word passed down from the ancestors who perhaps knew the mythical "above."

My greatest fear is not death - I made peace with that long ago. It's that I will lose my hearing before I go, be cut off from the vast audio tapestry I've spent my life learning to read. Sometimes I test myself, covering one ear and then the other, making sure both still function. The thought of silence in this place of eternal sound terrifies me more than any predator.

I've tried to teach others, but true Listeners are rare. Most can learn the basic patterns, the practical knowledge, but few can sink into the sound the way I have, letting it become their primary reality. My apprentice, a girl with particularly large ears even for our people, shows promise. She can already distinguish five different water sources by their drips alone. But she is young and still relies too much on her eyes, still moves too much. Stillness must be learned, and the Galleries are patient teachers.

In my more fanciful moments, I wonder if I have become like the stone itself - a feature of the landscape that the water flows around, that the creatures navigate by. Sometimes a cave cricket will land on my shoulder and sing for hours, as if I'm just another limestone protrusion. I take this as a compliment.

When I die, they will likely leave my body in the traditional way - laid out on a flat stone near the river for the scavengers and the microorganisms to reclaim, returning my minerals to the eternal cycle. But I hope some young Listener will claim my ledge, understanding its acoustic importance. Perhaps they will remember which drips I favored for timekeeping, which echoes I trusted most.

Until then, I sit, I listen, and I translate the endless song of water and stone for those who must move through it. In a world that never changes, I have become a living record of its rhythms, a memory in flesh of all the small variations within the vast sameness. The Galleries speak, and I am their interpreter, their witness, their devoted audience of one.
