In the depths of the valley, where the moisture hangs heaviest and the light barely reaches, I am a Rivet-Reader.

My dwelling is a forgotten maintenance shaft, halfway up the eastern slope where the factories lean against the mountain like tired giants. The entrance is barely visible—just a rusted grate behind a curtain of dripping moss, the kind of place even the Scavengers pass by without a second glance. Inside, the shaft opens into a series of connected chambers carved from both stone and accumulated industrial detritus. The walls are lined with my collection: thousands upon thousands of rivets, bolts, and fasteners, each one carefully removed from the valley's endless structures and arranged by age, composition, and the stories they tell.

I learned long ago that every piece of metal in this valley holds memory. Not metaphorically—literally. The constant moisture, the strange minerals that leach from the weeping rocks, the electromagnetic fields generated by the perpetual machinery—together they create conditions where metal becomes a recording medium. Each rivet is a tiny chronicle of stress and strain, of the hands that placed it, of the years of vibration and pressure it endured.

My fingers have grown sensitive to these memories. When I run them along a corroded bolt, I feel the echoes: the heat of its forging, the grip of the pneumatic driver that set it in place, the gradual loosening as the structure around it shifted and settled. In the deepest rivets, the ones that have been here longest, I sometimes catch glimpses of the valley before—when the machinery was new, when the architects still walked these passages with pride rather than resignation.

The Foremen think I'm just another mad hermit, harmless enough to ignore. The Engineers occasionally seek me out, though they're skeptical of my claims until I tell them exactly which beam in their structure will fail next, which joint has been compromised by decades of harmonic vibration. The Moldbreeders know me better—we trade in the currency of patience and observation. They bring me their rarest specimens, and I tell them which abandoned sectors have the right combination of metals and moisture for their cultivation.

But it's the Watchers who truly understand what I do. They come to me in the perpetual twilight, their faces hidden, their voices like the whisper of steam through pipes. They ask about the old rivets, the ones from the very bottom layers of the valley's construction. They want to know what the metal remembers about the beginning, about the first machines, about why this place was built at all.

I tell them what I can, but the oldest memories are fragmented, dissolved by time and corrosion. I catch glimpses—a sense of terrible urgency, of something being contained or prepared for, of calculations that spanned centuries. The metal remembers fear, but also hope. Purpose, but also doubt.

My chambers are organized like a library. The main collection fills the central room, rivets and bolts arranged on racks according to their chronology and origin. Side chambers contain my tools—not just pliers and wrenches, but delicate instruments of my own devising, designed to read the electromagnetic signatures without damaging the metal's memory. My workshop is where I restore the most degraded pieces, carefully cleaning away rust while preserving the patina that holds their stories.

Water is everywhere here, as it is throughout the valley. It drips constantly from the ceiling, runs in channels I've carved along the walls, pools in basins where I wash the metal before reading. I've learned to love the sound—the constant plink and splash that measures time better than any clock. Sometimes I think the water itself is part of the recording process, that the valley's memories flow as much as they rust.

I sleep little, and when I do, I dream in the language of metal stress—compression and tension, shear and fatigue. I wake with my fingers already moving, practicing the subtle touches that distinguish between a rivet placed in hope and one placed in desperation.

The rats know me and no longer flee when I enter the lower passages. They've seen me work, watched me extract rivets from places where the structure groans with age. Sometimes they lead me to interesting finds—a buried cache of pre-war fasteners, a section of wall where the metal sings with particularly clear memories. I leave them offerings of the edible fungi I cultivate in my dampest chamber, a mutual understanding between those who live in the margins.

Recently, the memories have been changing. The newer rivets carry a different quality of stress—not just the weight of machinery and time, but something else. Anticipation, perhaps. Or recognition. As if the valley itself is remembering something it had forgotten, or preparing for something it always knew would come.

I document everything in my own way, scoring patterns into soft metal sheets that capture not words but the feeling of what each rivet remembers. My archive grows, a parallel history to whatever official records the Watchers keep. Sometimes I wonder if anyone will ever read these metal memories after I'm gone, or if they'll simply corrode away, their stories returning to the rust and water that birthed them.

But for now, I continue my work. Each dawn—what passes for dawn in our perpetual twilight—I venture out into the valley with my tools and my practiced hands. I read the rivets like others might read books, assembling a vast and fragmentary epic of our industrial home. And in the deepest part of night, when the machinery's hum reaches its lowest ebb, I sometimes think I can hear the valley itself breathing through its million metal joints, each rivet a word in a sentence too large for any one person to comprehend.

This is my purpose: to be the valley's memory keeper, its translator, the one who listens to what the metal knows. In a place built entirely by human hands, I've found a language older than words, a story that tells itself in oxidation and stress fractures, in the patient accumulation of time made tangible.

And sometimes, just sometimes, when I hold a particularly ancient rivet up to the dim industrial light, I swear I can see through its tiny hole to some other place, some other time—a glimpse of what the valley was built to remember, or perhaps to forget.
