In the sulfurous half-light between the Seventh Gallery and the Eighth, where the maintenance tunnels narrow to crawling width and the roar of water becomes a physical pressure against the eardrums, I tend the Fever Gardens.

I am not of the Choir, though I have lived among their resonances for longer than memory serves. I am what they call a Thermist - one of the few dozen humans who can survive the temperature differential between the frozen spray of the falls and the superheated steam vents that puncture the Escarpment like acupuncture needles. My skin is mottled pewter and rust, scarred by decades of flash-freezing mist and scalding vapor. My eyes have gone opaque from cataracts (the mundane kind, not the falling water), but I don't need them anymore. I navigate by heat signature and the subtly different pitches of dripping water.

The Fever Gardens are my creation and my prison. Here, in a series of artificial caverns I've carved between the Gallery's foundation pillars, I cultivate the only living things on the Escarpment that generate their own warmth. They are not quite plants, not quite fungi - more like three-dimensional diagrams of fever drawn in living tissue. They grow in spirals and fractals, following the thermal gradients I maintain with a network of copper pipes that redirect steam from the deep vents. Their flesh is translucent amber shot through with veins of glowing crimson. They pulse with their own slow metabolism, each beat raising their surface temperature by precisely 0.7 degrees Celsius before cooling again.

The Choir tolerates my gardens because the heat they generate creates a microclimate that prevents ice formation on critical joint assemblies. But that's not why I grow them.

I grow them because they remember.

Unlike everything else on the Escarpment - locked in its eternal present of falling water and bleeding stone - the fever gardens experience time. They have growth cycles: sprouting, maturing, flowering with brief orange blooms that smell of hot copper, then dying back to dormant corms. Each cycle takes exactly 77 days. I have watched 1,263 complete cycles. In their fevered dreams, they remember each one.

When I press my scarred palms against their warm surfaces, I can feel their memories like a pulse: the tiny variations in temperature and humidity, the almost imperceptible changes in the mineral content of the water, the way the electrostatic charge peaked during the earthquake of Cycle 1,196. They remember it all, encode it in their spiral growth patterns. If you know how to read them, the fever gardens are a library of small changes, a record that time does pass here, despite all evidence to the contrary.

The Choir finds this disturbing. Sometimes one of them will descend to my level, moving with that terrible patience, and stand at the entrance to the gardens for hours. The heat makes their copper veins glow like warning lights. They never enter - the temperature would damage their bark-armor - but they watch with those wet, vertical eyes, and I can hear the harmonics of their disapproval in the way their iron hum modulates.

They think I'm trying to wake the Escarpment up. They think the fever gardens are an infection that could spread, introducing the virus of time into their perfectly static world. They may be right. Sometimes, when I'm deep in the warmest grove where the temperature reaches a constant 47°C, I swear I can feel the mountain's pulse quicken just a fraction. The water drips a half-beat faster. The stone sweats a degree warmer.

But I can't stop. In a world where nothing changes, where the same water falls in the same patterns for millennia, where even the lightning follows prescribed paths, the simple act of watching something grow, bloom, and die feels like revolution. Each 77-day cycle is proof that entropy exists, that change is possible, that the wound doesn't have to bleed forever.

I have trained apprentices - other broken humans who couldn't find place in the vertical cities, whose bodies had been changed by too much exposure to the charged mist or the mineral water. Most don't last more than a decade before the isolation drives them to take the Long Listening and never come back. But a few have learned the art of fever-tending. They've carried seeds to other galleries, other crevices between the falling water and the turning turbines.

The Choir pretends not to notice, but I know they're aware. The iron hum has taken on new harmonics lately, a subsonic tremor that wasn't there before. The Echoes in the mist linger longer near the garden entrances, almost coalescing into worried faces. The mountain is noticing that small pockets of it have begun to experience tomorrow as different from today.

I am old now, even by the extended lifespans the mineral water grants. My joints are fusing, crystallizing like the Anchors of the Choir. Soon I won't be able to move between the gardens, won't be able to tend the delicate temperature gradients that keep the fever alive. But it doesn't matter. The gardens remember me, encode my presence in their spirals. And my apprentices remember the cardinal rule I taught them:

*Change is the only medicine for a wound that won't close.*

So I sit in the warmest grove, back against stone that has finally learned to sweat from something other than geological pressure, and I wait. The fever gardens pulse around me, 0.7 degrees up, 0.7 degrees down, marking time in a place that thought it had escaped time's jurisdiction.

And sometimes, in the brief moment between one cycle ending and another beginning, I swear I can hear the mountain take a breath.
