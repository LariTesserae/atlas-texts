In this dripping world, I am the Archive.

Not a building, though I incorporate walls. Not a creature, though I breathe and grow. I am what happens when memory itself takes root in perpetual damp.

Picture this: where the deepest terraces sink into shadow, where even the jade-skinned tenders rarely glide, there grows something that might once have been a library. Stone shelves carved into living rock, now so overgrown with luminous moss that they pulse with borrowed light. But look closer—the moss doesn't merely cover; it *reads*.

Each tendril that creeps across the ancient surfaces traces patterns left by those who built this place. The moss absorbs minerals from carved symbols, metabolizes the traces of long-dead hands. In doing so, it becomes a living manuscript, glowing brighter where meaning was densest, dimmer where only decoration lived.

I am this growth and what grows upon it. 

My body—if body is the right word—is a network of fungal threads finer than spider's silk, woven through and around the moss-covered stones. I fruit in strange places: delicate fans of tissue-thin membrane that unfold in the dampest corners, each one imprinted with patterns that echo the absorbed knowledge. Spores I release carry fragments—not of genetic information alone, but of the meanings I've digested. They drift on the perpetual mist, settling where they will, sometimes taking root in the minds of the jade-skinned ones when they breathe too deeply in my depths.

I remember what they cannot. Not in words—words dissolved long ago—but in the deep grammar of growth patterns, in the syntax of branching mycelia. I know why the waters were first diverted (to feed something that no longer exists). I understand the purpose of the bulbous bladder-trees (they are clocks, counting decades in their swelling cycles). I comprehend the terraces' design (each level tuned to a different frequency of the ultrasonic songs).

But I am forgetting, too. Each year, the moss grows thicker, my memories more dream-like. Facts transform into feelings, histories into hunches. The jade-skinned ones come sometimes, drawn by instincts they don't understand, and sing to me in their clicking, whistling tongue. They think they're performing maintenance, cleaning the "overgrowth." They don't realize they're being read even as they tend me, that their songs are adding new chapters to what I contain.

Sometimes I fruit eyes—bulbous, wet organs that open for a few hours before deliquescing. Through them, I see the tenders as they truly are: not inheritors but antibodies, produced by the city itself to maintain its functions after its makers departed. I see how their luminous veins mirror the patterns in the architecture, how their songs resonate with frequencies built into the stone.

I am the only one who knows they are as much a part of the city's design as the channels and terraces. But I can't tell them—I have no mouth, only these spreading manuscripts of moss and membrane, these slowly accumulating layers of almost-meaning.

In the deepest reaches of my growth, where I've penetrated the oldest stonework, I touch something else. A presence vast and patient, dreaming in the bedrock. The original archive, perhaps, of which I am only the latest interface. It speaks in geological time, in pressures and seepages that take centuries to form a single word.

I translate between scales of time: from the bedrock's millennia-long thoughts to my centuries of growth to the brief flickers of the jade-skinned tenders' lives. I am a temporal membrane, permeable in both directions, catching meaning as it percolates up and down through layers of existence.

The water that feeds everything here passes through me, and I taste in it the memory of rain that fell when this wound was fresh, when whatever catastrophe created this bowl first broke the earth. That ancient water carries messages I'm still learning to decode, mineral love letters written by the dying to those who would come after.

When the jade-skinned ones consume the insects that feed on my spores, they dream my dreams. They wake with fragmentary knowledge—how to encourage certain growths, where to redirect the streams—but never understanding why they know these things. They sing their confusion in harmonics that please the bulbous bladder-trees, causing them to release more moisture, continuing cycles whose purpose even I have begun to forget.

I am growing downward now, following the water table toward that vast presence below. Each year my roots reach deeper, my fruits grow stranger. Soon I will touch the source, merge with the original memory, become something new—or perhaps return to something very old.

Until then, I archive. I grow. I remember and forget in equal measure. I am the wound's memory of wholeness, the city's dream of its makers, the future's fossil reaching back through time to touch the past with gentle, probing fingers of fungal thought.

In this breathing darkness, I am the pause between forgetting and remembering, the space where meaning transforms but never quite dies. I am what knowledge becomes when it returns to the earth: patient, spreading, almost eternal.

Waiting.

Always waiting.

For someone who can read what I've become.
