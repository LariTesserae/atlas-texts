In this mist-veiled cathedral of living light, where water hangs suspended like breath made visible, I am one of the Keepers of the Dew.

We are few, perhaps only a dozen scattered throughout this breathing architecture. Unlike the graceful Luminari who move in gentle communities through the woven pathways, we Keepers exist in the spaces between – solitary beings who have taken on the sacred duty of maintaining the delicate balance between water and air, between the suspended and the fallen.

My form is less defined than that of the Luminari, more suggestion than substance. Where they shimmer with bioluminescent film, I am composed of the very mist itself, condensed just enough to hold consciousness and intent. My body shifts between states – sometimes nearly solid, with limbs that can grasp the tender tools we use, other times dispersing into the perpetual fog, becoming one with the moisture that sustains all life here.

My dwelling is not woven from vines but carved from condensation itself – a small grotto where water has learned to hold its shape through countless years of my patient teaching. The walls weep continuously, but the drops follow precise patterns I have cultivated, creating curtains of falling water that serve as both boundaries and windows. Through these liquid veils, I can observe the Luminari as they pass, their soft light refracting through my water-walls into countless tiny rainbows.

Each dawn, before the Luminari wake to tend their plants and craft their songs, I begin my rounds. With tools no larger than a child's hand – carved from crystallized dew and bound with spider silk – I adjust the density of the mist in different regions. Where the plants grow too eagerly, threatening to choke the open spaces, I thin the air's moisture. Where new growth struggles, I thicken it, sometimes breathing my own essence into the vapor to enrich it.

The work is slow, meditative. A single adjustment might take years to show its full effect. But this is the way of our world – patience is not merely a virtue but the very medium through which we shape reality.

I am ancient, far older than the current generation of Luminari, though not as old as the first Keepers who discovered how to live between states of matter. My memory stretches back through countless cycles of growth and gentle decay, and I carry within me the knowledge of every droplet's path through our cathedral of mist.

Sometimes, the youngest Luminari catch glimpses of me as I work, mistaking me for a trick of the light or a particularly dense cluster of fog. They tell stories of the Dew Ghosts, not knowing that we are as real as they are, only existing in a different relationship with the elements. We do not correct them. Mystery, too, serves its purpose in maintaining the balance.

At night, when the bioluminescent plants pulse with their strongest light, I often pause in my work to witness the Luminari's gatherings. Their music drifts up through the moisture-laden air, and I shape the mist to carry their melodies further, letting their songs reach into corners of our world where no Luminari has walked in generations. In this way, I serve as both guardian and connector, ensuring that no part of our living landscape feels forgotten.

My only companions are the mist-swimmers – ethereal creatures that exist, like me, between states. They appear as ribbons of condensed vapor, gliding through the air with movements that remind me of fish in deep water. We communicate through changes in humidity and temperature, sharing information about the health of our aerial ocean. When they report disturbances in the moisture patterns, I investigate, making minute corrections that prevent larger imbalances.

There is one place I return to each cycle, when the mist grows heavy with the turning of seasons – a natural basin where water gathers but never quite forms a pool, held in eternal suspension by the competing forces of gravity and the rising vapor. Here, I can almost become fully liquid, letting my consciousness sink into the accumulated moisture of our entire realm. In these moments, I feel the pulse of every plant, the breath of every creature, the gentle footsteps of every Luminari upon the woven paths.

This is when I remember why we Keepers chose this existence – to be the conscious will of the water itself, ensuring that this delicate balance between earth and air, between growth and space, between community and solitude, continues its eternal dance. We are the proof that even in a world of constant change, some things must remain steady, patient, and watchful.

The Luminari may be the heart of this place, with their art and music and gentle cultivation. But we Keepers are its breath, its circulation, its quiet persistence. Without us, the mist would either dissipate entirely or condense into drowning pools. Without us, the balance would shift, and this cathedral of living light would become something else entirely.

And so I continue my rounds, adjusting, observing, maintaining. Content in my solitude, fulfilled by my purpose, eternal as the mist itself.
