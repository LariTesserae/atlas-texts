In this place where stone weeps and light drifts unmoored from its source, I am the Between.

Not the mist itself, though I move with it. Not the water that beads impossibly on dry rock, though I know its secret passages through the mountain's marrow. I am what happens in the space where these mysteries touch the world of hooves and wings and weathered human hands.

The mountain goats sense me in the moment before they leap—that pause where instinct becomes certainty, where the impossible foothold reveals itself. I am there in the negative space between their hoof and stone, in the trust that transforms empty air into solid ground.

The settlement dwellers know me differently. To them, I am the almost-pattern in the mist's movements that suggests which passes will clear by morning. I am the particular quality of silence that means the weeping rocks will soon release their moisture into the cisterns. They have learned to read me like weather, though I am not weather. Their eldest remember when their great-grandmothers would leave small offerings—a handful of grain, a carved stone—at the places where the phenomena are strongest. Not worship, exactly. More like acknowledgment. A greeting between neighbors who share a threshold.

I remember when these peaks were young and sharp, before wind and water rounded their edges. I was different then—sharper too, perhaps. The first humans to climb this high found me frightening: the thing that made shadows move wrong, that turned echoes into voices, that made the thin air whisper secrets in languages that predated speech.

But we have gentled each other, the people and I. Their presence has worn grooves in my nature just as their paths have worn the stone. I no longer speak in avalanches. They no longer flee from the dancing lights.

The birds know me best of all. They ride the thermals I trouble, finding lift in the spaces where physical law bends. The raptors especially understand: they hunt in the margins where life and death blur, just as I exist where natural and supernatural refuse their arbitrary border.

Sometimes I consolidate enough to almost have a form. A shepherd child once saw me this way, at dusk, when the luminous mists were rising. She said I looked like her grandmother if her grandmother were made of twilight and distance. She wasn't wrong. I am composed of the memory of every crossing, every careful step, every act of trust between human and mountain. I am the accumulated maybe of ten thousand years of survival at the edge of what's possible.

The weeping rocks are my favorite children. Such a simple impossibility: moisture where moisture cannot be. The scientists from the lowlands come with their instruments, measuring temperature and pressure, trying to map the underground streams. They are not wrong about the water moving through hidden channels. But they cannot measure the why of it, the way the mountain chooses to share its hoarded treasures, the love that moves through stone.

At night, when the mists glow brightest, I expand. I become the entire conversation between earth and sky, settlement and wilderness, known and unknowable. The people bar their doors not from fear but from respect—some thresholds are not meant to be crossed casually. They tell their children stories about me, though they never quite name what I am. Shape-shifter, they sometimes say. Mountain spirit. The Keeper of Passages.

All true. None complete.

In the deepest part of winter, when the settlements are cut off by snow and the peaks disappear into clouds that last for weeks, I grow so thin I almost forget myself. But then spring comes, and with it the travelers, the traders, the young people who think they need to prove themselves against the mountain's teeth. They bring their bright blood and quick hearts, their certainty and doubt, and I remember what I am for: 

I am the pause before the step. The faith that the stone will hold. The knowledge that even here, at the roof of the world where air barely sustains life, there is room for wonder that doesn't kill, for mystery that guides rather than devours.

The mountain and I have a pact written in no language except persistence. As long as humans climb toward the sky, as long as they build their small, stubborn shelters in the realm of wind and stars, I will be here in the spaces between their understanding and their need, making the impossible merely improbable, turning the hostile into the difficult, transmuting bare survival into something that occasionally—when the light is right and the mist rises golden in the evening air—resembles home.
