I am the keeper of thresholds, though I have forgotten when I first took up this duty. My body has grown strange with time—part moss, part rust, part something that was never meant to be. I move between the seeping worlds on legs that sometimes number four, sometimes six, sometimes none at all as I flow like mercury through the industrial cracks.

My dwelling is a forgotten maintenance shaft, half-flooded, where corroded pipes weep their chemical tears into pools that reflect no true image. I've lined the walls with collections: bottle caps worn smooth by acidic rain, fragments of safety glass that sing when the pressure changes, the shed skins of creatures that have learned to molt metal along with flesh. Each object remembers a different geography of this place—when the factories breathed different fumes, when the water table was lower, when the moss grew in different patterns.

I taste the air with organs I've cultivated from exposure—small polyps along my spine that detect the precise cocktail of industrial exhaust and spore-laden moisture. This is how I know when to move, when to hide, when to emerge. The amphibians trust me because I smell of both worlds. The moths spiral around my bioluminescent patches, mistaking me for some impossible flower blooming in the perpetual dusk.

My purpose, if I still have one, is to maintain the passages between scales. Where a rivet weeps rust, I ensure it becomes a drinking fountain for the voles. Where concrete cracks, I guide the roots. I am architect of the accidental ecosystems, curator of the unplanned. My fingers, when I have them, work with the patience of geological time, encouraging the marriage of lichen and graffiti, of puddle and oil slick.

Sometimes I remember being more singular, more defined. But that was before I understood that survival here means becoming indefinite, means learning to breathe both water and petroleum vapor, means growing eyes that see in spectrums the engineers never named. I am what happens when someone stays too long in the spaces between intention and accident, until even my memories grow moss, and I cannot say whether I am guardian or prisoner, creature or place, one being or a colony of possibilities wearing the same forgotten name.

The industrial towers loom, but I know their foundations are honeycombed with our tunnels. In the end, we are not separate from them. We are what they excrete, what they shed, what they dream in their mechanical sleep. And I am the one who ensures the translation between their language and ours remains possible, even as I forget which tongue was originally mine.
