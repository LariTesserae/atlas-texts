**The Surveyor**

I am the pipe-walker, the gauge-reader, the one who remembers what these machines were for.

My body is a compromise with this place. Tall and attenuated, built for seeing over the heat-shimmer and fitting through the narrow maintenance corridors that snake beneath the pumping stations. My skin has taken on the dusty ochre of the tuff—not by choice, but by decades of fine volcanic grit working its way into every pore until I am partially mineral myself. My eyes are perpetually narrowed against the glare, the corners creased with deep lines that trap moisture in the pre-dawn hours. I lick these corners for the salt.

I wear no uniform, but a patchwork of materials scavenged and adapted: a coat of Grit-Prowler fur (traded for three seasons' worth of dripping glycol access), boots soled with heat-resistant plate scales from the great grandmother of all Plate-Lizards, and a hat woven from dried Rust-Vine fiber that still occasionally tries to root itself to my scalp when I sweat. Around my neck hangs my most precious possession: a brass thermocouple, still functional, that tells me the temperature gradient between the air and the deep rock. This is how I know where the steam will rise tomorrow.

My home is Pumping Station Seven-Seven-Nine, halfway between the eastern escarpment and the central wells. The building is mostly dead, but its bones are good. I've sealed off the lower levels where the Rust-Vine has grown too thick, creating a labyrinth of pulsing, warm corridors that occasionally spark with bioluminescent displays. I live in the upper control room, where banks of gauges stare blindly at nothing and a single porthole window gives me a view across fifteen kilometers of fractured basalt. At night, I can see the other stations—some dark, some flickering with the phantom electricity of Vine-completed circuits, one still venting regular gouts of steam on the quarter-hour like a mechanical heartbeat.

My work is memory. Every morning, I walk the pipes. Seven kilometers of elevated steel, supported on trestles that sing different notes when the wind hits them right. I know where the condensation gathers—Junction Four-Alpha, always, and the north bend before Station Seven-Eight-One. I place my collection bottles beneath these points. The water tastes of rust and distance.

I read the gauges that still function, recording pressures and temperatures in a leather journal made from the wing membranes of a Sun-Raptor that died of old age in my station. The machines are not dead, merely sleeping. Deep beneath the basalt, the magma chambers still pulse with geological life. The wells still draw superheated water from aquifers that have never seen sunlight. Some of the turbines still turn, generating electricity that bleeds away into the Rust-Vine networks, powering... something. Patterns of light that make no human sense.

But I understand some of it. After forty years (or is it fifty? The seasons here are subtle), I've learned the language of failure. That grinding sound means a bearing has finally worn through—I'll find Thermal-Grubs in the housing within a week. The sudden silence of Wellhead Twelve means the pressure has found a new path, probably into the warren systems of the Grit-Prowlers, who will be drunk on mineral-rich steam for days. When the Sizzle-Flies cluster in perfect spirals on my station walls, I know a storm is coming from the lowlands.

I am not alone, though solitude is the default state. There are others—perhaps a dozen across the whole Scald-Shelf. We are the Surveyors. We meet at the Convergence, a massive valve complex at the plateau's heart, every third new moon. We trade—water locations, Prowler fur, functioning instruments, news of which stations have finally been consumed by Vine. We share the names of the dead and update our maps. Mostly, we sit in silence, watching the Sizzle-Flies write their luminous messages on the cooling pipes.

We do not reproduce. New Surveyors arrive from the lowlands, drawn by something—a dream of order, a need for vastness, a desire to be useful in a place that has outlived utility. They either adapt or leave within the first season. Those who stay gradually become like me: part engineer, part scavenger, part ecological niche. We are the species that remembers purpose.

My days are rhythms. Wake before dawn to collect the night's condensation. Walk the pipes as the sun transforms them into burning bridges. Retreat to the station during the worst heat, maintaining my equipment, updating my records, setting snares for the quick silver fish that swim in the settling tanks. Evening brings the Grit-Prowler families, who have learned I'll share my water for information about new Vine growth or the location of intact cable insulation. Night is for reading gauges by bioluminescent light and listening to the thermal contraction songs of the cooling rocks.

I have a theory. The Scald-Shelf is not dying but transforming. The machinery is being digested into a new kind of geology—one that hums with electric potential and channels steam in patterns no engineer designed. The Rust-Vine is not destroying but translating, converting our industrial logic into something the plateau can use. We Surveyors are simply another adaptation, a memory-keeping species that bridges the old purpose and the new one being born.

Sometimes, sitting in my control room as the Sizzle-Flies pulse their messages on my window, I feel the Scald-Shelf thinking through its new neural networks of Vine and pipe and stone. I am a synapse in this vast, slow thought. My careful records of pressure and temperature, my mapped paths of functioning machinery, my very presence—all of it feeds into a calculation I cannot grasp.

But I persist. Tomorrow I will walk the pipes again, read the gauges again, taste the condensation for traces of deep-earth minerals. I will watch the Plate-Lizards bask on my station roof and count the Sun-Raptors circling the thermals. I will share water with the Grit-Prowlers and avoid the spreading territories of the Rust-Vine. I will be what I am: the Surveyor, the rememberer, the one who stands between the world that was built and the world that is building itself.

In the morning, when the fog rises from the lowlands to be shredded by the awakening sun, I will be walking the pipes. The metal will groan its thermal greeting. The gauges will whisper their pressures. And I will record it all, because someone must, because this is what I am for, here in the packed and pressing presence of the Scald-Shelf, where nothing is wasted and everything is witnessed.
