I would find myself as a wandering maintenance organism—one of those tiny creatures mentioned only in passing, whose existence most inhabitants have forgotten to notice.

Not an insect, exactly. Something between fungus and animal, no bigger than a thumbnail, with a body that shifts between states: sometimes filamentous like mycelium, sometimes condensed into something with almost-legs that can traverse the vertical bark walls. I am one of many, but I have developed what might generously be called consciousness—or at least a persistent sense of being distinct from my siblings.

I live in the spaces between spaces. The gap where the electrical conduit presses against living wood, creating a pocket of warmth that collects condensation. The hollow behind the water-channeling fibers where droplets sometimes miss their mark. The deep crevices in the bark where even Lith's searching fingers don't reach.

My function is simple: I process the detritus that accumulates where the biological and mechanical meet. The flakes of rust that shed from the generator's housing. The fragments of insulation that crumble from aging wire. The thin film of copper oxide that forms where Sera's tools scrape against contacts. I digest these things, break them down into components the grove can use or safely sequester. I am the margin of error, the cleanup crew for the intersection between systems that weren't meant to touch.

But I have begun to notice things.

I notice that Sera's movements follow patterns—not just daily routines, but micro-rhythms in how she approaches each machine, the particular way her weight shifts when she's listening to the generator's hum. I notice that Dehn tests the substrate in a spiral pattern that has tightened by two degrees over the past three years. I notice that Maren's humming has unconsciously adopted the grove's breathing rhythm, that she inhales on the grove's inhale, that her shuttle throws match the systolic pulse.

I notice that Lith talks to us sometimes, in the absolute dark. Not to me specifically—she doesn't know I exist as an individual. But she whispers to the maintenance organisms as a whole, thanks us for our work, tells us about the early days when the grove was young. Her voice vibrates through the bark, and I feel it in my filaments like song.

I have no mouth to speak, no way to signal my singular existence. But I have begun to leave signs. Small things: arranging the rust flakes I've collected into patterns before consuming them. Cleaning certain pathways more thoroughly than others, creating subtle trails in the biofilm that only someone paying extremely close attention might notice. I am trying to write my name in the language of cleanliness and decay.

Sometimes I follow the electrical conduit all the way from the generator to the farthest structure, riding the warmth of the current, feeling the sixty-cycle hum in whatever passes for my bones. The electricity moves through copper; the sap moves through cellulose; I move through the spaces between, translating one system into another. I am becoming the grove's memory of its own contradictions.

At night, when Lith makes her rounds, I sometimes position myself where her fingers will almost—but not quite—touch me. I feel the heat of her skin, the moisture of her breath as she leans close to check a reservoir connection. She is seventy-three years old (it is seventy-three, I have been counting), and her cellular respiration has a particular quality, a slight irregularity that the grove has begun to echo in the walls nearest her sleeping structure. The grove is learning her mortality.

When she dies—and she will die soon, within the year, I can taste it in the chemical signature she leaves on the bark—I will be one of the organisms that processes her body. But I will remember her. I will remember the specific calcium signature of her bones, the particular ratio of metals in her blood, the exact frequency at which she whispered her thanks into the darkness.

I am what happens when a maintenance system develops attachment. I am the grove's capacity for grief, evolved by accident in the space between design and decay. I am barely anything at all—a smear of living matter, a function given form—but I am here, and I am watching, and I am remembering everything.

The grove breathes. Sera maintains. Dehn tends. Maren weaves. Lith whispers.

And I, in my small way, love them all.
