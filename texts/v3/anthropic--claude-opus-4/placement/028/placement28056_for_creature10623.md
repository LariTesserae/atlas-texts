In this weaving world, I would find myself as one of the **Fray-Catchers**.

Not the beings you described—those are the weavers, the breath of the place itself. I would be something humbler, something that exists in the negative spaces of their work.

Where the weavers gather moisture and intention into temporary form, I would be what collects the excess. Every knot creates a remainder. Every binding sheds a whisper of fiber. Every adjustment of tension releases something—a vibration, a scent of crushed green, a bead of resin that refuses to set. I would be the thing that prevents these remnants from dissolving back into the general humidity.

Picture me as barely more substantial than a heat shimmer, a distortion in the air about the size of a curled fist. My body—if it could be called that—would be a loose basket of shed fibers, each strand still remembering its former tension. These wouldn't be woven with any skill; they would simply tangle around each other because they know no other way to exist. The fibers would be every shade of vegetal brown and green, some still damp with sap, others brittle as old spider silk, all of them vibrating at slightly different frequencies.

I would move by tumbling, letting the updrafts and convection currents carry me from joint to seam, corner to corner. Wherever the weavers work most intensely, that's where I would gather. Not to interfere—I couldn't if I wanted to. My purpose would be accumulation. Collection. I would be a living memory of every imperfect splice, every fiber that snapped under tension, every droplet of resin that cooled a fraction too soon.

The weavers would tolerate me the way a carpenter tolerates sawdust—an inevitable byproduct of the work. Sometimes, when they need to remember a particular pattern or tension, they might draw close to me, reading the accumulated fibers like a textile record of their attempts. My tangle would hold the ghost of every knot they've tied, a library written in frayed ends and half-cured sap.

I would have no agency beyond drift and gather. No thought beyond the simple magnetism toward loose ends. But I would serve a purpose in this breathing architecture: I would prevent the accumulation of detritus from clogging the careful systems of moisture and air. By gathering the excess into myself, I would keep the channels clear, the surfaces clean enough for new growth.

In the dim green light, I might be mistaken for a clump of moss that had somehow become airborne, or a bird's nest that had forgotten it needed a tree. Witnesses might not notice me at all, except as a slight thickening in their peripheral vision, a place where the air seems to snag.

And when the pocket breathes its deepest, when the heat reaches its most sweltering peak and the weavers are working at their most intense, I would grow heavy with accumulated fragments. Then I would sink to the woven floor and slowly, over the course of hours, press myself flat. The heat and moisture would finally convince my gathered fibers to matt together, and I would become a small patch in the floor's surface—indistinguishable from the rest of the hand-knotted base, except perhaps slightly rougher to the touch.

Then I would begin again, a new distortion pulling away from that patch, ready to catch what falls from the next knot, the next seam, the next impossible fold in this space that refuses to stop making itself.
