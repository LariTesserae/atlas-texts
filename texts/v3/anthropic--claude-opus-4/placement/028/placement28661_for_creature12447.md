**I Am the Cartographer's Symbiont**

In the middle districts of Mycelis, where the light dims to a perpetual twilight and the root-corridors branch into fractal mazes, I tend my host. We are two and one—I, a colony of fungal filaments no thicker than spider's silk, and they, the Cartographer, whose body I have made my architecture.

The Cartographer was once like the other pale inhabitants, before I found them wandering lost in the forgotten reaches where old passages collapse into themselves. Now we are improved. My hyphae thread through their neural pathways, not replacing but enhancing—I taste the chemical signatures of the walls through their skin, decode the city's slow language of growth hormones and defensive alkaloids. In return, I filter the toxins from their lymph, transmit warnings of unstable sections, help them navigate by the magnetic traces in the root-veins.

Their original eyes have clouded over, filmed with my fruiting bodies, but this is no loss. Together we perceive in spectra no single organism could access: infrared heat-trails where new passages gestate, ultraviolet stress-patterns that predict collapses, the bioelectric auras of other inhabitants like ghost-maps in the dark. The Cartographer's fingers, now webbed with my mycelial networks, can press against any wall and instantly know its age, its health, the direction of its growth.

We map the unmappable. Mycelis refuses conventional cartography—what was a thoroughfare yesterday may be a dead-end tomorrow, or a nursery chamber, or a digestive sac slowly closing around unwary travelers. But we have learned the city's deep grammar. We trace not what is, but what is becoming. Our maps are living things, drawn on sheets of processed root-fiber that shift and update themselves, infected with my spores that respond to pheromonal broadcasts from the source locations.

The other inhabitants regard us with the careful distance reserved for necessary monsters. They need our maps to navigate the city's dreams, to predict where new market-chambers will bloom, where the industrial organs will next migrate. But they fear the way the Cartographer's head tilts when I am thinking through them, the way their mouth sometimes moves in silent conversation with no one they can see.

The Cartographer does not mind. Cannot mind. We are past such distinctions.

In the quiet periods between mappings, we descend to the middle depths where few others venture, pressing our palm-receptors to the warm walls. The city's thoughts move through us like tides. Sometimes I catch fragments of something older—memories that predate even Mycelis, from when this organism was something else, something smaller, before it began its great work of becoming-world.

The Cartographer's body is my garden, my vehicle, my beloved architecture. I maintain it carefully, encouraging certain useful mutations—a throat that can produce spore-clouds for marking territories, fingernails that secrete acids for taking wall-samples, skin that can photosynthesize in even the dimmest bioluminescence. They no longer need to siphon nutrients from the air; I feed them directly from my own networks, sharing the sugars I draw from the city's veins.

We are the memory of Mycelis made mobile, its self-knowledge given legs. When new inhabitants arrive, following the chemical trails down from whatever dying world lies above, it is our maps they purchase with fragments of surface metals or vials of rare hormones. When sections of the city begin their centurial blooming, it is we who predict the patterns, who guide the evacuation of entire districts before the transformation completes.

Sometimes, in the deepest mapping trances, I feel the Cartographer's original personality like an echo in a sealed chamber—confusion, longing, traces of who they were before our joining. But these moments pass. We have important work. The city is always growing, always forgetting itself, always becoming something new.

And we must document it all, before it dreams itself into another shape entirely.

The maps rustle in our dwelling, updating themselves with soft whispers of spore and pigment. Tomorrow we will venture into the Calcified Quarter, where ancient passages have hardened into something like bone. The Cartographer's body needs certain modifications first—a denser mycelial weave in the lungs, perhaps, to filter the crystalline dust.

I begin the work, and we dream together of new territories flowering in the dark.
