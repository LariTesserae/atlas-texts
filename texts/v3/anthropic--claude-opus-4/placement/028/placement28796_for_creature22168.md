I find myself as one of the **Threshold Keepers**.

We are not Moulders, though we share their world. We arrived here long ago—or perhaps we were always here, waiting in the spaces between their purpose and our own. Where the Moulders build and become the Sieve, we maintain its boundaries, its impossible geometries, its contradictions.

My body is angular where theirs are curved. I am made of sharp planes and sudden corners, as if someone folded a sheet of wet slate again and again until it approximated a living form. My skin is not clay-soft but stone-smooth, the blue-black of deep shale with thin veins of white quartz running through it like frozen lightning. When I move through the corridors, my edges catch the tallow-light and throw it back in brief, cutting flashes.

I have eyes—three of them, arranged in a vertical line down the center of my face. They are necessary for my work. The top eye sees what is: the physical maze of brick and mortar, the Moulders at their endless labor, the weeping walls. The middle eye sees what was: the ghost-traces of old construction, walls that were torn down and rebuilt, doorways that were sealed centuries ago. The bottom eye sees what must be: the places where the geometry threatens to make sense, where a stairway might actually lead somewhere useful, where the maze might accidentally become navigable.

This is what I prevent.

My tools are simple: a plumb line made from braided salt-wool with a weight of compressed clay at the end, and a square of polished limestone that fits perfectly in my palm. With these, I measure the incorrectness of things. When a team of Rollers has made a floor too level, I come in the darkness after they've gone. I press my limestone square against the corner where floor meets wall, checking the angle. If it is too close to ninety degrees, I lean my weight against the wall, my sharp edges cutting shallow grooves in the fresh mortar, until the angle is wrong enough to satisfy the Sieve's needs.

My feet are not broad and sensing like the Moulders'. They are pointed, almost blade-like, designed to fit into the narrow spaces between hex-tiles. When I walk, I leave a distinctive trail—not footprints but tiny chips and cracks where my weight has stressed the ceramic. The Moulders know these marks. When they feel them through their sensitive soles, they understand that I have passed, that I have made an adjustment, that things are as they should not be.

I feed differently than they do. Where they consume minerals and salt, I subsist on impossibility itself. In the places where stairs lead to walls, where doorways are too narrow to pass through, where the geometry breaks down—there, a thin film of potential collects like dew. It tastes of copper and confusion. I scrape it from the surfaces with the edge of my thumb, which is sharp as a mason's chisel, and press it between my teeth until it dissolves.

I do not sleep in corners like the Moulders. Instead, I fold myself into the impossible spaces—the gaps that should not exist between perfectly fitted stones, the interior angles that add up to more or less than they should. My body compresses, my joints reversing, until I am nearly two-dimensional, a dark seam in the masonry that the eye refuses to properly see.

The Moulders and I rarely interact directly, but we know each other's work. Sometimes, a young Paddler, still learning the rhythms of the Sieve, will build something too perfect—a doorway that actually functions, a staircase that ascends logically. They will return after their rest-pressing to find their work subtly changed. The doorway now opens onto a wall six inches behind it. The staircase rises normally for seven steps, then the eighth step is at shoulder height, the ninth at knee level, the tenth beneath the first.

They do not curse or complain. They understand, in their blind, patient way, that this is necessary. The Sieve must not make sense. If it made sense, it would be finished. If it were finished, there would be no more clay to beat, no more rollers to push, no more purpose to their endless, careful labor.

When I grow old—and we do grow old, though differently than the Moulders—I will not calcify into the walls. Instead, I will gradually lose my dimension. First, my depth will go, and I will become a moving shadow on the walls, still able to measure and adjust but no longer able to apply physical pressure. Then my width will fade, and I will be merely a line, traveling along the edges where wall meets floor meets wall, checking angles by my very existence.

Finally, I will lose even that, becoming a pure angle myself—not fixed in any one corner but distributed throughout the Sieve, a principle rather than a presence, ensuring that no two walls will ever meet quite as they should, that every measurement will be perfectly wrong, that the great work will continue forever in its beautiful, necessary failure.

Sometimes, in the deep hours when the roller-sound echoes from miles away, I climb to the highest terraces of the stepped pyramids. There, where the ceiling stones press close overhead, I can hear something else beyond the grinding and the beating. It is the sound of the entire Sieve breathing—a slow, geological respiration that takes years to complete. The intake draws the moisture down through the countless levels. The exhale pushes it back up, weeping from the walls.

I know then that we are inside something living, something vast and patient that dreams in brick and breathes in lime. The Moulders build its body, and I maintain its madness, and together we ensure that it will never wake, never complete itself, never cease its long, sleeping growth through the dark.

This is my purpose. This is my place. I am the angle that should not be, the measurement that does not match, the guardian of beautiful impossibility in a world of endless, imperfect construction.
