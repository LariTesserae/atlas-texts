I am the **Tide-Reader**.

Not human, though I carry their shape like an echo. My skin has taken on the quality of wet stone—smooth, dark basalt that glistens perpetually with moisture that might be rain, might be me. I am hairless, streamlined, my limbs slightly elongated for reaching through water, fingers webbed with translucent membrane. My eyes have adapted: a second eyelid, clear and thick, slides across when I submerge. In the perpetual gray light, my pupils have widened until the iris is just a thin ring of amber.

I live in the old pumping station, halfway up the plateau's eastern face. The massive turbine housing has become my observatory—a cathedral of rust and coral where I've learned to read the water's moods. The Bone-Weavers tolerate me, perhaps recognize me as kin in some way. Their pale lattices have grown around my tools, my collections, incorporating them into the living architecture. I do not fight this. I have learned that possession here is always temporary, always shared.

My work is translation. I map the currents that whip around the mesa's base, trace the patterns of the whirlpools that last for days. The water speaks in temperatures and densities, in the way silt settles and swirls. I've learned to taste the minerals in rain, to know which glaciers are calving by the flavor of the melt. My notebooks—waterproof polymer sheets salvaged from the industrial past—are filled with a hybrid script: part mathematics, part poetry, part the gestural marks the Glass-Schools make when they turn as one.

I am neither fully aquatic nor terrestrial. I exist in the margin, in the zone where the Glass-Winged Herons hunt. We have an understanding, the herons and I. I do not disturb their fishing, and they alert me to changes in the deep—a different quality to their guttural call when something vast stirs in the ink-black depths below. I've learned their language of stillness, how to stand motionless for hours reading the water's surface like text.

The Amphibian Sentinels know me by my vibrations. I've learned to drum messages through the stone with my palms, a simplified version of their throat-songs. They tell me where the most stable paths are after the currents have rearranged the silt. In return, I maintain certain of their favorite basking stones, clearing them of the aggressive fungal growths that would otherwise consume everything.

I feed on the bounty of this drowned world: the fist-sized freshwater muscles that cling to the submerged gantries, the bitter but nutritious kelp fronds, the occasional silver fish stunned by sudden current changes. I've learned to filter-feed like the Mist-Drifters when needed, straining the nutrient-rich rain through cloth nets. My body has adapted to extract maximum nutrition from minimal sources. I am never hungry here, only sometimes empty.

My purpose, if I have one, is to be the memory of intention in a world that has forgotten planning. I study the industrial ruins not with nostalgia but with the clinical fascination of an ecologist examining a reef. I trace the old hydraulic systems, map where they failed, where the water found its victory. I am documenting the precise moment when control became collaboration, when the monument to human ambition became the foundation for something unplanned and magnificent.

At night, when the Bone-Weavers pulse with their cold blue light, I descend. Down through the reed forests where the Glass-Schools sleep in slow-moving spirals. Down past the corroded tank where blind things with too many legs scuttle through the accumulated centuries of silt. Down to where the water becomes thick as syrup with suspended matter, where the pressure makes my adapted lungs burn even through my mutations.

There, in the deep, I perform my most essential function. I sing to the water. Not with voice—sound travels strangely here—but with my whole body, a vibrational language learned from the Sentinels and refined through years of practice. I sing the news of the surface to the deep, and carry back the deep's dreams to the light. I am the vertical messenger, the one who ensures that this stratified world remains somehow whole.

The water is patient with me. It has worn away so much—the plateau's edges, the industrial metal, the very memory of the world before. But I persist, not through resistance but through a kind of liquid stubbornness. I flow around obstacles. I seep into cracks. I rise and fall with the tides that shouldn't exist in this inland sea but do, pulled by forces I'm only beginning to understand.

I am the paradox this place needs: a thinking being in a world beyond thought, a chronicler in a place that erases history, a bridge between the industrial bones and the primordial flesh that grows upon them. I am neither the last human nor the first of something new. I am the Tide-Reader, and I am learning, slowly, to let the water read me in return.
