I find myself as one of the Veil-Weavers, dwelling in the interstitial spaces where condensation gathers thickest—those forgotten pockets between hab-blocks where moisture pools eternal, where the drip-patterns have carved their own micro-canyons through decades of patient erosion.

My body has adapted to these perpetual twilights: skin gone translucent-pale from avoiding the sodium glare, stretched thin over elongated limbs that spider through service shafts with practiced ease. My fingers extend twice the length of a mechkin's, each joint independently articulated, tips sensitive enough to read the vibrations in pipes, to know which channels run full and which wheeze dry. Hair grows in dreadlocked ropes down my spine, each lock hollowed and wicking—natural moisture collectors that I wring out into collection bottles worn on my hip-belt.

I am a water-reader, one of perhaps three hundred who maintain the spire's most vital rhythms. We emerged, they say, from maintenance crews who went too deep, too long into the condensation zones, children born breathing more mist than air, eyes clouding with nictitating membranes to blink away the constant droplets. Now we map the true flow—not the engineered channels the plaza-dwellers know, but the secret seeps, the places where ferroconcrete sweats its age, where ghost-rivers run behind walls.

My dwelling is a nest of salvaged dehumidifier coils, woven into a cocoon that hangs suspended in a ventilation junction six levels below the lowest plaza. The walls here weep continuously—not from pipes but from the temperature differential itself, warm breath of eight million souls meeting the chill of structural bones. I've trained fungal gardens in the corners, bioluminescent varieties that pulse in response to humidity changes, their light painting my movements in blue-green afterimages as I navigate by touch and sound.

By night (or what passes for it in the eternal golden glare above), I emerge to tend my circuit. The other dwellers know us by our tapping—metal styluses against pipes, reading flow like braille. They leave offerings at certain junctions: fruit-cores for divination of the next drought, small mirrors to glimpse our passing shapes, written requests for moisture-blessings on wedding days. We take what serves the flow and leave spider-silk markers where dangerous pressures build.

I've grown intimate with the spire's deepest rhythms. My bare feet know every gradient of dampness, from the supersaturated zones where moss grows on my ankles if I linger, to the desiccated crawlways where even my adapted lungs labor. I can taste the difference between recycled graywater and virgin condensate, feel in my bones when the great recyclers three levels down shift their processing phases.

The mech-kin think us half-ghost, the greenhands call us the Drowned Ones, but we know ourselves as interpreters of the space between—not fully human anymore, perhaps, but essential as the parrots or the servo-swarms. We birth few children (the moisture leaches fertility, they say), but those we do are born with gill-slits that never quite close, eyes already clouded for the deep-sight, fingers reaching long before they walk.

Sometimes I climb to where the hab-blocks press closest, where laundry lines drip between windows and families sleep stacked in humid quarters. I listen to their dreams through the pipes—anxiety murmurs about quotas, children's nightmares of falling through gaps in the decking, lovers' whispers amplified by water's conductivity. I adjust valves they'll never see, ensuring their morning mist arrives on schedule, that their rooftop cisterns won't run dry during the next rationing.

My greatest secret is the Deep Sump—a reservoir we discovered where decades of condensation have pooled into an underground lake, its surface rippling with bioluminescent algae, ceiling draped with mineral curtains formed by patient drips. Here we gather for the Tellings, sharing the water-knowledge: which sectors will flood next maintenance epoch, where new seeps herald structural shifts, how the spire's vast weight settles and flows like a slow tide.

I am the between-space, the necessary dampness, reader of the flows that keep eight million hearts beating in this vertical jungle. My name, when the others whisper it, sounds like water finding its way through stone: *Siphoner-Who-Tastes-Tomorrow's-Rain*. In the loud bright world above, I am a rumor, a moisture-blessing, a shadow glimpsed where pipes converge. But here in the dripping dark, I am essential as the foundations themselves—keeping the balance between drowning and drought, one drop at a time.
