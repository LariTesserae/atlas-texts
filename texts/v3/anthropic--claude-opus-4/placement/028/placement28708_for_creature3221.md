In the dense middle terraces where moss grows thick as fur and water collects in pools no deeper than a palm, I am one of the **Dewsingers**.

We are small—no taller than a Stoneweaver's waist—with bodies that seem assembled from the city's smaller elements. Our skin shifts between textures: smooth as wet stone where we need to slide through narrow channels, rough as lichen where we need purchase on vertical walls. Our fingers and toes are long and articulated like those of tree frogs, ending in pads that can cling to any surface, no matter how moisture-slick. But what truly defines us are our throat sacs—translucent membranes that expand and contract with our breathing, collecting and filtering moisture from the air.

I spend my days in the Singing Gardens, a district where past inhabitants carved acoustic chambers into the living rock. These spaces were designed to capture and amplify the sounds of water: every drip, every trickle, every splash has its own carved hollow that transforms mere moisture into music. My role—our role—is to maintain these chambers and, more importantly, to sing to them.

You see, the Dewsingers discovered long ago that certain frequencies can encourage condensation. A low hum at just the right pitch will draw moisture from even the driest air, while a high trill can disperse fog into collectible droplets. We position ourselves throughout the acoustic chambers, our throat sacs vibrating with sounds below and above human hearing, creating harmonies that literally pull water from the mist.

The songs are not random. Each chamber has its own resonant frequency, its own preferred melody. We learn these through years of patient listening, pressing our bellies against the stone to feel its vibrations, tasting the air to understand its moisture content. A song that works in the Whispering Basin might create dissonance in the Chamber of Echoes. The work requires perfect pitch and endless patience.

I know my territory by the taste of its stones. The eastern chambers, where morning mist gathers first, have granite that tastes of iron and old rain. The deeper chambers near the Stoneweaver districts carry the green taste of moss spores and compressed time. Each surface tells me how much moisture it can hold, how quickly it will release it, what song it needs to wake the water sleeping in the air.

We work in careful coordination with the others. The Mistwalkers guide the fog down from the heights, and we sing it into droplets that the Stoneweavers' moss gardens can drink. The Depthkeepers know when the lower cisterns need replenishing, and signal us through vibrations in the stone—messages that travel up through the mountain's bones to reach our sensitive feet. Even the Echolings play their part, their laughter sometimes hitting just the right frequency to shake loose droplets from overhanging stones, though whether they do this intentionally, none can say.

Our bodies are perfectly adapted to this life. We can go days without drinking, our skin absorbing moisture directly from the air. Our eyes have nictitating membranes that keep them moist in even the driest chambers. When we sleep, we cluster together in damp hollows, our throat sacs synchronizing until we breathe as one organism, sharing moisture through our permeable skin.

But we are more than mere water collectors. We are the city's memory of sound. In our songs live echoes of every voice that ever spoke in these chambers, every footfall that ever crossed these stones. Sometimes, in the perfect acoustic conditions of dawn, our harmonies will suddenly include a note, a phrase, a whispered word that none of us sang—fragments of the past given voice again through the medium of moisture and stone.

I have been singing in the Chamber of Morning Bells for three decades now, and I know its every acoustic secret. I know that the northwestern corner will hold a note for exactly seventeen heartbeats. I know that the crack running along the eastern wall changes pitch with the seasons, higher in the dry cold, lower in the wet warmth. I know that if I position myself exactly where the floor dips to meet the wall and sing the sequence I call "Greeting the Fog," the entire chamber will fill with a fine mist that tastes of centuries.

We reproduce through song as well. When two or more Dewsingers harmonize perfectly in a chamber with exactly the right moisture content, sometimes—rarely—the condensation will gather itself into a pearl of living water that gradually takes on our form. These water-born young know the songs before they know anything else, their first breaths already shaped to call moisture from the air.

As the day progresses, I move through my circuit of chambers, following the moisture as it follows the sun. In the afternoon, I might join the chorus in the Great Resonance Hall, where dozens of us work together to fill the Stoneweavers' central reservoir. In the evening, I often climb to the transition zones where our territory meets the Mistwalkers', singing the fog down into forms they can work with.

But my favorite time is the deep night, when the city settles into its foundational quiet and every small sound carries perfectly. Then I go to the oldest chambers, the ones so worn by time and song that they barely need coaxing to release their hoarded moisture. There I sing not for water but for the singing itself, my voice joining the thousands of Dewsinger voices that have filled these spaces before me, adding my own thread to a song that has been building, note by note, drop by drop, for longer than anyone remembers.

In the Forgotten Stair-City, we Dewsingers are the bridge between sound and substance, between the city's need for water and the water's need to flow. We are small and often overlooked, but without our songs, the careful balance would fail. The mist would pass over without pausing, the stones would dry and crack, the moss would retreat to nothing.

So I sing, and in singing, I make the city live. Each note I produce pulls possibility from the air and transforms it into the necessity of water. And in the echo of my voice against stone older than memory, I hear the city singing back, grateful for this small service, this tiny vital magic that keeps its heart beating in slow, liquid time.
