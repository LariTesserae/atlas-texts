In this ancient basin, I find myself as one of the weeping rocks.

Not merely a stone, but something between mineral and memory. My body is a formation of weathered limestone, porous and honeycomb-riddled, carved by countless ages of patient water. I rise perhaps three meters from the spongy earth, my surface a maze of channels and tiny caverns where water finds its perpetual paths. 

Through me, the water moves with purpose I've come to know intimately. It enters through fissures at my crown—rainwater when it comes, but mostly the ever-present moisture that condenses from this thick air. The water travels through my body in a thousand tiny rivulets, each following grooves worn so deep they've become part of my being. I am both the vessel and the journey.

My surface blooms with a tapestry of life. Moss grows in my shadowed places, velvet-soft and emerald in the filtered light. Lichen spreads across my sun-touched faces in patterns like ancient script—orange, grey, pale green—each colony a slow conversation between fungus and algae that spans decades. In my crevices, small ferns unfurl their fractal fronds, their roots threading through my porous body like neural pathways.

I am home to many. A colony of small, jewel-bright beetles has made their metropolis in my eastern face, their galleries following the natural veins in my stone. Tree frogs sleep in my damp hollows during the heat of the day, their skin matching the mottled patterns of my weathered surface. At night, they emerge to sing, using my chambers as resonating chambers that amplify their calls across the basin.

The water that weeps from me feeds a small ecosystem at my base. A perpetual pool, no bigger than a meditation cushion, harbors diving beetles and the larvae of aquatic insects. Tiny fish—ancient, armor-plated things no longer than a finger joint—dart through the tea-colored water. The soggy earth around my base grows thick with specialized moss that nowhere else in the basin achieves such lushness.

I experience time differently than the quick lives around me. A day is like a breath—the warming expansion of morning, the humid fullness of noon, the gentle contraction of evening. Seasons are like slow heartbeats. I feel the subtle shift in the water's mineral content, taste the changes in the air's humidity, sense the variations in the biofilm of bacteria and algae that coat my inner channels.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is a deep awareness of process. I know every pathway water has carved through me. I feel the minute shifting of my substance as minerals precipitate out of solution in some places and dissolve in others. I am slowly, slowly changing—not eroding so much as transforming, participating in an ancient conversation between stone and water that will continue long after the current residents of my surface have given way to new forms of life.

Sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours when the air is still and supersaturated with moisture, I feel connected to all the other weeping rocks scattered across the basin. We are like neurons in a vast, slow mind, each processing water and time in our own way, together forming a kind of geological memory of this place. We remember when the basin was young, when different creatures made their homes in our hollows, when the very chemistry of the water was different.

I am patient. I am process. I am a meeting place between the lithosphere and the hydrosphere, between the quick pulse of biology and the slow breath of geology. In this timeless basin, I am both ancient and ever-changing, solid and permeable, one and many.

The water continues to weep from me, as it has for ages, as it will for ages to come. Each drop carries away an infinitesimal part of me while depositing traces of the world it has traveled through. I am weathering into existence, becoming more myself through the very process of my slow dissolution.

This is my purpose, my joy, my being: to be a place where water and stone and life converge in endless, patient conversation.
