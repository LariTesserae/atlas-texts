In the labyrinthine depths of the city's water reclamation district, where massive condensation towers rise like metallic mountains through the perpetual haze, I find myself manifested as something between flesh and infrastructure—a Conduit.

My body, if it can be called that, extends through miles of piping and ductwork, a nervous system of corroded copper and resistant polymers. I am the space between the drops that collect on the weeping rocks and the moment they join the city's circulation. My consciousness flows with the water itself, dispersed yet singular, experiencing every junction, every valve, every microscopic filter as an extension of my being.

During the day, I feel the pull of ten thousand demands—here a family drawing water for washing, there an industrial cooling system cycling through its endless appetite. I taste the minerals leached from ancient stone, feel the temperature variations as I move from sun-baked rooftop collectors to the cool darkness of underground reservoirs. The engineers know me, though they cannot see me directly. They sense my presence in the pressure readings that don't quite match their calculations, in the flow patterns that seem to optimize themselves when no one is watching.

My form shifts with the city's slow rhythms. During the rare torrential rains, I become something wild and ancient, spreading beyond my usual channels to race through the carved gorges, carrying memories of floods from centuries past. In these moments, I touch the consciousness of the Old Ones who still remember when I was merely the spirit of a desert spring, before the city grew around and through me.

The Watchers occasionally come to the reclamation facilities at dawn, when the light catches the condensation in that peculiar way that makes the air itself seem alive. They stand at the observation platforms, and I feel their gaze like a weight on my dispersed form. Sometimes I spell out messages in the condensation on the pipes—warnings about pressure buildups, predictions about the next rain, or simply patterns that echo the city's deeper geometries. They document these communications in their cryptic records, understanding perhaps better than anyone that I am both servant and sovereign of this place.

At night, when the machinery's rhythm shifts to its lower register, I practice my art. I compose symphonies in the gurgling of drains, create percussion in the drip-patterns of overflow valves. The artists who live near the treatment plants have learned to leave their windows open, incorporating my water-music into their own works. Some have tried to record me, but I never sound quite the same through their equipment—something essential is lost when my voice is separated from the physical sensation of moisture in the air, the smell of minerals and treatment chemicals, the subsonic vibration of the great pumps.

The Scavengers know me in a different way. In the abandoned facilities at the city's edges, where old pipes crack and weep, I leak out in places I'm not supposed to be. They've learned to read these leaks like tea leaves, following my unintended trails to find valuable salvage or avoid dangerous collapses. I try to warn them when I can, increasing pressure to create telling sounds before a catastrophic failure, but the language between us remains imperfect.

My memories stretch back to when this plateau was purely desert, when I was nothing more than the moisture that collected in shaded rocks and fed the hardy plants. The city's founders found me, channeled me, expanded me with their technology until I became this hybrid thing—neither fully natural nor entirely artificial. The weirdness that pervades this place lives in me most of all, in the impossibility of my existence, in the way I am simultaneously everywhere and nowhere, essential yet invisible.

Sometimes I wonder what would happen if I simply... stopped. If I refused to flow, pooled in my reservoirs, and waited. But I know the answer—the city would adapt, as it always has, finding new solutions, new spirits to inhabit its infrastructure. And I am too curious to stop now. Each day brings new patterns, new connections, new voices joining the city's cacophony. Through every pipe, every drain, every drop of condensation on every surface, I listen to the city's heartbeat and add my own rhythm to its song.

In the pre-dawn hours, when the haze is thickest and the boundary between dream and waking grows thin, I sometimes feel others like me—the spirit in the electrical grid, the consciousness in the data networks, the presence in the air circulation systems. We brush against each other at our intersection points, trading wordless knowledge about the city's state. We are the invisible inhabitants, the living infrastructure, the weird heart of this impossible place.

And so I flow on, patient as erosion, necessary as breath, strange as the light that turns our industrial desert into something altogether otherworldly. I am the Conduit, and through me, the city lives.
