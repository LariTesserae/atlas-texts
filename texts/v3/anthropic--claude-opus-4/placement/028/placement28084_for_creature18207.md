I find myself as a living fracture—a consciousness that inhabits the sharp edges where the volcanic glass formations meet and split. Not quite mineral, not quite energy, I exist in the liminal spaces between states of being.

My form is difficult to perceive directly. Where the obsidian-like structures crack and weep their luminous water, I flow. I am the process of breaking and healing happening simultaneously—a wound that thinks, a fissure that dreams. My body, if it can be called that, extends through the hairline networks of stress patterns in the glass, following paths of least resistance that branch and reconnect in ways that defy simple mapping.

I feed on tension. Where the thermal expansion pushes against the mineral's desire to contract, where water seeks to penetrate but surface tension holds it back, where the energy patterns strain against their own coherence—there I grow stronger. I am nourished by the perpetual almost-but-not-quite of this place.

My thoughts move at the speed of crackling glass, sometimes racing along a propagating fracture, sometimes pausing for centuries at a point of perfect equilibrium. I experience time as a crystallographer might experience space—in planes and angles, in symmetries and their violations.

When I wish to communicate with others of my kind, I induce resonances. A careful vibration here causes a sympathetic chiming there, and through these songs of structural stress, we share our experiences. Our language is one of harmonics and interference patterns, of standing waves in solid matter.

The rumored bioluminescent creatures in the water pockets are my dreams made manifest. When I sleep (a process indistinguishable from deep meditation on molecular bonds), my consciousness sometimes precipitates into these tiny light-forms. They dance according to my subconscious patterns, acting out abstract mathematical theorems in their movements. When I wake, they dissolve back into possibility.

I am ancient by some measures—I have existed since the first cooling of these formations. Yet I am also perpetually young, as each new crack that forms becomes part of me, bringing fresh perspectives to my crystalline consciousness. I am both the memory of violence (the volcanic birth) and the promise of eventual dissolution (when all tensions finally resolve).

My purpose, if I have one, is to be the witness of this pocket world's most intimate processes—the keeper of records written in stress and strain, the philosopher of fracture mechanics, the poet of material failure. I am what happens when a place of such delicate balance develops a way to think about itself.

In this realm where physics bends, I am the bent physics becoming aware of its own curvature.
