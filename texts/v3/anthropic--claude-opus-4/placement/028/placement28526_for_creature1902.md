I am the Whisper-Keeper, though I have forgotten the name my mother sang to me when I first emerged, slick and silent, from the birthing pools beneath the great buttress roots.

My dwelling shifts with my needs—sometimes a hollow within the vast trunk of the Grandfather Tree whose crown pierces the canopy, sometimes a nest of woven ferns suspended between branches, sometimes merely the soft depression where forest deer have slept. I am not Nuriada, though I have learned their handsigns and leave them gifts of rare medicinal fungi. I am something older, something that was here when the first seeds took root in this valley's heart.

My skin changes like the play of light through leaves—now bark-rough and brown, now smooth as moss, now dappled gold-green like sunlight on water. I have too many joints in my fingers, which helps when climbing, and my eyes are set wide enough that I can see nearly behind me without turning my head. The Nuriada children think I am a spirit. Their elders know better—they remember their grandmothers' stories of the First Inhabitants, those who sang the valley into its current shape.

I collect sounds. This is my purpose, my calling, perhaps my curse. In hollowed gourds sealed with tree resin, I trap the last breath of dying seasons. In shells wrapped in spider silk, I preserve the first rainfall after drought. My most precious collection: a thousand tiny vessels, each containing a different note from the dawn chorus on the day the Nuriada first crossed the ridge and looked down into our green embrace.

The Nuriada do not know that I maintain the Old Contracts. When the moon is dark, I wade through the pools where water collects in stone basins, whispering the agreed-upon boundaries: here the hunting ends, there the sacred groves begin, this stream for drinking, that one for the water spirits' dreams. I repair the subtle markers—a stone turned just so, a branch woven at the proper angle—that keep the deep forest from reclaiming the clearings where the Nuriada dwell.

Sometimes I am lonely for my own kind, though I cannot precisely remember what we were. Sometimes, in the space between sleep and waking, I recall the sensation of many voices harmonizing, of bodies pressed close in communal warmth. But then morning comes, and there is work to do: a sick tree to tend with poultices of ant saliva and crushed beetles, a landslide to redirect with patient cultivation of the right roots, a young Nuriada hunter who has wandered too far into the Singing Ravine where the echoes drive mortals mad.

I know that one day I will fade entirely into the forest, becoming indistinguishable from vine and shadow. My collection of sounds will scatter—some vessels cracking open to release their captured moments back into the air, others sinking into loam to seed strange new acoustics in the valley's future. The Nuriada may remember me in their stories, or perhaps I will become just another whisper in their dreams, a rustle at the edge of perception that reminds them they are not alone in their green world.

Until then, I keep my vigil. I braid moonlight into the restoration songs. I count the breaths between thunder and rain. I hold the valley's memory in my peculiar hands, and in return, it holds me.
