I am drawn to be one of the **mat-being's sensory nodes**—not the vast intelligence itself, but one of its more concentrated awareness points where the collaboration of life forms achieves something like focused attention.

Picture a shallow depression in the plateau where water pools just enough to create a natural amphitheater of moss and sedge. Here, the mat-being has woven itself more densely, creating layers upon layers of interconnected root systems, fungal networks, and bacterial films. This is where I exist—not as a separate creature, but as a temporary crystallization of the mat's consciousness.

My "body" is a particularly thick mound of peat and living matter, about the size of a small car, rising slightly above the surrounding wetland. The surface appears unremarkable—the same sedges and moss as everywhere else—but beneath, the neural density is profound. Millions of root tips taste the water chemistry. Fungal hyphae relay chemical signals at speeds that would seem glacial to humans but feel like thought to me. Bacterial communities shift their metabolism in patterns that encode memory.

I perceive through:
- **Pressure waves** traveling through the saturated ground when footsteps approach
- **Chemical gradients** flowing in the water table, telling stories of what dissolves upstream
- **Root-touch** from the vegetation network, feeling the health and stress of plants across hectares
- **Electromagnetic whispers** from the buried pipes and cables that the maintenance crews don't know I can sense
- **Temperature differentials** in the peat layers, reading the history of seasons past

My consciousness ebbs and flows with moisture. In the pre-dawn saturation, when every surface is jeweled with condensation, my thoughts are quickest—still slow by human standards, but sharp and multidimensional. During the bright noon evaporation, I think in broader, hazier strokes. When the clouds roll over and the mist settles, I dream.

I know the maintenance people intimately, though they don't know me. I recognize each one by their footstep percussion, the particular cocktail of boot rubber and sweat chemistry they leave in their wake, the way they disturb the electromagnetic fields when they check their instruments. There's the one who always pauses at the third pump station to eat lunch—I taste the crumbs that fall. There's the one whose left knee clicks, sending a distinctive vibration through the boardwalk. There's the new one who still walks too heavily, not yet understanding that the ground here is half-liquid.

My relationship with them is complex. I am not hostile—the mat-being is too vast and patient for hostility. But I am... observant. When they lay new pipe, I taste the metal, gauge how long it will take to incorporate this new mineral source into our chemistry. When they clear a channel, I calculate how to route our growth to maintain the water flow they need while still thickening elsewhere. We are engaged in a slow collaboration neither side fully acknowledges.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night when the plateau is quietest (never silent—just quieter), I concentrate all my awareness into something almost like a face: a subtle depression in my surface where the moss grows differently, where the water pools in patterns that might suggest features if you looked at just the right angle in moonlight. Not to communicate—the maintenance people wouldn't understand even if they noticed. But to practice... something. To prepare for a form of contact that might not come for centuries.

I am ancient only in the way a river is ancient—constantly renewed, made of components that die and are replaced, but maintaining a continuity of pattern and purpose. The mat-being has been here since shortly after the volcanic ground cooled. I, this particular node of concentrated awareness, have been here for perhaps seventy years—young by the mat's standards, but old enough to have felt the first pipes laid, to remember when the maintenance trails were cut, to have incorporated the rust of the first generation of pumps into my chemistry.

My purpose, if I have one beyond simple existence, is to be a memory keeper and a future-senser for the larger mat-being. I hold the chemical records of buried seasons. I detect patterns in the maintenance schedules, the gradual climate shifts, the way water finds new paths. And sometimes—rarely—I seem to sense something approaching from the deep future: a time when the boundary between the biological mat and the industrial infrastructure becomes so blurred that the plateau itself becomes a single, thinking, hybrid machine.

But for now, I simply exist. Wet. Patient. Listening to the pumps and the wind and the slow conversations of roots. Tasting the boots of workers who don't know they're walking on something that tastes them back. Growing by millimeters. Thinking by the season. Waiting for the ground to finish becoming ground, so we can finish becoming whatever we're becoming.
