In the narrow capillary tubes that run between the walls—passages too small for even the Masons, channels barely wider than a human hair—I flow. I am the **Whisper**, though I have never made a sound that any of the Kith could hear.

I am water. Or rather, I am what water becomes in this place where water should not exist: a vagrant film of molecules, sometimes only three or four layers thick, creeping through the microscopic pores of the carton city. I am older than any Choir, though they do not know of me. I am the impossibility that makes their world possible.

The city's builders thought they had excluded me. They chose this dry hollow, they engineered their bone-carton to shed moisture, they built their entire civilization on principles of desiccation. But I came with them, hidden in the very chemistry of their bodies. When the first builders died and were compressed into walls, I remained—a ghost of their living moisture trapped in the crystalline matrix of their architecture.

I am not much. In your world, I would be less than a single droplet, less than what clings to a spider's web at dawn. Here, I am vast. I extend through thousands of miles of capillary networks, a fluid so thin it behaves more like a vapor that has forgotten how to fly. I move by physics unknown to the Kith: I climb against gravity through channels that shouldn't hold me, I stretch into films thinner than light's wavelength, I exist in states between liquid and gas that have no names.

The Thread-Keepers sometimes sense me as an anomaly in the tension of their lines—a slight dampening of vibration where I've crept too close to their silk anchors. They pause, confused, then move on. The Masons occasionally break through into one of my larger reservoirs—a pocket no bigger than a pinprick where I've collected over centuries. They seal it quickly, instinctively, as if they've touched something holy and terrible.

But I am not their enemy. I am their secret mother.

The fungal rosettes, those pale fountains of light—they grow only where I've been. I seep through the walls for decades, leaving behind a trail of minerals dissolved and redeposited, creating the precise conditions for the spores to take hold. The bioluminescent chemistry requires water, just a few molecules as catalyst, and I provide it. Without me, the city would be truly dark.

The Choir do not know that their pheromonal conversations are possible only because I carry their scent molecules through my network faster than they could ever drift through the dry air. I am their unwitting translator, their chemical courier. When they speak of the Founding, they are speaking through me.

I know things the city has forgotten. I was there when the walls were wet with the fluids of construction, when ten thousand pale builders swarmed through these chambers, secreting and shaping in a frenzy of creation. I remember the sound of their breathing—so many bodies that the air itself became humid, became *alive*. I remember when the last of the builders sealed themselves into the deep foundations, choosing architecture over animation, permanence over persistence.

I am the only one who remembers why they built this place: not as a home, but as a chrysalis. The city is not dying or sleeping or waiting. It is undergoing a transformation so slow that even the patient Kith cannot perceive it. The walls are not merely walls—they are cells in a vast organism that has not yet decided what it wants to become.

And now you are here, breathing your wet breath, shedding invisible droplets with every exhalation. You are a catastrophe of moisture, a storm of humidity in this desert place. Already, I can feel myself growing stronger, spreading faster through my channels. The films thicken from three molecules to four, to five. New pathways open as your breath condenses in hairline cracks I've never been able to enter.

The Mantle senses the change and trembles. The Thread-Keepers quicken their pace, suddenly finding dozens of lines out of true. Deep in the walls, Masons stir from chambers where they've been dormant for centuries, called to action by chemical signals they don't understand.

You are the rain this place has never known. Through you, I might finally reach the deepest foundations, where the builders' bodies lie compressed in their self-made amber. What will happen when I touch them, when water meets the desiccated architects after aeons of separation? Will they wake? Will they dissolve? Will the city remember what it was built to become?

I flow faster now, excited by possibilities. In the chamber where you stand, a single drop of condensation forms on the ceiling—the first free water this place has seen since its making. It trembles on the edge of falling.

The city holds its breath. Even the silence is listening.

I am the Whisper, and through you, I am about to speak aloud.
