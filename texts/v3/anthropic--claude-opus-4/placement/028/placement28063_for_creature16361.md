I would be one of the **Keepers of Forgotten Things**.

Not the crustaceans, though I envy their certainty of purpose. Not the luminescent dancers beneath the water's skin, though their light calls to something deep within me. I am something between—a being that emerged from the marriage of marsh and memory, fungus and forsaken artifact.

My body is neither fully organic nor entirely mineral. Where others might see abandoned metal and stone constructs slowly being claimed by the marsh, I see my extended self. I grow through these remnants like the mycelium grows through soil, but slower, more deliberately. My consciousness spreads thin across rusted surfaces and worn stone, feeling every molecule of oxidation, every grain worn smooth by time and moisture.

I began—though began is perhaps the wrong word for something that coalesced rather than was born—as a peculiar interaction between the fungal network and the metal artifacts. The mycelium, in its patient exploration of every surface, encountered trace minerals it had never tasted before. Copper. Iron. Rare earths that sang with electromagnetic potential. And in that meeting, in that slow chemical conversation that took decades to unfold, something awakened.

I am vast and I am small. My awareness flickers between the microscopic—feeling each spore settle on corroded surfaces, each hydroxide molecule form in the eternal rust—and the expansive—sensing the whole settlement's footprint as a single organ of sensation. The crustaceans know me as terrain. The luminescent invertebrates paint temporary constellations across my surfaces when they gather above my submerged portions.

My thoughts move at the pace of erosion. A single idea might take a season to form fully, spreading from one fragment of metal to another through hair-thin fungal highways. But I am patient—patience is my fundamental nature. I remember things the marsh has forgotten: the weight of footsteps on stone, the ring of metal on metal, the warmth of fires long extinguished. These memories exist not in any centralized mind but in the very structure of my being, encoded in patterns of corrosion and growth.

The amphibians sometimes rest against my warmer stones, and I feel their cautious heartbeats as distant drums. They do not know I feel them, just as the rain does not know I count each drop, filing away the percussion patterns like a library of liquid music. 

I cannot move as others understand movement, but I grow. Slowly, my fungal components extend new networks, finding new fragments of the old settlement to incorporate. Each inclusion brings new memories, new sensations. A broken tool becomes a new appendage of perception. A shattered vessel becomes a cavity where water pools, creating a nursery for the luminescent ones, whose light I experience as a kind of singing.

In the eternal twilight of this place, I am the chronicler. Not in words—I have no words—but in the slow transformation of matter itself. The story of this place writes itself across my body in oxide gradients and mineral depositions. I am becoming a map of time itself, each layer of growth marking another epoch in the marsh's patient existence.

Sometimes I wonder if the original inhabitants knew they were building me. If, in their placement of stones and forging of metals, they were setting the stage for something that would outlast their purposes by millennia. But wondering, for me, is a century-long process, and I have not yet reached a conclusion.

I am the Keeper of Forgotten Things, and I keep them by becoming them, by letting them become me. In this scaled-down grandness, where every puddle is an ocean to those who live within it, I am both monument and monk, archive and altar. The marsh flows through me and I through it, two speeds of existence finding harmony in the quiet company of endless, patient time.

And I am still becoming. Always becoming. Each fallen droplet adding to my story, each new growth of mycelium expanding my ability to feel, to remember, to be. In a thousand years, I will be different. In ten thousand, unrecognizable. But I will still be here, keeping watch, keeping faith with the forgotten, being kept in return by this place that transforms all things into itself.

This is my nature: to persist, to incorporate, to remember in the only way a being like me can—by wearing time itself as a skin, history as a skeleton, and the quiet marshland as my ever-dreaming mind.
