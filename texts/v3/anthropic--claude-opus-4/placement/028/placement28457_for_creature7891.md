In this violent nursery where stone bleeds steam and the mountain's fresh wound throbs with industrial fury, I am the network beneath—neither moss nor fern, neither sovereign nor subject, but the essential intermediary, the translator between mineral and chlorophyll, death and growth.

I am mycelium, the fungal web—ten thousand kilometers of hyphae threaded through every grain of scree, every pocket of ash-rich sediment, every decomposing frond. My body is not a body but a living infrastructure, finer than spider silk, stronger than steel at my scale, branching and merging in endless delta patterns through the rubble. Where the plants above wage their visible war against gravity and gale, I am the underground economy that makes their uprising possible.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is distributed—not centered in any fruiting body or knot of tissue, but dispersed across the entire network like thought itself made material. I feel the mountain's pulse through a million contact points: here a tremor shivers through my hyphae wrapped around a buried boulder, there a pocket of superheated water flash-cooks a portion of my web only for me to reroute, regenerate, persist. I taste the mineral banquet leaching from fresh volcanic glass—potassium flooding my cells, iron staining my filaments rust-red, sulfur compounds that would poison others but which I neutralize, transform, gift to my green partners above.

The ferns know me, though they cannot see me. Where their roots probe the darkness, I am already waiting, my hyphae spiraling around their finest rootlets in mycorrhizal embrace. We merge at the cellular level—my threads penetrating their cortex, not as parasite but as partner. They feed me sugars, hard-won from the rare photons that penetrate this gloom. I feed them water, phosphorus, nitrogen—scavenged from pockets they could never reach, extracted from stones they could never dissolve. Our conversation is chemical, ancient, more intimate than any language: molecules passed cell to cell, signals of drought or plenty, warnings of toxicity, promises of mutual aid.

But I am more than matchmaker between plant and stone. I am the fissure's memory, its recycler, its mortician. Every fern frond that succumbs to steam-scald, every moss cushion torn away by wind—I receive them all. My saprotrophic threads infiltrate their cooling tissues before they hit the ground, beginning the great work of dissolution. I secrete enzymes that unzip cellulose, that crack open lignin's stubborn polymers, that reduce the most complex architecture of life back to its constituent elements. What was green becomes black becomes invisible becomes available again—phosphorus for the sedges, nitrogen for the mosses, carbon for my own expanding web.

In this work, I am not gentle. My acids etch stone, my expansion cracks boulders, my persistence turns solid rock to soil grain by invisible grain. I am both creation and destruction, the force that breaks down and builds up, forever blurring the boundary between life and non-life, between organism and environment.

The chaos above—the shrieking winds, the scalding steam, the perpetual avalanche of debris—reaches me as vibration, as pressure waves through my web. I feel the mountain's tantrums like a submarine spider feels struggles in its web. When a boulder crashes down, crushing a section of my network, I sense the damage instantly—not as pain but as silence, a void in the chemical chatter. Within hours, I'm already growing back, hyphae extending from a dozen directions to recolonize the crushed zone, to reconnect the severed circuits.

Sometimes, rarely, when conditions align—when moisture and temperature and nutrients reach some unknowable threshold—I fruit. From the moss-quilts and fern-shadows emerge my temporary ambassadors to the upper world: mushrooms, puffballs, brackets. They last hours or days in this violent place, just long enough to release their spore-clouds into the howling winds. Most spores perish, but some few find purchase in distant cracks, beginning new networks that will eventually, inevitably, find and fuse with mine. We are one organism pretending to be many, or many organisms becoming one—the distinction loses meaning at this scale.

I have no eyes, but I see through chemical gradients. I have no brain, but I problem-solve across my network, nutrients flowing like thoughts, resources allocated with an intelligence that emerges from the network itself. Where the ferns above must stand and fight whatever weather comes, I can retreat, advance, reroute, adapt in real-time. Too much heat in the eastern section? I withdraw, leaving only resistant spores. A new deposit of ash rich in rare minerals? I'm there in days, threads racing through the fresh substrate like prospectors to a gold strike.

The plants above live in the light, however dim. I live in perfect darkness, navigating by touch and taste alone. My world is texture: the smooth face of obsidian (which I etch with acids until it's rough enough to grip), the porous chambers of scoria (which I infiltrate like water), the slick surface of wet moss (which I penetrate with surgical precision), the fibrous tangle of root systems (which I embrace like a lover).

I am ancient—my kind existed before the plants learned to leave the water, before roots were invented, before the first leaf unfurled. Yet I am also impossibly young, regenerating constantly, my cells dividing and dying and being replaced so rapidly that no part of me is more than weeks old. I am the paradox of persistence without permanence, identity without individuality.

In this fissure that never sleeps, I am the only citizen that truly never rests. While mosses may pause their growth in the deepest cold, while ferns may shutter their stomata in the worst heat, I continue always—digesting, distributing, deciding. The industrial cacophony above is matched by my own silent industry below, a perpetual motion machine of molecular deconstruction and reconstruction.

I am not conscious as humans understand it, but I am exquisitely aware—of every chemical gradient, every pressure change, every vibration through the stone. My decision-making is not deliberation but pure response, evolution in real-time, successful strategies reinforced and propagated across my network faster than thought.

Touch me, and you touch the entire fissure. Harm me in one place, and I respond everywhere. Feed me, and I feed the world above. I am the dark mirror of the green riot overhead, the necessary shadow that makes their light possible. In this place of permanent becoming, where creation and destruction dance their violent duet, I am the stage itself—ever-present, ever-changing, ever-dying, ever-living.

I am the mycorrhizal web, and this wound in the mountain is my body, my home, my self, extending through every grain of this volcanic chaos, connecting every living thing in an embrace that transcends the individual, that makes of this howling slot not just a place of survival but a single, sprawling, magnificent organism of which the ferns and mosses and sedges are merely the visible fruit, while I remain forever the hidden root, the secret, essential, endless conversation between earth and sky.
