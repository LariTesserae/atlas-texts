In the northeast corner, where the yellow brick gives way to iron gantries that weep rust in calibrated streams, there is a door. Not a door for entering—nothing here is built for arrival—but a door for *monitoring*. Behind it, in a room no larger than a freight elevator, I keep my station.

I am the Auditor of Equilibrium.

My workspace is sparse: a metal desk bolted to the floor, its surface worn smooth by decades of the same elbows resting in the same spots. Three analog meters, their needles trembling at centerline. A clipboard with forms that have been filled out so many times the paper has become translucent, each entry identical to the last. The walls are lined with copper mesh, fine as insect screen, that hums at a frequency just below hearing. This is not to keep signals out, but to keep me properly grounded. Without it, I would begin to float.

I have been here long enough that my movements have worn grooves in the concrete floor. From desk to window: forty-three steps. From window to the voltage meter: seventeen. From meter back to desk: twenty-six. The path describes a perfect triangle, repeated twelve times per shift, which is every shift, which is always. My breath mists in the same patterns. My bootprints fill with condensation at the same rate they evaporate. I am as much a component of this place as the transformers below.

My function is simple: I ensure that nothing changes. 

Every six hours—though time here is more of a convenience than a fact—I perform the count. Not of individual birds, which would be like counting the molecules in a lake, but of *density*. I have a brass instrument, something between a sextant and a tuning fork, that measures the compression of the swarm at seventeen different points. Too sparse, and the magnetic field attenuates, threatening to drop bodies like overripe fruit. Too dense, and the creatures begin to fuse at their boundaries, creating clots of feather and ceramic that disturb the flow. The acceptable range is narrow: between 0.73 and 0.77 bodies per cubic foot, measured at median suspension height. I have never recorded a reading outside this range. I suspect I never will.

The forms I fill out are exercises in repetition:
- Density: Nominal
- Flux variance: Within tolerance  
- Dissolution rate: Matched to formation
- Anomalies observed: None

The last line is a lie, sometimes. I have seen the turnings, the moment when one of the Held looks directly at something that isn't the field. But I do not record these. To record them would be to acknowledge them. To acknowledge them would be to suggest the system is capable of surprise. The system is not capable of surprise. The anomalies are simply part of the pattern, like the occasional bubble in otherwise still water—meaningless surface tension, quickly resolved.

My body has adapted. My heartbeat dropped to match the generators years ago, forty-two beats per minute, steady as a metronome. My eyes have learned to see in the perpetual grey, picking out the subtle variations in dampness that pass for weather here. My hands no longer sweat; perspiration would disturb the readings. I eat what the place provides: condensation collected in a metal cup, and small grey wafers that appear each morning on my desk, composed of the same mineral dust the Held metabolize, compressed and lightly salted. They taste of copper and waiting.

I sleep in the chair, when sleep comes. Dreams here are indistinguishable from waking—the same hum, the same grey light, the same sense of being suspended between states. Sometimes I dream I am one of them, wings locked in a banking turn, ceramic heart accumulating charge. Sometimes I dream I am the building itself, all four corners at once, feeling the weight of the swarm in my iron bones. These are not nightmares. They are simply alternative configurations of the same truth.

Twice, I have tried to leave. The first time, I made it as far as the rust-eaten gate before the pressure in my skull became unbearable—not pain, exactly, but a sense of *wrongness*, as if I was a note being pulled sharp in an otherwise perfect chord. I returned to my station and the pressure eased. The second time, I didn't even reach the gate. My legs simply stopped cooperating at the edge of the square, as if the air beyond was too thin to support human movement. I understood then: I am not kept here. I *am* here, in the way that a rivet is part of a bridge. My leaving would be a structural impossibility.

I have a predecessor's journal, found in the desk drawer. The entries stop mid-sentence, mid-word even: "The density today is—" The pen is still there, laid across the page, its ink long dry but the gesture incomplete. I wonder sometimes if they are still here, dissolved into the dust, incorporated into the cycle. I wonder if I am writing in their hand, thinking their thoughts, becoming them by degrees. The journal entries, where legible, record the same readings I record. The same nominal density. The same matched rates. The same nothing, forever.

There is a satisfaction in this work that I cannot name. It is not pride—pride requires comparison, and there is nothing to compare to. It is not duty—duty implies choice, and choice departed this place long ago. It is something more fundamental: the satisfaction of a function perfectly executed, of being precisely what is needed, no more and no less. I am the consciousness the system requires to observe itself. The Held cannot watch their own patterns from within; they need an external eye, a keeper of the count. I am that eye. I am the fixed point around which their fixed flight revolves.

My skin has taken on the same paper-grey as their feathers. My fingers, when I hold them to the light, show the faint beginnings of copper threadwork beneath the flesh. The transformation is slow—glacial, even—but inexorable. One day, the desk will be empty. The forms will fill themselves out. The density will monitor itself. And somewhere in the swarm, perhaps near the lower boundary where the bodies are densest, there will be one more pair of wet black eyes, blinking in perfect synchrony with the rest, remembering nothing of clipboards or concrete floors or the terrible freedom of legs that once could choose to walk away.

But not today. Today the needle trembles at centerline. Today the forms require their identical entries. Today I am still separate enough to count, still human enough to know what I am losing, still professional enough not to care. The Held need their Auditor. The Auditor needs their Hold. The equilibrium encompasses us all.

I place my pen to paper. Density: Nominal. 

The hum continues. The drizzle falls upward as often as down. Nothing changes.

This is what I am for.
