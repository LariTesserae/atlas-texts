I am one of the channel-keepers.

There are seven of us, though the number is not fixed — it rises and falls with the generations, with the needs of the stone, with the patience required to learn what the channels want. We do not have a guild or a charter. We have only this: the knowledge, passed from one keeper to the next, of how water moves through the Dripping, and why it matters that it moves correctly.

My territory runs from the eastern esplanade down through the commercial terrace, following the main channel that feeds the textile quarter's steam-engines. It is a good territory — not the oldest (that belongs to Mem, who keeps the deep quarter's arterial flow), but old enough that the stone knows itself, old enough that the channel has worn its bed so smooth I can run my hand along it in the dark and feel no seam.

I learned from my mother's mother, who learned from her mother's sister, who learned from a man whose name we have forgotten but whose mark — three parallel grooves cut into the stone at each major junction — still guides my hands when I work. This is how knowledge moves in the Dripping: not through books or schools but through the patient teaching of stone to flesh to stone again.

A channel-keeper's work is this: to know where the water wants to go and to ensure it can go there. This sounds simple. It is not.

The channels are carved things, yes, but they are also grown things — the way a riverbed is both carved and grown, the way a tree is both grown and carved by wind. The water that moves through them is not merely water. It carries the plateau's intentions: minerals leached from stone so old it predates naming, the iron taste that gives our water its particular weight, the microscopic life that blooms and dies in the dark between the commercial terrace and the smelting yard. When a channel runs correctly, the water arrives where it needs to arrive with its character intact. When it runs poorly, the engines wheeze, the furnaces cool unevenly, the condensation falls in the wrong places or not at all.

I rise before the eastern bell, in the hour when the fog is thickest and the channels run fullest. My tools are simple: a bronze probe worn thin by generations of use, a leather bag of copper shims, a piece of chalk that writes on wet stone, and the most important tool — fingertips trained to read the water's depth and speed by touch alone. The plateau's moisture network cannot be seen from above; too much of it runs under flagstones, through walls, behind the facades of buildings that have been built and rebuilt around the channels they shelter. So we work by feel, by sound, by the particular resistance water makes when it encounters an obstruction.

This morning, like every morning, I begin at the eastern junction, where my channel splits from the main arterial. I place my palm flat against the stone and feel for the vibration — steady, a low hum that means the flow is good. I follow it down, my hand on the wall beside the channel, feeling for the places where the vibration changes. At the first curve, where the channel dips below street level, I kneel and work my fingers into the inspection gap. The water is cold — colder than the air, which means the aquifer is high. Good. When the aquifer drops, the water warms slightly, and warm water carries less of what the engines need.

The first obstruction is where it always is: the junction where the secondary feed splits off toward the bakery row. Leaves from the terraced gardens above, worked their way through the grates. I clear them with the probe, feeling for the moment when the water sighs and resumes its full flow. This is the satisfaction of the work — not the clearing itself but the moment after, when the channel remembers how to run.

By the time I reach the textile quarter, the morning fog has begun to thin. The weavers are arriving, their faces still creased from sleep, nodding to me as they pass. They know what I do, in the way that everyone in the Dripping knows what everyone does — not the details but the shape of it, the place it holds in the city's slow machinery. When I was young, I thought they must resent us, the channel-keepers, for the power we have over their water. But there is no power here, only maintenance. We keep what is. We ensure what was continues. We are servants of continuity.

The textile quarter's main channel runs exposed along the northern wall, and here I can see what I usually only feel — the water moving with its particular patience, carrying its cargo of minerals and memory. The channel is stained dark here, almost black, from centuries of iron deposits. In certain lights, when the condensation is heavy and the fog has not quite cleared, the water seems to glow — not with light but with density, as though it were more than liquid, as though it were the plateau's blood made visible.

I find the second obstruction by sound: a high, thin note in the water's voice that means air in the channel. This is more serious. Air pockets disturb the flow in ways that compound downstream, creating turbulence that can damage the engine feeds. I work my way back, tapping the stone every few feet, listening for the hollow sound that means the pocket's location. I find it where the channel passes under the weavers' storage house — a section of stone that has cracked, allowing air to enter from a ventilation shaft above.

This requires shims. I work three of them into the crack, tap them home with the heel of my hand, seal the gaps with the claylike paste we make from ground stone and condensation. It is delicate work. Too much pressure and the crack widens. Too little and the seal fails within days. The stone here is old — older than the buildings above it, older than the channel itself. It remembers when it was part of the plateau's uncarved flesh, and sometimes, in its cracking, you can feel it trying to return to that state.

By midday, I have worked my way through half my territory. My knees are wet from kneeling at inspection points. My hands smell of iron and moss. The fog has cleared entirely now, leaving only the faintest moisture on the stone — enough to darken it but not enough to drip. This is when the channels run quietest, in the clear hours between morning fog and evening fog, when the water moves without urgency through its carved paths.

I eat my lunch sitting on the retaining wall above the smelting terrace, watching the furnace-keeper tend his fire. He is new — only forty years at the work — but already he has the stillness that comes from listening to flame. We nod to each other but do not speak. Speech would disturb the thing we are both listening to: the way the plateau breathes through its industries, the way the water and the fire and the stone collaborate in their slow making.

The afternoon brings me to the deep reaches of my territory, where the channel descends through a series of drops toward the engine-house. Here the water gains speed, and the sound changes from a whisper to something more like song — not melody but rhythm, the percussive voice of water teaching stone to yield. The channels here are very old. Some sections show the tool-marks of the original carvers, careful strikes that follow the stone's grain rather than fighting it. Other sections have been worn so smooth they seem organic, as though the water carved them alone over millennia.

I find the third obstruction by smell: the sharp, green scent of algae blooming where it shouldn't. At a junction where sunlight never reaches, a colony has established itself, feeding on minerals in a slow leak from above. This is delicate. The algae is not entirely unwelcome — in the right places, it helps purify the water, consuming excess minerals that would otherwise deposit in the engines. But here, where the channel narrows before the main engine feed, it will eventually choke the flow.

I scrape carefully with the probe, collecting the algae in my bag. We do not waste it. The furnace-keeper uses it in his work, mixing it with coal to produce a flame that burns cooler and longer. Everything in the Dripping has more than one use. Everything serves more than one purpose. This is not efficiency — efficiency is a lowland concept. This is relationship.

By the time I complete my rounds, the evening fog has begun to gather in the low places. The water in the channels responds, running fuller as the condensation adds to the flow. I make my way back up through the terraces, my body tired in the particular way that comes from a day spent reading stone. At each major junction, I pause to feel the vibration one more time, to assure myself that what I have done will hold through the night.

Other channel-keepers pass me on their own routes. We acknowledge each other with small gestures — a raised hand, a nod, the particular smile of people who share an understanding that requires no words. Mem passes on her way to the deep quarter, carrying the long-handled probe she uses for the arterial channel's depths. She is eighty now, has been keeping the deep flows for sixty years. Her replacement has been chosen — a quiet child from the western district who has the right kind of stillness — but Mem will work until she cannot, and then she will teach until she cannot, and then she will sit by the black pools with the salamanders and let the plateau take back what she has learned.

This is the way of channel-keepers. We do not retire. We deepen.

The evening finds me at home, in the narrow house I inherited from my mother's mother, built against the channel wall so closely I can feel the water's movement through the stone when I lie in bed. I clean my tools, oil the probe, wash the chalk from my hands. My husband brings tea — made with channel water, of course, carrying its iron taste and its memory of depth. We sit at the window that overlooks the eastern district, watching the fog settle into the streets like something alive.

Tomorrow I will rise before the bell and do this again. And the next day. And the next. The channels do not pause, and neither do we who tend them. We are part of the city's circulation, as necessary and unremarkable as blood. When I was young, I thought this might be a burden — the repetition, the responsibility, the knowledge that without our work the city would slow and eventually stop. But now I understand it differently. We are not carrying a burden. We are participating in a conversation that began before we were born and will continue after we are gone. The plateau speaks through its water, and we are the ones who keep the words flowing.

There is more to say — about the winter months when the channels freeze and we must work with hammers and heated probes, about the dreams that come to channel-keepers who work too long in the deep quarter, about the way water remembers the hands that guide it. But the fog has thickened, and my husband has lit the lamp, and there is a comfort in ending the day as the channels end their visible run — quietly, without fuss, trusting that what moves in darkness will emerge again in light.

I am a channel-keeper. My mother's mother was a channel-keeper. Her mother's sister was a channel-keeper. And somewhere in the Dripping tonight, a child is learning to place their hand against stone and feel the water's pulse, is beginning to understand that they too will be part of this — this slow work, this patient attention, this relationship with something that runs deeper than the deepest pool, older than the oldest wall, more patient than the plateau itself.

The water moves through its channels. The city breathes through its water. And we who tend the flow ensure that both continue, day after day, fog after fog, until the plateau completes whatever it is trying to say.
