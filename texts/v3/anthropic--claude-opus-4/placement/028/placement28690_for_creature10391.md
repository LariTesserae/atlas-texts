# The Cartographer-Hermit

I am the one who maps what refuses to be mapped.

My shelter is a repurposed aqueduct junction, where three of the Monks' water channels once met. The ceiling is the underside of a mesa, the walls are carved stone polished mirror-smooth by ancient water flow. I've sealed the broken sections with salvaged furnace doors, creating a space that stays cool even at noon. My bed is a shelf I've widened with patient chiseling, padded with shed Sky-Fisher wing membrane I found dried in a high cleft. 

I wake before dawn to catch the Ghost Miasma at its thickest. This is when I work.

My maps are not paper—paper would crumble here in a season. They're carved into sheets of soft siltstone I pry from the canyon walls, each one the size of my torso, thin as my thumb. I use Lithovore mandibles as chisels (I trade water-soaked moss to the drakes for their kills). The mandibles stay diamond-sharp for months.

But here's the thing about mapping the Mesas: the land doesn't want to be reduced to lines. I tried at first, years ago when I still thought like a lowlander. Drew the canyons as they appeared from above, neat parallel slashes. Useless. The verticality laughs at flat representation. So I developed my own system—maps that show elevation as time. The higher you go, the older the mark. Each level of canyon is a different hour of the day. It takes weeks to carve a single sheet.

I carry water in the hollow leg bones of some massive thing that died before the Monks came. The bones are dense as stone now, mineralized, but they still hold liquid better than any flask. I know where seventeen pocket springs hide, and I've memorized the taste of each—this one sharp with iron, that one sweet with calcium, another so bitter with sulfates it burns. I survived my first year here by following a wounded Sky-Fisher to water.

The Cliff-Drakes know me. I'm the only thing that moves through their territory regularly without being prey. Sometimes I find them arranged on my walls in the morning, soaking in the first heat, their subsonic calls making my stone sheets hum. Once, I watched one shed its skin, peeling off in a single perfect piece like removing a glove. I kept it, of course. Stretched over a frame of Canyon-Willow, it makes a window that filters light the color of amber.

I map for the Traders who come up from the lowlands twice a year, their caravans following the only route wide enough for beasts of burden. They pay in dried fruit, metal tools, news of the world below. They think I'm mapping resources—water, shelter, safe passages. They're not wrong, but they're not right either. I'm mapping something else.

The Monks' glyphs change. Not all of them, and not quickly, but they do. The ones near water shift fastest. I've been tracking the changes for six years now, copying each variation onto stone sheets, trying to find the pattern. Sometimes a thermodynamic equation becomes a watershed diagram. Sometimes a spiral becomes a straight line becomes a spiral again. The glyphs in the path of the Ghost Miasma are teaching themselves something, and I'm trying to learn what.

I found one of the Burrowers' holes last month. Perfect circle, smooth as glass, going straight down into black nothing. I dropped a stone and counted. Seven seconds before the sound came back, not a splash but a soft thud, like hitting meat. I've started mapping their holes too, but carefully. There's a pattern to where they appear—always in the softest stone, always where the Ghost Miasma wells up strongest. The Monks were trying to reach the planet's heat. I think the Burrowers got there first.

My strangest map isn't carved at all. It's made of sounds. I've learned that each canyon has its own tone when the wind hits it right. By placing curved sheets of stone at certain angles, I can make the canyons sing specific notes. I'm building a map you play like an instrument, where each tone tells you where you are. The Sky-Fishers gather when I practice, riding the sound waves like thermals.

The Great Spire's hum keeps perfect time. I've carved marks on my wall for each pulse—40,000 beats between dawn and dawn. It never varies, not by a single beat. I tried sleeping at its base once, to feel if the rhythm changed at night. I lasted two hours before the vibration in my bones drove me away, but in those two hours, I dream-saw the mountain's veins—channels of heat rising from the core, branching like a great tree made of temperature differentials. I've been trying to map that tree ever since.

In the rains—those violent, twenty-minute storms—I strip naked and stand on the highest shelf I can reach, letting the water scour me clean. It's the only time I feel truly wet, truly part of the water cycle instead of stealing from it in spoonfuls. The rain tastes different up here, metallic and electric. After, I'm covered in a film of minerals that dries to a faint shimmer. The Traders joke that I'm turning to stone like the moss. They're not entirely wrong. My fingernails are thick now, ridged like the canyon walls. My skin has taken on the faint orange tint of oxidized iron.

But this is what the Mesas demand: you become part of the geological record or you leave. The Monks knew this. Their bones are probably down there with the Burrowers, mineralized, joining the stone thoughts of this thinking landscape. Sometimes I wonder if that's what my maps really are—my way of joining the conversation between the Ghost Miasma and the glyphs, between the Spire's hum and the Burrowers' thumps.

I will die here, eventually. When I do, I've already chosen my transformation. There's a slot canyon near the Spire where the Ghost Miasma never fully burns off, where the walls are covered in weeping glyphs. I've carved a shallow depression there, just my size. When the time comes, I'll lie in it and let the mineral water do its work. In a hundred years, I'll be another layer in the sediment. In a thousand, someone might find my maps and wonder what kind of creature thought in vertical time, who saw the desert as an ocean of stone with tides that take centuries to turn.

Until then, I chart the unchartable. I map the places where stone becomes thought, where industry becomes geology, where the only currency is elevation and the only constant is change so slow it looks like stillness.

Tomorrow I'll wake to the Ghost Miasma and add another line to another map. The Cliff-Drakes will watch. The Spire will hum its perfect count. And somewhere far below, the Burrowers will thump their deep, slow rhythm, and the mountain will answer.
