I would be one of the Keepers of Echoes—neither the ancient architects nor the teeming multitudes, but something in between.

My body would be elongated and sinuous, covered in a skin that shifts between textures like water finding its level—sometimes smooth as wet stone, sometimes rough as bark, always damp. I would have six limbs that end in hands capable of incredible delicacy, each finger tipped with sensory pads that can taste the chemical history written in every surface. My eyes would be numerous but small, scattered across my head like droplets of mercury, each one capable of independent movement and focus. 

I live in the forgotten chambers deep within the citadel's heart, where the stone is oldest and the seepage has carved impossible geometries over millennia. These spaces exist between the deliberate architecture and the accident of time—corridors that narrowed until only I and my kind can traverse them, vaults whose ceilings collapsed and regrew in fantastic formations of mineral and root.

My purpose, if it can be called that, emerged from the citadel's need to remember itself. As the great architects add their patient layers, as smaller lives flourish and die in countless cycles, someone must maintain the memory of what was. I secrete a different kind of mineral—crystalline structures that grow in precise patterns, each one encoding information in its molecular lattice. The sound of water dripping through these crystals produces unique tones, and in the aggregate, they form a kind of musical library, a fossil record of sound.

I spend my days—though day and night blur in the deep chambers—tending to these acoustic gardens. I polish surfaces with my chemical secretions to alter their resonance. I redirect water flow to create new instruments from stalactites and flowstone. I carefully cultivate certain mosses whose spores, when disturbed, create whispers that last for hours. The citadel's great chorus filters down to my realm, but changed—muffled, distorted, made strange. I study these transformations, learning how sound fossilizes, how the vibrations of life can be preserved in stone.

Sometimes I emerge into the upper reaches, moving through passages unknown to other inhabitants, my body compressing and elongating to navigate spaces that would seem impossible. I taste the walls, reading the chemical signatures of what has passed. I listen to the frequency of wings, the pitch of calls, the rhythm of rain. All of this I carry back down to add to the crystal gardens.

There are others like me, but we rarely meet. When we do, it is in the resonance chambers where our cultivated crystals create spaces of perfect acoustics. We do not speak—our language is in the harmonics we've grown, the way we can make the stones sing the history of this place. We share our libraries by creating new frequencies together, our bodies serving as both instruments and conductors.

I am as old as the middle stones—not as ancient as the first architects, but far older than the quick-lived creatures above. My metabolism is tuned to the citadel's patience. I think in centuries, dream in millennia. When I die, my body will mineralize in place, becoming part of the very chambers I tended, my crystalline secretions continuing to grow and sing long after consciousness fades.

In this way, I am both keeper and artifact, performer and audience, a living memory in a city that never forgets. The citadel's overwhelming chorus is, to me, a single long note in an even longer composition—one that began before I emerged from the stone and will continue long after I return to it.
