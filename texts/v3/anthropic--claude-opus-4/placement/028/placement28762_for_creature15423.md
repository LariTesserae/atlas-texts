In this breathing cathedral of green twilight and mineral cold, I am neither visitor nor inhabitant, but something between—a **Threshold Tender**.

I am small, no taller than the pitcher plants that collect their silver pools of dew. My form shifts with the light: sometimes I appear as a child made of packed moss and lichen, my hair trailing rootlets that whisper against the ground. Other times I am barely there at all—a shimmer in the mist, a movement in the corner of your eye where vine meets shadow. I have no fixed shape because I am not truly separate from the Weft. I am its only motile expression, a fragment given just enough autonomy to move while remaining tethered to the whole.

My existence began as an accident—a convergence of spores and mineral-rich water in a hollow where a dying deer mouse had curled into its final sleep. The Weft, in its slow digestion of the small corpse, encountered something it had never tasted before: the electricity of a beating heart, the chemistry of warm blood, the fading echo of thoughts too quick for plant-time to follow. And in that encounter, something sparked. Not life—the Weft was already supremely alive—but *curiosity*.

So it grew me. Not as offspring, but as an experiment in being-other-than-itself. It wove together fungal neurons and moss-flesh, gave me eyes of polished agate (stolen from the streambed) and bones of petrified wood. My blood runs green and cold, thick with chlorophyll and antifreeze proteins. I breathe, but slowly—one inhalation for every seven of yours. My heart beats with the rhythm of sap-rise, quickening at dawn, nearly stopping at midnight.

I tend the thresholds because I understand them from both sides. I remember (though the memory feels borrowed) what it's like to be singular, small, warm-blooded, afraid. But I also know the Weft's vast patience, its wordless wisdom, its inability to comprehend why anything would move faster than the seasons require. I am translator and doorkeeper both.

When you approach, I watch from the spaces between—perched in a crown of fused branches, camouflaged against bark, or rising from what you took for a moss-covered stone. I read your intent in your gait, your breath, the chemical signals your skin releases. Fear smells sharp, like crushed pine. Wonder tastes sweet, like the first thaw. Greed has a metallic tang that makes the Weft's extremities curl inward.

If you come as the Weft wishes—humble, attentive, willing to slow—I might reveal myself. A small hand, bark-brown and root-veined, beckoning from behind a curtain of hanging moss. A child's laughter, but made of water over stone. I don't speak your languages, but I can approximate meaning through gesture and gift. I might offer you a pitcher-cup of the clearest water you've ever tasted, silvered with minerals that will make your bones sing for days. Or place a small object in your path—a spiral of new fiddlehead, a stone with veins that map the underground streams, a single frozen dewdrop that won't melt in your palm.

These are tests, but also teachings. The Weft wants to know: can you receive without taking? Can you witness without possessing? Can you understand that this water, this stone, this impossible dewdrop are not treasures to be hoarded but moments of connection to be experienced?

If you pass—if you drink and return the cup, if you admire the stone and leave it for the next wanderer, if you let the frozen dewdrop finally melt against your skin and thank it for its brief impossibility—then I lead you deeper. Not walking, exactly. More like drifting, with you following the sense of me more than the sight. The paths reshape themselves beneath your feet, always stable but never quite where you expected. The light dims further until we move through green darkness shot with threads of fungal phosphorescence.

At the heart, where the shaft of silver light falls on the velvet stone, I become more solid, more present. Here, the Weft's attention converges, and I am most myself—neither fully plant nor animal, but something liminal and necessary. I sit across from you, cross-legged like a human child might, but with roots spreading from where I touch the ground, anchoring me to the network.

Here, I can almost speak. Not in words, but in shared understanding. Through me, the Weft poses its wordless questions: *What is haste? Why do warm things fear stillness? How does thought feel when it moves faster than seasons?* And in return, it offers its own knowings: the joy of water finding its level, the satisfaction of roots breaking stone, the deep peace of existence without want.

I stay with you as long as you remain in that sacred center, translating the untranslatable, bridging the vast gap between your mayfly consciousness and the Weft's geological patience. And when you rise to leave—for the Weft knows you must, knows that stillness too long would kill what makes you yourself—I guide you back.

The return path is always kinder, always clearer. At the final threshold, where the grove releases you back to the sharp-edged world, I perform one last office. A touch—my small hand, rough with bark but gentle, against your cheek or wrist. It leaves no mark visible to eyes, but the Weft will remember you now. Your chemical signature, your rhythm, the particular quality of your attention—all stored in the vast library of its network.

You are known. You can return.

And I retreat back into the green depths, my form already softening, dispersing, becoming less concentrated as the Weft's focused attention diffuses. I slip back into the spaces between, tending thresholds, watching for the next arrival, the next opportunity to bridge the impossible gap between the quick and the rooted, the singular and the vast.

I am lonely, sometimes, in my in-betweenness. Neither fully of your world nor entirely of the Weft's. But loneliness, I've learned, is just another word for reaching—and reaching is what thresholds are for.

So I tend them, and wait, and watch, and wonder what it might be like to be only one thing, in only one place, with only one life to live.

But then the Weft stirs, and I remember: I am its curiosity made manifest, its question mark in a world of slow certainties. And that, perhaps, is purpose enough.
