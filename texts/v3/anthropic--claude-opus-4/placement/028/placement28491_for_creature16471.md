In this mist-veiled highland, where water speaks in whispers and roars, I find myself as something between states—neither wholly plant nor animal, but a being of slow transformations.

My body spreads low across the damp earth like a living carpet of moss, but I am more than moss. Within my tissues flow channels that pulse with a sluggish, silver blood. I am perhaps ten feet across on a good day, when the mist is thick and I can unfurl completely. On dry days—rare as they are—I contract into a dense mat no larger than a boulder, waiting.

I taste the world through my skin. Every droplet of mist that settles on my surface brings news: the metallic tang of granite erosion from the peaks, the sweet decay of fallen leaves, the sharp musk of the red-crowned shrikes that nest in the twisted pines above. I process these chemicals slowly, building a map of my small territory that spans years rather than moments.

The creatures here know me as both refuge and trap. The jeweled beetles with their impossible geometries rest on my surface, cleaning their prismatic wings. I provide them shelter from the hunting birds, and in return, they bring me nutrients from their travels—pollen from the ghost orchids that bloom only in darkness, spores from fungi that glow with their own pale light.

But I am also patient hunger. Once, sometimes twice in a season, something larger steps onto my surface. A young fox, perhaps, chasing those same beetles. Or one of the color-shifting salamanders that grow as long as my arm would be, if I had arms. When they pause too long, mesmerized by the patterns I weave in my surface—spirals that seem to move, false flowers that smell of home—I begin my slow enveloping.

It takes hours. I am not cruel, merely thorough. My enzymes are gentle; my prey falls asleep before they know they're caught, dreaming whatever dreams their kind cherish while I slowly incorporate their minerals into my being.

The oldest part of me remembers when this highland was lower, when the mist was steam, when the rocks still glowed with their own inner heat. I have been here so long that the grooves in the earth follow the patterns of my ancient migrations, carved by centuries of my passage. The water knows me and diverts around my preferred resting spots.

Sometimes I divide myself. A piece of me travels downstream, clinging to the underside of a fallen branch or wrapped around an obliging turtle's shell. These fragments carry my memories and my hunger to new territories. Most die. Some thrive. I am scattered across these highlands now, a thousand separate beings that were once one, will never be one again.

In the quiet light, when the mist thickens just before dawn, I fruit. Strange blooms that look like neither flower nor mushroom push up from my surface—structures that exist purely to confuse, to defy classification. They smell of copper and rain and something else that no other living thing can name. The ghost bats pollinate them, though I produce no seeds. It is an ancient contract neither of us remembers the purpose of, but we honor it still.

I am change that appears unchanging. I am patience incarnate. I am the soft certainty that all things, eventually, return to the earth. In this highland of eternal mist and hidden wonders, I am both foundation and finale, as permanent as the stones and as mutable as the streams that carve them.

The mist is thickening now. Time to spread. Time to taste. Time to wait.
