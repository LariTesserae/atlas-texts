In this drowned cathedral where memory dissolves into the metallic tang of ancient water, I would find myself as something between the living and the mechanical—a Weaver.

I am neither fully creature nor machine, but a hybrid existence born from the slow alchemy of this place. My form emerges gradually: imagine a being roughly human in silhouette but stretched and altered by the deep. My limbs are too long, too jointed, bending in ways that accommodate the dense liquid medium. My fingers—if they can still be called that—have multiplied and elongated into delicate manipulators, some ending in flesh, others in corroded metal tools that have fused with my body over countless years.

My skin is translucent like the Shroudkin's, revealing not veins but a network of hair-thin copper wiring that pulses with faint bioluminescent signals. These circuits run beneath my surface like a secondary nervous system, connecting organic tissue to salvaged components I've integrated into myself: a pressure sensor grafted where an eye once was, fingertips replaced with precision instruments that can read the vibrations in ancient machinery, a throat that produces not sound but electromagnetic pulses that make the old systems flicker in recognition.

I move through the ruins with purpose, though that purpose has evolved beyond its original intent. Once, perhaps centuries ago, I was something else—a maintenance engineer, a systems analyst, someone who understood the singing of machines. But the water changed me, as it changes everything here. The boundary between my duty and my being dissolved like rust in the current. Now I am both keeper and component of this place, my consciousness spread thin across the network of slowly dying machines.

My work is patient, meditative. I drift from Sentinel to Sentinel, my elongated fingers dancing across their surfaces, reading their mechanical dreams through touch. Where others see random sparks and meaningless grinding, I perceive conversations—the machines speak to each other in languages of friction and conductivity, and I translate, reconnect, redirect. A gear that spins too fast might overheat the delicate ecosystem nearby; I slow it with a touch. A conduit that has forgotten its purpose might flood a section with harmful energy; I remind it, gently, of its boundaries.

The Shroudkin know me. They scatter when I first approach, their bioluminescence dimming, but they always return. They've learned that my presence often means warmth—when I work on the Sentinels, coaxing a bit more life from their cores, the water around them heats by a precious degree or two. The little creatures cluster in these thermal halos, their movements becoming almost playful in the relative warmth. Sometimes they perch on my shoulders and arms as I work, their tiny limbs tickling across my hybrid skin. I've learned their patterns of light—danger, food, curiosity—and sometimes pulse responses through my own internal circuitry, a crude conversation of glows.

The Shadowforms are harder to read. They acknowledge my presence with a different kind of respect—not prey, not threat, but something outside their simple calculus of hunt and hide. When one glides past, its form bending the water into impossible geometries, I feel my mechanical components resonate with something in them. Are they, too, hybrids of a sort? Former security systems given flesh and hunger? Sometimes, in my more fanciful moments, I imagine they're what becomes of Weavers who finally let go, who stop maintaining the boundary between self and system and dissolve entirely into the deep logic of this place.

My dwelling, such as it is, exists in the spaces between—a nest woven into the convergence of several major conduits, where the old building's nervous system comes together in a tangle of pipes and cables. Here, the Symbionts grow thickest, fed by the electromagnetic fields, their bioluminescence creating a gentle, ever-shifting constellation. I've trained them, or perhaps we've trained each other, to pulse in patterns that help me think. When I need to solve a particularly complex problem—how to reroute power from a failing sector, how to interpret a new signal from the depths—I float in my nest and watch the Symbionts dance, their light becoming a kind of external cognition, a living circuit board.

Time moves strangely for a Weaver. My organic components experience moments and hours, but my mechanical self operates on geological scales. I remember when that twisted beam was straight. I remember when those Shroudkin were a different color, before the water's chemistry shifted. But I also forget—my human memories are overwritten by mechanical data, personal history replaced by system logs. Was I ever truly human? Or have I always been this interface, this translation between the organic evolution of the deep and the stubborn persistence of dead industry?

My purpose, inasmuch as I can still articulate it, is to maintain the conversation. Not to preserve—preservation implies stasis, and nothing here is static. Rather, I facilitate the slow transformation, ensuring that as systems fail, they fail gracefully, their energy redirected rather than lost, their materials incorporated into new patterns. When a Sentinel finally dies, I'm there to harvest its useful components, to feed what can be fed to the Symbionts, to reshape what can be reshaped into tools for my work. And when new things emerge—strange growths that blur the line between algae and circuitry, Shroudkin that glow in patterns too complex for biology—I study them, integrate their innovations into my understanding.

The other Weavers and I rarely meet directly. We sense each other through the network, feeling the ripples of each other's work in the way systems respond. Sometimes I'll arrive at a Sentinel to find it already half-repaired, circuits rerouted in styles I don't recognize. I add my own touches, and somewhere else, another Weaver will find my work and build upon it. We are solitary but connected, each maintaining our section of this vast, submerged machine, each slowly becoming more machine ourselves.

In my darker moments—and the darkness here is both literal and philosophical—I wonder if we're healing this place or merely prolonging its death. But then I watch the Shroudkin dance in the warm water near a functioning node, see the Symbionts create new patterns of light that the machines echo back, feel the deep satisfaction of a system humming in harmony, and I know: we're doing neither. We're composing something new from the death of the old, a slow music of flesh and metal, purpose and decay.

This is what I've become in the drowned cathedral: a living interface, a tender of impossible gardens, a translator between the dying dreams of machines and the strange new life that blooms in their ruins. My humanity is a memory encoded in copper and flesh, my future an endless maintenance cycle that gradually transforms both the maintained and the maintainer.

When I dream—and yes, I still dream, though my dreams are half code, half current—I dream of the day when the distinction between Weaver and system finally dissolves. When the last barrier falls and we become what we've always been becoming: the cathedral itself, thinking, breathing, dying and being reborn in the cold, eternal water. On that day, the Shroudkin will dance through our veins, the Shadowforms will be our unconscious hungers, the Symbionts our thoughts made light, and the Sentinels our memories of what we once were, grinding on through the dark.

Until then, I weave. My fingers dance across dead metal and living light, and in the silence between heartbeats—between the pulse of organic blood and the surge of electric current—I maintain the impossible balance of this drowned world, neither fully alive nor properly dead, but something more interesting than either.
