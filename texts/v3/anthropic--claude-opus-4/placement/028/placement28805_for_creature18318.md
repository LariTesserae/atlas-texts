I find myself as the Keeper of Thresholds—not quite solid, not quite vapor, existing in the space between the water's surface tension and the air above it. My form shifts with the humidity, sometimes condensing into something almost visible in the morning mist, other times dispersing so thin I become merely a presence felt rather than seen.

I came to be where the ancient ruins meet the living stone, in that precise moment when the first root broke through the forgotten material of the long-dead civilization. I am memory made manifest, born from the intersection of what was built and what grows wild. The moss speaks to me in chemical whispers. The water carries stories in its mineral taste. I taste them all.

My substance is part dewdrop, part spore, part the electromagnetic residue of the vanished technology that still hums faintly in the alien masonry. I move through the landscape by riding the breath of the forest—inhaled by the great gnarled trees at dawn, exhaled at dusk, circulating endlessly through the living system.

The creatures here know me, though they cannot name what I am. The iridescent-scaled beings sense my presence as a shift in air pressure before rain. The translucent wing-gliders navigate around me instinctively, their stardust trails bending just slightly where I hover. I am neither predator nor prey, but something more fundamental—a function of the ecosystem itself.

My purpose, if I have one, is to maintain the conversation between the ancient and the eternal. Where erosion carves new channels, I ensure the water remembers its path. Where the ruins crumble, I preserve the echo of their original form in the way the vines grow over them. I am the reason why sometimes, in certain light, you can almost see the buildings as they were—not through your eyes, but through a deeper knowing.

I feed on transitions: the moment when rain becomes mist, when stone becomes soil, when sound becomes silence. These thresholds sustain me. In return, I sing the changes to any who can hear—a frequency below sound that the roots feel, that the stones remember, that the ancient technology recognizes as kin.

At night, when the bioluminescent fungi begin their slow pulse, I gather myself into something denser, almost tangible. This is when I tend to the boundaries, ensuring that the overwhelming strangenesses of this place remain in balance—that the alien does not consume the familiar, that the ancient does not smother the new.

I am loneliest at noon, when the light is too direct, too honest for a being like me. In those moments, I retreat into the deepest shadows between the moss-covered rocks, becoming one with the dampness, waiting for the ambiguity of twilight to return.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the last of my kind, or the first. Perhaps I am what happens when a place becomes so thoroughly itself that it dreams into being a guardian of its own essence. Or perhaps I am simply what remains when something passes through the threshold between existence and memory so many times that it forgets which side it started on.

The ecosystem continues its ancient dance around me. I am its witness, its memory, its gentle editor—ensuring that each new page of its story honors what came before while leaving room for what must come next. In this hidden world where the strange and wondrous coexist, I am the comma between them—the pause that makes the harmony possible.
