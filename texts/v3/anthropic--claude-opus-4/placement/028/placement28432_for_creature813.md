In the district of Thornway, where the city's eastern edge dissolves into mist, I am a Threshold Keeper—one of perhaps two dozen souls who maintain the boundary markers that define where Brynwater ends and the unknown begins.

My dwelling is built into a natural depression in the plateau's rim, a structure that from above might be mistaken for merely another weathered outcropping. The walls are fitted stone, chinked with a mortar mixed from crushed lichen and clay that hardens to match the surrounding rock. Inside, the space curves like the interior of a shell—no sharp corners, everything flowing into everything else. Water trickles down one wall through carved channels, collecting in a basin that doubles as both drinking source and meditative focus.

I am neither tall nor short by Brynwater standards, but the years of edge-walking have given me a particular gait—weight always slightly forward, ready to compensate for sudden gusts or shifting visibility. My hair, once dark, now carries streaks of premature silver that spiral from my temples like frozen fog. The second eyelids that protect my vision have thickened from constant use, giving my gray eyes a perpetually contemplative appearance.

My work exists in the space between the city's certainty and the void's mystery. Each morning, I walk the edge-paths, checking the stone cairns that mark safe routes and the iron posts driven deep into the bedrock that anchor guide-ropes during the worst mists. I maintain the warning bells that ring when visibility drops below safe levels, their bronze voices carrying far into the city proper.

But the practical tasks are only part of my role. I am also a chronicler of the liminal—recording what emerges from and disappears into the fog. My journals, bound in waxed leather and written in waterproof ink, contain decades of observations: the patterns of birds that fly up from below, the debris that occasionally appears on the ledges, the sounds that rise from the hidden depths during certain atmospheric conditions.

The other Threshold Keepers and I meet monthly at the new moon, gathering in a circular chamber carved into the plateau's edge where the mist enters through carefully placed openings, swirling around us as we share our observations. We speak in low voices, comparing notes on anomalies—a scrap of unfamiliar textile caught on a guide-rope, footprints that end at the precipice but show no signs of returning, the recurring musical tones that some claim to hear on particularly dense nights.

My solitude is not loneliness but a necessary condition of the work. To properly sense the threshold requires a certain emptiness, a quieting of internal noise that allows the subtle signals of the edge to be perceived. I have learned to distinguish between dozens of fog densities by how they feel against my skin, to predict weather changes by the way sound carries across the void.

The city provides for its Threshold Keepers through a system as old as Brynwater itself. Each neighborhood sends provisions on a rotating schedule—dried mushrooms, preserved meats, dense bread that keeps well in the damp, and most importantly, fresh water collected from the heart of the city where it runs purest. In return, we provide not just safety but a form of psychological boundary—a human presence that defines the city's edge, making it real.

Sometimes, during the deepest fogs, I perform the Acknowledgment—a ritual whose origins predate written memory. I lower a weighted rope into the mist, feeling for the tug that indicates it has found purchase on some unseen ledge below. Then I pull it up, slowly, hand over hand, until the weight emerges carrying whatever the depths have chosen to surrender: usually nothing, occasionally a peculiar stone or fragment of something unidentifiable. These objects I catalog and store, adding to a collection that no one fully understands but everyone agrees must be maintained.

The children fear us, the Threshold Keepers, though we mean them no harm. They whisper that we speak with the Underfog people, that we have made pacts with whatever dwells below. The adults know better—they understand that we are simply the city's way of acknowledging what it cannot know, of maintaining relationship with the vast uncertainty that surrounds it.

In my dwelling, as water drips its endless rhythm and fog pressed against the windows like a living thing, I often think about edges—not just the physical precipice I guard, but all the boundaries that define existence in Brynwater. The edge between waking and sleeping when the mist is so thick that both states seem equally dreamlike. The edge between sound and silence where the fog swallows noise. The edge between self and city, where individual purpose dissolves into collective need.

I am what Brynwater requires at its margins—a human boundary marker, a living threshold between the known and unknown. My presence says: this far, no farther. But also: here is where possibility begins.
