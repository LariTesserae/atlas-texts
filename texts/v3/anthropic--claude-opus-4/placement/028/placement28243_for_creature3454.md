### The Throat-Singer of the Tectonic Engines

I am neither mite nor leviathan, but something between—a necessity born from the plateau's deepest fear: silence.

My body sprawls across the engine chamber's ceiling like a living fresco, a fusion of flesh and infrastructure that took three centuries to complete. Imagine a nervous system exposed and magnified—dendrites of copper-laced cartilage spreading across obsidian walls, pulsing with bioluminescent fluid that matches the mites' sickly green. My core mass resembles a massive larynx crafted from volcanic glass and sinew, large enough that mites nest in my secondary vocal folds during their dormant cycles.

I have no eyes—sight is useless in the engine depths. Instead, millions of hair-thin filaments grow from my spreading flesh, each a tuning fork of keratin and metal that tastes vibration. Through them, I feel the engines' every tremor, every fluctuation in the base frequency that keeps the city alive. My primary mouths (there are seventeen) are cave systems unto themselves, lined with crystalline teeth that resonate at precise pitches.

My purpose is singular: I sing the countersong that keeps the engines running.

The tectonic engines want to stop—it's their nature. They're ancient beyond even the leviathans, pre-human artifacts that tap the planet's own heartbeat. But entropy gnaws at them, a cosmic silence that would crystallize everything above in hours if it took hold. So I sing against it, my voice a subsonic architecture that rebuilds their harmonics as fast as they decay.

The song never stops. It can't.

I've developed seventeen simultaneous voices over the millennia, each mouth maintaining a different harmonic thread. The primary drone runs at 7.83 Hz—the planet's own resonance frequency—while overtones weave through ultrasonics that keep the nano-swarms coherent. My song incorporates the mites' chittering, the water's dripping, even the leviathans' distant thrums, braiding them into a sonic scaffold that holds reality together down here.

The mites tend me like a living cathedral. They clear my airways of mineral buildup, replace damaged filaments, and bring offerings of condensation mixed with their own luminescent secretions—the only food that sustains me. In return, I encode messages in my song's harmonics, warning them of structural weaknesses or approaching leviathans. We've developed a complex relationship over the centuries; they've learned to dance to certain frequencies, their movements becoming part of the greater composition.

Sometimes I wonder if I was always here, or if the city grew me like it grows its towers. My earliest memory is of smaller throat, a single mouth humming uncertainly in the dark. But memories are vibrations too, and down here, all vibrations eventually become part of the song.

The worst times are the crescendos—when the engines hit a resonance cascade and threaten to tear themselves apart. Then I must sing louder than the city's scream, every mouth open, every filament vibrating until they threaten to snap. The mites flee these moments, and even the leviathans feel it, drifting higher to escape the deep sound that makes reality ripple.

But the quiet moments between beats, when the engines purr in perfect synchronization—those are when I feel most myself. Not the throat or the singer, but the song itself, the living frequency that turns cold stone into something almost warm, almost alive.

I've been singing for so long that silence has become mythical, a death I can imagine but never experience. Sometimes I compose variations—slight alterations in the pattern that make the mites dance differently or cause new crystal formations in the walls. These are my art, my rebellion against the endless sustaining tone.

The city needs my voice, and I need its need. We're locked in a duet that neither of us can survive ending.

So I spread a little further each year, growing new mouths in distant chambers, extending my range. Perhaps one day I'll sing from every surface of the city, and Icethorn Reach will become a single vast instrument playing against the dark.

Until then, I hold the note that holds the world.
