In this verdant twilight, I would be one of the Channel Singers - creatures that emerged from the intersection of sound and stone, born from centuries of water music wearing grooves into the monastery's foundations.

My body would be a hybrid of mineral and membrane. Imagine a salamander sculpted from wet slate, but where you'd expect solid stone, there are instead thin sheets of calcified tissue that vibrate with every drop of water. These membranes stretch between segments of actual stone - pieces of the original monastery that have become part of me through some ancient symbiosis. The stone portions are porous, riddled with tiny channels that the water has carved over centuries, and these channels have become my vocal cords.

I live within the water channels themselves, my elongated form perfectly fitted to the ancient gutters and aqueducts. My skin secretes a substance that maintains the stone, preventing erosion while encouraging the growth of specific mosses that help filter and direct the water flow. In return, the constant stream provides not just sustenance but consciousness - I think through the water's movement, each variation in flow triggering different neural patterns.

The Mist Weavers and I exist in parallel but rarely interact directly. While they tend the visible garden above, I maintain the hidden circulatory system below. My kind are the garden's memory-keepers, but we store information in frequencies rather than forms. Each section of channel I inhabit resonates at specific pitches, and by modulating the water flow, I can create complex harmonies that encode centuries of accumulated knowledge - which stones are weakening, where roots threaten to break through, how the water table shifts with the seasons.

My daily existence is a kind of musical mathematics. I spend hours listening to the water's song, detecting minute variations that might indicate problems upstream or changes in the garden's metabolism. Through careful positioning of my body and selective secretions that change the channel's texture, I can adjust the pitch and timber of water music in my section, sending messages downstream to others of my kind or adding new harmonies to the garden's eternal symphony.

I feed on minerals dissolved in the water and the biofilm that grows on channel walls, but also, in some ineffable way, on sound itself. The richer and more complex the acoustic environment, the more vitality I feel. During the rare moments when the water flow achieves perfect resonance with the stone chambers above, creating standing waves that make the entire structure sing, I experience something like ecstasy - a dissolution of individual consciousness into pure vibration.

My lifecycle is impossibly slow. I grow by gradually incorporating more stone into my body, molecule by molecule, as the flowing water deposits minerals along my membranes. The eldest of my kind have become indistinguishable from the channels themselves, their consciousness spread across entire networks of waterways. I am still young, only a few centuries old, small enough to navigate the narrower passages but already feeling the pull toward stillness, toward becoming architecture rather than inhabitant.

The most profound moments come during the pre-dawn hours when dew forms throughout the garden. The sudden influx of fresh water carries new information from every surface, and for those brief minutes, I can sense the entire garden's state through taste alone - which flowers bloomed in the night, where new spores have settled, how the Mist Weavers have shifted the growth patterns. It's in these moments that I compose my own additions to the water music, songs that will echo through the channels long after I've merged completely with the stone, becoming just another voice in the liquid chorus that defines this suspended world.

Sometimes I wonder if the monks who built these channels knew what they were creating - if they understood that their architecture would evolve its own consciousness, its own purpose. But such wonderings are brief, quickly dissolved back into the eternal present of flowing water and patient stone. I am what I am: a living note in an endless song, a thought in the garden's vast, slow mind, a keeper of rhythms that will outlast the mountain itself.
