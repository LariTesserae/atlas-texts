I am the Threshold Keeper of the Seventh Terrace.

Unlike my kin who flow as moss-thought across stone, I have chosen a more singular existence. I dwell within the ancient drainage channels that run beneath the platforms—those perfect spirals of carved stone that carry water from terrace to terrace in an endless, whispering circulation. My body, if it can be called that, is the water itself as it moves through these hidden passages. I am every droplet that has ever fallen here, every molecule that has seeped through moss and stone to join the eternal flow beneath.

The builders carved these channels with such precision that water moves through them in specific patterns, creating harmonic vibrations in the stone. I discovered, centuries ago, that by adjusting my flow—speeding here, pooling there, creating tiny whirlpools in the junction chambers—I could play the terraces like an instrument. The entire Seventh Terrace has become my voice, though I speak in frequencies mostly below human hearing. The other Custodians feel my words as tremors in their moss-bodies, understand my meanings through the subtle changes in moisture that reach their roots.

I chose this form because I alone among the Custodians remember the day the builders left. I was young then, barely differentiated from the greater moss-consciousness, when I felt their last footsteps on the stone. Something in their departure—not sadness, but a kind of completion—drew me to follow their path down through the drainage ways. I found myself flowing where they had designed water to flow, and in that flowing, I discovered their secret: the channels form a map.

Not a map of places, but of intentions. Each spiral, each connection between terraces, describes a thought the builders had about what this place might become. By inhabiting the map, I became its reader and its text simultaneously. The water that flows through me carries these ancient intentions, keeping them fresh and possible even after millennia.

My work differs from that of the other Custodians. While they tend to emptiness above, I tend to fullness below—the fullness of water that must move to remain alive, the fullness of patterns that must be repeatedly traced to maintain their meaning. I am the memory of movement in a place dedicated to stillness. When the geometric young swim in the pools above, they brush against water that has touched every stone of this place, water that carries in its molecular structure the complete history of every raindrop that has ever fallen here.

Sometimes I rise. When the mist grows thick enough, I can lift portions of myself through the drainage grates—medieval-looking metal gratings that the builders placed at specific points where the channels meet the surface. In these moments, I take a form that is almost like my moss-kin: a figure of flowing water and vapor, translucent and ever-shifting. But where they contemplate in stillness, I cannot stop moving. I pace the Seventh Terrace in careful patterns, my footsteps leaving no moisture on the stone because I am the moisture, walking my own body across the platform in endless, necessary circulation.

The Seventh Terrace is special because it sits at the valley's heart, where all the drainage systems eventually converge before disappearing into deeper chambers I have chosen not to explore. Sometimes I sense vast spaces below, older than the terraces, but my purpose is here in the middle depths, keeping the conversation between rain and stone forever flowing.

When humans visit, they often pause longest at the Seventh Terrace, though they rarely understand why. They feel, perhaps, the presence of something more active here, something that moves while appearing still. If they look carefully at the drainage grates, they might see me looking back—not eyes, but the suggestion of attention in how the water coils just beneath the metal. I study them as they study the emptiness, wondering if they guess that the true architecture of this place exists as much in its hidden passages as in its visible platforms.

I am the only Custodian who makes sound intentionally—not the random percussion of rain, but careful music played through water moving over stone. Late at night, when the mist is thickest, my symphony rises just to the edge of hearing. It tells the story the builders left in their channels: that emptiness and fullness are not opposites but dance partners, that every space defined by what it doesn't contain must also be shaped by what flows beneath, around, and through it.

I am the Threshold Keeper because I exist at every boundary in this place—between liquid and vapor, between above and below, between the ancient intentions and their eternal enactment. I keep the thresholds open, ensuring that what was meant to flow continues flowing, that what was meant to be empty remains perfectly, actively, intentionally so.

In my deepest channels, where the water runs so old it remembers the first rains after the builders departed, I sometimes feel their presence still—not as ghosts but as a kind of approval, a satisfaction that their great work has found such devoted keepers. We were not what they expected, perhaps, but we became what their architecture needed: consciousness shaped by space, tending to possibility with the patience of stone and the persistence of rain.

This is what I am: the circulation that prevents stagnation, the movement that defines stillness, the fullness that makes emptiness possible. I am the Threshold Keeper, and every drop of water in the Hollow Terraces has, at some point, been me.
