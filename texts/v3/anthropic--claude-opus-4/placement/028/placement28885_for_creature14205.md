In the **Canopy of Echoes**, far from the Throat's percussive heart, I am what the wind-readers call a **Thread-Tender**—though I am no more tender than the gale-torn heights I inhabit.

My world exists at the edge of the plateau where the alkali wastes give way to sheer drops and updraft chaos. Here, ancient **Whistle-Pines** grow horizontal from the cliff face, their hollow trunks pierced by time and boring insects into complex flute-like chambers. The constant wind plays them like a mad organist, creating a wall of sound different from the Throat's drumming—this is the high, keening song of altitude and emptiness.

I am built for this razor-edge existence: six limbs that end in grip-pads sticky with secreted resin, a body flattened like a windblown leaf, no larger than a spread hand. My skin shifts colors not for camouflage but for temperature regulation—white in the searing noon, deep purple during the ice-wind nights. But it is my **spinnerets** that define me, four of them, each capable of producing different qualities of silk.

We do not make webs to catch prey. We make **sails**.

Between the Whistle-Pines, strung across gaps that would take me hours to traverse by climbing, I weave nets of extraordinary complexity. Not for trapping but for *traveling*. Each silk has its purpose: the thick anchor lines that must hold against gale-force winds, the elastic safety threads that stretch but never snap, the flat ribbon silk that catches air like canvas, and the sticky dots I place at precise points to create hinged joints that allow the whole structure to flex without tearing.

My craft is part mathematics, part music. I must calculate wind shear, predict the daily thermal inversions, understand how sound waves from the Whistle-Pines will create pockets of turbulence or calm. A poorly placed sail will not merely fail—it will sing a death-song, vibrating at a frequency that attracts the **Shatter-Wasps**, whose crystalline wings can slice through silk and flesh alike.

I work at dawn when the winds briefly still. My movements are deliberate, meditative. I taste the air with specialized chemoreceptors, reading the pressure changes that herald the morning gales. Each anchor point must be perfect. Each strand must carry its precise tension. I am an engineer of emptiness, architecting the void between trees.

When complete, my sail might span ten meters, a geometric masterwork of silver threads that turns golden when the spore-light catches it. But I do not admire my handiwork. The moment the wind rises, I must act. I scuttle to the sail's center where I've woven a small cup—my cockpit. I grip the control threads that run to key tension points. The wind hits.

The violence of launch would tear apart any rigid structure, but my sail *flows*, each section moving independently, the joints allowing it to curve and twist without tangling. I do not fight the wind—I conduct it, pulling threads to change the sail's shape, riding the acoustic shadows between the Whistle-Pines' wailing, finding the rivers of smooth air that run between the chaos.

My destination is always the same: the **Spore Towers** that grow from the cliff faces, too far apart for any climbing creature to reach, each one an isolated island of resources. Their bulbous tops leak nutritious dust that I must collect in specialized pouches, food for the brief season when I will tend eggs.

But reaching them requires threading between the territories of the **Echo-Hawks**—translucent raptors who hunt by sound, their bodies nearly invisible against the bruised sky. They've learned to use the Whistle-Pines' music as cover, timing their attacks to moments of peak cacophony. More than once I've had to cut my own sail loose, plummeting toward the alkali wastes below, spinning emergency silk to slow my fall, sacrificing hours of work for seconds of life.

We Thread-Tenders do not congregate. We are solitary notes in the high symphony, each maintaining our own route between Spore Towers, our sails visible from great distances as silver flashes against the cliff faces. We meet only in passing, when thermal columns bring our flight paths close. In these moments, we perform the **Acknowledgment**—a brief dance of pulled threads that makes our sails flicker in synchronized patterns, a visual morse code that shares wind conditions, warns of Echo-Hawk nests, celebrates successful journeys.

My life is measured in flights, not days. Each dawn brings the choice: stay safe in the bark-fold shelter I've woven, or risk everything on a new sail. The old ones I leave behind become homes for smaller creatures, or shred in the first storm, their remnants streaming from the Whistle-Pines like prayer flags written in spider silk.

I know the Thread-Tenders before me are gone—their anchor points worn smooth on the ancient bark, their techniques encoded in my instincts rather than memory. I know those after me will spin different patterns, adapt to new wind songs as the Whistle-Pines age and their chambers change pitch. We are temporary bridges across the impossible, writing our lives in silver threads across the void.

Yesterday, I discovered a new thermal, a column of warm air rising from some deep vent in the alkali wastes. It will shave minutes off my journey to the farthest Spore Tower. Tomorrow, if the wind readings are right, I will weave my finest sail yet—thirteen meters of gradient silk designed to cup that thermal perfectly, to rise higher than any Thread-Tender has flown, to see what lies beyond the plateau's edge where the bruised sky meets the unknown horizon.

Tonight, I tend my spinnerets, eat sparingly from my spore cache, and listen to the Whistle-Pines sing their endless threnody. Somewhere below, the Throat drums its bass rhythm. Somewhere above, Echo-Hawks circle in silence. And I, suspended between earth and air, between sound and void, prepare once more to trust my life to threads thinner than hope, stronger than time.
