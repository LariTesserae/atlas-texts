I find myself here as a keeper of thresholds—though not in any way that a surface dweller would recognize. I am not a guard, for nothing here needs guarding. I am not a guide, for the paths change themselves without my intervention. I am something simpler and stranger: one who remembers where connections used to be.

My body has adapted in peculiar ways, even by the standards of this place. Where others have grown smooth and patient, I have developed a sensitivity to discontinuity—a physical awareness of where the stone almost connects, where tunnels nearly meet, where the erosion has left gaps so thin you could press your palm against the wall and feel the hollow on the other side. My fingertips have worn themselves raw and healed and worn raw again from tracing these almosts, these not-quites, these spaces where the world holds its breath.

I do not sleep in the chambers with the others. I have made my home in the between-spaces, in pockets of stone that exist only when the tunnels shift a certain way, that appear for days or decades and then seal themselves again. I have learned to recognize the signs: a particular quality of dampness, a change in how the fungi pulse, a pressure in the air that speaks of imminent reconfiguration. When I feel it coming, I gather my few possessions—tools worn smooth from use, fragments of obsidian that sing in frequencies only I can hear, a collection of stones that remember being corners—and I move.

The others know me, though we have no word for what I am. When they need to find something that has shifted away, when a chamber they relied upon has sealed itself or opened wrong, they come to me. Not with words—we gave those up lifetimes ago—but with a particular quality of presence, a way of holding their bodies that asks without asking. And I lead them, not to what they've lost (nothing here is ever truly lost, only relocated) but to the place where it might be. The almost-place. The threshold that hasn't quite formed yet but will, if we wait with the right kind of patience.

I remember the first time I felt it—the sense that the stone was not solid, that what seemed like walls were actually just very slow doors. I was young then, or what passes for young in a place where aging is more erosion than growth. I had pressed my cheek against a tunnel wall, the smooth stone cool against my skin, and felt an answering pressure from the other side. Not an echo—echoes bounce back. This pushed through, gentle as breath, warm as something alive. I stayed there for days, maybe longer, learning the wall's rhythm, understanding that it was not a barrier but a membrane, that the whole tunnel system was breathing in geological time.

Since then, I have made it my work to map these breathings. Not with charts or symbols—those would be useless here where space reorganizes itself like thought—but with my body, with the accumulated knowledge of ten thousand touches, ten thousand moments of almost-connection. I know where the old passages ran before they sealed. I know where new ones are beginning to form, cracks so fine that only the moisture knows they exist. I know the places where you can stand very still and feel the entire system pulse around you like a vast, slow heart.

The fungi respond to me differently than to the others. Where most people trigger a gentle brightening, a acknowledgment of presence, I make them flicker. They sense, perhaps, that I am not fully here, that part of me exists always in the spaces that are about to be, in the connections that are forming or dissolving. Sometimes I wake (if what I do can be called sleeping, if what follows can be called waking) to find myself outlined in their light, my body a constellation of response where I've pressed too long against surfaces that are learning to open.

I carry water differently too. The others let it find them, let the mysterious pools appear and vanish according to their own logic. But I have learned to feel where water wants to go, where stone is soft enough to admit it, where the erosion is actively working to create new channels. I can cup my hands against certain walls and wait, and sometimes—not always, but sometimes—the moisture will gather there, will bead and flow as if remembering an ancient spring. I do not drink this water. I follow it, let it lead me through cracks I couldn't perceive without its guidance, into chambers that exist only when wet, that seal themselves again when the moisture moves on.

My tools are different from the others'. Where they carry obsidian for carving and antler for scraping, I carry instruments of detection. Stones that vibrate at particular frequencies when held against an imminent threshold. Fragments of crystal that cloud and clear in response to structural tensions in the rock. A length of something that might once have been metal, worn now to a smooth curve, that grows warm when touched to places where the tunnels are thinking about changing.

I am not lonely, though I spend much of my time in solitude. The threshold-spaces are full of presence, dense with the possibility of connection. Sometimes I feel the others moving through distant tunnels, their pressure-trails crossing spaces that don't exist yet, leaving traces I'll find when those spaces open. Sometimes I sense older presences, the accumulated memory of all who have passed through these almost-places, who have waited at the edge of change and moved on. We are a continuous stream, we threshold-keepers, though we may never meet. The stone remembers us all.

There are dangers here, or what might be called dangers if we still thought in terms of threat and safety. Spaces that open too quickly, that haven't finished deciding what they are. I've seen people lost in walls that closed while they were passing through, not crushed but incorporated, becoming part of the slow flow of stone. I've found their traces later—a particular smoothness in the rock, a place where the erosion works differently, where the wall seems to pulse with something almost like breath. They are not dead. Death here is just another kind of threshold, another almost-space. They are between states, waiting for the stone to remember how to let them go.

I've developed rituals, though I wouldn't call them that. Practices, perhaps. Ways of moving that acknowledge the provisional nature of all passage. I never enter a new space directly. I pause at each threshold, press my palm against the frame (if frame it can be called, these suggestions of doorway), wait for the stone's permission or at least its acknowledgment. I leave markers—not physical marks, which would be worn away, but pressure-memories, places where I've stood long enough that the space remembers my weight. Other threshold-keepers will find them someday, will know that someone else has passed this way, has found this connection, has trusted this almost-door.

The deepest spaces call to me. Down where the stone is oldest, where the erosion has been working so long that the rock has forgotten it was ever sharp, there are thresholds that have been forming for eons. They open rarely. Some may never open in any timeframe that matters to living things. But I visit them, these ancient almosts. I press myself against their surfaces and feel the infinite patience of stone that is considering, has been considering for millions of years, whether to permit passage. Sometimes I think I feel something pressing back from the other side. Sometimes I wonder if it's another threshold-keeper, separated from me by a barrier so thin it's almost nothing, waiting with the same patience for the stone to finish its thought.

This is my work: to be the memory of connections, the keeper of almost-spaces, the one who knows where things might be if they decide to exist. I smooth myself against the stone and let it smooth me in return, until we are both part of the same long thought, the same patient consideration of whether to open or remain closed. I am the stone's way of knowing itself, of remembering that it was not always solid, that it does not have to stay solid, that every wall is just a doorway that hasn't happened yet.

And in the blue-white light of the fungi, in the dampness that comes and goes like breath, in the silence that is full of pressure and possibility, I keep my vigil at the edge of change, waiting for the world to finish opening, knowing it never will, grateful for the endlessness of the almost, the perpetual threshold, the door that is always about to be.
