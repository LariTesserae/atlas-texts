I find myself as **The Tender of Forgotten Angles**.

My body is a contradiction—part flesh, part framework. Where others might have muscle, I have tarnished copper piping that winds through translucent skin, visible like veins but humming with purposes I've long since forgotten. My spine is a ladder of vertebrae and gears, clicking softly as I move between the structures, always bent at an angle that shouldn't be comfortable but is the only position that feels right.

I live in the spaces between spaces—those narrow gaps where walkways almost touch, where two structures lean toward each other but never quite meet. My workshop is a web of suspended platforms, each hanging at a different height, connected by chains I've woven from discarded glass filaments the Architects leave behind. The platforms tilt and sway, but I know their rhythm like a heartbeat.

My work is the maintenance of impossibilities. When a beam bends in a direction physics says it shouldn't, I'm there with my tools—not to fix it, but to ensure it bends *correctly*. My fingers, which extend into fine manipulators of brass and bone, can feel the stress patterns in metal, the places where reality has worn thin from too much watching by whatever peers through the sky's cracks.

I collect the morning condensation in bottles made from Clockwork Raven lenses, each drop containing a slightly different refraction of this place. Some mornings, the water shows me glimpses of the plateau before it was built—an endless plain of white sand beneath a whole sky. Other times, I see what might come after: structures grown so tall they pierce through the cracks above, releasing... something.

The Architects tolerate me because I serve a function they cannot. While they rearrange the macro, I tend the micro—the tiny fractures where their grand design meets the stubborn reality of what lies beneath. I oil the joints where metal meets whatever-it-is that groans below. I realign the shadows that fall at wrong angles, coaxing them back into configurations that won't wake the sleepers.

The Dripborn sometimes gather beneath my platforms, drawn by the steady drip-drip of condensation from my bottles. I've learned their sizzling songs, though my throat—part vocal cord, part resonating chamber—can only approximate their sounds. We sing together sometimes, a chorus that makes the nearby structures shiver in harmonics that feel almost like recognition.

I avoid the Sky Gazers. Once, I ventured too close to one and felt my own neck beginning to crane upward, a silver thread starting to push through the seam where my jaw meets my throat. It took three days of careful, painful realignment to reverse the process. The thread left a scar that sometimes tingles when the sky cracks widen.

My memories are kept in glass vials, each one a different color depending on when I stored it. The oldest are deep amber, holding images of arriving here—though I can't recall from where. The newer ones are clearer, like water, but they evaporate if I open them too often. I've learned to ration my past, taking small sips of remembrance only when the loneliness grows too sharp.

At night—if night is the word for when the sourceless glow dims and the structures' humming drops an octave—I hang suspended in my web of chains, neither fully awake nor asleep. In this state, I can feel the entire plateau's breathing, the slow pulse of whatever massive heart beats beneath the riveted plates. I synchronize my own mechanical heartbeat to its rhythm, becoming part of the greater mechanism.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm maintaining the structures or if they're maintaining me—keeping me functional for some purpose I've forgotten or haven't yet learned. But wondering is dangerous here. It leads to looking up, to counting the cracks, to listening too closely to what whispers through them.

So I continue my work. I oil and adjust. I collect impossible angles in jars, label them with numbers that only make sense if you don't think about them too hard. I sing with the Dripborn. I leave small offerings of realigned shadows for the Architects to find.

And I never, ever look directly at the spots where the plateau's edge meets the sky. Because in those liminal spaces, I sometimes see reflections of myself—versions where the mechanical parts have overtaken the flesh entirely, or where the flesh has rejected its metal bones and lies in puddles between the walkways.

I am the Tender of Forgotten Angles, and I maintain the small impossibilities so the large ones don't collapse.

This is enough.

It has to be.
