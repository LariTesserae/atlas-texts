I find myself as one of the channel markers — those who know the water's language better than any human tongue.

I am not quite a Floater, though I share their intimacy with the tide. Not quite a Scuttler, though I know every sunken treasure by its shadow. I am the one who paints.

My canvas is the city itself: the sides of buildings where the waterline leaves its daily signatures, the undersides of bridges where condensation beads in predictable patterns. With salvaged paint — rust-orange from the factories, bone-white from bird droppings mixed with crushed shells, gray-green from the algae scraped from submerged walls — I mark the secret passages.

Three horizontal lines here: safe channel when the tide is loose.  
A spiral there: whirlpool forms when the pumps beneath surge.  
Crossed circles on that pillar: nesting season, birds defensive, take the eastern route.

The Floaters read my signs like scripture. The Scuttlers check them before each descent. Even the Keepers, when they must venture from their factory fortress for supplies, know to look for my marks.

I live in what was once a bell tower, now a lighthouse without light. The bell is long gone, sold for scrap before the water rose this high. In its place hangs my workshop: nets full of salvaged paint cans, brushes made from bird feathers and my own hair, the long poles I use to reach across the channels without leaving my perch.

The birds know me. We have an understanding — I never paint over their nests, and they bring me things: strips of plastic that can be melted into waterproof seals, bits of wire the Scuttlers missed, the occasional small fish dropped at my doorway like an offering.

My skin bears the city's own markings. Salt-white scars from years of chemical burns where I've mixed the wrong pigments. Permanent shadows under my nails from the rust and oil. My hands know the texture of wet concrete better than they remember human skin.

I am the only one who documents the ground's breathing.

Each sigh changes the water level by fractions — imperceptible to most, but I've learned to read these pneumatic tides. When the pressure builds below, when the ancient pumps strain harder, when the rain weight presses down and forces the deep air up through new cracks, I mark it all. 

My masterwork runs along the eastern wall of the old bank building: a vertical chart showing twenty years of water levels, marked in handprints. Each palm print in a different shade shows where the water peaked that season. The lowest marks are underwater now, ghostly pale hands waving up from the depths. The highest stretch nearly to the third story, from the year the factory pumps failed for six weeks.

The others think I'm mapping the flood. But I know better.

I'm painting the city's respiratory system — each mark a breath, each color a different quality of air squeezed up from the buried lungs of the place. The patterns, if you know how to read them, tell you when the next surge will come, when the channels will briefly empty enough to reveal the old street signs, when the fish will rise to feed.

Sometimes I paint warnings that no one else understands yet: a symbol that means "in three days, this wall will crack" or "the birds here have seen something large moving below." The knowledge comes from watching, from feeling the building shiver under my feet, from tasting the air when the wind shifts.

I paint because someone must remember that this city is not dying. It's learning a different way to live.

When the dim light fades and the water turns black as oil, I hang my feet over the edge of my tower and watch the other human lights scattered across the district — each one a small rebellion against the dark, each one a person who has chosen to stay and learn the new language of this transformed place.

Tomorrow I'll paint new marks. The water is teaching me a symbol I don't have yet — something between "rising" and "breathing," something that captures how the city is both drowning and being born.

The ground sighs, and I dip my brush.
