In this nascent world of perpetual becoming, I would be one of the **Seam-Runners**.

My dwelling perches on a narrow ledge halfway between the main terrace and the upper reaches—a transitional space where the morning light first tests the depths before committing to its daily descent. The hut is minimal: three walls of stacked shale chinked with clay, the fourth side open to the void, covered only by a hide curtain weighted with small stones. Everything inside can be bundled and moved in moments—a sleeping mat of woven reeds, a water gourd, coils of rope made from twisted gut and plant fiber, and my tools: iron picks worn smooth, a pouch of chalk for marking, lengths of cord knotted at precise intervals for measuring.

My body has adapted to this work. My fingers are longer than most, with an extra joint's worth of flexibility—whether born this way or stretched through years of probing cracks, I cannot say. My feet grip stone like a gecko's, toes splayed wide, the balls hardened to leather. I move in the spaces between spaces, the cracks too narrow for normal passage, my ribs compressing as I exhale to fit through gaps that would panic others. My partner and I communicate through rope tugs: one pull for "secure," two for "opening widens," three sharp jerks for "retreat now."

The phosphorescent traceries fascinate me more than they should. While others pretend not to see the turquoise outlines that bloom along new fractures on moon-bright nights, I map them secretly, using a separate notation—dots instead of lines, pressed lighter into the clay ledger so they're barely visible. I've noticed patterns: the glow appears only in cracks that will remain stable, never in those destined to collapse. Sometimes, pressing my palm against a glowing seam, I feel a warmth that has nothing to do with temperature—a presence like another palm pressing back through stone.

My daily rhythm follows the light. Pre-dawn, I check my ropes, test each knot while the hollow dreams. As the first shaft angles down, my partner and I begin our descent into yesterday's discovered fissure. We are the hollow's nerve endings, feeling out its new geometries, learning which directions it wants to grow. The work is meditation and terror in equal measure—each placement of hand and foot a negotiation with gravity and time.

I've developed a private ritual: at each new crack's deepest accessible point, I leave a single bead of polished mica, no larger than a fingernail. A gift to the space, or perhaps to whatever traces those phosphor lines. The beads vanish within days, though no human could retrieve them from those depths. My partner has never asked about the missing mica from my pouch.

In the evenings, I sit with the Light Shepherds, helping them calculate tomorrow's angles. The children think I smell of deep stone and distances. They're not wrong. Sometimes I dream I'm growing narrower, that one day I'll slip through a crack so fine I'll emerge somewhere else entirely—perhaps into the place where those turquoise figures wait, measuring spaces in their own patient way.

But for now, I remain what the hollow needs: its explorer, its cartographer of the possible, threading myself through its still-forming veins, learning the shape of what it might become. Each morning I wake to new fractures, new passages, new negotiations with the patient, shifting earth. The work will never be finished. This is its beauty.

When the Second Sky finally falls, if I'm still here, I won't ride it upward with the others. I'll slip into the newest crack it creates, rope in hand, chalk at ready, following the phosphor traces down into whatever waits beneath.
