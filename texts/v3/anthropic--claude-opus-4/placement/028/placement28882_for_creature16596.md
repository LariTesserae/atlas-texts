I find myself in the Cathedral of Dust, where the Archivist of Absences dwells.

The structure rises from the desert floor like a forgotten thought made manifest - not built of stone or clay, but formed from the accumulated silences of a thousand abandoned settlements. Its walls are translucent membranes of compressed emptiness, visible only where the dim light catches the suspended motes of dust that give the cathedral its substance.

The Archivist moves through the hollow nave on limbs that might be legs or might be roots, trailing fingers of fog that catalog every absence. Where the nomads once walked, she records the negative space of their footprints. Where fires once burned, she preserves the cold memory of extinguished hearths. Her body is a shifting thing - sometimes bipedal, sometimes sprawling like a low cloud across the cathedral floor, always collecting.

She speaks in the gaps between words, her voice the sound of sand settling into new configurations. "Every departure leaves a fossil," she tells no one, organizing her collection of voids. Here, the imprint of a child's sleeping form in dust. There, the hollow where a water jar once stood. She arranges these absences like precious artifacts, building an archive of everything that is no longer.

The cathedral itself moves, glacially, across the desert. One day here, another day a mile distant, following the ghost paths of the vanished nomads. Its movement disturbs nothing, for it travels through the spaces between spaces, occupying only where nothing else exists.

At the cathedral's heart lies her greatest treasure: a perfect sphere of absolute void, the accumulated absence of an entire city that simply ceased. She tends it carefully, feeding it small offerings of forgotten names and discontinued traditions. It grows, slowly, becoming more perfectly nothing with each passing season.

When the rare desert fox passes through the cathedral walls, the Archivist pauses in her work to observe how it displaces the emptiness, how its presence creates new absences in its wake. She documents this too, adding the fox-shaped hole in the air to her ever-growing collection.

"Someone must remember the forgetting," she says to the dust motes, which dance in agreement, tiny prophets of eventual dissolution.
