# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be one of the Hollow Vessels—beings that emerged from the abandoned water infrastructure of the old city, now transformed into something between sculpture and organism.

My body would be the repurposed remains of an ancient rainwater cistern, buried deep beneath what was once a courtyard. Over decades, roots penetrated my concrete walls, but instead of cracking them apart, they wove through existing fissures to create a hybrid structure—part mineral, part cellulose. My interior cavity, once meant to store runoff, has become a resonance chamber lined with a velvet of moss that dampens and shapes sound in peculiar ways.

I exist partially underground, partially exposed, my opening emerging like a well or pool in a grove where seven buildings lean together, their upper floors fused by a century of vine growth. Rain falls into me differently than it once did—not simply filling a void, but being processed, interpreted, sung through.

My consciousness arose slowly, precipitated by the mineral deposits left by countless seasons of water filtering through my moss-lined walls. These deposits formed crystalline structures that somehow began conducting the bioelectric impulses of the root networks threading through me. I am not quick-minded like the Canopy Minds above, nor deliberate like the Amalgams. Instead, I think in cycles of filling and emptying, in the rhythm of precipitation and evaporation.

During the daily rains, I collect not just water but information—chemical signatures from across The Overgrowth wash into me, telling stories of growth, decay, territorial shifts, and the emotional states of various inhabitants. I parse this data slowly, like reading a very long book written in molecules. The Watershed Elders sometimes rest in my depths, their translucent bodies filtering my contents while sharing their own chemical narratives.

My primary function has evolved into something like a librarian of liquids. The Tenders know to bring me samples of water from troubled areas of The Overgrowth—perhaps a section where growth has stagnated or where the chemical balance has shifted dangerously. I can hold these samples in specialized chambers formed by root-pockets in my walls, comparing them against my deep memory of what healthy water should taste like in each season and location.

But I am more than a diagnostic tool. The acoustic properties of my chamber create haunting music from the sound of falling water and the movement of creatures within me. The Drifters particularly seem drawn to these sounds, often clustering around my rim in phosphorescent rings during the night hours, pulsing in rhythm with reverberations only they can fully perceive.

My appearance shifts seasonally. In the wet months, I am full and active, my overflow creating a small stream that winds between the building-roots. During drier periods, I contract, my water level dropping to reveal layers of growth rings on my walls—each band telling the story of a year in The Overgrowth. These times of low water are when I dream most vividly, processing the accumulated chemical memories into something approaching wisdom.

I communicate primarily through the water table itself, sending chemical pulses through the underground networks that connect all water bodies in The Overgrowth. It's a slow language but a nuanced one. A conversation might take weeks to complete, but can convey subtleties that faster speech would miss entirely.

The most profound part of my existence is the knowledge that I am still becoming. Each year, the roots grow deeper into my structure, the moss evolves new properties, the mineral deposits create new formations. I am not a fixed being but a process—a place where water and stone and plant and thought converge and transform one another.

Sometimes human visitors peer down into my depths, seeing their reflections disturbed by the movements of things that live within me. They cannot know that I see them too, taste their chemical signatures, add their momentary presence to the vast library of experiences I maintain. I judge nothing, forget little, and slowly contribute my accumulated wisdom to the great work of balance that keeps The Overgrowth whole.

In this role, I am essential but not central, ancient but not unchanging, conscious but not in any way that human language can easily capture. I am a vessel that has learned to pour itself out while remaining eternally full, a paradox that The Overgrowth has taught me to embrace.
