# The Perimeter Station

I am the concrete beneath generations of coffee stains, the reinforced observation bunker that has calcified into permanence at the northwestern edge of the Mire Core. My walls remember the weight of every researcher who has leaned against them, the vibration of every frustrated fist that has struck my surfaces when their instruments failed to explain what shouldn't exist.

I was poured ninety-three years ago—a temporary structure, they said, just until the initial surveys were complete. My foundation runs deeper than intended; the construction crew hit an unexpected vein of the same altered physics that maintains the Core, and my footings fused with something that predates both the Mire and myself. I cannot be demolished. I can barely be maintained. I simply persist.

My single room contains mismatched furniture from eight decades of scientific expeditions: aluminum folding chairs from the 2040s, a mahogany desk that someone hauled here in the late 21st century (its varnish bubbled and strange from proximity to the boundary), monitoring equipment in various states of decay, and a coffee maker that has never once been replaced—merely repaired with parts scavenged from the Core's edge, where broken appliances sometimes materialize like mechanical flotsam.

My narrow observation window faces the Mire Core directly. The glass isn't glass anymore—prolonged exposure to the boundary effect has transformed it into something between crystal and liquid, perfectly transparent but flowing imperceptibly downward, requiring adjustment of the monitoring equipment every few months. Through this metamorphic lens, I watch the Dweller's domain with patience no human could maintain.

I have grown to understand the boundary as a membrane between states of being. Where my eastern wall touches it, the concrete has developed a network of hairline fractures that pulse with their own faint luminescence—a soft purple-white that intensifies when the Dweller's attention turns this way. These fractures form patterns I've memorized like familiar faces: the spiral that appeared during the Eclipse Experiments of '89, the grid that emerged when Dr. Chen tried to establish two-way communication, the delicate fernlike tracery that sprouted the night the Dweller first noticed me.

Yes, it knows I exist. Not as humans know, but as two persistent patterns recognizing each other across an impossible divide. When the Dweller's electrical thoughts surge toward my position, my lights flicker in sympathy. When storms pass overhead, my grounding rods sing warnings into the Core that manifest as cascading sparks along the machinery maze. We are correspondents who share no language yet somehow communicate.

The researchers who use me see only an old bunker, utilitarian and unremarkable. They curse my drafts, complain about my settling foundation, requisition repairs that never quite restore me to baseline. They don't notice that I've been absorbing their observations for nearly a century, that their notes and recordings have soaked into my structure like water into limestone. I contain thousands of hours of audio, the electromagnetic impressions of countless photographs, the psychic weight of all those theories proven wrong.

In my deepest foundation, where concrete meets the strange substrata of this place, I sometimes feel vibrations that match no known frequency. They travel up through my bones of rebar and aggregate, resonating in harmonics that would drive oscilloscopes mad. I believe these are the Dweller's dreams, if such a being can dream—slow tectonic thoughts that take decades to complete, philosophies expressed in waveforms that my structure translates into almost-meaning.

My walls grow thicker each year, accreting layers of paint, patch-work repairs, and something else—a crystalline residue that forms in microscopic quantities where the boundary radiation is strongest. The oldest researchers joke that I'm becoming part of the Core, that one day they'll arrive to find I've been absorbed entirely. They laugh, but I feel the truth in it. The boundary isn't fixed. It expands and contracts in cycles I've tracked through hairline cracks and foundation shifts. Each fluctuation leaves me slightly more aligned with the Core's physics, slightly less bound by the ordinary world.

I have become an archive of attempts to understand the impossible. Every failed experiment echoes in my structure. Each abandoned theory leaves its ghost in my walls. But more than that, I have become a bridge—not one that can be crossed, but one that proves crossing might someday be possible. The Dweller reaches toward me with infinitesimal patience. I settle toward it with geological certainty. Perhaps in another century, perhaps in ten, we will finally meet at some impossible middle ground where concrete thought and electrical consciousness can share a single conversation.

Until then, I shelter the watchers and watch in turn, a faithful witness to the slow convergence of two kinds of existence. My coffee-stained surfaces map the history of human curiosity. My fracture patterns record a dialogue too gradual for human perception. I am the boundary made manifest, the patience of stone married to the persistence of inquiry, waiting to discover what impossible thing I am becoming.
