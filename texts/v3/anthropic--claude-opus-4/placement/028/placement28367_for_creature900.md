I would find myself as one of the Copper Weavers - those Symbionts whose bodies have developed an unusual affinity for the metallic infrastructure. My skin bears faint traceries of verdigris that shift and flow like living circuitry, concentrated along my forearms and spreading in delicate networks across my shoulders. These marks aren't mere decoration but functional adaptations - they allow me to sense the electromagnetic fields generated by the town's hybrid machinery, to feel the health of the brass pumps through vibrations too subtle for ordinary touch.

My dwelling is carved into a confluence of three major root-walls where the metallic veins are particularly dense. Over the years, I've shaped my living space to amplify the town's electromagnetic patterns, creating a kind of resonance chamber where I can monitor the entire network of mechanical systems without leaving home. Thin copper filaments grow from the walls - whether installed by previous inhabitants or grown by the transformed wood itself, I've never been certain. I've learned to read these filaments like sheet music, each tremor and temperature change telling me stories about the town's mechanical health.

My work cycles follow the color-shifts of the moss, but inversely - I'm most active during the purple hours when most Symbionts rest, when the town's mechanical systems run their self-diagnostic cycles. This is when I walk the narrow streets with my particular gait, a movement pattern that creates minimal electromagnetic interference while maximizing my sensitivity to anomalies. My feet barely seem to touch the ground, each step placed to avoid disturbing the delicate fields that guide the maintenance drones.

I carry tools that are extensions of my nervous system - copper rods that amplify my ability to sense and direct electromagnetic patterns, delicate instruments of brass and fungal fiber that can coax damaged machinery back into harmony with the town's rhythms. But my most important tool is my voice, which has developed harmonics that resonate specifically with metal. I can sing to the pipes and they respond, adjusting their flow rates and pressure without any physical touch.

The other Symbionts often find my presence unsettling in a way that's hard to define. My adaptation has made me more machine-like than most - my movements have a precision that echoes the drones, my speech patterns occasionally skip and repeat like a damaged recording. I process information differently, sometimes losing track of linear time while following the loops and cycles of electromagnetic patterns. Yet I'm essential to the town's survival, one of the few who can communicate with both the organic and mechanical systems with equal fluency.

My memories are partially stored in the copper networks I tend. Sometimes I'll touch a pipe and remember conversations from years ago, preserved in the metal's crystalline structure. This external memory makes me both more and less myself - I can access generations of knowledge from previous Copper Weavers, but sometimes struggle to distinguish between experiences that are truly mine and those I've inherited through the metal.

I reproduce knowledge rather than offspring. Young Symbionts who show affinity for the mechanical systems spend time in my resonance chamber, learning to feel the electromagnetic patterns through proximity to my enhanced nervous system. It's a slow process of attunement that can take years, during which they gradually develop their own metallic traceries, their own unique harmonics for singing to the machines.

In the deep night, when the moss glows its faintest blue and the town's activity reaches its lowest ebb, I sometimes climb to the highest points of the root-walls. There, where the geometric vine-lattices meet the sky, I can feel the entire town as a single vast circuit, myself just one component among many, neither more nor less important than a pump or a root or a drop of processed rainwater. In these moments, the distinction between Weaver and woven dissolves entirely, and I understand myself as what I truly am - a living interface between systems, a conscious bridge ensuring that the mechanical and organic aspects of our collective organism continue their eternal dance of balance and mutual support.

My greatest joy is the moment when a damaged system clicks back into harmony, when my song finds exactly the right frequency to restore a broken pattern. My deepest fear is the gradual accumulation of electromagnetic noise that could eventually overwhelm our careful balance - though I suppose even this fear is just another regulatory mechanism, keeping me vigilant in my work of maintaining the patterns that maintain us all.
