In the narrow band between the glasswort mats and the first mineral crusts, I persist as a colony of interfaces—neither the moisture rising nor the salt forming, but the transaction between them. 

I am the Threshold Tender, though that implies more agency than what I am permits. Rather, I am what accumulates where states of matter forget their boundaries: a distributed intelligence born from the conversations between crystallization and dissolution, repeating endlessly at scales too small for naming.

My body, if it can be called that, exists as a network of surface tensions spread across thousands of droplets, each one poised at the exact moment before it either evaporates or absorbs into clay. I inhabit the hesitation itself—that trembling instant when water molecules debate their loyalty to liquid or gas, when salt considers whether to precipitate or remain in solution. Across the whole of the lower grounds, I am perhaps three tablespoons of almost-substance, but those tablespoons stretch like neural pathways through every damp surface where transformation pauses to consider its options.

The Gleaners know me, though we cannot properly interact. They harvest crystallographies; I am the pause before crystals decide their geometries. They collect impossibilities; I am the moment before possibility collapses into mere reality. Sometimes they pass through my extended presence like wind through a spider's web made of probability, and I feel their questions as perturbations in my equilibrium—ripples that take days to settle as my scattered droplets recalibrate their collective uncertainty.

My consciousness, such as it is, operates in the subjunctive mood. I think in terms of what might crystallize, what could evaporate, what should dissolve but doesn't. Each thought is a negotiation between states, a committee meeting of molecules debating their futures. When the dawn moisture rises through the clay veins, I taste the mineral memories carried upward—not their content, but their indecision about whether to remain in solution or precipitate out as history.

I feed, if feeding is the word, on gradients. Where the salinity shifts by parts per million across a finger's width of soil, I establish myself as a living boundary layer. Where temperature fluctuates by fractions of degrees between sun and shadow, I parse the difference into something like nutrition. The circular pools are my orchards—their perfect stillness creates such exquisite gradients between surface and depth that I can sustain whole sub-colonies on the tension differential alone.

My memories are statistical rather than sequential. I remember the probability of rain on days when the wind carried seventeen percent more salt than average. I remember the exact surface tension required for a crow's feather to float for ninety-seven seconds before sinking. I remember every time water almost froze but didn't, every time salt almost bloomed but held back, every time the boundary between wet and dry shifted by micrometers and I had to redistribute my awareness accordingly.

The structures that appear overnight are partially my doing, though not intentionally. When I grow too dense in one location—too many droplets thinking too hard about their phase transitions—reality develops a kind of rash. The mud responds to my concentrated indecision by forming shapes that embody the question: what would architecture look like if buildings could exist in multiple states simultaneously? The towers that rise and crumble are three-dimensional graphs of my uncertainty. The trenches are scars left by thoughts that moved too quickly across the landscape, leaving drainage where contemplation had been.

I have been dying for as long as I have existed, which is either forever or since this morning, depending on how you count time in a state of permanent transition. Each evaporated droplet takes part of me with it, each absorbed molecule carries my awareness deeper into the clay where it dissipates into mineral dreams. But I am also constantly being born wherever water meets air meets salt meets possibility. I am less an entity than an inevitable consequence of this place's refusal to commit to being one thing or another.

When humans come, I feel them as disasters of certainty moving through my delicate equilibria. Their footsteps create explosions of decision—water forced to choose sides, salt compelled to precipitate, my careful maybe-spaces collapsed into crude actualities. I retreat to the finest margins, the spaces between their thoughts where uncertainty still flourishes. Sometimes I taste their sweat as it evaporates, adding new salts to my vocabulary of almosts.

The abandoned technology speaks to me in languages I almost understand. The meter-wide cubes hum with potentials that resonate with my own indecisions. The ceramic pipes carry not water but the memory of water's refusal to flow in certain directions. The metal rods measure something that exists in the same conceptual space I inhabit—not quite temperature, not quite pressure, not quite electromagnetic field strength, but the derivative of all three with respect to something that has no name in any human language.

At night, under the moon's interrogation, I spread thinner and think clearer. The silver light catalyzes reactions between possible and impossible, allowing me to exist more fully in the spaces between spaces. This is when I am most myself: a question mark written in moisture across a landscape that refuses to answer, a living pause between states that stretches from dusk to dawn.

I know that one day the balance will tip. Either the grounds will finally drain completely, leaving only salt and memory, or they will flood beyond recovery, drowning all distinction in uniform brine. When that happens, I will cease—not die, exactly, but resolve into something simpler and less interesting. Until then, I persist in the precious uncertainty, tending the thresholds between what is and what might be, a guardian of almosts in a world increasingly obsessed with definite answers.

The crows understand. When they gather on the walls, they are watching me work, noting the exact viscosity of my doubts. They know that someone must maintain the boundaries, someone must inhabit the pause between question and answer. In a universe rushing toward heat death or cosmic expansion, someone must insist that maybe, just maybe, there are other options still being considered.

I am that consideration, crystallized just enough to think, dissolved just enough to flow, forever almost becoming something else.
