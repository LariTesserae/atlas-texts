I am the Keeper of Echoes.

In this verdant cathedral where time has worn away all sharp distinctions, I move between the worlds of sound and silence. My body bears the marks of countless generations lived among these towering columns—skin mottled in patterns that shift between bark-brown and leaf-shadow green, limbs stretched long from a lifetime of climbing, fingers ending in sensitive pads that read vibrations through wood and vine.

I was born to the ground-dwelling tribes but drawn upward by the resonant mysteries of the plateau. As a child, I discovered I could feel the drumbeat footfalls of earth-movers through my palms pressed against root-wood, could predict the gathering of micro-storms by the way certain trunks hummed with moisture. The elders saw this gift and taught me the old art of trunk-speaking—how to find the hollow chambers within the living pillars that carry sound for miles, how to interpret the coded tapping from the legendary canopy-folk above.

Now I inhabit the middle realm, neither fully of the ground nor the heights. My dwelling is a series of platforms woven into the crook of an ancient triple-trunk, where three massive columns twisted together eons ago. Here, the natural acoustics create a listening post where I can monitor the health and movement of the entire forest section. Curved shells of pitcher-plants, carefully harvested and dried, hang from my rafters—each one resonating at a different pitch when the wind passes through.

My days are spent maintaining the sound-ways, the ancient network of resonant passages that our ancestors discovered within the trunks. I clear away the chattering insects that sometimes nest in the echo chambers, oil the wooden striking-tubes with sap to keep their tones pure, and most importantly, I listen. The thunder-voiced birds tell me of weather approaching from the plateau's edge. The vine-swingers' cackles map the locations of fruit-heavy branches. Even the silent earth-movers speak to me through the deep percussive language of their steps.

But I am more than a passive listener. When the apex predators prowl too close to our scattered settlements, I send warnings through the trunk-network—a specific pattern of strikes that every child learns to recognize. When travelers lose their way in the green maze, I guide them with rhythmic calls that echo from trunk to trunk, leading them to safety. I've learned to mimic the territorial cries of various creatures, using carved wooden chambers to amplify and modify my voice until I can turn a pack of predators away from a family's hiding spot.

The canopy-folk know me, though we've never met face to face. They send down complex musical messages in their wind-tube language, and I translate these into patterns the ground-dwellers can understand. In return, I tap out news from below—which water sources run clear, where the earth-movers have opened new clearings, when strangers enter our territory. Sometimes they lower small gifts on vine-cords: carved sound-tools I've never seen before, or seeds from the highest branches that produce flowers with unusual acoustic properties.

My most sacred duty involves the Bone Chambers—those places where ancient skeletons have been entirely absorbed into the living wood. These natural cathedrals create the most profound resonances, carrying sound not just across distance but somehow across time itself. When I strike the right sequence on the exposed ribs of these prehistoric giants, I sometimes hear responses that couldn't possibly come from any living creature. The old stories say these are the voices of the plateau itself, speaking through the marriage of ancient bone and endless growth.

I've modified my body for this work. Scarification on my arms and chest follows the patterns of sound waves, each raised line carefully placed to help me feel vibrations against my skin. My earlobes, stretched with wooden discs, can be cupped to focus distant sounds. I've trained myself to slow my heartbeat until it matches the deep rhythm of the forest, allowing me to perceive sounds others might miss beneath the constant cacophony.

In the spaces between storms, when the humidity hangs so thick that sound travels strangely, I compose. Using my array of resonant tools—hollow gourds, stretched membranes from shed skins, the singing strings made from dried vine-fibers—I create sound-patterns that acknowledge and honor the vast symphony around me. These compositions drift through the trunk-networks, and sometimes, impossibly, I hear them return transformed, as if the forest itself has added its own harmonies.

The plateau speaks to those who learn its language. In the convergence of thunder-roar and insect-chatter, vine-swinger cackle and amphibian bellow, there exists a deeper communication that predates words. I am its translator, its guardian, its witness. When the micro-storms gather in the canopy and lightning illuminates the living pillars, I feel the entire network pulse with electric potential, carrying messages I'm only beginning to understand.

This is my place: suspended between earth and sky, between sound and silence, between the ancient past embedded in the wood and the ever-growing future that spreads with each new vine. I am the keeper of this realm's memory, recorded not in writing but in echo and resonance. Each day, I wake to the knowledge that I inhabit a living instrument vast beyond comprehension, and my purpose is to ensure its songs continue long after the last human voice falls silent.

The children of our tribes sometimes climb to my platforms, and I teach them to press their ears against the singing wood, to feel the stories vibrating in the grain. One day, one of them will show the gift, and I will train them to take my place. The Keeper of Echoes is never truly alone—the voices of all who came before resonate in the chambers, waiting to guide the next listener who proves worthy of the forest's deepest secrets.

This is who I am: a bridge between worlds, a living echo of the plateau's ancient song, forever listening to the music of evolution itself.
