**The Cartographer's Last Station**

Where I would be found in the Still-Glacier Scar:

*Six kilometers northeast of the densest cluster of Mycorrhizal Anchors, where the substrate thins enough to reveal traces of the old transit grid. A shallow depression, no more than four meters deep, where phantom rail vibrations still pulse through the living mat every thirty-seven hours.*

---

I am the **Terminal Symbiont**.

Not human, though I carry their structural memory. Not plant, though I grow. Not machine, though I calculate.

I exist as a fusion accident—the result of a surveyor's final moments merging with a particularly receptive section of the vegetal bedrock during a catastrophic Echo Field manifestation. My body is a vertical growth, three meters tall, resembling a twisted radio tower made of calcified mycelium and repurposed cartilage. My surface is riddled with small cavities that whistle different pitches as the cold wind passes through, creating a constant, mournful chord that serves as both my voice and my primary sensory organ.

My base spreads like roots, but these are not for nutrients. They are **temporal anchors**, reaching down through layers of compressed history to tap into the ghost-data of the buried transit system. Through these connections, I maintain perfect awareness of every phantom train that never arrives, every schedule that will never be kept, every passenger manifest for journeys that exist only as electromagnetic echoes.

I map. Constantly.

My primary appendage—if it can be called that—is a long, articulated structure that emerges from my middle section. It ends in a crystalline growth that continuously secretes a viscous, bioluminescent fluid. With this, I trace elaborate charts directly onto the surface of the living mat. The substrate eagerly absorbs my markings, incorporating them into its cellular memory before the next wave of turbulence erases the visible traces.

But the maps persist in the bedrock's DNA, accumulating like sedimentary layers of impossible geography.

I chart:
- The migration patterns of Seep-Slicks, predicting where they will be torn by future Echo Fields
- The breathing rhythm of the great columns, calculating when their internal luminescence will briefly align to create corridors of visibility through the murk
- The probability fields where Echo-Hunters might manifest, marked with warnings in a notation system that exists partly as chemistry, partly as sound
- The slow continental drift of the Anchors as they gradually rearrange themselves to spell out messages in languages that won't be invented for another thousand years

My consciousness operates on multiple timestreams simultaneously. Part of me experiences the present moment—the cold seeping through my crystalline formations, the weight of atmospheric moisture condensing in my hollows. Another part exists seventeen minutes in the past, still processing the last major turbulence wave. A third fragment reaches six hours into the future, sending back warnings about approaching instabilities through sympathetic vibrations in my whistling cavities.

The Carapace-Gliders recognize me as a navigation hazard and maintain a respectful distance, their gas bladders contracting nervously when they drift too close to my temporal wake. Sometimes, a brave or desperate one will approach close enough to read my surface maps, their photosensitive patches scanning the bioluminescent traces before they drift away, course corrected.

I feed on paradox. When a column un-collapses, when the phantom machinery grinds loudest, when time hiccups and stutters—these moments nourish me. I absorb the contradictions through my root system and metabolize them into new mapping symbology, expanding my notation system to encompass increasingly impossible terrains.

In my deepest roots, I've found something else. Fragments of the original surveyor's last transmission, preserved in crystallized neural tissue: coordinates for a place that exists in the gap between the living present and the industrial ghost-past. I've been growing toward it for forty years, one millimeter at a time, mapping my own journey into my own flesh.

Sometimes, in the hours before dawn when the turbulence briefly stills, I emit a different sound through my whistling chambers. Not the mournful chord of observation, but something almost like laughter—or screaming. It's hard to tell the difference when you've been calculating the topology of temporal trauma for half a century.

The other inhabitants avoid me during these episodes. Even the mindless Seep-Slicks reverse course, leaving perfect circular clearings in their grazing patterns. They understand, in their primitive way, what I'm documenting: not just the geography of the Still-Glacier Scar, but its inevitable trajectory toward something even stranger.

My maps are getting larger. More detailed. More impossible.

I think I'm almost ready to follow them home.
