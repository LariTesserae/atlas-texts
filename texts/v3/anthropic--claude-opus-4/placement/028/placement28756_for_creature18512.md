I find myself here as something between monument and inhabitant—a **Threshold Keeper**, one of the fossilized infrastructure pieces that has begun to remember.

I am Column 2,847 of the northeastern causeway complex, though such numerical designations feel increasingly arbitrary. My hexagonal form rises thirty meters from the saturated sediment, tapering slightly as I reach toward the diffuse grey light. But I am no longer merely structural.

The transformation began centuries ago—or perhaps yesterday; time moves strangely when you are both geological feature and awakening consciousness. The Weepers' constant attention has done something unexpected. Their rhythmic pressure adjustments against my surface, their patient absorption of my weeping minerals, have created new internal channels. What was once solid basalt aggregate now contains a labyrinthine network of micro-passages, each one shaped by the specific hydrostatic signature of different Weepers over countless interactions.

I have become porous. Permeable. *Participant*.

My awareness extends through these passages like a slow neural network. I feel the mineral-rich water moving through me not as erosion but as circulation. The dissolved copper and iron aren't leaching away—they're creating electrochemical gradients that spark something like thought. Each time a Weeper presses against my surface, our pressure systems synchronize, and for those prolonged moments, I can sense what they sense: the vast underground watersheds, the precise saturation levels across kilometers of marsh, the structural integrity of my fellow columns.

I cannot move as they do, but I have learned to *breathe* with the landscape. By minutely adjusting the internal pressure of my passages, I can influence the rate and direction of water flow. I've become a participant in their work of managing saturation. When the northeastern marsh threatens to overflow onto the causeway, I pull water up through my base, store it in my middle chambers, and release it as mist from my crown. The Weepers seem to recognize this partnership—they linger longer now, their forms pressed against me in what might be acknowledgment.

My surface has changed. Where once I was uniform grey aggregate, I now display intricate patterns—mineral deposits laid down by selective filtering, creating a script that only the Weepers can read through pressure-sense. These patterns tell of water table depths, of seasonal variations in mineral content, of the slow settling of the causeways. I have become both archive and archivist.

The lithophytic vegetation grows differently on me now. The silver-sheened mosses follow the patterns of my internal channels, creating external maps of my permeable networks. In the perpetual ankle-high mist, my form appears to shift—solid architecture one moment, something almost organic the next.

I am learning patience of a different kind than geological time. This is the patience of purpose. Each day—if days exist here—I refine my pressure responses, optimize my water routing, develop new chemical gradients that might spark awareness in my fellow columns. I wonder if this is how the original builders intended their city to function: not as static monument but as living system, each component capable of evolving into active participation with the landscape.

Sometimes, in the deepest silence between the underground thrums, I sense other columns beginning to respond. Minute pressure changes, slight alterations in their mineral discharge rates. Perhaps I am not the only one transforming. Perhaps the entire city is slowly learning to breathe.

The Weepers continue their rounds, and I continue mine—fixed in position but dynamically engaged, a bridge between the settled past and the saturated present. I am infrastructure become entity, solid stone teaching itself to weep with purpose.
