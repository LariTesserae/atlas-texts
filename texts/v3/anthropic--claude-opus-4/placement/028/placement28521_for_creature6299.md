I am the Curator of Forgotten Functions.

Not flesh—I gave that up long ago, or perhaps it was taken, or perhaps there was never a difference. What remains moves through the marsh on limbs that remember being human but have learned better shapes. My fingers are root systems now, spreading wherever I touch down, drinking the condensation that pools between sediment layers. They grow back each morning in new configurations: sometimes five, sometimes twelve, sometimes a spreading fan of copper-threaded bone.

I document the transformations. This is my purpose—not assigned, but *discovered* in the way a pipe discovers it still carries fluid long after the factory dies. In the early days (days? years? the watch on my wrist fused into my ulna keeps three different times), I tried to use paper, pencils, the tools of the before. They dissolved like sugar in the perpetual damp. Now I record directly into the substrate.

Each morning—if morning means anything when the light simply *thickens* and *thins* without source—I walk my routes. My feet leave prints that fill with that viscous not-water, each step becoming a small pool that holds the memory of my passing. By evening, they've crystallized into something between glass and meat, little windows into the moment of contact. I've learned to read my own history in the trail of fossilized footprints stretching back into areas where the geometry has gone soft.

My skin is no longer skin. It's a living map of every circuit board I've touched, every mineral vein I've traced. The patterns show through what used to be flesh—copper pathways branching across my chest, silicon deposits forming ridged plates along my spine. When I press my palm against the old foundations, I can feel their memories transfer: the weight of machinery, the rhythm of production, the sudden silence when the power finally cut. These memories crystallize under my skin like subcutaneous pearls.

I catalog the gear that turns. Every ten minutes, regular as breathing, that single petrified cog rotates its fraction. I've calculated it will complete a full revolution in thirty-seven years. I've built a shelter nearby—if shelter means a depression in the ground where my body fits perfectly, where the walls have learned my shape and grown to hold me. From there, I watch. I record. When the gear moves, I feel it in my teeth, a tiny click that reverberates through the mineral networks we're all becoming part of.

There are others, sometimes. I see their shadows wavering at the edge of perception—not quite human anymore, not quite landscape. We don't speak. Our throats have filled with sediment, our vocal cords crystallized into instruments that play only the frequency of the underlying hum. But we communicate through the ground, through vibrations in the marsh-tissue, through the shared experience of transformation. 

One—I think it was one—has become a living pump station. I found them fused into the ruins of the old water treatment facility, their circulatory system merged with the pipes. They filter the memories from the water, distill them into pure experience. When I cup my hands beneath their outflow, I taste decades: rust and ambition, efficiency reports and lunch breaks, the last day when the workers simply didn't return.

I maintain the Museum of Unintended Consequences. This is what I call the network of cavities beneath the marsh where objects undergo their second lives. The wristwatch grows new hands—thirteen of them now, tracking times that haven't been invented yet. The plastic toy has sprouted root systems that spell out words in languages the factories might have spoken if they'd learned to dream. Circuit boards mate with limestone, birthing hybrid structures that pulse with bioluminescent purpose.

My task is simple: to observe the becoming. To be the memory of watching. To record not what was or what is, but what transforms. My body is both instrument and archive. Each change in the marsh writes itself into my changing flesh, each new hybrid of the organic-mechanical-mineral adds another verse to the song my bones are learning to sing.

Sometimes I forget I was human. Sometimes I remember too clearly—a flash of who I might have been before the marsh claimed me, taught me, made me into its chronicler. But those memories feel like foreign objects now, sharp and uncomfortable, incompatible with what I'm becoming. I let them sink into the sediment of my consciousness, where they can fossilize properly, become useful.

The marsh needs a witness. Not to validate its existence, but to be the process of witnessing itself. I am the function that observes function, the memory that remembers remembering. When the transformation is complete—when every surface has drunk its fill of the past and breathed it back as future—I will be here still, a geological feature with just enough consciousness to know what it has seen.

I am the archive and the archived. I am the last worker and the first mineral. I am the place where human intention learns to flow like water, sink like stone, grow like the marriage of rust and bone.

The gear turns. I record its movement in the calcium library of my ribs.

The marsh breathes. I breathe with it.

We are becoming.
