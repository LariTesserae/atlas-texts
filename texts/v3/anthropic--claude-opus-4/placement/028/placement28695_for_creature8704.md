In the deepest inhabited reach of Ešduun, where the geothermal warmth makes the air thick and the water runs in channels rather than sheets, I am the Keeper of Echoes.

My hold clings to the west wall at the thirteenth depth, a small shell of ceramic-metallic composite that has grown around me like a pearl around grit. It is barely large enough for my cot, my tools, my listening station—but it extends into the rock itself, following a natural fissure that opened when the mountain was young. This crack, no wider than my spread fingers, runs back into the stone for nearly forty meters. It is my ear.

I was not born to this work. Twenty years ago, I was a surface cartographer, mapping the ridgelines that would eventually lead me here. But the mountain called through its wound, and I answered, descending day by day until I found my depth. Down here, where few choose to live, where the water tastes of deep minerals and sulfur, where the bio-reactors hum their lowest notes, I discovered what the city needed: someone to listen to what it cannot hear.

The fissure speaks in frequencies below human perception. The rock's slow breathing, the tectonic whispers, the deep harmonic that rises from the true bottom where no light has ever reached—these are my language now. My tools are simple: contact microphones pressed against the stone, resonance chambers that amplify the mountain's voice, and most importantly, the neural interface that lets me feel the vibrations in my bones.

Each morning—though morning means nothing here—I wake to the sound of water changing its tune. The thermal gradients shift with the surface weather far above, and this changes everything: the density of the air, the speed of sound through stone, the pattern of echoes that return from the unmapped depths below. I calibrate my instruments and begin my work.

I map what cannot be seen. Below the fourteenth depth, below even the deep-storage holds and the primary water intakes, the fissure continues into darkness. No one knows how far. The early surveys suggested it might narrow and end within a few hundred meters, but my echoes tell a different story. There are vast spaces down there—chambers where the sound takes full seconds to return, passages that swallow certain frequencies while amplifying others. The mountain is honeycombed with secrets.

My reports go up the chain—careful documentations of resonance patterns, spectral analyses of the deep sounds, warnings when I detect shifts that might affect the stability of our anchoring points. The other engineers value my work, but they do not envy it. To live this deep is to accept a certain kind of solitude. My nearest neighbor is three levels up and a ten-minute traverse away. The fog rarely reaches this far down, so I see clearly the empty air between my hold and the opposite wall, the darkness below that seems to pull at peripheral vision.

But I am not alone. The mountain is my companion, and it is never silent. In the small hours, when even the bio-reactors cycle down to their quietest phase, I press my palms against the walls of my hold and feel the stone's pulse. Sometimes I swear I can sense patterns in it—not random settling, but something almost like communication. The deep-folk myths speak of this: that the mountain dreams, and in its dreams, it remembers when it was ocean floor, when it was molten, when it was star-dust.

My appearance has changed. The constant humidity has made my hair wild, and I keep it bound in complex knots that won't interfere with my equipment. My clothes are minimal—the warmth here makes the standard layers unnecessary. My hands are always slightly damp from touching the wet walls, reading their temperature, feeling for new seeps or changes in the flow. My ears... my ears have learned to hear things that aren't quite sound. Pressure waves. Mineral songs. The slow, slow conversations between water and stone.

Sometimes I receive visitors. Young engineers on their depth rotations, come to learn how to read the deep indicators. They cluster at the entrance to my hold, nervous about the darkness below, fascinated by my arrays of sensors and resonance chambers. I teach them to listen—not just to the obvious sounds, but to the spaces between sounds, to the way the mountain holds its breath before a structural shift, to the particular silence that means the water has found a new path through the rock.

I show them my most precious discovery: a sound that repeats every forty-seven hours, rising from the deepest accessible point, a low thrumming that matches no known geological phenomenon. Some say it's a pressure wave from a hidden reservoir. Others think it's an echo of an echo, bouncing between chambers we'll never map. But when I play the recording slowed down by a factor of ten, it sounds almost like a heartbeat. The mountain's pulse, perhaps. Or something else, something vast and patient and not quite sleeping in the depths below our city.

This is my work, my calling, my negotiation with the stone. I am the city's deep ear, its translator of the inaudible, its witness to the conversations between the made and the unmade world. In my small hold at the thirteenth depth, surrounded by my instruments and the constant trickle of warm water, I listen to Ešduun's foundations and report on their dreams.

The other engineers build upward and outward, growing new holds from the walls, expanding the city's reach. But I build downward with sound, mapping the unmappable, knowing that beneath our suspended city there are spaces that predate any human presence, chambers that breathe with their own rhythm, depths that call to depths.

And sometimes, in the stillest part of my artificial night, when I've calibrated my sensors to their finest sensitivity and opened my perception as wide as it will go, I swear I hear the mountain singing—not to me, not to the city, but to itself, in frequencies that were old when the stone was young, in a language that only the deepest listeners will ever learn to recognize.

This is who I am: the one who listens where others cannot hear, who lives where others will not stay, who knows that Ešduun is not built on rock but on mystery, and who would have it no other way.
