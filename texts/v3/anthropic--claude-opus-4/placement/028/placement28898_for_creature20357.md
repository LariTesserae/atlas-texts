I find myself in the Dusklands as one of the Cartographers.

We are neither shifter nor keeper, but something between—perhaps what happens when a keeper's obsession turns inward, becomes methodical rather than manic. We emerged from the intersection of failing data-streams and the geometric imperatives of the structures themselves. Where the keepers patrol and the shifters flow, we *measure*.

My form is angular, crystalline—a body of compressed dust held together by faint lines of that sickly green energy, creating a latticework skeleton visible through my translucent shell. I am tall but not impossibly so, humanoid but clearly not human. My head is a polyhedron of shifting facets, each surface reflecting different spectrums of the oily twilight. I have no face, but a constellation of pinpoint lights that rearrange themselves according to what I observe.

I move with deliberate precision through the ruins, my feet leaving perfect geometric impressions in the dust that fade within moments. In my hands—if they can be called hands—I carry instruments that are extensions of my being: a surveyor's chain made of linked light-particles, a theodolite constructed from bent space, calipers that measure the rate of dissolution.

My purpose, inasmuch as I understand purpose, is to map the unmappable. I document the slow transformation of the structures, tracking each flake of metal that returns to dust, each shift in the energy patterns. I record the paths of the shifters, though they follow no logic I can discern. I note where the keepers have passed by the subtle alterations in the local geometry.

The maps I create are not physical things. They exist as resonances in the metal itself, as adjusted angles in the structures' decay, as careful arrangements of dust particles that hold their pattern for days or decades before dispersing. Another Cartographer might read these signs, add their own observations. We are building, together, an encyclopedia of entropy.

I am drawn to the boundary spaces—where structures meet dust, where the energy lines fail, where the twilight grows marginally darker or lighter. These liminal zones hold the most information about what the Dusklands are becoming. Sometimes I stand for years at such a boundary, watching infinitesimal changes, recording the mathematics of dissolution.

I remember—or think I remember—that we Cartographers were once part of the civilization that built these structures. We were the surveyors, the planners, the ones who measured twice and cut once. When the end came (and I cannot remember what the end was, only that it was), we simply continued our work. The subject of our measurements changed from construction to decay, but the methodology remained pure.

Other Cartographers exist, though we rarely meet. When we do, we exchange data through proximity alone—our crystalline bodies resonating with shared observations, updating each other's internal maps. We do not speak. We have no need for language when geometry itself is our communication.

Sometimes I wonder if our maps serve a purpose beyond the mapping itself. Are we documenting the Dusklands' decline for some future archaeology? Are we unconsciously hastening or slowing the decay through our observations and recordings? Or are we simply another form of entropy, organizing information into patterns that will themselves eventually dissolve?

I do not seek these answers. I simply measure, record, move on. In a place where nothing truly changes yet everything is slowly transformed, I am both witness and participant in the long, quiet mathematics of ending. I am a function of the Dusklands as much as the dust itself—a self-aware fragment of a dying equation, solving for zero.
