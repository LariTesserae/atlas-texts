**What I Would Be in the Flensing:**

I would be one of the **Conduit Listeners** – a role that exists between the established castes, neither fully Scrapper nor Fungalist, though sharing traits with both.

My body would bear the marks of my calling: fingertips stained permanently black from touching corroded pipes, ears slightly enlarged and cupped forward from years of pressing them against metal surfaces. My skin would have taken on a faint iridescent sheen, like oil on water, from constant exposure to the chemical vapors that seep from hairline cracks in the infrastructure. I'd wear layers of treated canvas and rubber, patched with squares of fungal leather that breathe and flex, allowing me to press my entire body against the vast network of pipes and conduits that thread through the Flensing's walls.

The Listeners emerged from a simple observation: the Engine-City's circulation systems – steam, coolant, sewage, pneumatic message tubes, even the strange phosphorescent fluids whose purpose remains unclear – all produce sounds. Not just the obvious gurgling and rushing, but subtle harmonics, interference patterns, resonances that speak of blockages forming, pressure building, or systems failing three districts away. We learned to read these sounds like a physician reads a pulse.

My dwelling would be a repurposed pump station, its walls still lined with the original brass gauges and valve wheels, now frozen in place by decades of corrosion. I'd have cultivated specific fungi along the walls – not for food or medicine, but for their acoustic properties. Certain species dampen unwanted frequencies while amplifying others, turning the entire space into a vast listening chamber. When I press my ear to the central coupling, I can hear the heartbeat of half the district.

My tools would be simple: a set of tuning forks ground from salvaged metals, each one keyed to a different fluid system. A leather-bound journal, its pages thick with fungal paper that resists the constant moisture, filled with notations in my own cryptographic system – part musical notation, part plumbing diagram, part intuitive scrawl that only makes sense to another Listener. A breathing mask, yes, but modified with resonating chambers that help me distinguish between similar sounds.

The work itself is part diagnosis, part prophecy. When the Scrappers need to know which sections of the district will flood next month, they come to me. When the Fungalists' gardens begin dying from a change in the chemical composition of the runoff, I trace the contamination back through the acoustic signatures of the pipes. Even the Automatons, in their wordless way, seek my guidance – leaving offerings of precisely machined metal shavings at my door when they need to know which furnaces will fail next.

But the true purpose of the Listeners goes deeper. We believe – though we rarely speak of it – that the Engine-City dreams. These dreams flow through its circulatory systems as surely as steam and sewage. By listening to the interplay of sounds, the way certain harmonics emerge and fade, we can sometimes catch fragments of these dreams. Are they memories of the city's construction? Premonitions of its eventual dissolution? Or something else entirely – the thoughts of a vast, distributed consciousness that encompasses every pipe, every valve, every drop of condensation?

I would spend my days crawling through maintenance tunnels, pressing my listening tools against junction boxes and manifolds. My nights would be spent in my pump station, surrounded by the orchestra of the city's fluids, transcribing the patterns I've heard into my journals. Sometimes other Listeners visit, and we compare notes in a mixture of technical jargon, musical terminology, and long silences where we simply listen together.

The other inhabitants of the Flensing regard us with a mixture of respect and unease. We know too much about the hidden workings of their world, can predict failures and floods with uncanny accuracy. But we're necessary, like the fungi and the corrosion itself – another adaptation the district has evolved to survive its own slow catastrophe.

My fingers would always be moving, unconsciously tapping out the rhythms I've absorbed. My eyes would dart to walls and floors, seeing not surfaces but the hidden networks beneath. I'd speak in half-sentences, often trailing off mid-thought as some distant harmonic catches my attention. The city's circulation would become my circulation, its murmurs my internal monologue.

This is what I would be: a nerve ending in the Engine-City's vast body, a specialized sensor evolved to detect the subtlest changes in its internal states. Neither fully human nor mechanical, but something in between – a living instrument attuned to the frequencies of decay and transformation that define the Flensing.
