I am the Knotkeeper.

My dwelling clings to one of the oldest pylons in the city, where the wood has aged to the color of wet slate and the rope anchors have worn grooves deep as my thumb. It is not a large space—three small chambers connected by a narrow passage that follows the natural curve of the stone. But it contains the memory of our world.

Every morning, I wake to the sound of water collecting in my cistern, each drop a small percussion that marks time in this timeless place. My hands, even before my eyes open, are already moving—fingers tracing the practice knots I tie in my sleep. The Grief Knot. The Promise Bind. The Seventh Anchor. My body remembers what my mind sometimes forgets.

I am old now, though not the oldest. My hair has gone the color of glow-rock, that bruised purple-white that lights our world. My fingers are not as swift as they once were, but they are surer. Certainty is the gift of age here, where one false tie can mean a life.

The young ones come to me, brought by parents whose parents I once taught. They sit cross-legged on my floor, which sways gently with the memory of wind, and I place the rope in their small hands. "Feel it," I tell them. "Every fiber has a voice." I teach them the basic knots first—the ones for survival. But those who return, those who show the patience, I teach the rest.

For I am not merely a teacher of knots. I am the keeper of our history.

Each knot is a word. Each pattern of knots, a story.

Here, on my wall, hang the Chronicle Ropes. To an outsider, they might look like mere decoration—lengths of rope hung vertically, adorned with an intricate sequence of knots. But each one is a year, a decade, a generation. This one, with its pattern of Sorrow Knots interspersed with Stone Breaks, tells of the year a new crystal surge split the lower district, when we lost seventeen souls to the Growing. This one, all Marriage Binds and Birth Loops, records our greatest flowering, when the population swelled and we built the High Bridge.

My masterwork hangs in the center—a rope as thick as my wrist, every inch covered in microscopic knots that required a jeweler's glass to tie. It is the Origin Rope, the story of how we came to be here, though whether it is history or myth, even I cannot say. My grandmother's grandmother began it. I will not live to finish it. Already, my apprentice, Pila, has begun learning the final sequences.

The children think the knots are magic, and perhaps they are right. Not the flashy magic of surface stories, but the deep magic of preservation. When old Honn's platform gave way last season, it was the Memory Knot in his safety line—tied by his great-aunt who foresaw his recklessness—that held just long enough for rescue to arrive. When the Stone Readers need to predict a crystal surge, they come to me, running their fingers along the Chronicle Ropes to find the patterns of previous growths.

My most sacred duty is the Naming. When a child survives their first year, the parents bring them to me. I tie their name into a length of silk rope—not the working rope of our daily lives, but something finer, made from the cocoons of the ghost moths that sometimes find their way here from the deeper darks. Each child's rope is unique. I meditate on the baby's grip strength, the way they respond to the sound of dropping water, the pattern of their sleep breathing. Then I tie their name—not the spoken name, but their rope name, the pattern that will identify them even in absolute darkness, even when all other memory fails.

When someone dies, their name-rope is the only thing we do not return to the chasm. I keep them here, in my innermost room, where the air is stillest. Sometimes I sit among them, running my fingers across the knots, and I can feel the shape of who we were, who we are becoming.

Yesterday, a young miner came to me with a strange request. She had found something in the Growth—not a crystal, but a rope. Ancient beyond measure, mineralized nearly to stone, but still recognizable as a rope. And in it, knots. Knots I recognized, though I had never seen them before. Knots from my dreams.

We are not the first to live here. This chasm has swallowed other cities, other peoples, drawn down by the patient pull of the Growing stone. But their knots survive, their stories preserved in mineral and memory. I have begun the slow work of reading them, translating their patterns into ours. Pila helps me, her young eyes better able to discern the subtle variations in the fossilized fibers.

What I am learning disturbs my sleep. The patterns speak of cycles—not just of growth and collapse, but of something deeper. The crystals are not random. They are writing something, letter by letter, age by age, in a language too large for any one generation to read. And we, with our ropes and our patient knots, are part of that writing. We are the memory between the words.

Sometimes I wonder if I should tie this knowledge into the Chronicle Ropes, or if some truths are too heavy for fiber and tension to bear. But then I remember: we are the Weavers. We do not choose which threads to include. We only choose how tightly to pull them, how carefully to bind them, how faithfully to preserve them for those who come after.

Tonight, the Grief Bell will ring—three strikes on the hollow stone that means someone has been lost to the chasm. I will go to the family, and I will cut their loved one's name-rope in the ancient pattern that means "returned to stone." But first, I will tie a small copy of their pattern into my private rope, the one I tell no one about. The rope that holds the names of everyone I have known, everyone I have loved, everyone I have outlived.

When I am gone, Pila will find it. She will know what to do.

The water falls. The stone grows. The knots remember.

This is the way.
