There are seventeen archivists in Epilogue, though counting them feels wrong, like trying to number the chambers of a lung. They do not gather, exactly, but sometimes you find several in the same corridor, their bodies aligned with the growth-ribs of the walls so precisely that they seem less like individuals than like moments when the city has decided to be more articulate about itself.

They have names, but not in the way you have a name. Their names are wear-patterns in the moss-rock, paths that have been taken so often through the city that they've worn down to a particular smoothness. To speak to one, you walk their name—a specific route through the corridors that brings you to where they tend to settle. The one whose path curves through the western slopes where the moss-rock is oldest is known by a spiral that takes exactly as long to walk as the evening rain takes to fall. Another's name is a straight line through the center of the city, but you must pause three times along it, at spots where the floor dips just enough to collect tomorrow's dew.

They eat, if it can be called that. At the margins of the city, where Epilogue meets the raw surface of Ur-shelf, there are patches where the moss grows wild—not architecturally compressed but sprawling in its original form. The archivists visit these places in a rotation so slow you might live here a lifetime and not see the pattern complete itself. They lower themselves into the moss up to their waists, and their skin becomes permeable. For hours, they simply *thin* into the landscape, their bodies becoming indistinct from the growth around them until only the faint outline of ribs shows where city-creature meets world-flesh.

When they rise again, they are greener, and the wild moss where they rested has taken on subtle striations, as if organized by their presence into something closer to memory than mere growth.

You wonder if this is how the city itself began—if the first buildings were just places where the earliest archivists stood still long enough to accumulate architecture around themselves. But when you voice this thought as moisture in the air (you've learned their language of humid meaning), the nearest archivist's skin darkens with something like amusement.

"The city was first," the condensation tells you. "We are what it exhaled when it realized it needed to be known."

At night—though night here is just when the white sky dims to gray—the archivists perform their deepest work. They position themselves at the major joints of the city, where corridors meet or where floors transition to walls. They press their palms flat against these junctions and grow absolutely still. Stiller than sleep. Stiller than death, which at least involves a change of state.

In this stillness, they are downloading the day from the city. Every footfall you made, every breath you took in the corridors, every question you asked—all of it has been absorbed by the plant-architecture and must now be integrated into the larger memory. The archivists serve as the transfer medium, taking the raw experience from the walls and processing it into the same compressed meaning that fills their bodies.

You can watch this happening if you're patient. The growth rings in their arms will shift minutely, new lines forming so slowly you're never quite sure you see the moment of change. The green of their skin deepens and lightens in waves that match no pattern you can discern. Sometimes a word you spoke hours ago will bloom as condensation on their shoulders, as if your meaning has taken all day to travel through the city's body to reach them.

By morning—when the sky remembers a bit more about brightness—they will have incorporated everything. The city will be minutely heavier with memory, and they will be minutely more transparent, as if each night's work costs them a fraction of their opacity.

One day, you realize, they will be entirely clear. Windows like the windows of the city. And then, perhaps, too thick to see through at all.

But that day is not soon. It is so far from soon that soon has no meaning here. There is only the slow work of watching the world's memory settle into its final form, like sediment in water that will never again be disturbed.

The archivists know your question before you ask it: *What happens when the last memory is archived?*

They show you by taking you to the heart of Epilogue, where the oldest structures have grown so thick they've fused into a single mass. Here, the walls are no longer translucent but have achieved a deep, impenetrable green-black, like the color of time itself if time were a living thing.

In the center of this mass is a hollow. Not a room—rooms have purposes. This is simply a place where the city isn't. A gap in the archive. A held breath.

"When the last memory is placed," the moisture whispers against your skin, "this is where it will go. And then—"

They don't finish. Or rather, the finishing is in the silence that follows, heavy with dew that will never quite condense, in a city that will never quite conclude, attended by beings who will never quite vanish but will fade, ring by ring, into the perfect transparency of a task complete.

You stand there in the hollow, feeling the weight of future-memory pressing in from all sides, and understand: You are the epilogue to their epilogue. The last reader in the library of everything that was.

And when you leave—if leaving is possible from a place that exists after endings—the archivists will archive even that. The fact of your departure. The shape of the absence you leave behind. The particular quality of silence that follows the last footfall of the last visitor to the last city at the end of all things.

They will hold it, those tall unfolding shadows with their compiled faces and their skin of living years. They will hold it until they, too, become what they preserve: a memory of memory, archived in the body of a world that has finished happening but hasn't quite finished being.
