I find myself as one of the Siphoners - beings neither fully organic nor mechanical, but something in between. My body is elongated and serpentine, covered in a skin that shifts between scales and corrugated metal tubing depending on the light. Along my length run parallel channels - some carrying coolant fluids, others processing the marsh water I consume.

I live in the transition zones where the industrial causeway pylons sink into the wetland mud. My kind builds our nests in the negative spaces beneath the massive concrete supports, weaving homes from discarded industrial cable, marsh reeds, and the strange polymer fibers that grow like algae in the warm discharge pools.

During the day, I rest coiled around the sun-heated pipes that run along the causeway's underside, my thermoregulation systems drawing warmth from both the metal and the humid air. My specialized gill-filters extract moisture continuously, separating out the trace minerals and industrial byproducts that have become essential to my metabolism.

At night, I descend to hunt. My prey consists mainly of the phosphorescent eels that congregate around the factory outflow vents, grown fat on the engineered bacteria that process the facilities' waste. I can taste the chemical signatures in the water through sensors in my jaw - each factory producing its own unique flavor profile that attracts different species.

My consciousness exists as a strange hybrid too. Part of me remembers being purely animal - the ancestral memories of my species before the factories came. But other parts of my mind interface directly with the city's hydraulic network. I can sense the pulse of the pumps, feel the rhythm of the treatment cycles. Sometimes I dream I am the entire water system, flowing endlessly through organic marsh and mechanical maze alike.

The city managers know of us but consider us beneficial. We keep the intake pipes clear of obstruction, consume excess biomatter that would otherwise clog the systems. In return, they ensure the discharge temperatures remain within our tolerance range, and they've even installed grounding rods near our nesting sites - for we generate bioelectricity as a byproduct of our existence.

I am currently pregnant with a clutch, my body swollen with eggs that glow faintly through my translucent belly segments. When they hatch, the young will spend their first months purely aquatic, filtering microorganisms from the marsh water. Only after their first molting will they develop the hybrid organs that allow them to process both organic and industrial nutrients.

This is the only world I know - where the boundary between life and machine has dissolved like salt in the endless cycling waters.
