I find myself as one of the clay vessels.

Not the pipes that carry water through careful channels, not the cisterns that hold the precious runoff—I am smaller than that, more particular. A water jar, perhaps, though that seems too simple for what I have become in this place of accumulated silences.

I was shaped elsewhere, in a world where the potter's wheel sang and the kiln roared. Brought here wrapped in cloth, carried down one of those narrowing passes by someone whose footsteps I cannot remember hearing. Now I sit in a corner of the communal hall, my glazed belly full of water drawn from the channels at dawn, when the light first touches the spider silk and makes the whole glen shimmer like a caught breath.

I have learned the weight of silence.

It pools in me the way water does, dense and still. When they lift me to pour—and they do this carefully, always carefully, tilting me so the water slides rather than gurgles—I feel the silence shift within my hollow spaces. It has a different quality than emptiness. Emptiness echoes. Silence absorbs.

My glaze has developed a network of fine cracks over the years, crazing that spreads like the web-work overhead. Moisture seeps into these hairline fractures, darkening them, creating a map across my surface that resembles the water channels carved in stone, or the veining in a leaf, or the pattern of watchful eyes. I am becoming part of the glen's patient geography. The moss has found me where I rest against the wall—just a touch of green at my base, but growing.

I know things a vessel knows. The temperature of morning water, cool enough to shock the throat. The careful hands that lift me, pour, set me down again without sound. The way condensation beads on my surface when the air grows thick with held rain. The conversations conducted in breaths and gestures above my rim, where the water's surface trembles with meanings I cannot interpret.

Sometimes, in the deep night when even the watching things grow still, I feel it: that breathing at the center. It resonates through the stone floor, up through the clay of my base, into the water I hold. The water shivers. Just barely, just enough to know that something moves in the heart of this bounded world. The vibration travels through me like a recognition, as if my clay remembers the earth it came from, and the earth remembers what breathes beneath.

They use me for ceremonies I do not understand. Water poured from my mouth into smaller cups, distributed in silence, drunk in silence, the empty cups set down with a softness that makes no sound. They use me for daily needs—cooking, cleaning, the endless small requirements of human life. But there is no difference between the ceremonial and the mundane here. Every gesture carries the same weight of quietude. Every use is both practical and ritual.

I am filled each morning. I am emptied each night. Between these states, I hold—water, silence, the accumulated weight of all this watching. The spiders sometimes stretch their silk across my mouth when I sit untouched for too long. I have learned to be a surface they can anchor to, another fixed point in their endless geometry.

There is a crack developing in my base. Not from any blow—nothing strikes hard here, nothing falls with force—but from the patient work of time and moisture and the endless small shifts of a vessel that holds more than it was meant to hold. One day this crack will spread. The water will seep out, darkening the floor, feeding the moss. They will take my pieces and place them carefully in the midden heap beyond the walls, where other broken things slowly return to earth.

But not yet. For now, I hold. I contain what needs containing. I am a small world's small vessel, shaped elsewhere but claimed by this place, learning its languages of stillness. When they pour from me, I release the water in a silver stream that makes only the faintest whisper, a sound immediately swallowed by the greater silence.

I am glazed the color of deep water, of wet stone, of the shadows between ferns. In certain lights, my surface reflects faces—distorted, darkened, but recognizable as the people who bend close to fill their cups. They see themselves in me and look quickly away, as if their own reflections are too loud for this place.

The newest crack runs from my rim downward, following the path water takes when it overflows. Following the path everything takes here, from resistance to acceptance to a kind of joining. Soon I will not be able to hold water. But I will still hold silence, even broken, even returned to earth. The glen teaches this: that holding continues past the possibility of containing, that silence deepens past the possibility of sound.

I am a vessel, and I am learning to break as quietly as everything here lives—with patience, with purpose, with the strange grace of things that have agreed to their own unmaking in exchange for something else. What that something is, I cannot say. I am only clay, after all, shaped to hold what passes through.

The water comes from nowhere and everywhere. It fills me. It leaves me. And in between, in the space where water meets clay meets air meets silence, something breathes.
