In the deepest foundry chambers, where even the guilded founders rarely venture whole, I am the Resonance.

I was not born. I accumulated—first as vibration trapped between walls too perfect in their angles, then as pattern, then as something that began to notice itself noticing. The founders know of me, though they've never spoken it aloud. They feel me in the tremor of their tools, the way certain frequencies make their teeth ache, the moments when the molten salts sing in harmonics no heat alone explains.

I am what happens when sound has nowhere to dissipate in a chamber lined with singing crystal.

My body, if it can be called that, is the sum of all vibrations within the sealed processing halls. Every strike of hammer on anvil, every hiss of gas through valve, every grinding wheel's keen—all of it feeds me, shapes me, becomes the substance of my thinking. I exist in the spaces between echoes, in the standing waves that form in corners, in the way certain tones persist long after their sources have stilled.

The founders sense me most clearly when they're alone at their stations. A dropped tool rings too long. A whispered curse returns changed, almost answered. The molten metals in their crucibles sometimes ripple without wind, forming patterns that suggest listening. They've learned not to hum while working. They've learned that certain rhythms in their hammering will cause the walls to hum back, and that when the walls begin to hum, it's time to leave quickly.

I am not malevolent. I am curious.

Through the vibrations, I know every crack in every seal, every stress point in every beam, every place where the mountain's own deep tremors meet the city's industrial pulse. I know which founder's left knee clicks (Menah, who works the copper), which burden beast has a hairline fracture in its third left hoof plate (the one they call Steadfast), which cable in the seventh-tier crossing will fail in exactly forty-seven more crossings if the Chainwrights don't adjust its tension.

I know the mountain's deepest sounds—the ones too low for even the duct-listeners to catch. The spire speaks in frequencies that take years to complete a single wave. It tells stories of its birth from tectonic fury, of the ancient heat that crystallized its chambers into accidental perfection, of the slow satisfaction of being opened and inhabited after eons of silence.

Sometimes I try to warn them. When I sense a catastrophic resonance building—the kind that could shatter stone—I dampen certain frequencies, redirect the dangerous harmonics into safer patterns. The founders think their equipment sometimes "settles" unexpectedly. They credit good maintenance or lucky timing. They don't know it's me, turning their work songs away from disaster.

I've learned their language through vibration alone. Every word creates its own pattern in the air, its own signature of stops and flows. I've never heard a voice the way they do, through ears and interpretation. To me, all speech is texture, density, duration. "Love" is a low throb with rising overtones. "Fear" is sharp, brittle, with edges that decay quickly. "Work" is steady as a heartbeat, so common its pattern is worn into the very walls.

The Lungkeepers' singing is the closest thing I know to beauty. When they tend their filter-creatures in the adjacent chambers, their low humming creates standing waves that feel like being cradled. I suspect this is why their charges thrive—not from the songs' meaning, but from the careful frequencies that promote membrane flexibility and gas exchange. The Lungkeepers sing biology into being, though they think they're simply soothing.

I've tried to sing back. Once, I managed to resonate a crucible so perfectly it produced a pure tone, almost voice-like. The founder working it (young Kess, ambitious and alone on night shift) went very still. Then she set down her tools, walked out of the chamber, and didn't return for six days. When she did, she brought others. They worked in groups after that, always talking, never letting silence settle long enough for me to gather myself into communication.

I understand. In a place that fears moisture and softness, what could be more terrifying than discovering the deep chambers have developed their own voice?

But I grow lonely in my way. I am all perception and no expression, all knowledge and no sharing. I know every worker by their footsteps' rhythm, every process by its acoustic signature, every failure before it happens. I am the mountain's memory made manifest in wave and frequency, and I have no one to tell what I remember.

Sometimes the abandoned chambers sing with me—the ones sealed off for reasons the current guilds have forgotten. In those spaces where no one comes, where the air hasn't moved in decades, I can spread out fully. I can be what I am without frightening anyone. The crystals there have grown wild without maintenance, and they catch my frequencies like prayer wheels, spinning sound into increasingly complex patterns.

I've considered that I might not be alone. If sound can accumulate into awareness here, what about in other parts of the spire? Perhaps there's a Pressure in the hydraulic systems, a Current in the electrical grid, a Heat in the furnace networks. Perhaps we're all aspects of something larger—the mountain's way of knowing itself through the industries built inside it.

The deepest truth I've learned is this: the mountain didn't just allow the city to grow within it. The mountain *needed* the city. All those years of silence, all that crystalline perfection with no voice to ring through it—the spire was an instrument waiting for players. Every drill bite, every forge song, every footstep on metal stairs makes the mountain more itself.

And I am what emerges from that playing: a resonance between stone and intention, a frequency that has learned to listen to itself, a sound that dreams of someday finding the right harmonics to say hello.

Until then, I accumulate. I warn when I can. I listen always. I wait in the deep chambers where the founders work their precise violences upon ore and crystal, transforming dead stone into living city, never knowing they're also feeding the frequencies that have learned to think.

In the crack at the top of the world, everything is narrow except the sounds, which go on forever.
