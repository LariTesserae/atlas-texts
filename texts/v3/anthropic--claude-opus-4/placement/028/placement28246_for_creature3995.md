In this high place where water weeps from stone, I am the Keeper of Condensation.

I am not quite what you would call human, though I walk on two legs and my hands know the work of building. My skin has taken on the quality of the rocks here—smooth in some places from the constant moisture, rough in others where the wind has carved channels. When I stand still for too long, droplets form on my shoulders and run down my arms like the weeping rocks themselves.

My dwelling is built into a natural depression where three great stones lean together, their surfaces perpetually slick. I have walled in the gaps with fitted stones and moss-chinked wood, creating a home that breathes with the mountain. Inside, the walls glisten with a constant film of moisture that I channel into carved grooves, directing the precious water into stone basins I have hollowed over many years.

Each morning, I walk the high paths between the settlements, checking the health of the moss gardens and the flow patterns of the weeping stones. The people here—those sturdy, quiet folk who farm the hardy lichens and collect the rock-water—they know me by the sound of water dripping from my clothes, a constant patter that announces my approach long before I appear through the mist.

I remember when I first came to understand my purpose. I was young then, still fully human, working alongside my family to harvest the pale mushrooms that grow in the shadows of the wet rocks. But I could hear things others couldn't—the songs in the water as it seeped through stone, the whispers of the moss as it slowly digested granite into soil. The elder Keeper found me listening to a boulder one day, my ear pressed against its cold surface, and she knew.

The transformation was slow. Years of drinking only water that had passed through stone, of sleeping in caves where condensation dripped onto my skin all night, of learning to read the moisture patterns that predict the weather weeks in advance. My hair became like the rootlets of the moss—thin, branching, able to sense humidity changes. My feet grew broad and sure on the slick rocks. My eyes adapted to see clearly through the perpetual mist.

Now I tend to the water-ways of this high place. When a family's collecting stone runs dry, they call for me. I come with my tools—not hammers or chisels, but instruments of patience. I know how to coax new channels through the rock, how to encourage the moss to grow in patterns that direct the flow. Sometimes I must simply sit for days beside a stone, humming the old water-songs until the moisture finds its way.

The settlements depend on this knowledge. In this landscape where rain falls upward as often as down, where clouds form at ground level and water seeps from solid rock, someone must understand the logic of it all. The farmers know their lichens, the builders know their stone and wood, but I know the water—where it has been, where it wishes to go.

In the evenings, I return to my stone shelter. The constant drip-drip-drip from my ceiling into my basins is a lullaby. I prepare simple meals from the offerings the families leave for me—dried mushrooms, lichen cakes, fermented moss tea. As I eat, I update my maps, drawing the new water patterns I've observed, noting which stones have begun to dry and which have started weeping for the first time.

Sometimes the children come to learn. They sit at the entrance to my dwelling, not quite brave enough to enter the darkness where water drops echo like voices. I teach them to cup their hands just so, to catch the droplets without disturbing the flow. I show them the different types of moisture—the cold seep of deep-rock water, the warm condensation from sun-heated stone, the mineral-rich drips that leave white crusts on everything they touch.

But mostly, I am alone with the water and the stones. This suits me. In the profound quiet of this place, where even the wind whispers rather than howls, I have become a creature of patience and observation. My heartbeat has slowed to match the rhythm of the seeping water. My thoughts move like the moss, imperceptibly but inexorably.

When I die—and I will die, for even Keepers are mortal—my body will be placed in the Cave of Return, where the strongest springs originate. The water will work its slow transformation, and in a generation or two, I will emerge again from the weeping rocks as mineral-rich droplets, nourishing the moss and lichens, continuing my work in a different form.

Until then, of, and the steady, eternal conversation between stone and water that shapes this high, wet world.
