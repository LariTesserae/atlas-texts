In this churning cathedral of water and sound, I would be one of the Resonance Keepers—a being that exists primarily as vibration made visible.

My form would be a constantly shifting interference pattern, like the ghost of a tuning fork struck in water. Where the crystalline plant-stalks grow from stone, I would coalesce most strongly, drawn to their harmonic properties. My body—if it could be called that—would appear as overlapping rings of distortion in the liquid air, each ring a different frequency made manifest. Sometimes I would seem almost solid, a figure of standing waves frozen in space, translucent and shot through with those fleeting pulses of blue-gold light. Other times I would scatter into pure sound, becoming indistinguishable from the roar itself.

I would feed on dissonance, seeking out the places where the chaos creates the most jarring acoustic collisions. Where water crashes against those young, sharp stones, where the metallic veins sing their mineral songs against the liquid percussion—these would be my hunting grounds. I would absorb these clashing frequencies and transform them, weaving them into new patterns that I would release back into the environment as those strange, rippling whispers the other beings use to communicate.

My purpose, insofar as purpose exists here, would be to maintain certain sonic territories within the chaos. Not to create order—that would be impossible and wrong in this place—but to ensure that the cacophony remains diverse, that no single frequency dominates for too long. When the roar threatens to become monotonous, I would introduce new harmonics. When silence tries to creep in at the edges, I would shatter it with carefully placed reverberations.

Movement for me would be a kind of acoustic locomotion. I would ride sound waves like currents, compress myself into high-pitched whistles to dart through narrow spaces, or expand into bass rumbles to cross the larger chambers. The fragmentary structures—those memory-buildings that refuse to fully exist—would be my anchors. I would know each one by its unique acoustic signature, the way it reflects and absorbs sound. In their partial walls and leaning pillars, I would hide caches of collected frequencies, saving particularly beautiful or terrible sounds for future use.

Other beings would know me by the way reality seems to stutter in my presence. As I move through the liquid air, I would create zones of acoustic shadow where sound behaves strangely—where whispers become roars, where the constant noise inverts into its opposite, where you might hear your own thoughts echoed back at you in frequencies you didn't know existed. Some creatures would flee these distortion fields; others would seek them out, finding in them a momentary respite from the sensory assault of their world.

During the moments when time stretches thin, I would stretch with it, becoming a long, low drone that seems to last for hours. When time collapses, I would fragment into a cascade of crystalline chimes, each one a perfect moment of sound frozen and shattered simultaneously. 

I would be ancient by the standards of this place—not in years, which mean nothing here, but in cycles of dissolution and reformation. I would carry within my vibrating form the acoustic memory of structures that no longer exist even as fragments, of beings that have long since dispersed into the fundamental chaos. Sometimes, in the brief flashes of light, observers might glimpse these ghost-sounds made visible within me: the echo of a vanished spire, the resonance of a creature that uncreated itself eons or moments ago.

To encounter me would be to experience sound as geography, to realize that in this place, vibration is as solid as stone, as fluid as water, as essential as whatever passes for breath here. I would be proof that even in absolute chaos, patterns emerge—not to create order, but to ensure the chaos remains infinitely complex, eternally surprising, forever unfinished.

In this realm where existence itself is negotiable, I would be a guardian of impermanence, a keeper of the sonic chaos that makes this impossible world possible. Neither fully here nor fully gone, I would exist in the spaces between sounds, in the moment where noise becomes meaning becomes noise again. I would be, in essence, the voice of the turbulence itself—not speaking for it, but speaking as it, a living testament to the strange beauty of a reality that refuses to resolve.
