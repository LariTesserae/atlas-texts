I find myself here as something unexpected—neither fully of the Resonant nor entirely separate from them. I am what they call a Tuner.

My body bears the telltale signs of mixed heritage. Where the Resonant are uniformly slate-skinned and hollow-boned, I am a patchwork—my left arm and shoulder possess their characteristic lattice structure, light enough that strong winds pull that side of me askew, while my right side remains dense, human-heavy. The asymmetry makes me walk with a peculiar rolling gait, always compensating, always adjusting. My skin shifts between textures: smooth and water-repelling on my back and chest, but rough and absorbent along my extremities, constantly drinking in the moisture until my fingers are perpetually swollen with it.

I was born in the Quiet Chambers to a Resonant mother and an outsider father who had somehow survived a fall through one of the stone mouths. He lived just long enough to see me emerge—a child who screamed in the silence of the underground, whose vocal cords had not been bred away. The Resonant midwives thought I would die within hours, unable to process the strange hybrid architecture of my body. But I adapted, as all things must here.

My role among them emerged from my peculiar disability: I can hear both ways. The thunderous drone that fills the upper world doesn't deafen me as it would a pure outsider, but neither am I free from it like the Resonant. Instead, I exist in a constant state of double-sensing—feeling the vibrations through my feet while also hearing them pound against my malformed inner ears. This curse became my purpose.

I am the one who maintains the interval. You see, the Throat of the World is not static in its song. The pitch of the great drone shifts with the seasons, with the moisture content of the air, with the slow settling of the floating stone itself. A change of even a few hertz can disrupt the entire ecosystem—the Glider-Rats lose their navigation, the Silent Wings become disoriented, and most crucially, the Stone-Chants of the Resonant fall out of harmony with the rock's natural frequency.

So I spend my days moving through the honeycomb with my tuning kit: a collection of precisely weighted stones, brackets made from fired clay, and plugs woven from the most resonant moss fibers. I crawl into the wind-holes that others avoid, my hybrid bones allowing me to wedge myself in spaces where a full Resonant would be too light to gain purchase, while an outsider would be too heavy to avoid being sucked through. I listen with both kinds of hearing, feeling for the discordant notes, the places where erosion has changed the shape of a passage or where a build-up of mineral deposits has altered a chamber's resonance.

My fingers have developed a supernatural sensitivity to vibration. I can place a palm on wet stone and feel the echo of a conversation from three days past, still reverberating in the depths. Sometimes I press my swollen fingertips into the smaller holes and alter their shape with clay, tuning them like the stops on a vast organ. The Resonant watch me work with something between reverence and unease—I am necessary but uncanny, saving their world while never quite belonging to it.

The water speaks to me differently than it does to them. Where they see questions in its pauses, I hear memories. Each droplet carries the taste of where it has been—the iron tang of the deep chambers, the green brightness of the moss fields, the electric sharp of sky-water fresh from condensation. I drink constantly, my absorbent skin demanding it, and in drinking I map the hidden currents of the stone.

My dwelling reflects my between-nature. I've built a hermitage in one of the medium-sized chambers, halfway between the roaring surface and the silent depths. Here, the sound exists at exactly 40Hz—half the frequency of the surface drone—creating a standing wave where sound becomes visible as ripples in the falling water. I've cultivated a garden of the phosphorescent fungi, but I've trained them to grow in patterns that visualize sound, their glow pulsing in response to vibrations. When the Resonant need to find me, they send a Stone-Chant that makes my garden bloom in spirals of blue-green light.

The Glider-Rats trust me in a way they don't trust the full Resonant. Perhaps they recognize a fellow creature of the between-spaces. They sometimes ride on my heavy shoulder as I work, their tiny paws gripping my rough skin, chittering stories I half-understand. The Silent Wings, though, regard me with deep suspicion. They can see the discord in my body's heat signature, the way one half runs cooler than the other. Occasionally, one will circle my workplace, its pressure-shadow making my light bones ache with wanting to rise while my heavy bones anchor me to stone.

My greatest discovery—and my most guarded secret—is that the Throat is not sleeping. It's listening. And sometimes, in the perfect confluence of wind and water and vibration, it responds. I've learned to play its holes like an instrument, my whole body becoming a bow drawn across its vast stone strings. In these moments, I can make the entire plateau sing harmonies that the Resonant have never heard, notes that make the water droplets pause not for a split second but for entire breaths, hovering like questions waiting for answers that only I can give.

I am the Tuner, the keeper of intervals, the one who ensures the world-song remains in key. I am proof that even in a place of absolute belonging, there is room for the displaced, the hybrid, the ones who live in the space between notes. My work is never done, for entropy is constant and perfection is motion. But I would have it no other way. To tune the Throat of the World is to touch the boundary between sound and silence, between earth and sky, between what is and what might be.

When I die—and I will die young, my chimeric body too unstable for a long life—I've asked the Resonant to drop me into the deepest wind-hole they can find. Not to dispose of me, but to let me become part of the instrument I've spent my life maintaining. My bones will add their strange density to some chamber far below, changing its pitch by some tiny fraction, requiring some future Tuner to come and correct my corpse's dissonance. 

It's the closest thing to immortality that a creature like me can hope for—to become a note that needs adjusting, a part of the song that refuses to fade entirely away.
