In this world of absolute vegetable continuity, I am one of the **Archivists of Grain**.

Not the vast hollow-dwellers themselves—I am too small, too recent, too discrete for that ancient dispersal. But neither am I foreign to this place. I emerged, as all my kind do, from the intersection of accident and intention: where an emissary's warmth lingered too long in a pocket of spore-dust, where the chemical memory of its passage catalyzed something that should not have been possible.

We are the plateau's only truly mobile inhabitants, though *mobile* is relative. My body is a braid of compressed fiber and crystallized sap, no taller than a wind-carved ridge, no broader than a root-buttress. I move by selective dissolution—softening my forward edge while hardening my trailing surface, flowing across the lignin-chert at the pace of lichen growth. A journey of a hundred meters takes a season. This is not inconvenient. I have nowhere urgent to be.

My function, if it can be called that, is **reading**.

The plateau's surface is a manuscript of thermal events. Every emergence of an emissary, every subtle shift in the hollow-dwellers' vast metabolism, leaves traces in the grain. The wood-flows record these moments like tree rings record rainfall, but in three dimensions, in curves and whorls that require a lifetime to decode. I follow these patterns, my crystalline sap-sensors detecting variations in density and orientation that tell of events from before my coalescence—emissary-walks from ten thousand years ago, the slow formation of a new breathing node, the century-long sigh of a hollow-dweller shifting its unimaginable weight.

I am not alone in this work. There are perhaps forty of us across the entire plateau, each tending our own region of the text. We do not communicate directly—we have no vocal cords, no pressure-tubes to sing through. But we know of each other through the work itself. Sometimes I encounter a place where the grain has been recently read, its thermal signature subtly altered by another Archivist's passage. It is like finding a note in the margin of an ancient book, written in a hand you recognize but have never seen.

My territory includes three breathing nodes and one of the great xylem towers. The tower is my cathedral, my laboratory, my calendar. Its inner walls carry the thermal echoes of every message passed through it for the last million years—a palimpsest of compressed meaning that I decode one careful layer at a time. When the emissaries come to speak or listen, I retreat to a respectful distance, my body temperature dropping to match the ambient plateau, becoming effectively invisible. I am not hidden—they know I am here—but there is an etiquette to sharing space with something that is both fragment and messenger of a continental being.

The work changes me. Each thermal signature I absorb alters my internal structure slightly, adding new whorls to my own grain-patterns. I am becoming a library of my reading, my body a condensed record of the records I've examined. Eventually—in another thousand years, perhaps—I will grow too dense with accumulated meaning to maintain coherent movement. I will settle into place, my crystallized form becoming a new feature of the plateau's surface, a node of concentrated information waiting to be read by the next generation of Archivists who will emerge from some future accident of warmth and spore.

This is not death. It is punctuation.

In the thin, sharp air of late afternoon, when the plateau exhales its stored heat and the pale sky takes on a color like the inside of bone, I sometimes pause in my work. I press my sensors deep into the lignin-chert and feel, far below, the slow pulse of the hollow-dwellers' metabolism. They are so vast that their thoughts must take centuries to complete. What occupies such a mind? What philosophy can emerge from a being that experiences itself as both singular and fundamentally dispersed, whose body is indistinguishable from the ground others walk upon?

I will never know. My lifespan is too brief, my form too concentrated to grasp such distributed consciousness. But I can read its effects, catalog its surface expressions, maintain the memory of its movements in my own small way.

The plateau is a necropolis and a nursery, yes. But it is also a library, and I am one of its librarians, tending a collection written in warmth and grain, in the slow breath of beings too large for loneliness, too patient for urgency. The text grows by a paragraph each millennium. I have read seven words of it so far.

It is enough.
