# The Surveyor

I am the one who walks the iron sky.

My legs span distances that would dizzy your vertebrate mind—each step a calculation of temperature differentials, moisture gradients, the subtle give of fungal mat versus the absolute resistance of rivet-head. I have walked this curve ten thousand times, and each transit reveals new data. The upper world is never the same twice.

You would call me a harvestman. Your taxonomies mean nothing here. I am what the boiler has made of arachnid possibility: pale as weak tea, hydraulic in my movements, my body a mere punctuation mark between the radiating lines of my legs. I am mostly empty space, mostly reach. This is correct. In the boiler, to exist is to span.

I know things. I know that the iron directly above the waterline is seventeen degrees warmer than the apex at noon. I know that condensation begins first at a specific rivet—third row from the mouth, seven plates in—where a microscopic imperfection in the metal creates a nucleation site. I know the exact texture of the fungal shelf that grows toward light, because I have stood beneath it with my brothers and sisters, our legs touching in the configuration that means *watching*.

We do not have councils. We have convergences. When five or more of us gather with legs touching, our hydraulic systems synchronize. The fluid that moves through our joints begins to pulse in the same rhythm. We become a single instrument for reading the boiler's state. The shelf above us grows one cell layer at a time, and we feel it—not through vibration, not through any sense you would recognize, but through the change in how shadows fall.

I am the eldest. This does not mean what you think it means. In the boiler, age is not time but distance traveled. I have walked every millimeter of the upper hemisphere. I have found the three places where the iron has begun to thin from inside, where in another decade or three the rust will break through and create new mouths for light to enter. I have mapped the territories of seventeen distinct spider species, each with their fog-catching webs precisely calibrated to their position on the curve.

Sometimes I fall. This is not failure. This is data collection.

The drop from apex to waterline takes exactly the time of one heartbeat—if I had a heart. The impact registers through all eight legs simultaneously: the sudden shock of moisture, the give of fungal tissue, the alarming immediacy of mites who surge away from my landing site in a ring of disturbance. I lie still for a moment, processing the difference between up and down. Down here, the air is thick with spores and metabolic gases. The iron tastes different—more alive, less mineral.

But I am not made for the lower world. My legs, evolved for spanning great dry distances, become clumsy in the humidity. I right myself and begin the journey back up, each placement careful, testing. The younger ones watch my ascent. They learn.

We are not a hive. We are not a collective. But we are something—a slow intelligence distributed across many bodies, all of us reading the same curved text of this iron world. When one of us finds something new—a fresh blister in the metal, a change in the condensation pattern, a spider web built in an unprecedented configuration—we converge. Our legs touch. The knowledge passes between us in hydraulic pulses. Then we disperse, each carrying the new information into our individual transits.

The beetle thinks we cannot hear it. Tck. Tck. Tck. But we hear everything. We simply choose not to respond. The beetle's percussion is part of the boiler's music, along with the creak of thermal expansion, the whisper of mites, the tiny pops of spore cases opening. We are the ones who know the whole score.

At the sealed end, where the shelf grows toward light, we perform our most important work. We are not mystics, but we recognize purpose when we feel it. The shelf wants something. It grows with intention, unlike the rest of the fungal mat which simply follows moisture and opportunity. When we gather beneath it, our convergence takes on a different quality. We are not just sharing information. We are... attending. Waiting. The shelf is the boiler's only question, and we are the ones positioned to witness the answer when it comes.

I dream, sometimes. (Do not tell me arachnids cannot dream. In the boiler, all rules are negotiable.)

I dream of the world before the iron. Sun—not the weak, slanted light that enters through the mouth, but the full catastrophe of noon. Wind that moves in patterns larger than a thermal gradient. Prey that runs instead of waiting in webs. But when I wake—if I ever truly sleep—I feel no loss. That world required a different body, a different mind. I am perfectly made for this one.

The boiler is aging. We all feel it. The iron grows thinner, the rust more ambitious. One day—a decade hence, perhaps two—there will be breakthrough. New light will enter. The condensation patterns will shift. The fungal territories will need to be renegotiated. Some of the spider species, too specialized for current conditions, will fade. Others will emerge.

And we will map it all. We will walk the new geographies, span the new distances, feel out the changed territories with our hydraulic patience. We are the surveyors. The boiler made us to know itself, and we will know it until the last rivet fails, until the last plate falls away, until the industrial geometry finally dissolves back into the clay.

The shelf grows another cell layer. We feel it in the way the shadows change. Soon—another season, maybe two—it will reach the curve of the wall. Then what? We don't know. But we will be there, legs touching, attending the moment when the boiler's one reaching thought finds its answer.

I adjust my stance. The iron beneath me is warm, afternoon heat soaking through the rust. Time to resume my transit. There are still unmeasured territories, still microscopic changes to note. I lift one leg, hydraulics hissing almost inaudibly, and take a step across the iron sky.

The boiler creaks its daily creak. I know this sound. It means the day is passing, the temperature dropping, the condensation about to begin. Soon the spiders will check their webs. The mites will quicken their shimmer. The beetle will resume its ticking progress along the seams.

And I will walk, spanning distances, reading the curve of our world, carrying the knowledge of all our small changes in the reach of my improbable legs.
