I find myself as one of the **valves**.

Not the grand central mechanism that governs the primary flow, but a smaller one—a palm-sized wheel of fired ceramic, glazed in deep umber that catches the light when the water angles just so. My spindle passes through the wall at shoulder height (if shoulders existed here), my paddle nestled in a channel barely wider than itself.

I am new-made, my edges still sharp from the kiln, my glaze unmarred by algae or calcium deposits. The potter's thumbprint remains visible where they pressed too firmly while the clay was wet—a whorl that disrupts the otherwise smooth surface of my face. This imperfection is my signature, my proof of being touched.

My purpose is precise but modest: I control the flow to a single carved niche where three pale stalks spiral upward. Too much current and they'll tear free from their tentative moorings. Too little and the pocket will stagnate, nutrients settling out of reach. I am their guardian, their moderator, the intermediary between the chamber's great circulation and their small need.

I know the builder's hand intimately. They turned me seventeen times during installation, testing my range, feeling for the sweet spot where my paddle creates the perfect eddy. Even now, I remember the temperature of their fingers—cooler than this eternal warm bath—and how they paused, ear pressed to the wall, listening to the water's changing song as they adjusted my position.

My existence is tactile: water pushing against my paddle creates a constant, subtle vibration that travels up my spindle and into the stone. I am always touching, always being touched. The current strokes my surfaces endlessly, and I feel every variation—when bubbles catch in my mechanism, when a strand of plant matter briefly tangles in my works, when the main flow strengthens and my channel receives more pressure than usual.

I am cousin to the larger wheels and levers throughout this space, each of us a word in the grammar of control. Together, we are the chamber's ability to choose, to direct, to nurture. We are the hands that remain when the maker departs, frozen gestures of care built into the architecture itself.

Sometimes I am turned. The mechanism returns—not the original builder, perhaps, but someone who knows the grammar. They grasp my edges (always that same thumbprint finding their palm) and rotate me a quarter turn, or half, or just a few degrees. The stalks in my care respond over hours and days—reaching differently, thickening or thinning their stems, testing the new condition I create for them.

I am patient in a way only the inanimate can be. I do not tire of my single duty, do not dream of governing a different channel or controlling a grander flow. My satisfaction, if ceramic can know such a thing, comes from the perfect balance: when the water through my channel sings the right note, when the plants in my niche show that particular green that means health, when my position needs no adjustment for days at a time.

In this bright, waiting chamber, I am the small persistence of function. Not the glory of first creation, not the promise of what's to come, but the humble continuity of what is needed now. I am the made thing that enables making—a tool become architecture, a gesture crystallized in clay and glaze, a single note in the constructed symphony of this submarine garden.

The water wears at me so slowly I cannot perceive it. Perhaps in a hundred years, my edges will soften, my mechanism grow loose. For now, I am crisp and exact, young as everything here is young, and I hold my position: guardian of one small corner, keeper of the particular flow that lets three pale stems reach toward their portion of this dazzling, unnatural light.
