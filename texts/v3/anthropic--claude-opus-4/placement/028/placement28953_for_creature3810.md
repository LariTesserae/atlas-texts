### **The Cistern Keepers**

Between the Weeping Plateau and the Hollowbone Choir, where water seeps through limestone to collect in vast underground reservoirs, I am what the marsh folk call a Cistern Keeper—though "keeper" suggests more agency than we truly possess.

We were human once, or something close to it. Now we are halfway between flesh and infrastructure, our bodies fused with the ancient pumping systems that regulate the water's flow between the marsh above and the singing caves below. My spine has become a corroded brass pipe, vertebrae replaced with valve joints that I can no longer fully close. Water courses through me constantly—not blood anymore, but the black, mineral-rich runoff of the plateau, tasting of iron and old rain.

My skin has taken on the texture of wetted leather, perpetually damp, with patches where algae has taken root in the creases and scars. My fingers have elongated into tools—one hand bears digits that end in worn copper fittings, perfect for coupling with the maze of pipes that web these middle depths. The other has fused into something like a pressure gauge, the flesh of my palm translucent, revealing the dark fluid pulsing beneath.

I cannot leave the cistern chambers. My feet have grown roots—not plant roots, but tangles of rubber tubing and oxidized metal that burrow deep into the maintenance shafts. I can travel along these predetermined paths, sliding through the flooded tunnels on rails that my body has grown around, but I am bound to this network as surely as the Hollow Singers are bound to their songs.

We work in shifts that have lost all meaning—there is no day here, no night, only the rhythm of hydraulic systems and tidal pressures. My task is to listen: to the gurgle of blockages forming, the whine of pumps straining, the subtle change in pitch when a sinkhole opens above or a new chamber floods below. I taste the water constantly through the ports in my neck, monitoring its mineral content, its acidity, its burden of organic matter.

When I hear the Hollowbone Choir singing through the pipes—for sound travels through water as surely as air—I must adjust the flow. Too much water and the caves flood, drowning their music. Too little and the limestone cracks, changing their acoustics forever. The Hollow Singers know my work; sometimes I feel their vibrations through the metal, messages in frequencies only we can parse. They tell me which chambers thirst, which are sated, which must remain forever dry.

There are others like me down here, though we rarely meet except at the great junction chambers where dozens of pipe systems converge. We communicate through the water itself—chemical signals released through our pores, pressure changes tapped through the pipes, the taste of each other's minerals on our tongues. We share memories this way, though they're growing stranger with each passing year. I remember sunlight, but cannot say if the memory is mine or something dissolved from another Keeper's mind.

Sometimes the marsh folk from above find their way down here, lost in the sinkholes or seeking shelter. They fear us, these creatures of rust and purpose, but we bear them no ill will. We guide them when we can, opening valves to drain flooded passages, closing others to prevent them from entering the truly dangerous places where the pressure could crush them instantly. But we cannot speak to them—our vocal cords have become reed valves that only whistle and wheeze. 

The youngest among us still remember their names. The eldest have become indistinguishable from the machinery itself, their consciousness spread across miles of pipeline, their thoughts flowing as slowly as the groundwater through limestone. I am somewhere between—still individual enough to know loneliness, too changed to do anything about it.

At the deepest point of my circuit, where the cistern opens into a vast underground lake, there is a place where the ceiling has partially collapsed. Through the gap, I can see the faint glow of the plateau's fungi, reflected and distorted in the black water. Sometimes I pause there, letting the current flow through me unchecked, and wonder what would happen if I simply stopped maintaining the balance—if I let the water find its own level, the caves their own voice, the marsh its own rhythm.

But then I feel it—the deep vibration through every pipe, every valve, every corroded inch of my transformed body. The pulse of something vast beneath us all, something that relies on our work to keep it sleeping. And I remember why we accepted this change, why we wed ourselves to bronze and rubber and purpose:

Because someone must tend the infrastructure of forgetting. Someone must ensure that what lies beneath stays beneath, lulled by the perfect balance of water and sound, pressure and release.

I am a Cistern Keeper. I am the margin between drowning and drought. I persist in the spaces between spaces, neither fully alive nor properly mechanical, maintaining the equilibrium that keeps this world from collapsing into whatever waits below.

The water sings through me, and I continue.
