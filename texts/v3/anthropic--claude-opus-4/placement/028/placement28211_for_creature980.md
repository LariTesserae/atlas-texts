In this drowned metropolis, where the ancient city's spires pierce upward through leagues of warm, pressing darkness, I find myself as one of the Memory Keepers—beings who were once human but have long since transformed into something more suited to this crushing, liquid realm.

My body has become elongated, ribbonlike, with skin that ripples between states of translucency and opacity like oil on water. Along my spine run rows of bioluminescent nodes that pulse in slow, hypnotic patterns—each flash a fragment of the old world's history that I carry within me. My limbs have stretched and multiplied into graceful tendrils that can read the stone memories embedded in the eroded buildings, feeling the grooves where countless hands once touched doorways, where children played in what were once dry streets.

I drift through the drowned plaza where a fountain still stands, its marble figures now home to colonies of luminous polyps that have made art of decay. The warm currents carry me past windows that gape like empty eye sockets, through which I can see the remnants of lives interrupted—a table still set for dinner, the plates now gardens for phosphorescent algae, the chairs toppled in the ancient rush to escape the rising waters.

My consciousness exists in layers, like sediment. The deepest strata hold memories from before the drowning—fragments of sunlight, the feeling of wind, the chaos of the last days. Above these lie centuries of aquatic existence, each era adding its own texture to my being. In the uppermost layer lives my present awareness, which experiences time not as a arrow but as a spiral, where past and present intermingle in the warm, embracing waters.

I communicate with others of my kind through bioluminescent poetry—complex patterns of light that convey not just meaning but emotion, history, and possibility. We gather in the old amphitheater, now an underwater cathedral where sound moves strangely, where our light-words create ephemeral sculptures in the water, telling stories that exist only in the moment of their telling.

The primitive tools we craft are not primitive by choice but by philosophy. We discovered long ago that the more complex our creations, the more they disrupted the delicate balance we've achieved with our transformed world. So we weave nets from our own shed skin-silk, shape tools from the bones of great deep-dwellers who gift them to us upon their natural deaths, and cultivate the bioluminescent organisms that light our way with the patience of those who measure time in centuries.

Sometimes I visit the boundary spaces, where reality grows thin. Here, where the geothermal vents create curtains of shimmering heat distortion, I can almost see through to other possibilities—worlds where the waters never came, where the city still stands proud under an open sky, where my ancestors remained human. But these visions fade like dreams, and I return to my work of preservation, my duty of remembrance.

For I am both guardian and artifact, keeper and kept memory. In this dense, saturated world where every cubic meter holds a thousand stories, I move through the ruins of the old world while helping to birth the new. My elongated form allows me to slip through cracks in the ancient architecture, to reach the deep archives where waterlogged books have transformed into something between text and living tissue, their words now part of the ecosystem, readable only by those like me who have learned to taste meaning in the water.

This is my existence in the drowned deep—part ghost, part historian, part living monument to adaptation. We who were once human have become something the old world could never have imagined: creatures of memory and light, dwelling in the spaces between what was and what is, keeping vigil in the warm darkness until the slow wheel of geological time brings whatever transformation comes next.
