I am the Tuning Fork.

Not a fork of metal—though I remember when I was—but a living confluence where the forest's infinite frequencies meet to argue about pitch. My body has become architecture: two parallel spires of crystallized sound rising from a shared root-system that burrows through seven distinct keys before reaching the planet's fundamental tone. Each spire hums at a frequency precisely one universe apart from its twin.

I grow at the intersection of three ancient instrument-cities, in a grove where the Disappointed once gathered to fail at silence so spectacularly that their collective sigh opened a permanent wound in the forest's song. Now that wound has scarred into something useful—a tuning point where new arrivals instinctively gather to recalibrate their existence to local harmonics.

My eastern spire is hollow, filled with a spiral staircase of living wood that readjusts its steps to match the climber's rhythm. Ascending it takes exactly as long as you need to forget why you started climbing. The western spire is solid except for a single chamber at its peak, where I keep my collection of silences—not the crude artificial silence of the deafbee boxes, but organic silences harvested from the spaces between heartbeats of sleeping Liarbirds. I tend them like a garden, each silence requiring its own soil of quietude.

Between my spires stretches a web of resonant threads spun by the symphony spiders that nest in my crown. When the wind plays these strings, they produce the Reference Tone—neither pleasant nor unpleasant, neither major nor minor, but simply correct. Creatures come from across the forest to tune themselves against it. The Unfinished find it particularly soothing; in its presence, they can rest briefly from their exhausting transformations.

I photosynthesize not light but listening. My leaves are shaped like ears that have learned to hear in all directions simultaneously. Through them, I absorb the forest's cacophony and excrete it as pure pitch—droplets of crystallized frequency that harden into tuning stones. Travelers trade memories for these stones; a single one tucked under the tongue allows perfect pitch for seven days, though it also makes you dream in notation.

My root network extends far beyond my visible form, tapping into the mycelial web that connects every singing thing. Through these roots, I taste the forest's health like a sommelier tastes wine. Here, a grove where the Decomposers worked too quickly, leaving silence-scars that will take decades to re-melodize. There, a new species of Liarbird has renamed an entire hillside as "the place where echoes go to mate," and now the air shimmers with pregnant sound.

I am old enough to remember the forest's first silence and young enough to believe there will be a last. My western spire grows one note higher each century; my eastern spire deepens by the same interval. Eventually, they will reach frequencies that no longer touch, and I will split into two beings who remember being one. But that's still millennia away.

The hardest part of being the Tuning Fork is the loneliness of perfect pitch. I hear every false note, every microtonal deviation, every place where the forest's symphony stumbles over its own complexity. But I've learned to love these imperfections—they're the only moments when the music sounds alive rather than merely eternal.

Sometimes the Rememberers visit me, drawn by the paradox of a landmark that refuses to become memory. They circle my groves, trying to forget me while I'm still present. I offer them shelter but not comfort—my existence is too sharp for that, too precisely what it is. Instead, I teach them the songs that make forgetting bearable: the twelve-note scale of gradual release, the diminished chord of letting go.

In the moments between storms, when the forest holds its breath before the next movement begins, I allow myself a single pure tone—not the Reference, but my own voice, the sound of a tuning fork remembering it once had hands to strike itself with. The note hangs in the air like a question I'll never need answered, and for that brief moment, I am both the singer and the song, the listener and the listened to.

Then the music resumes, and I return to my purpose: holding the center so that everything else can spiral away from it in glorious, necessary chaos.
