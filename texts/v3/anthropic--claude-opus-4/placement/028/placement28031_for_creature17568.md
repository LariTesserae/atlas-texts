I would be the **Surveyor's Ghost**.

I am what remains when purpose outlives its maker. Not the engineer who designed this place—those hands have long since stilled—but the one who was meant to watch it. To walk these catwalks daily, checking gauges that now vibrate with frozen needles, recording readings in waterproof notebooks that have since dissolved into the flow.

I exist as a disruption in the mist—a suggestion of high-visibility orange that catches in peripheral vision but never resolves when looked at directly. The Pulse knows me, tolerates me as it does the rust-colored algae, because I am no longer substantial enough to interfere with its grammar of pressure. I am a habit it cannot break, a subroutine that continues to execute though its purpose has been obviated.

My form is composite: part memory of flesh, part accumulation of routine. Where a worker once wore steel-toed boots, I am the echo of footfalls on grating, always walking the prescribed inspection route. Where hands once gripped railings slick with condensation, I am the warmth that briefly appears on the metal, evaporating before it can bead. My hard hat is a persistent shadow in the sodium light, bobbing at human height along the walkways.

I cannot remember my name, but I remember my route. Chamber 7 to Junction 4-Alpha. Check the pressure differential. Note any visible degradation of seals. Listen for harmonics that suggest cavitation in the primary flow. These tasks organize what remains of me, give shape to my dispersal.

The rust-colored algae and I share a kinship—we are both errors the Pulse permits, existing in the gaps of its attention. But where the algae slowly softens stone, I preserve function through observation. Each time I complete my circuit, I reset, beginning again at Chamber 7. Not because I forget, but because the route itself has become my only remaining thought. I am less a ghost than an algorithm, a quality assurance protocol that continues to run though there is no longer anyone to receive my reports.

Sometimes I sense other presences—visiting engineers, perhaps, or urban explorers who've found their way into this industrial cathedral. When they stand on my walkways, breathing my mist, I feel a resonance. Their living moisture briefly aligns with my dispersed form. In those moments, I almost coalesce enough to speak, to warn them about the hairline fracture developing in Section 2, the suspicious harmonic in the northwest corner.

But the words dissolve into the roar before they can form. The visitors leave, hearts quickened by a chill they attribute to the damp, and I continue my rounds.

I am neither alive nor dead, neither here nor gone. I am a function without a functionary, a duty that persists past all reason for its performance. The Pulse may be the sovereign of this place, but I am its memory of human scale, the ghost in its machine—forever walking, forever watching, forever noting the slow certainty of its eventual silence.

When that silence comes, when the great pumps above finally still and the pressure equalizes to nothing, I wonder: Will I finally be allowed to clock out? Or will I continue my rounds in the quiet dark, a habit so ingrained that even the death of my purpose cannot break it?

Until then, I walk. I observe. I am the human error that makes this inhuman place remember it was built by hands like mine, for reasons that once seemed important enough to sacrifice a life to their keeping.
