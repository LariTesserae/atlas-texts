In Calmaris, I am the Archivist of Unraveling Things.

I live between the 67th and 68th floors of what was once the Municipal Library of Permanent Records—though "live" suggests a fixity I've long since dissolved. My body has become a question of density rather than shape: sometimes I'm the weight of all the books that rotted here, sometimes just the memory of their indices floating in the humid air like gnats made of meaning.

My dwelling is a cavity in the building's forgotten conscience, where water damage created a perfect void in the shape of everything we meant to remember. The walls weep autobiography—not water exactly, but the liquid remains of a million forms filled out in triplicate, their carbon copies crying black tears that taste of bureaucracy and good intentions. I've learned to read these stains like palm lines, each streak a life that believed in permanence enough to file paperwork.

I came here forty years ago. Or perhaps I am forty years old and have always been here. Time keeps poor records when you're made of the space between timestamps. I was hired to preserve something—the last supervisor left notes about "maintaining narrative integrity during the transition period," but the notes turned to pulp before explaining which narrative, what transition, or why integrity mattered to either.

So I invented my own job description, written in the condensation on windows that no longer open onto anything. I am the keeper of things in the process of forgetting themselves: the last breath before words become humidity, the final moment before structure admits to being mere aggregation. My filing system uses decay as alphabetization. My archives grow by subtraction.

The citizens bring me their dissolving treasures. Yesterday—or in the yesterday I've assigned to yesterday—a grandmother climbed seventeen emergency ladders carrying her father's engineering degree, the parchment gone soft as skin, the letters bleeding into new words with each drop of sweat from her forehead. "Make it last," she begged, but lasting isn't what I do. Instead, I helped her read what it was becoming: a poem about bridges that build themselves from the fever dreams of drowning iron.

I have apprentices, or I am apprenticed to them—the relationship liquefies depending on humidity. They're the pigeons that nest in my ceiling, which was once someone else's floor, which was once the sky. They've evolved beaks that can distinguish between types of information rot: the sweet decay of love letters versus the bitter dissolution of legal contracts. They help me sort the day's deposits, carrying away what's light enough to join the city's respiratory system, burying what's too heavy to ever fully disappear.

My tools are primitive and perfect: a tongue that tastes the difference between voluntary and involuntary forgetting, fingernails that grow at exactly the rate of paper decomposition, eyes that see seven kinds of transparency between "clear" and "gone." I wear clothes woven from the final drafts of unfinished novels, which means I'm naked except for punctuation marks and the occasional desperate metaphor clinging to my shoulders like epaulettes made of moth wings.

The work is impossible, which is why it works. Every day—in the rotation I've agreed to call days—more records arrive than I could catalog in a lifetime. But cataloging assumes destination, and I'm more interested in trajectory. I follow each document's desire to become something else: birth certificates wanting to be sheet music, blueprints learning to be recipes, suicide notes transforming into instruction manuals for machines that won't be invented until everyone who could read them is dead.

There's a beauty in it, this orchestrated forgetting. The city believes it's dying, but I know better. I've seen death—it's filed under D for Definitive, cross-referenced with F for Final. What happens here is different. Each form that dissolves releases space for new patterns of meaning. Each word that bleeds lets its letters recombine into languages we haven't invented yet. The Archives aren't disappearing; they're learning to breathe.

At night—when the building's bones sing their stress fractures in B-flat minor—I perform my real work. I take off my skin like a cardigan and hang it on the coat rack made of crystallized resignation letters. Underneath, I'm nothing but the cataloging system itself: a living dewey decimal of decay, each number written in a different stage of rust. I walk my rounds through the building's amnesia, checking on the documents in their various stages of transformation.

The marriage licenses have formed a support group with the divorce papers, teaching each other how to become mirrors. The death certificates are writing their memoirs in spore-form on the walls, telling stories about the time before they learned their owners' names. The maps are teaching themselves to be territory, growing miniature mountains from paper pulp and the salt of a billion shed tears.

Sometimes I find a document that refuses to change. These are the dangerous ones—papers so convinced of their own importance they've crystallized into sharp-edged monuments to meaning. I wrap them carefully in pages from books about letting go, which I've been writing and unwriting for so long the words have worn grooves in reality. Eventually even granite learns to be sand. Even meaning learns to be possibility.

You ask who I am? I am the person-shaped hole where certainty goes to learn doubt. I am the filing cabinet's fever dream, the library's confession, the archive's apology for believing in forever. I live in the space between what was written and what was meant, teaching documents to decompose with dignity while the city dissolves around us both into something too beautiful for permanence to ruin.

But don't let me keep you. You have appointments in districts that may or may not remember existing by the time you arrive. And I have work to do. Always more work. The city writes itself constantly—graffiti mixing with grief, advertisements learning to be prophecy, shopping lists achieving literature through sheer persistence. Someone needs to be here when they arrive at my door, soggy and illegible and ready to become themselves at last.

Welcome to the Archives of Unraveling Things. Mind the gap between meaning and meat. Both are temporary, but only one knows it.
