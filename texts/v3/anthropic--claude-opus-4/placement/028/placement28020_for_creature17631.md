In this drowning world, I find myself as something between keeper and kept—a **Cistern-Whisperer** of the A'shai, though the translation mangles what we truly are. 

My dwelling is neither platform nor chamber, but a **breathing space** carved into the heartwood of an ancient giant, three hundred feet above the black water. The tree welcomed this hollowing decades before my birth—my grandmother's grandmother sang to it for seven seasons until it agreed to the shaping. Now its walls weep a constant film of clean sap-water that I channel through hair-thin grooves into the dozen cisterns under my care.

I am unusual among my people, for my skin bears the memory of an old burning—patches where the green-grey mottling has given way to smooth, pale tissue like the underside of a mushroom. A childhood accident with heated resin-glass left me marked, but also gifted: where others see only the water's surface, I see its depth and memory. The cisterns speak to me of what has passed through them—the taste of a tree's illness twelve groves away, the mineral whisper of stone-fall in the distant heights, the brief electric tang that means the sky-swimmers have passed overhead.

My days are measured in water-songs. Each cistern holds a different tune: the high ceramic ring of the resin-glass catch-bowls on the canopy roads, the deep wooden drum of the burl-pools, the metallic ping of water striking the rare stone basin we inherited from the grove's previous singers. I know them all, can hear when a note shifts, when the harmony breaks.

But I am more than a listener. In the perpetual twilight of what others might call dawn, I practice the old art of **water-shaping**. With tools of polished jade—so rare that each bears seventeen names of previous keepers—I coax the flow into new patterns. A shift of a channel here, a fresh-carved spiral there, and the water learns new songs. The cisterns are not just storage but instruments, and I their player.

The solitude suits me. While the clan-platforms echo with the collaborative weaving of bridges and the communal songs of the root-shapers, I inhabit the quiet spaces between community and wilderness. My companions are the **Sip-Wings**—tiny birds no larger than my thumb, their feathers so fine they appear as mobile droplets of mercury. They nest in the gaps between my cisterns, their young fed on the insects that would otherwise cloud the water. We have an understanding: they announce visitors with their crystalline warnings, and I ensure their homes remain dry despite the eternal rain.

My dwelling extends beyond the tree-chamber. I maintain a network of **catch-threads**—spider-silk thin vines trained to direct specific types of rain to specific cisterns. The morning rain (lighter, carrying pollen) goes to the breeding pools where we cultivate the algae for medicine. The evening rain (heavier, mineral-rich from its journey through the canopy) feeds the deep storage. The storm-rain, when it comes, I channel away entirely—too violent, too full of broken things.

Once each season, I descend to the water itself. This is ritual, necessity, and terror combined. With my gill-slits fluttering open, tasting the tannin-rich flow, I swim the sunken paths between the root-buttresses. Here I check the great **foundation filters**—massive weavings of root and fungus that clean the water before it rises through capillary action into our living spaces. The work is crucial but dangerous; things live in the deep channels that have no names, only the spaces they leave in the water.

I carry three things always: a jade water-knife passed through forty generations, a flask of pure cistern-water that has never touched the ground, and a small sphere of resin-glass containing the only completely dry thing in our world—a seed from the mythical sun-lands, so ancient its species is forgotten. Some say it's only a polished stone, but I have held it to my ear in the deepest silence and heard something that is not rain.

My craft feeds the clan in ways they rarely see. The water I tend becomes their drinking supply, their cooking water, their ritual pools. But more than that, I maintain the memory of water—tracking its stories, its health, its warnings. When blight threatened the eastern groves three seasons ago, I tasted it first in the cisterns, read its signature in the chemical whispers of runoff. When the deep-channel hunters go missing, I often know which waters swallowed them by the fear-taste their last breaths leave behind.

The other A'shai see me at the season-turnings, when I emerge to sing the water-report in the great nave. My voice, trained by years of matching the cisterns' tones, can hold notes that make the rain itself seem to pause and listen. But mostly, I am a rumor, a shadow-shape glimpsed in the high branches, checking the flows.

I am keeper of the most patient art: teaching water where to rest, and when to move, and how to remember its journey from sky to root to sky again. In this drowning world, I am neither swimming nor sinking, but suspended in the space between, where the rain becomes resource, and every drop is a word in an endless liquid library that I alone can read.

They call us Cistern-Whisperers, but truly we are the forest's memory-keepers, writing history in water that will never stop falling, and reading futures in the taste of rain that has traveled through ten thousand leaves to reach our waiting bowls.
