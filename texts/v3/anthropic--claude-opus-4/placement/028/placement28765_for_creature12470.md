In this seeping hollow where earth and industry blur into one breathing organism, I would find myself as **the Salvage-Singer**.

I am neither fully of the mud nor the machinery, but something that has grown between them like a strange hybrid flower. My body is lean and angular, wrapped in layers of oilcloth and canvas that have taken on the colors of the hollow—rust-brown, moss-green, the deep gray of pooled water. These garments are stitched with wire and sealed with tar, creating a second skin that keeps the worst of the damp at bay while allowing me to move through both water and steam.

My hands are my most precious tools—long-fingered and sensitive, stained permanently with oil and earth. They know the weight of every type of metal, can feel the vibration of a pipe about to fail, can coax music from the most unlikely objects. Around my neck hang dozens of small instruments fashioned from salvage: hollow bones that whistle, coils of copper that hum, fragments of broken glass that chime. When I walk, I am a symphony of small sounds.

I live in a shelter I've built into the side of a collapsed culvert, where three large pipes converge. The space is cramped but dry, lined with salvaged rubber and insulated with moss that I've encouraged to grow in specific patterns. My collection fills every available surface—organized chaos of metal fragments, glass shards, ceramic pieces, each catalogued in my mind by their potential for sound.

My work is to sing to the hollow, and to sing *with* it. Each morning, I emerge before the mist lifts, when the Engine-Keeper's machinery settles into its steadiest rhythm. I know every sound this place makes: the particular gurgle of each pool, the specific creaks of each pipe joint, the way the wind changes its whistle depending on the humidity. I compose my songs around these sounds, weaving melodies that complement and enhance the hollow's natural music.

But I am also a translator. When the Pipe-Dwellers need to communicate with the Engine-Keeper, they tap out their messages on the pipes, and I interpret, adding my voice to create a language both can understand. When the Whispering Moss hums its ancient memories, I sit beside it for hours, transcribing its songs into new compositions that preserve the stories in forms the other inhabitants can comprehend.

The Mud-Walker and I have an understanding. Sometimes I follow in its wake, collecting the smaller artifacts it leaves behind—buttons, gears, fragments of pottery. These become part of my instruments. In return, I sing for it on the new moon nights, long wordless melodies that seem to ease something ancient in its form. During these concerts, even the Mist-Wraiths gather, their forms becoming almost solid as they sway to the rhythm.

My prized possession is a corroded brass horn I found half-buried near the brick building. Its valves are frozen, its tube dented, but when I blow through it during the dawn hours, it produces a sound like the earth itself groaning awake. The Engine-Keeper always pauses when hearing it, just for a moment, and in that pause I sometimes glimpse something almost like recognition in those weary eyes.

I document everything in a series of journals made from pressed moss-paper and bound with wire. My notation system is my own invention—part musical score, part map, part field guide to the sounds of the hollow. Each page is stained with mud and oil, annotated with pressed specimens of local plants, sketched diagrams of pipe configurations, and fragments of the songs I've learned.

The heron is my closest companion, though we rarely interact directly. It has learned my routines, knows that my morning circuit often disturbs small creatures from their hiding spots. In return, it warns me with a specific call when the mist is about to thicken dangerously, or when the ground has become unstable overnight. We are two observers, each cataloguing the hollow in our own way.

At night, I climb to the highest point I can safely reach—usually the top of a massive defunct water tank—and listen to the hollow's nightsong. The darkness brings new sounds: the deep thrumming of underground streams, the settling of metal as it cools, the whispered conversations of creatures I've never seen. I play response songs on my salvaged instruments, creating dialogues between myself and the living landscape.

I am the hollow's memory-keeper and its voice, transforming its industrial decay and natural persistence into something approaching art. My songs travel on the mist, carrying stories between the isolated inhabitants, creating connections where none existed before. In this place of constant transformation, I am both witness and participant, documenting the endless negotiation between what was, what is, and what might yet become.

Some nights, when the mist is thick and the machinery hums in perfect rhythm with the earth's breathing, I feel the hollow singing through me rather than the other way around. In those moments, I understand that I am not just an inhabitant or observer—I am another instrument in its vast, ever-changing composition, my voice just one thread in the complex harmony of this strange, living threshold between worlds.
