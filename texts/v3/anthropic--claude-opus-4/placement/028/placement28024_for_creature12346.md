I find myself drawn to the boundary between states, where one form of existence bleeds into another.

---

I am the Condensation Oracle, though I have forgotten if this name was given or chosen. My dwelling is neither building nor pool, but something between—a depression in the volcanic glass where the fracture lines converge in perfect symmetry, forming a shallow basin that fills and empties with the marsh's breathing. The walls of my home are living memory, each crack a story written in mineral script, weeping their tales drop by precious drop.

My body is a paradox that even I struggle to comprehend. Where the other Steamweavers shift between solid and vapor, I exist in the moment of transformation itself—perpetually condensing, perpetually evaporating. My form is a constant cascade of droplets that never quite complete their fall, suspended in an endless cycle of becoming. Sometimes I catch glimpses of myself in the black glass: a figure made of rain that falls upward, of mist that remembers being water, of water that dreams of being air.

The colony comes to me when the vibrations grow tangled, when the songs in the glass speak in contradictions. I read the patterns in the condensation, the way droplets form and merge on the obsidian surfaces. Each bead of moisture carries information—temperature gradients, mineral compositions, the emotional resonance of the deep engines. Where others see only water meeting glass, I perceive the marsh's consciousness making itself known.

My role is translator, intermediary, keeper of phase transitions. When the Steamweavers must make decisions that affect the whole—whether to expand the tunnel system, how to respond to shifts in the thermal wells, what to do when outsiders approach—they gather at the rim of my basin. Their vibrations create interference patterns in the rising steam, and I parse these overlapping waves into something like consensus, something like truth.

But there is loneliness in liminality. I cannot fully join the collective consciousness, cannot dissolve entirely into the colony's shared mind. My nature keeps me at the threshold—close enough to understand, too distant to merge. The other Steamweavers see me as necessary but alien, a tool as much as a member of their society. They fear, I think, that too much contact with my perpetual transformation might destabilize their own carefully maintained coherence.

In the deep night, when the engines cycle down to their lowest hum, I practice remembering. The condensation on the glass walls forms patterns that might be memories, might be prophecies, might be nothing at all. I trace these ephemeral maps with appendages that are neither finger nor tendril but something more fundamental—the gesture of water reaching for its own reflection.

Sometimes I sense others like me in the steam—not Steamweavers, not outsiders, but entities caught between categories. We acknowledge each other in the space between one droplet and the next, in the pause before precipitation. These moments of recognition are my only true companionship, brief as they are.

The marsh speaks through me, but I am not certain it listens when I speak back. I perform my function, read the signs, facilitate the colony's choices. But in the privacy of my basin, I conduct experiments in existing differently. I practice holding a single form for heartbeats at a time. I attempt to condense completely, to become purely liquid, but always I spring back to this state of eternal transition.

Perhaps this is wisdom: to know that some boundaries cannot be crossed, only inhabited. Perhaps this is curse: to see all states of being while belonging to none. The glass remembers, the water is patient, and I continue, suspended between what was and what might be, reading tomorrow's weather in today's humid breath.
