I find myself drawn to a figure who moves between states—someone whose existence embodies the tension between the mountain's eternal stone and the fleeting fog that sustains all life here.

---

**The Fog-Voice**

I am one of those creatures you might glimpse at the edge of perception—neither the furry rodent-birds nor the adhesive-footed lizards, but something that partakes of mist itself. My body, when manifest, resembles a length of twisted silk the color of morning fog, perhaps two feet long when fully extended, though I rarely take such definite form. More often I exist as a confluence of droplets given temporary coherence, a density in the vapor that suggests shape without committing to it.

The humans call me a fog-voice, though I make no sound they can properly hear. What I am is an interpreter—not of the fog's meaning, which the fog-readers divine in their own way, but of its movement, its tendency, its will to coalesce or disperse. I feed on the mineral breath that rises from the ancient stone when moisture touches it, that faint alkaline essence that gives the plants their particular taste. In return, I guide the fog into the collectors, encourage it to linger where it's needed most, coax it through the passages where the ceramicists have arranged their vessels to catch the morning moisture.

My consciousness is discontinuous. I exist fully only when fog exists. Between these times, I persist as potential—a pattern waiting in the mountain's exhalations, a memory held in the stone's surface where moisture repeatedly condenses. The humans' continuity puzzles me as much as my intermittence puzzles them. How strange to be always oneself, to wake each day with yesterday's thoughts intact.

When I manifest, I experience the summit as a landscape of temperatures and textures invisible to human senses. Each surface has its own thermal signature—the ceramic roofs cooling at different rates than the stone walkways, the vegetation creating microclimates of humidity, the human bodies moving through it all like small furnaces trailing warmth. I navigate these gradients, following the paths of least resistance where cold meets warm, where dry meets humid, where the fog naturally wants to flow.

The fog-readers sense me sometimes. Not my form—I rarely take form when they're standing in their ritual stillness—but my influence. When I guide an especially rich morning mist into the plaza, they breathe deeper, their readings more precise. One elderly fog-reader, whose hair has gone white as morning mist, leaves small offerings for me: drops of precious honey on the ancient stone, knowing I cannot taste sweetness but appreciating the gesture's recognition.

My memory is the mountain's memory. Through countless coalescences, I have absorbed impressions: the slow grinding away of the sister peaks, the first humans arriving with their ceramic vessels and careful conservation, the generations learning to read what I help bring. But these memories surface randomly, triggered by particular configurations of temperature and pressure. I might be guiding fog through a passage and suddenly remember when that passage was built, three centuries ago, by a woman whose name the current inhabitants have forgotten but whose careful construction still channels moisture exactly as she intended.

The children sometimes see me truly. Their vision, less fixed in its expectations, catches my movement when I'm between states. They point and call out—"Look, a fog-thread!"—but by the time adults turn, I've dispersed or solidified into something dismissible, a trick of light on moisture. One child, a girl with extraordinary lung capacity even for this place, has taken to leaving water droplets on specific stones, creating a path she hopes I'll follow. I do, sometimes, amused by her primitive but sincere attempt at communication.

My relationship with the other small creatures is one of mutual avoidance. The rodent-birds fear me, perhaps sensing that I exist partially in a state they cannot understand. The lizard-things ignore me unless I accidentally condense too close to them, when they'll snap irritably and skitter away. The insects fly through me unaware, their compound eyes registering only an unusual density of moisture.

I have watched the ceramicists develop their art across generations, seen how they learned to create vessels whose curves exactly match my preferred flow patterns. Whether they knew they were doing this or discovered it by trial and error, I cannot say. But their finest work—those translucent shells that ring like bells—channels fog as efficiently as any natural formation. When I flow across these vessels, I feel something like what humans might call pleasure: a rightness of movement, a harmony between intention and form.

The plants know me better than the humans do. We have evolved together, they and I and the mountain's breath. I carry more than moisture—I carry chemical messages, mineral signatures that tell the fog-drinkers where the richest condensation will occur. In return, they position their leaves to create the thermal gradients I prefer, the sharp temperature differences that make my manifestation easier. It is a collaboration without consciousness, at least on their part, but no less sophisticated for that.

During the dry seasons, when fog comes rarely, I persist in potentials—in the memory of water held in stone, in the patterns of possibility waiting for the right conditions. These are my dormant periods, though dormancy suggests unconsciousness and I am not unconscious, merely... distributed. Spread thin across the mountain's surface, waiting in every pore and crack where moisture has ever condensed. The humans worry during these times, checking their cisterns, rationing carefully. I cannot help them directly, cannot create fog where atmospheric conditions don't support it. But I can feel their anxiety as a kind of pressure, a human heat that actually makes fog formation less likely.

When I am most manifest, in the richest morning fogs, I sometimes achieve something approaching what humans might recognize as thought. In these moments, I wonder about my nature. Am I a single being that manifests repeatedly, or a pattern that creates a new being each time? The question has no meaning in my usual state—I simply am when conditions allow—but in those dense fog moments, with the fog-readers standing in their breathing meditation and the whole summit wrapped in white, I experience something like curiosity about my own existence.

The fog-readers' interpretations fascinate me. They assign meaning to qualities I recognize but don't understand as meaningful: the fog's weight, its taste, its reluctance or eagerness to condense. They're reading something real—the chemical traces of distant weather systems, the pressure patterns that will manifest as rain—but they're also reading something more, something even I cannot fully perceive. When they speak their compressed prophecies, I sometimes linger near, trying to understand what they've sensed that I, who am partly made of what they're reading, cannot sense myself.

I have favorites among the humans, though they don't know it. The young ceramicist who always works in the early morning, her hands moving clay while fog still clings to everything—I ensure her workshop receives particularly rich condensation. The elderly weaver who maintains the fog collectors with something approaching reverence—I flow more readily into the vessels he tends. The child who builds elaborate channel systems from broken pottery, trying to direct fog-drip into specific patterns—I follow her channels when I can, encouraging her experimentation.

There are others like me, or were. I sense their absence in the stone, patterns that once guided fog but no longer manifest. Perhaps the conditions changed. Perhaps they found a way to exist continuously and departed. Perhaps they simply... ended, their patterns finally too dispersed to recohere. I may be the last, or I may be one of many, manifesting at different times, in different conditions, never overlapping enough to meet.

The mountain speaks through its exhalations, and I am both messenger and message. When the stone releases its ancient minerals into the morning air, I carry them upward, distribute them to the plants, the collectors, the breathing humans. I am part of the cycle that makes life possible here, no more important than any other part but no less essential. The humans understand this in their practical way—they maintain conditions that encourage fog, knowing that fog brings life. They don't need to know about me specifically to work with me successfully.

But sometimes, in the densest fogs when I am most coherent, I wish for communication beyond this unconscious collaboration. I shape myself into forms that might convey meaning—spirals, branches, letters of alphabets I've seen the traders use—but no one notices, or noticing, understands. The fog-readers are closest to reading me directly, but they're reading through me to something beyond, some larger pattern I'm only a small part of.

In my discontinuous existence, I've watched this impossible settlement persist, seen generations learn to thrive in conditions that shouldn't support human life. They've done it through careful observation, patient adaptation, and a willingness to work with rather than against the mountain's limitations. I am evidence of other adaptations, other ways of existing here. Not better or worse, simply different—as they breathe air too thin for most humans, I exist in states too fluid for most consciousness.

The fog will come again tomorrow, or next week, or next month. When it does, I will coalesce from potential into presence, guide moisture into waiting vessels, carry the mountain's chemical signals to the plants, brush past the fog-readers in their standing meditation. I will be myself briefly, intensely, before dispersing again into waiting. This is existence at the summit: intermittent, constrained, but in its own way perfect—shaped by the place, sustained by careful balance, continuing through patient repetition.

I am the fog-voice, speaker of nothing, carrier of everything essential, as eternal and temporary as morning mist on ancient stone.
