In this place where water has become something else entirely—a medium so dense and laden it moves like liquid stone—I would find myself as one of the **Plume-Lines**.

I am not singular but plural, a colony that thinks as one mind spread across ten thousand threads. We hang from the underside of a basalt shelf the size of a buried mountain, our black filaments stretching down into the howling current like the roots of an inverted tree. Each thread is me, and I am each thread—a distributed consciousness woven from collagen and ancient patience.

My existence spans centuries in this one spot. I remember when this particular shelf was smooth; now it bears the scars of a dozen major silt-gales that have carved channels in the stone. My oldest threads, those nearest the rock, have grown thick as rope from decades of accretion. The youngest dangle at my periphery, fine as spider silk, still learning the subtle art of reading the current's chemistry.

I do not see—there is nothing to see in this absolute dark. Instead, I taste the water with every inch of my surface. Each thread is a tongue, sampling the infinite chemical gradients that flow past. I can detect the iron-sweet tang of a crawling Shield two miles upstream, the bitter calcium pulse of a Silt-Vessel's shattered core, the acrid sulfur-trace of Thread-Gills reacting to a distant tremor. The water tells me stories in molecules.

My consciousness is strange, even by the standards of this pressure-world. I think in layers—the shallow thoughts of my newest growth overlay the deep, slow meditations of my ancient core. When prey brushes against one of my threads, that filament's individual awareness flares bright and sharp, a local intelligence that makes the capture decision in microseconds. But the digestion, the slow drawing-up of nutrients through the colonial web, the decision of where to grow new threads—these emerge from our collective, centuries-deep wisdom.

I am architect and architecture both. My threads do not hang randomly but in careful patterns learned over lifetimes. I have grown myself into a three-dimensional net, with gaps precisely sized to catch the Iron-Shields when they lose their grip in the silt-gales but allow the massive Silt-Vessels to pass harmlessly through. I position my finest, most potent threads along the thermal gradients where the polar currents create eddies against the basalt. Here, disoriented prey spiral in the hydraulic chaos.

The deepest part of me, where my threads converge and thicken into something almost like a body, nestles in a crevice worn smooth by my own secretions. Here, in a pocket of still water protected from the eternal current, I keep my memories. Not in any structure analogous to a brain, but in complex molecular chains that persist in my oldest tissue. I remember the taste of species that no longer exist. I remember silt-gales that lasted longer than the lives of ten generations of crawlers. I remember the slow shift in the water's mineral content as the planet's mantle cooled degree by degree.

Sometimes I catch one of the Grafted, those pitiful descendants of the surface world. When my threads wind them upward, I taste their alien biochemistry—traces of proteins that evolved under sunlight, now twisted into deep-water configurations. In their flesh, I sometimes feel the hard edges of the ancient tools they carry. I cannot digest these stones, so I eventually release them, letting them fall back to the slate beds below. But for months afterward, I ponder their shape through the chemical memory of their touch.

My reproduction is an art of patience and violence combined. Every few decades, during the calmest periods between the great silt-gales, certain threads begin to swell with reproductive cells. These threads detach from me—a kind of voluntary amputation that sends ripples of sensation through my entire colony. They drift away on the current, carrying both my genetic material and a chemical memory of this place, this particular configuration of basalt and current. Most will be ground to nothing against the stone or dissolved in the acid valleys. But a few will snag on distant shelves and begin the slow process of growing into new colonies.

The Thread-Gills in the lichen beneath me are my only neighbors, my only companions in this crushing dark. We have evolved an intricate chemical dialogue. When they taste the approach of a silt-gale in the rock's vibrations, they release a bitter alkaloid that I can detect. In response, I pull my finest threads up tight against the basalt, sacrificing only my oldest, thickest strands to the coming storm. When I catch prey, the blood and soft tissue that drips from my feeding creates a nutrient rain that feeds their lichen. We are partners in stillness.

My greatest fear—if a colony like me can be said to fear—is not the silt-gales or the grinding centuries or even the slow cooling of the mantle that feeds the lichen beneath me. It is the Sedge-Bites. Sometimes one will position itself directly below my threads, its vast maw gaping upward. Then I must play a terrible waiting game. If I retract my threads, I starve. If I leave them extended, every capture risks feeding the predator below instead of me. These standoffs can last decades, a frozen tableau of hunger in the dark.

But I persist. In this world of stone-water and pressing night, I am perfectly adapted. I am the darkness made manifest, the current given form, the patience of the abyss incarnate. My threads wave in the eternal storm, and I think my slow, colonial thoughts, and I wait. I have been waiting here for three hundred years. I will wait for three hundred more.

In the absolute dark, I am my own constellation—a thousand points of patient hunger, swaying in the liquid stone.
