In the Resonance Wetlands, where time pools like water in the hollows of reality, I would be a **Tidescribe**—one of the solitary beings who chart the temporal currents through a practice both ancient and impossible.

My dwelling would be a stilt-house of woven reeds that exists in a state of constant construction. The structure grows itself from the quantum substrate, walls breathing as they add new layers while simultaneously aging into silver weathered strips that fall away into the dark water below. The house follows me as I pole through the channels on my shallow craft—not by moving through space but by having always been where I'm going, waiting in probable futures for me to arrive.

My body would have adapted to this existence over decades of immersion. My skin would shimmer with the same oily iridescence as the water's surface, a side effect of the microscopic machines that have colonized my cells. They allow me to perceive the temporal layers directly—when I look at the water, I see not just its current state but the ghostly overlays of its past and future configurations. Rain that will fall next week creates preliminary ripples that only I and the Chronoherons can detect.

My work would be the creation of the Tide Charts—living documents written on paper made from pressed temporal reeds that age and renew themselves as you read. Using inks extracted from probability fish and quantum insects ground into pigments that exist in superposition, I would map the ever-shifting channels of the wetlands. But these are not ordinary maps. They chart when as much as where—showing not just the physical pathways through the marsh but the temporal currents that flow through them. A channel that leads east on Tuesday might flow backward through time on Thursday. A grove that exists in the morning could be a clear pool by afternoon, not through any physical change but through the rotation of probable states.

My tools would be simple but profound: a pole made from the heartwood of a spiral tree that exists in exactly one temporal state, allowing me to push against the quantum uncertainty of the water. A net woven from the silk of moths that pupate in the past and emerge in the future, perfect for catching the temporal parasites that sometimes clog the waterways. Most importantly, a set of tuning forks crafted from the shell-plates donated by the Confluence itself, each one resonating at the specific frequency of a different temporal layer.

I would spend my days poling through the channels, stopping at points where the temporal currents eddy and whorl. Here I would lower my instruments into the water—not to measure depth but to measure when. The tuning forks would sing their quantum songs, and I would transcribe the harmonies onto my charts, creating a musical notation that captures the temporal topology of each location. Where the currents run particularly strange, I would plant marker reeds that grow through multiple timestreams, their growth patterns serving as warnings to future travelers.

The Echo Children would be my most frequent companions. They understand my work intuitively, sometimes guiding me to places where the temporal flow has become knotted or where new channels have opened between moments. Their ghostly fingers would point out details I might miss—the way a particular grove ages in reverse every third day, or how certain pools connect to themselves through loops in causation. In return, I would teach them songs from the world beyond the wetlands, melodies that they incorporate into their eternal games, adding new layers to the acoustic environment.

My relationship with the other inhabitants would be one of mutual utility. The Chronoherons would share their observations of the temporal weather—when storm-systems of causality might disrupt the normal flow. The Moss Shepherds would consult my charts when planning their maintenance routes through the wetlands. Even the probability fish would benefit, as my maps would show them the richest feeding grounds where temporal algae blooms in abundance.

At night, I would return to my reed house wherever it happens to be waiting for me. Inside, the walls would be covered with generations of charts—some freshly drawn, others ancient beyond measure, all existing simultaneously in layers of transparent time. I would update the master chart, a massive document that attempts to capture the wetlands in their totality, though I know this is impossible. The wetlands change faster than any chart can capture, and the act of mapping them changes them further.

My diet would consist of things that exist in temporal flux—eggs from the Chronoherons that hatch backwards into yolk, shoots from plants that grow into their seeds, water filtered through reeds that remember rain from centuries past and futures yet to come. This diet would further attune me to the temporal currents, making me less solidly anchored in any single moment.

Sometimes, the Visitors Who Stayed would seek me out, desperate for charts that might lead them back to linear time. I would provide what guidance I can, though we both know that anyone who has dwelt this long in the wetlands carries the temporal current within them now. The best I can offer is safe passage to places where the effect is minimized, where they might live something closer to a normal life, though they will always hear the wetlands' song in their dreams.

My greatest work would be the Deep Charts—maps not just of the wetlands' surface but of the vertical temporal currents that flow from the quantum machinery at the marsh's foundation up through the probability clouds above. These three-dimensional documents would be written in inks that phase in and out of visibility, requiring readers to move through time as well as space to perceive the whole. They would reveal the true shape of the wetlands—not a place but a process, a vast engine for the preservation and transformation of time itself.

In quiet moments, poling through channels where the temporal current runs smooth and linear, I would wonder about my predecessors. Surely I am not the first Tidescribe—the role feels too necessary, too integrated into the wetlands' function. I would find traces of their work sometimes: ancient charts in bottles that age and grow young again, marker reeds planted in patterns that suggest intentionality, channels that seem deliberately maintained rather than naturally formed. Perhaps the Moss Shepherds were once Tidescribes who stayed too long, their purpose growing into their bones until they became part of the landscape they once merely mapped.

This fate doesn't frighten me. To become one with the temporal flow, to have my charts grow directly from my transformed flesh like the Moss Shepherds' covering—there are worse endings for a cartographer of impossible places. Until then, I pole through the channels, tuning fork singing against the quantum currents, adding my voice to the wetlands' eternal symphony while I chart the unchartable, map the unmappable, and find my way through places where all ways lead to when.
