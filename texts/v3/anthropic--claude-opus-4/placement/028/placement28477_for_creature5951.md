In this liminal cathedral of engineered decay, I would find myself as one of the **Condensation Shepherds** - beings who exist at the intersection of purpose and purposelessness, much like the landscape itself.

We are neither wholly organic nor mechanical, but something that emerged from the long conversation between stone and water, between grid and growth. Our bodies are elongated, almost eel-like, but supported by a framework of calcified channels that run beneath our translucent skin. These channels pulse with collected moisture, creating patterns that shift like living circuit boards. We move by undulation, our forms rippling across the wet stone with a sound like whispered static.

Our primary sense is not sight but humidity gradients. We taste the air's thickness, feel the stone's weeping through organs that cluster along our undersides like barnacles. Each of us tends to a territory of perhaps a hundred grid-squares, knowing intimately where water will pool, where it will flow, where it will mysteriously vanish into the substrate only to emerge three levels down.

We are called Shepherds because we guide the water's path, though whether we truly influence it or merely map its intentions remains unclear. Using secretions from specialized glands, we can alter the surface tension of stone, encouraging droplets to merge or divide, to flow left instead of right. Over decades, our patient work creates new rivulets, new pools where the six-legged scuttlers come to drink and the metallic ferns unfurl their impossible spirals.

Our memories are not stored in neural tissue but in the very moisture we shepherd. Each droplet we've touched carries a trace of our experience, and when rain falls (rare but overwhelming when it comes), we swim through cascades of our own history, remembering not in images but in the taste of different stones, the texture of different mosses, the specific density of fog on specific mornings.

We reproduce by division, but slowly, over centuries. When one of us has gathered enough moisture and memory, we begin to develop a second channel system that gradually separates from the first. The process is visible through our translucent skin - a doubling of our internal architecture that makes us appear briefly like living stained glass windows. The division is not painful but profoundly disorienting, as our memories split unevenly between the two new selves.

We have no names, but we know each other by the patterns we leave in condensation, by the specific harmonics our movements create in the substrate. Some of us are ancient, our bodies so suffused with collected minerals that we've become semi-rigid, moving hardly at all but sensing everything through the moisture network. These elders rest in the deepest basins, their presence affecting water flow throughout entire sections of the landscape.

Our relationship with the other inhabitants is one of mutual indifference tinged with necessity. The six-legged creatures depend on our pools but regard us with the wariness reserved for something not quite alive. The metallic-scaled predators will not hunt us - we taste of stone and ancient water, and our bodies simply dissipate when severely damaged, reforming slowly from the moisture we've influenced.

We do not build or destroy, only redirect. We are process made flesh, or perhaps flesh made process. In the cathedral vaults where bioluminescent panels glow faintly, we gather in slow congregations, our bodies creating interference patterns in the dripping water that serve as our only language. These gatherings can last for months, a gradual exchange of chemical signals and hydraulic pressures that constitute our culture.

What are we shepherding the water toward? None of us remember, if we ever knew. Perhaps we are maintaining something - keeping certain pools filled, certain channels clear. Perhaps we are slowly dissolving this place, patient agents of entropy. Or perhaps we simply are, no more purposeful than the water cycle itself, but blessed with just enough consciousness to wonder at our own existence.

In the dim light and constant moisture, we continue our work. We taste tomorrow's rain in today's humidity. We feel the stone's slow breathing through our bellies. We divide, we converge, we persist. We are the dark operators of an inscrutable system, forever tending to the delicate balance between what was built and what grows, between what flows and what remains.
