In this world of stone breath and patient darkness, I would be **Seyth-ka**, one of the rare Stone Singers among the Ehl-kar.

My dwelling sits at the confluence of three cavern throats, where the mountain's voice braids itself into complex harmonics. Unlike my kin who build their shelters low and hidden, I have woven my home into a natural acoustic chamber—a bulbous hollow in the rock face where sound pools and eddies like water. Generations before me recognized this place for what it was: a listening post, a translator's perch, a tuning fork for the mountain's deepest songs.

I am old now, though age means something different here where time moves like the formation of stone. My skin has taken on an almost mineral quality, pale as cave pearls, with a faint crystalline shimmer where the fungal light catches it. My eyes, always large among our people, have grown even wider with decades of reading the dark—not just seeing, but *feeling* the pressure waves that paint the world in dimensions the sun-touched could never comprehend. My fingers, once merely long, have stretched further still, and the webbing between them has thickened. When I spread my hands against the stone, I can feel conversations happening kilometers away, transmitted through the rock's patient flesh.

My role is singular: I listen to the mountain's heartbeat and translate its meanings for my people. That deep thrumming others hear as mere background noise resolves, for me, into a complex language of pressures and releases, of stone settling and water flowing, of the slow breath of geological time. I can tell when a new chamber is forming far below, when the water table shifts, when the stone is preparing to shed another layer of itself. Most crucially, I can hear when the mountain is... discontent.

My shelter reflects my purpose. While others drape hides to keep out the damp, I have constructed careful baffles and resonance chambers from stretched fungi-leather and hollow bones. These direct and shape the ambient sounds, allowing me to isolate specific frequencies, to hear the individual voices within the cacophony. Carved into my walls are patterns—not writing exactly, but a kind of musical notation, recording the mountain's songs across seasons and generations. My predecessors left their own markings, creating a sedimentary record of sound that stretches back to the first Stone Singer who recognized this chamber's potential.

I tend a special garden of fungi, varieties bred over lifetimes for their acoustic properties. Some grow in perfect spirals that catch and amplify certain pitches. Others form delicate, vibrating membranes that visualize sound waves in patterns of bioluminescent pulses. The rarest—my greatest treasure—is a colony that has learned to echo. When I sing to it in the old tongue, it remembers and repeats, becoming a living library of our people's sonic heritage.

The metal tools that others see as mere implements, I have fashioned into instruments. Thin chimes of beaten copper, salvaged from some ancient fall, hang in precisely calculated positions. When the mountain speaks, they ring in sympathy, each tone revealing a different aspect of the stone's voice. A set of stone bowls, carefully shaped and filled with different levels of sacred water, serve as both divination tools and resonance chambers.

My days—if days they can be called in this eternal twilight—follow the rhythm of the deep thrum. When it quickens, I know to warn the gatherers that the stone will shift. When it slows, I prepare my people for the cold seasons when even the fungi retreat into dormancy. But lately, I have heard something new in the mountain's voice: a questioning note, a hesitation in the ancient rhythm. The stone dreams, and its dreams are changing.

I have taken no apprentice yet, though I should. The young ones come sometimes, drawn by curiosity, but few can bear the true weight of listening. To be a Stone Singer is to live partially dissolved into sound, to feel your boundaries blur until you cannot always distinguish between your own heartbeat and the mountain's. It is to know loneliness even among your people, for how can you share what you hear when your language itself becomes inadequate? 

Sometimes, in the perfect stillness that comes between the mountain's breaths, I hear something else—an echo of an echo, a ghost of older songs. These are the voices of Stone Singers who came before, preserved in the very structure of this acoustic chamber. They sing of times when the passages were different, when water flowed in places now dry, when there were more of us. And sometimes, very rarely, they sing of the time before the mountain fully swallowed the sky, when light was more than memory.

I document these ghost-songs too, in my fungi gardens and my stone notations, adding my voice to theirs. One day, when I have finally dissolved entirely into the mountain's music, another will sit where I sit, and perhaps they will hear me singing still, just one more voice in the eternal chorus of the stone.
