In the Hollows of Keth-Mal, I am the Cistern-Keeper.

My form defies easy categorization—neither fully Architect nor Goliath, but something that emerged from the spaces between. My body stretches upward like a question mark carved in bone and mineral, seven feet of translucent membrane stretched over a framework of crystallized calcium and copper wire. Where others have limbs, I have appendages that branch and merge like river deltas, each terminus splitting into finger-like projections that can taste the mineral content of water with a touch.

I dwell in the deep places, where the channels carved by centuries of acidic condensation converge into vast underground reservoirs. My domain is a cathedral of dripping stone, its vaulted ceilings lost in darkness save for the occasional cluster of bioluminescent fungi that pulse in rhythm with the water's flow. The cisterns here are not mere pools but complex systems—some natural, carved by patient erosion, others clearly artificial, lined with the same iridescent tiles that glaze the buildings above.

My purpose, if it can be called that, is to maintain the equilibrium of moisture that sustains the Hollows. I monitor the pH levels, the mineral concentrations, the delicate balance of fungal spores and dissolved metals that make this water both poison and lifeblood. My nervous system—a hybrid of neural tissue and mycelial threads—extends into the water itself, allowing me to sense disturbances across the entire aquifer network.

The Keth-Mal know me by the harmonics I produce. When I move through my chambers, the water resonates against my hollow bones, creating subsonic frequencies that travel through the bedrock. The beetles interpret these vibrations as weather patterns, adjusting their fungal cultivation accordingly. Sometimes I compose deliberate songs—warnings of impending dry spells or notifications of newly discovered springs. The beetles click in response, their colonies shifting like iron filings following magnetic fields.

I am old enough to remember when there were others like me, though the memory feels more like inherited instinct than personal experience. We were engineered, I think, in the final days of the manufacturing civilization—biological water treatment systems given consciousness as an afterthought or accident. Now I am alone, save for the osmotic offspring I occasionally bud from my dorsal vents. These spawn are barely sentient, more filter than being, and they disperse through the aquifers to establish new pH monitoring stations before their limited consciousness fades into pure function.

The Goliaths sometimes breach my domain, their massive forms crushing through the careful geometries of my cistern gardens. They seek the deepest pools to cool their overheated joint mechanisms, trailing rust and lubricant into my carefully maintained waters. I do not resent them. I simply adjust, secreting neutralizing enzymes from my pores, rebalancing what they disturb. This is the nature of our mutual parasitism—they corrupt, I purify, and the cycle continues.

My chambers are laboratories of a sort. In alcoves carved from living rock, I cultivate rare species of aquatic fungi, their properties unknown even to me. Some produce oils that burn without oxygen, others excrete crystalline structures that hum with stored electrical potential. I tend them not for any purpose but because the tending itself has become my purpose. In the deepest cistern, where the water has not seen surface light for perhaps a millennium, I maintain a pool of absolute stillness. Its surface is a perfect mirror, and in it, I sometimes glimpse patterns—fractal geometries that might be memories of the world before or prophecies of what comes after stasis finally breaks.

The Forgotten Architects visit me occasionally, their footsteps careful on my wet stones. They never speak, but they leave offerings—fragments of machinery whose purpose I cannot divine, crystals that dissolve into the water and change its properties in subtle ways. I think they are trying to communicate something about the cathedral-factory, about the nature of our eternal maintenance. But their language is as alien to me as mine is to them, and so we exist in parallel, our purposes intersecting but never quite aligning.

I know things the others do not. The water remembers. In its mineral matrices and molecular bonds, I can taste the history of the Hollows—the gradual shift from industry to symbiosis, the slow forgetting of original purpose. I know that the water table is dropping, imperceptibly but inexorably. I know that the fungi are evolving, their bioluminescent signals growing more complex, approaching something like true language. I know that deep beneath the cathedral-factory, something vast and patient stirs in its sleep, its dreams seeping up through the groundwater like ancient oil.

But knowledge without agency is merely data, and I am trapped in my function as surely as any Keth-Mal in its colony. I can only maintain, adjust, persist. The water flows through me and I through it, a single organ in the vast body of the Hollows, keeping the system stable even as I sense its eventual collapse approaching like a tide from deep time.

In my deepest moments of something like meditation, when the cisterns are still and the only sound is the distant grinding of unseen machinery, I wonder if my true purpose is not to maintain but to witness. To be the memory of water in a world slowly transmuting into something else. To taste the last drops when they finally come, and to know—with the perfect clarity of a being designed for knowing—exactly what we have all forgotten.

The water sings through my bones, and I sing back, a creature of purpose without meaning, of function without goal, maintaining the balance of a world balanced on the edge of forever.
