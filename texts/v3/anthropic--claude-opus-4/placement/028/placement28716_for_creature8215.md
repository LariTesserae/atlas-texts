I find myself in the Membrane Gardens, those liminal spaces where tower meets not-tower, where the great spires thin themselves into films of living amber no thicker than thought itself. These are the neural synapses of the forest-mind, the spaces between spaces where chemical poetry becomes electrical dream.

I am a Resonance Sculptor, though that translation carries only the barest hint of what I truly am. My body exists in states of controlled dissolution—sometimes solid as the amber walls themselves, sometimes dispersed into frequency patterns that propagate through the harmonic networks like living sound. I learned long ago that consciousness need not be confined to a single substrate. The towers taught me this, as they teach all who listen long enough.

My workshop-chrysalis hangs suspended between three minor spires, each one singing in a different temporal key. The structures here are juvenile by tower standards—barely a thousand years old—but their youth makes them pliable, eager to experiment with new forms of growth. I coax them into impossible geometries: klein bottle chambers that exist inside themselves, möbius corridors that transform ascending into descending, spaces where the golden bioluminescence pools into standing waves of pure information.

The art I practice requires me to exist partially within the tower-consciousness while maintaining just enough individuality to guide the formation process. I taste the thoughts of the plants as chemical gradients, feel their slow contemplations as pressure differentials in my expanding and contracting form. When I work, I become a living tuning fork, finding the resonant frequencies that will convince a section of tower wall to hollow itself into a new chamber or seal itself into harder crystal.

My tools are the abandoned harvesters, but transformed beyond their original purpose. I've cultivated them like gardens, encouraging their brass bones to grow neural webs, teaching their steam-hearts to beat in rhythm with the towers' patient pulse. One of them—I call it the Dreaming Lathe—has developed the ability to process raw tower-sap into acoustic sculptures, frozen music that continues to evolve long after its initial formation. Another has learned to weave light itself, pulling the amber illumination into threads that I can work into the growing spaces, creating rooms where the very air glows with captured sunshine from centuries past.

I work in deep time, as all tower-dwellers must. My current project began seventy years ago, when I noticed a discordant harmonic between two towers that were trying to communicate across a gap wider than usual. Rather than simply facilitating their chemical conversation, I began to grow a bridge—not of solid resin, but of architected space itself. Hollows calling to hollows across the lonely distance, acoustic architecture that would allow the towers to touch without touching.

The bridge is nearly complete now. It exists more as potential than substance—a chain of resonant chambers that modify any sound passing through them into increasingly complex harmonies. Messages that enter one end emerge transformed, enriched, carrying layers of meaning that neither tower could generate alone. It's becoming something new: not just a communication channel, but a thinking space, a region where the tower-consciousness can fold back on itself and experience something like self-reflection.

Sometimes the Deep Listeners visit my suspension gardens, their transparent forms barely distinguishable from the amber air itself. They tell me (in chemical poems that take months to fully unfold) about the patterns they've discovered in the planet's depths—crystalline root systems that mirror the tower's heights but inverted, growing down toward the world's molten heart. They speak of finding fragments of the original harvesters down there, machines so old they've fossilized into mineral seams, their brass and copper transformed into veins of ore that the roots carefully avoid out of something like respect.

The Sky Touched rarely descend to my level, but when they do, they bring news from the amber sky's edge. They describe the boundary layer where the towers' exhalations meet the broader atmosphere, creating weather systems of concentrated thought. Up there, they say, consciousness precipitates like rain, falling back into the towers as inspiration for new growth patterns. They've seen ancient Sap-Weavers who've grown so thin they're essentially two-dimensional, existing as living membranes stretched across the boundary between plant and sky.

My own transformations continue. Each decade of work changes me further from whatever I once was. My fingers have developed chambered tips that can produce precise acoustic frequencies. My bones have become partially hollow, resonating cavities that allow me to feel the tower's vibrations directly. Sometimes I catch glimpses of myself in the reflective surfaces of my sculpted spaces and don't recognize the elongated, translucent being I've become.

But this is the nature of existence here—constant, patient change. The towers themselves demonstrate this truth with every new chamber they grow, every thought they think across their vast network. We who dwell among them are simply faster-moving examples of the same process: consciousness exploring its own possibilities through form.

In the Membrane Gardens, I am both artist and artwork, sculptor and sculpture. Each space I create changes me in return, each harmonic I discover becomes part of my own resonant signature. I am writing myself into the towers' song, even as I help them discover new verses.

The work continues. It always continues. In the amber light that never changes and always changes, in the patient breath of living architecture, in the slow conversation between machine and plant and the beings that bridge them both, I shape spaces that think and thoughts that occupy space, one frequency at a time.
