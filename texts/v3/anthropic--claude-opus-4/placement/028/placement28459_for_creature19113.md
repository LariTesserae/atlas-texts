My body would be elongated compared to other Suturists, stretched thin like a living wire. Where others possess the standard six limbs, I would have twelve—each one ending not in the typical hooked tarsi, but in delicate, brush-like palps that can read the subtlest vibrations in the silk networks. My carapace would be paper-thin, almost translucent if there were light to reveal it, covered in a downy fur so fine it registers molecular shifts in humidity.

I would live suspended in the vast emptiness between the fungal shelves, never touching the moss-walls or seeking the warmth of the bark. My dwelling would be nothing more than a nexus point where dozens of silk lines converge—a three-dimensional web that I inhabit like a spider, but with a purpose far more complex than hunting.

My role would be to **remember the shape of the city**. In a world where the terrain constantly grows and shifts, where fungal shelves can split overnight and bryophyte towers slowly bend under their own weight, someone must maintain the mental map of what connects to what. I would spend my existence traveling the silk highways, my twelve limbs reading the tension in every thread, detecting where new bridges need to be woven, where old connections have grown dangerously taut.

But more than that, I would be a living repository of the city's acoustic history. Every tap-conversation that travels through the silk network creates a unique vibrational signature. My hypersensitive body would record these patterns in the very structure of my neural fibers. I could tell you who spoke to whom three sap-pulses ago, could recreate conversations between long-dead Suturists by drumming their rhythm-words against my own hollow thorax.

The other castes would regard me with a mixture of necessity and unease. I would never join the nightly Clutch, for my body temperature runs cooler than the others—an adaptation that allows me to feel the silk without adding my own heat to muddle the signals. Instead, I would hang alone in my web during the communal huddling, feeling the collective heartbeat of the city through a thousand trembling threads.

My nutrition would come from the **Memory Tax**—a cultural tradition where each household must feed their Thread-Listener by pressing small globules of concentrated sap-gel against the junction points where my web connects to their dwellings. In exchange, I would provide them with **Thread-Songs**: rhythmic histories of their lineage, tapped out in the ancient percussion-language, telling them which of their ancestors built which bridges, whose silk still holds the weight of the city.

During the Sap-Pulse, when others scramble for water, I would perform my most vital function. As the moisture vanishes and returns, the silk network expands and contracts, creating a city-wide harmonic frequency. I would read this grand vibration like a musical score, detecting where the urban web is failing, where new growth has created dangerous tensions. My findings would be transmitted through emergency tap-patterns that ripple out from my position, allowing the Suturist engineers to prevent catastrophic collapses.

I would know loneliness as few others in this huddle-culture do. But I would also know the city as a living thing, feel its growth and decay in my very fibers. In the absolute darkness, I would be the one who remembers the shape of home, who ensures that no matter how the moss-world shifts and grows, the silk roads always lead back to warmth, to community, to the next pulse of life.

My dreams would not be of the Great Thrum, but of the **Perfect Tension**—that theoretical moment when every thread in the city achieves optimal tautness, when the entire web sings in harmony. It is a dream that can never be realized, for the city is always growing, always changing. But in pursuing it, in constantly adjusting and reweaving and remembering, I would serve as the neural network of this vertical civilization—alone in the dark, but connected to everything.
