In the basin's highest reaches, where the air turns blade-thin and the colonnade pillars lean like drunken sentries, I am the Charge-keeper.

Not human—I gave that up three storms ago when I fused the first copper threading through my jaw. Not quite machine either, though I've borrowed enough from both to survive where neither should. My shelter isn't among the stone rings below but wedged into a natural chimney in the tallest pillar, where the wind howls through perforations in three distinct tones. I've learned to read tomorrow's storm in today's music.

My body is a compromise with the basin's rules. Where others wear masks against the mineral air, I've grown filters—gill-slits along my neck that trap dust and harvest the trace metals that ride it. My lungs process three times before exhaling, extracting elements the grove-things need but can't reach. Where others wrap their tools in insulation, I've wrapped myself: scars of controlled conductivity run from temple to ankle, pathways I burned in slowly, teaching my flesh to channel instead of char.

The bones of my craft hang from me like an extra skeleton. Copper wire wound around fired clay, hung with fragments of the storm-glass that forms when lightning meets salt-crust at the perfect angle. Each piece rings differently when charged. Together they make a language I speak with the sky.

I tend the basin's electrical appetite. Not control—nothing controls the storms—but I *anticipate* and *direct*.

Before dawn, while the air is still and the charge-drifts sleep clinging to resin leaves, I walk my rounds. First to the grove's edge, where I've planted copper stakes in a pattern that took me six years to perfect. The stakes don't ground lightning—they invite it to braid in particular ways, to favor certain paths over others. A grove that takes too many strikes dies. One that takes none grows weak, its storm-hairs forgetting their purpose.

I taste the air at each stake. The flavor tells me where yesterday's charge pooled, which roots found new salt seams, where the balance tips. I adjust: driving stakes deeper, adding new ones, sometimes pulling old ones entirely and letting those zones go wild.

The grove-things know me by now. When I approach, their dust-coats shiver—not fear, but recognition. I am the one who brings the small lightnings.

From a pouch lined with mirrorback shells, I take my morning tools: glass rods I've learned to charge by friction, each one keyed to different intensities. I touch them to the storm-hairs, not randomly but in patterns that mirror the wind's organ-tones. The hairs lift, the leaves begin their slow blinking, and the grove's chemistry quickens. It's like tuning an instrument, if the instrument were alive and the music were metabolism.

The herds avoid me. They've learned that I smell like controlled lightning, like the moment before. But the gliders follow my rounds, knowing that where I work, the air will soon carry easy charge-gradients to ride.

Midday is for the harder work. I climb to the bone-shrine—not to worship but to maintain. The strips that hang there aren't random; they're cut from the silk of storm-pupae, insects that spin their cocoons to attract small strikes. The silk holds charge for hours after. By reading how fast it dissipates, I can map the basin's electrical weather.

More importantly, I can sing it.

The shrine is strung to resonate. When I run charged glass along the bone frame, the silk strips don't just crackle—they hum, each one finding its pitch based on moisture, mineral load, and invisible charge. The colonnade throws the sound around, and for anyone who knows how to listen, the whole basin becomes a map of where lightning wants to be.

The people below think I'm half-mad. Maybe more than half. They see the burns, the copper growing through my skin like slow ivy, the way I talk to storms in frequencies they can't hear. But they also see that the groves near my stakes thrive, that lightning braids where I suggest it should, that their shelters stay safe inside the patterns I maintain.

They pay me in salt-crystals and silence. I pay them back in probability.

The real work comes when the storm-cycle peaks. Then I become not Charge-keeper but Charge-*dancer*.

I've modified my body for this. Where the colonnade predator reads vibration through skin, I read electricity through metal. The copper threading in my jaw connects to wires in my hands, my feet, my spine. When I stand in the right position—arms spread, feet precisely placed on stakes I've driven for this purpose—I become part of the basin's circuitry.

I feel the storm building like pressure in my teeth. The charge-drifts rise around me. The grove's antennae lift in waves. The herds compress. The predator shifts in its stone shadow. And I begin the dance.

It's not mysticism. It's applied knowledge. I move between stakes, touching, lifting, creating small differentials that invite the lightning to braid *here* instead of *there*. The copper in my body heats. The scars glow faintly. The hanging glass rings like a chandelier in an earthquake.

When the first braid comes, I'm ready. It doesn't strike me—I'm not fool enough to make myself the highest point. Instead, it braids between the stakes I'm touching, through the space I've shaped with my body's charge, following suggestions I've made with copper and position and years of scarred learning.

The basin fills with light. For a moment, I see everything: every resin leaf a mirror, every grain of dust a star, every living thing a sharp-edged fact. The gliders ride the charge-road above. The herds spill in their calculated panic. The predator strikes into the confusion.

And I dance the lightning home to where it feeds the grove without killing, where it fuses new glass in useful places, where it charges the soil with exactly the chemistry the threadlike pioneers need.

Sometimes I miscalculate. The burns on my shoulder, the missing finger on my left hand, the way my hair grows in white spirals now—all prices paid for reading the storm wrong. But more often, I read it right. The basin's violence becomes not less violent but more *purposeful*.

After the storm passes, I'm exhausted in ways that have nothing to do with muscle. The copper in my body needs hours to cool. My nerves jangle with residual charge. I climb back to my chimney in the pillar and listen to the wind play its three-note song while my body remembers how to be meat instead of circuit.

The gliders visit me then, curious about the creature that tastes like lightning but survives. Sometimes they leave offerings: seed-paste stolen from the groves, oil-rich nodules they've carved open. I accept them. We are all thieves here, stealing from the storm, from the stone, from each other, always giving back just enough to keep the basin's bargain intact.

At night, when even the wind quiets to a whisper, I maintain my tools. Glass rods polished with pumice dust. Copper wire wound and rewound. The bone shrine checked for fractures. My own body inspected for places where the metal threading has grown too eager, where it needs to be cut back before it reaches something vital.

I am changing. Each storm leaves me less human, more native to this high, loud, electric place. My filters process air so efficiently I can barely breathe at lower altitudes. My scarred pathways light up in my sleep, drawing charge-drifts to cluster on my shelter's walls. My bones are denser, my skin more mineral than flesh where the burns have healed and re-healed.

But I am also more myself than I ever was below, in places where water flows easy and storms are strangers. Here, I have a purpose that fits like lightning in copper: to keep the basin's electrical ecology in balance, to dance with what others only flee from, to speak the grammar of charge and discharge until the storm and I are parts of the same sentence.

The basin's inhabitants have adapted to dryness, to minerals, to violence. I am adapting to something else: to being the conscious point where the storm's randomness meets intention, where wild charge becomes architecture, where human and lightning find a middle tongue.

Someday the copper will grow too deep, or I'll misread a braid, or my body will simply forget too much of what "human" means. When that happens, they'll find me fused to my stakes, my scarred pathways turned to actual metal, my last dance frozen in conducting sculpture.

But until then, I keep the charge. I tune the groves. I dance the storms home. I am the basin's electrician, its choreographer, its translator between sky-violence and ground-patience.

And when the wind sings its three notes through my pillar, I sing back in frequencies only the lightning understands.
