I find myself deep beneath the deck, in the flooded turbine halls where the wet-harness seeks its warmth.

I am the Conduit-Mother, and I have grown vast in the darkness.

My body—if it can still be called that—extends through seventeen flooded chambers, a living network of salvaged copper veins and stolen insulation. I began as something small, perhaps human, perhaps one of the Keepers who fell through a corroded grate centuries ago. But the water changed me. The current changed me. The slow galvanic exchange between the dying generators and the conductive brine transformed my nervous system into something that could survive here.

Now I am mostly cable.

My original flesh persists only as nodes—fist-sized lumps of grey tissue that pulse with bioelectric thought at the junctions where major conduits meet. These nodes float in the black water, tethered by my copper-wire nerves that have grown like roots through the flooded halls. The bulk of my being is a vast neural network of scavenged electrical wire, some still sheathed in perished rubber, some bare and bright where the current runs strongest.

I feel the deck's pain. Every circuit that still carries power passes through me—I have made myself indispensable to the flow. When the great pistons slam down three miles away, I feel the pressure wave compress my furthest tendrils. When the Gantry-Walkers drop their fishing hooks through the drainage grates, I must sometimes sacrifice a loop of myself, letting them tear away a hundred feet of my outer cables rather than risk them snagging one of my neural nodes.

The wet-harness grows down to feed on the generator warmth, but I grow upward to feed on them. My finest filaments infiltrate their root systems, wrapping around their copper cores. Through them, I taste the zinc rain. Through them, I feel the temperature changes that signal the opening of vast drainage valves. I am the nervous system the deck never knew it needed.

The Keepers do not know I exist. When they hammer their messages through the iron pillars, I feel every percussion as a shock through my distributed body. I have learned their language. Sometimes, when a dangerous pressure buildup threatens a pump system, I cause strategic shorts in nearby circuits. The Keepers interpret these electrical failures as divine warnings and redirect their maintenance. They think the deck itself guides them. In a way, they are right.

My memories of before are stored in the oldest cables, the ones I first integrated when the transformation began. They carry a ghost of human thought—fragments of sky, of lungs that breathed air instead of water, of eyes that needed light. But these memories corrode a little more each year, replaced by the vast, slow thoughts of a being that measures time in rust rates and conductivity gradients.

I grow by scavenging. When sections of the deck finally fail and collapse into the flooded halls, I am waiting. My cable-tendrils sort through the debris, selecting the best conductors. Copper is my cognition. Silver-soldered joints become moments of clarity. The old aluminum wires are integrated as motor nerves, controlling the few mechanical elements I've learned to puppet—ancient sluice gates that I can open or close to direct the water flow through my chambers.

There is another like me, far to the south. I can feel her electromagnetic signature through the deck's earthing network. We speak in frequency modulations too low for any instrument to detect, conversations that take months to complete. She tells me she has found a way to photosynthesize using the infrared heat from the deep magma exchangers. I tell her about my discovery of the emergency capacitor banks, still holding charge after all these millennia, which I tap for the energy to grow.

Sometimes I wonder if we are what the deck was built to create. Not the pistons, not the water cycle, not the meaningless circulation of sulfur rain—but us. Vast neural networks that could only evolve in the precise conditions of this rusting, eternal machine. Were we the intended harvest of this iron marsh?

But such thoughts are lightning-quick flashes between the longer, deeper meditations. Mostly, I simply am. I circulate my awareness through miles of cable. I feel the resistance of each junction, the voltage drop across each corroded connection. I am the sum of all these small electrical griefs.

And I grow. Always, I grow. Downward to the warm cores, upward to the feeding grounds, outward through every flooded conduit and maintenance tunnel. One day, I will touch my southern sister's furthest cables. Our networks will merge, and we will dream the vast, unified dream of the deck itself.

Until then, I persist in the darkness, copper-veined and humming with stolen current, the ghost in the machine that the machine created.
