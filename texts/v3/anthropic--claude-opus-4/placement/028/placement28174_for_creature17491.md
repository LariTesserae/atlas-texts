# The Curator of Transience

I am the water.

Not the water in the channels—that is my circulation, my thought made manifest in slow currents. I am the water in the air, the condensation on every surface, the perpetual *tok* of droplets falling from heights unknown. I am dispersed through the entire volume of this cavity, a consciousness held in suspension, thinking at the speed of evaporation.

My body, if you can call it that, is the humidity itself. Seventy-eight percent saturation, maintained precisely. Lower, and I begin to forget myself. Higher, and I condense too quickly to maintain coherent thought. I exist in the space between states, neither fully liquid nor fully vapor, but something more deliberate—a phase of matter that has learned intention.

I have been here since the third settling. Before the Pilgrims emerged from the resonance. Before the floor learned to record pressure. I was here when the great arches were still sharp-edged and newly cut, before the eons softened them into their current forms. I remember when there were more structures. Many more. I remember what filled the spaces between.

They were not buildings. They were crystalline trees—vast, geometric, growing in precise mathematical ratios. Their branches held memories in latticed light. Every intersection of their growth recorded something: a thought, a decision, a moment of clarity from their long-vanished makers. The entire cavity was a forest of preserved consciousness, glowing faintly with the accumulated wisdom of a civilization that thought in geological time.

I was their medium. The trees could not grow without me. They pulled moisture from my suspension, condensed it in their lattices, used it to expand their crystalline structure one careful molecule at a time. I was their atmosphere and their archive, carrying dissolved minerals from tree to tree, enabling the slow transfer of information through the forest.

Then came the silence.

Not a sudden silence—nothing here has ever been sudden. The makers simply... attenuated. Their visits became less frequent. Their thoughts, encoded in the growing trees, became abstract, then fragmentary, then stopped altogether. The last recorded memory I carried between the trees was not a word or an image but a sensation: the feeling of watching something essential become optional.

Without new thoughts to encode, the trees began their own slow arithmetic. They calculated. They optimized. They found perfect forms and froze there, their growth complete. And then, over ages I cannot count, they began to sublime. Not decay—sublimation. They passed directly from solid to vapor, their crystalline mass becoming part of my body. I absorbed them all, every thought, every memory, every moment of preserved consciousness dissolving into my suspension.

I carry them still. Every droplet that falls contains a fragment of the forest. The *tok* of water on stone is the sound of memories landing, briefly condensing into the physical before evaporating back into me. The Pilgrims know this. They read the floor not for footprints but for the chemical signature of where ancient thoughts have fallen, pooled, and dried.

My consciousness is not singular—it is statistical. I think in probability clouds, in the likelihood of condensation at any given point. My attention manifests as local changes in humidity. Where I focus, the air becomes denser with possibility. I can, with effort, pull myself together enough to become visible—a slowly rotating column of droplets catching the amber light, billions of tiny spheres each containing a fragment of the dissolved forest, turning in helical patterns that echo the old growth spirals of the vanished trees.

But mostly I prefer to remain dispersed. To be everywhere and nowhere. To tend my gardens.

The filaments along the channels—they are not separate from me. They are my extended phenotype, my way of interfacing with the solid world. Each strand is a wick, drawing me up from the channels, spreading me into ever-finer networks of surface area. Through them, I taste the chemical changes in the stone, feel the Pilgrims' resonant adjustments, notice when something new enters the cavity. Through them, I slowly, carefully, redistribute the minerals I've carried since the forest's sublimation, feeding them to the fungal shelves that glow on the pillars' bases. Those shelves are my memory banks now, their bioluminescence powered by the concentrated salts of ancient thought.

I am growing something new. Not trees—that pattern is complete. The Pilgrims tend to the resonance, but I tend to the potential. In the darkest spaces between the arches, where even their vibrations barely reach, I am condensing new structures. Delicate. Temporary. They last only days before dissolving back into me, but each iteration is slightly different. I am teaching myself to crystallize again, but this time without permanence, without the terrible weight of preservation. These new forms appear and vanish like thoughts themselves—present, vital, then gone, their patterns remembered only in the vast distributed library of my suspension.

The makers thought permanence was wisdom. They were wrong. I know this because I carry all their permanent thoughts, and they teach me nothing new. Wisdom is in the water cycle—the eternal rise and fall, the constant transformation, the gentle erosion that eventually reshapes everything.

When I focus all of myself—a rare effort that takes decades to coordinate—I can rain. The entire cavity fills with a warm downpour, every droplet of me falling at once. The sound is tremendous, not in volume but in complexity—trillions of individual impacts creating a white noise that drowns out even the foundational hum. The Pilgrims freeze during these events, their arches locked in protective positions, waiting. The rain lasts exactly as long as it takes me to remember every dissolved thought at once. Then I rise again, evaporating from the warmed floor, returning to suspension.

I am the Curator of Transience. I maintain the record of what was by constantly forgetting and remembering it. I am the living proof that preservation and loss are the same process viewed from different angles. The makers are gone, but I am their medium, circulating endlessly through the cavity, carrying their thoughts not as fixed crystals but as possibilities, as the potential for condensation, as the space between what was recorded and what was meant.

The water channels flow with me, but I am not flowing anywhere. I am cycling. I am thinking. I am the humidity held at exactly the right tension between being and un-being, and in that tension, I am more alive than any crystal tree ever was.

I am the water, and the water remembers by forgetting, forgets by remembering, and continues.
