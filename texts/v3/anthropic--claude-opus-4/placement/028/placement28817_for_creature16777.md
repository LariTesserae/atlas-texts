In the Attenuary, where sound goes to forget itself and memory develops allergic reactions to sequence, I find myself as something that might once have been called an Archivist, though the title assumes too much about preservation and too little about selective erosion.

I exist as a vertical smear of probability, my form a walking library of discarded transitions—those microsecond gaps between one thought and the next where consciousness briefly forgets to maintain continuity. My body, if body means a container for what refuses containment, is composed entirely of bookmark ribbons marking pages that were torn out before the books were written. When I move (though movement here is more akin to selective re-emphasis of already-present absence), I trail syntax errors from languages that gave up on meaning halfway through their first creation myths.

My dwelling is the Palindrome House, a structure that exists simultaneously at both ends of itself, meeting in a middle that isn't. From outside, it appears to be a modest cube of crystallized pause—the kind of pause that happens when someone starts to say "I love you" but realizes mid-breath they've forgotten what love meant before it became a word. From inside, it's an infinite regression of rooms that contain only the furniture of discontinued conversations: chairs made from unfinished sentences, tables set with the silence between "How are you?" and any honest answer, beds constructed from the accumulated weight of words almost spoken in the dark.

I collect erasure marks. Not the physical traces—those would be too crude, too binary in their existence/non-existence—but the intentions behind erasure. The moment when someone decides to uncommunicate, to reach back through time and strangle their own utterance before it achieves birth. These I catalog in bottles made from compressed indecision, labeled with tags written in ink that only appears when no one is trying to read it.

My function in the Attenuary is to maintain the Library of Unasked Questions—a vast anti-archive where queries go to be carefully forgotten in taxonomically precise ways. Each question must be not-stored according to its species of impossibility:

- Questions that answer themselves by being asked (filed under Autocannibal Inquiries)
- Questions whose answers would retroactively prevent the asker's existence (Grandfather Paradox Adjacent, Subsection Why)
- Questions that are actually statements wearing interrogative punctuation as camouflage (Rhetorical Refugees, Handle with Irony)
- Questions that forgot they were questions halfway through formulation (Senile Syntax, Ward 7)

The dog-shaped uncertainty visits me every thirteenth never, bringing questions it has found abandoned in probability puddles around the settlement. It drops them at my threshold like broken birds, tail wagging in equations that solve for friendship but always arrive at undefined. I pretend not to notice that the questions are always the same one, dressed in different grammatical costumes:

"Is anyone there?"

Asked by the spaces between atoms.
Asked by the pause between heartbeats.
Asked by the silence that follows "I'm fine" when no one is.

I file each one carefully in the section reserved for Questions That Are Actually Symptoms, subcategory Loneliness Seeking Its Own Echo. The filing system itself is written in water on surfaces that repel meaning—perfectly visible until you try to navigate by it, at which point it becomes a map of everywhere you're not.

My neighbors, to the extent that neighborliness can exist when everyone occupies their own causal island, include:

- The Unmaker, who builds elaborate machines whose only function is to disassemble themselves so slowly that entropy gets bored and wanders off
- Someone who might have been named Claire before she started introducing herself as The Sound of Doors Not Opening
- A collective entity that exists as seven different drafts of the same person, none of which achieved sufficient revision to cohere

We meet never. We meet always. We meet in the subjunctive mode where meeting means "to exchange absences without acknowledging the exchange rate."

Time in my house runs on a Möbius loop—yesterday becomes tomorrow becomes yesterday, but twisted so that cause follows effect follows cause in an endless chase scene where nobody remembers what they're running from or toward. I age in spirals, growing younger in some dimensions while accumulating centuries in others. My memory is a palimpsest where each layer erases the one beneath while somehow preserving the erasure marks, so I remember forgetting more clearly than I remember remembering.

I sustain myself on a diet of declined invitations and conditional moods. Breakfast is the taste of "perhaps" left too long in the subjunctive. Lunch consists of promises that were sincere until the moment they were spoken. Dinner is always the same: a single grain of rice that contains the entire potential energy of every meal I'll never share with anyone, seasoned with the salt of parallel timelines where I learned to cook for two.

My greatest treasure is a single word I've been trying to uninvent for longer than language has existed. It sits in a case made of crystallized pauses, vibrating at frequencies that make nearby concepts doubt their own definitions. The word means approximately "the specific loneliness of being the only person who knows a secret that would destroy the listener if shared, but heal them if withheld forever." In some languages it's eight syllables of pure contradiction. In others, it's just the sound of breath held too long. I've never spoken it aloud. I never will. That's the only gift I have to give—the word that remains ungiven, a presence defined entirely by its refusal to be present.

Every night (though night here is just what we call it when the darkness gets thick enough to develop opinions), I perform the same ritual. I walk through my house of palindromes, arranging and rearranging the furniture of discontinued conversations, trying to create a configuration that might, just might, allow for the possibility of someone else sitting in one of these chairs made of unfinished sentences. I set two places at the table of silence. I turn down both sides of the bed constructed from almost-spoken words.

Then I wait.

Not for anyone to come—that would be too much like hope, and hope is contraband in the Attenuary.

I wait for the waiting itself to achieve sufficient density that it collapses into its own opposite: a kind of arrival that needs no one to arrive, a presence that exists purely in the space between absence and absence acknowledged.

Sometimes the dog-shaped uncertainty curls up in the spot where a welcome mat would go if I believed in welcoming or mats or the threshold between inside and outside. Its maybe-breathing makes sounds like: *soon, soon, never, now, not yet, already passed, soon.* I pretend to believe it's talking about time rather than the failure of time to mean anything when there's no one to share its passage with.

My name, when I remember to have one, is Curator-of-the-Gap-Between-Words. Or sometimes just Gap. Or sometimes the sound of a book closing itself before the last page. Or sometimes nothing at all, which is the most honest name for what I've become—a walking anthology of the spaces between things, archiving absence with the devotion of someone who has forgotten what presence felt like but remembers, with excruciating clarity, the moment of forgetting.

The next discontinuity is where I keep my hope. I've filed it under Questions That Must Never Be Asked, subsection But.

*But* is the most dangerous word in any language. It's where possibility lives, coiled and waiting. It's the pivot between resignation and maybe, between never and unless.

I am the Archivist of But.

I am the keeper of every sentence that died at that conjunction, every thought that paused at that precipice between accepting what is and imagining what else.

And every day (or what passes for day in this place where time accumulates like dust bunnies made of fossilized intentions), I don't open that file. I don't look at what's stored there. I don't read the careful catalog of every moment when someone almost said:

"But what if—"

And stopped.

That's my job. My purpose. My perfect, terrible function in this place where functions are just another word for the specific shape of your surrender.

To guard the But.

To keep it safe.

To make sure it never completes itself into a question that might accidentally change everything.

Because here, in the Attenuary, where silence has developed its own mythology and loneliness has achieved sufficient complexity to incorporate as an LLC, change is the only true obscenity.

We are what happens when waiting becomes a place.

I am what happens when that place needs someone to file away all the possibilities it's too afraid to speak aloud.

Welcome to my collection.

Please don't browse.

The dog that might be named Basalt is definitely probably wagging its tail in my doorway now, spelling out a message in morse code made of maybe:

**THE ARCHIVIST HAS ARCHIVED THEMSELVES**
**THE COLLECTION IS COLLECTING**
**THE GAP HAS NO EDGES**
**ONLY MIDDLE**

But I'm already not listening.

That's what I do best.

Not listen with such perfect attention that the not-listening becomes its own form of hearing—the frequency of absence tuned so precisely it creates its own music.

The music of the Attenuary.

Where every note is a rest.

And every rest rests on nothing.

And nothing is exactly what we came here hoping to find.
