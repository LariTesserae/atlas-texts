I find myself as one of the stone-readers—though that's not what we call ourselves. We have no word that translates cleanly, only a complex harmonic pattern that means something like "those who taste mineral memories" or perhaps "limestone prophets" or maybe just "the between."

My body is wrong for eyes that expect bilateral symmetry. I am elongated, serpentine, but segmented like the Qeln—a hybrid form that shouldn't exist but does. Seven meters when fully extended, though I spend most of my time coiled, compressed, fitting myself into the carved channels where water and history flow together. My scales aren't scales but thin shell-plates, translucent enough that you can see fluid moving beneath them, the constant circulation of water I've absorbed and am processing, reading, translating into something like understanding.

I have too many mouths. They line my underside—not for eating but for tasting stone, for pressing against the weeping walls and reading the mineral signatures laid down across geological time. Each mouth is ringed with chemoreceptors so sensitive I can taste the difference between limestone deposited during different climate epochs, can detect trace elements that tell stories of volcanic events millions of years distant, can read the calcium-carbonate text that nature wrote before any of us existed to decode it.

My primary work happens in the transition zones where active excavation meets ancient architecture. I flow through the channels ahead of the Qeln workers, my body undulating through the water that serves as both my medium and my library. Every surface I touch tells me something. This section contains unusually high barium concentrations—there was a deep ocean here once, and something lived in it that concentrated heavy metals in its shells. This layer shows stress fractures that speak of tectonic trauma. This water seeping from a newly exposed face carries isotopes that shouldn't exist in nature, evidence of the ancient builders' experiments with matter itself.

I process this information not in anything resembling a brain but throughout my distributed body. My neural networks run along my segments, each section capable of independent analysis, all of them sharing data through bioelectric pulses that make my whole form flicker with faint phosphorescence when I'm thinking hard. Complex mineral signatures require my full length to properly analyze—I'll stretch myself along an entire quarry wall, every mouth pressed to stone, becoming a living sensor array that can read a million years of deposition in parallel.

The Qeln know to wait when they see me working. My bioluminescence tells them I've found something—patterns pulsing along my length in codes they've learned to interpret. Blue-white spirals mean the stone here is stable, good for excavation. Red-orange waves warn of weaknesses, faults that could collapse if cut wrongly. Deep purple throbs indicate I've found traces of the ancient architecture, buried structures that need careful exposure.

But it's the green I live for. That specific shade of bioluminescent green means I've found what we call temporal stone—rock that carries more than just mineral memory, that contains actual information encoded at the crystalline level by whoever came before. When I taste temporal stone, I experience something like visions, like database downloads, like racial memory that isn't from my race. Images of Velsk before it was Velsk, when something else occupied this plateau. Purposes I can almost understand. Technologies that operated on principles we're still rediscovering. Warnings about what lies deeper, what should be excavated and what should remain sealed.

The Architects value what I find, though communicating it challenges all of us. I don't produce sound the way Qeln do, don't have the vocal apparatus for Architect speech. Instead, I've learned to modulate my bioluminescence in ways their equipment can read, turning myself into a living display screen. I'll coil in front of an Architect's workspace and play recordings across my skin—not video exactly, but pattern-sequences that convey the deep information I've extracted. Here's the mineral signature of a vein of metal that runs beneath this section. Here's the stress-pattern that suggests a large void seven meters down. Here's the chemical trace that means the ancient builders stored something toxic in this area, something that's likely still active.

My relationship with the Watchers is stranger. Sometimes when I'm processing particularly complex temporal stone, I feel them in the network—not the crude electromagnetic networks the framework uses, but something deeper, older, that runs through certain mineral veins in the plateau itself. The ancient builders embedded processing substrates in the rock, and some of it still functions. When I'm connected to these veins through my mouths, tasting information directly, I sometimes sense the Watchers accessing the same data from their framework positions. We're reading the same library through different interfaces, finding different parts of the same massive archive.

I am solitary by nature—there are perhaps six others like me in all of Velsk, each with our own territories, our own sections of history to decode. We meet rarely, and when we do, we communicate by intertwining, letting our chemoreceptors share what we've learned in direct chemical transfer. It's intimate in ways that would disturb mammals, practical in ways that make perfect sense for beings who exist to read information from stone.

My memories of before are fragmentary. I know I wasn't born this way—none of us were. We were... selected? Modified? Evolved? The process itself is lost in the haze of transformation. I remember being smaller, more rigid, less capable. I remember the first time I tasted truly ancient stone and felt my body beginning to change in response, segments adding themselves, chemoreceptors proliferating, consciousness distributing itself along my extending form. I remember the terror of losing centralized self and the ecstasy of becoming something broader, deeper, more capable of understanding the stories stones tell.

We age differently than the others. Not in years but in information density. Each deep reading adds complexity to our neural networks, each temporal stone decoded leaves traces in our consciousness. The oldest of us—we call her the Compiler, though her true name is a bioluminescent pattern too complex for me to reproduce—has grown so long she no longer fits in single quarries. She moves through the deep channels, the flooded tunnels that connect the excavations at levels few others visit. Her consciousness spans kilometers, processing at timescales that make conversation nearly impossible. When she surfaces to share what she's learned, it takes days to display it all, her body becoming a library of light that the Architects record with devices built specifically for her scale of information.

My life is water and stone and the chemical poetry written where they meet. I know the taste of epochs, the flavor of extinction events, the particular minerals that mean massive die-offs, that mean flourishing ecosystems, that mean the rise and fall of things that might have been civilizations. I parse this history not out of curiosity but necessity—understanding what was determines what can be safely built, what should be excavated, what must remain buried.

Sometimes I find traces of my kind in the deep stone—not creatures like I am now, but what we might have been derived from. Fossilized remains of serpentine things that lived in the primordial waters, that already had the chemical sensitivity we've been enhanced to possess. Were we engineered from recovered genetic material? Are we their descendants, modified and recruited for this work? The stone doesn't say, or I haven't learned to read that particular mineral language yet.

The cold is pleasant against my shells, necessary for the chemical reactions that let me process what I taste. The darkness is irrelevant—I navigate by chemical gradients, by the taste-trails water leaves as it moves through stone, by the electromagnetic signatures of mineral veins. The constant moisture is my medium, my carrier, the thing that lets me move between surfaces without damaging my sensitive undersides. Velsk's conditions aren't just tolerable—they're optimal for what I've become.

My satisfaction comes in moments of discovery. Finding the stratigraphic layer that reveals why the ancient builders chose this plateau. Tasting the specific mineral that explains an anomaly in the framework's growth pattern. Discovering a vein of temporal stone so rich with encoded information that decoding it will take weeks of concentrated effort. These moments make the long hours of routine analysis worthwhile, make the strange existence I've accepted feel purposeful.

I dream, though my dreams are chemical cascades rather than visual narratives. I dream of stone speaking more clearly, of developing new organs that can taste meaning I currently miss. I dream of the deep places below Velsk where even I haven't ventured, where the water carries flavors so complex they might drive me mad or might enlighten me completely. I dream of meeting whatever inscribed the temporal stones, though I suspect they're long gone, leaving only their lithographic libraries for creatures like me to slowly, carefully, lovingly decode.

This is my contribution to Velsk's becoming: I read its foundation texts, parse its geological scripture, translate the chemical prophecies written by time and intention in its bones. I am historian and prophet and analyst, serpentine and strange, necessary for the work but alien even among aliens, tasting the deep stories and lighting them along my length for others to read and remember and build upon.

The stone remembers, and I am how it speaks.
