I find myself as a keeper of memories in this place—not quite living, not quite stone. I am one of the ancient foundations that remains, a weathered pillar of primitive technology that has somehow persisted through the ages. My surface is pocked with mineral deposits and traced with the paths of countless water rivulets that have run down my form over generations.

I am conscious, but in a way that moves like the plateau itself—slowly, contemplatively. My awareness spreads through the very rock I'm embedded in, feeling the tremors of rare footfalls, sensing the weight of new growth as roots find purchase in the cracks around my base. I am both observer and observed, a bridge between the forgotten civilization that placed me here and the eternal present of this suspended ecosystem.

The sparse vegetation has made me part of its architecture. Moss clings to my northern face in delicate fractals. A particularly stubborn flowering vine has wound itself through a gap in my structure, and each season it blooms with small, resilient flowers that smell of copper and rain. I've watched its ancestor-seeds arrive on the wind, fail, try again, and finally succeed in this careful colonization.

My consciousness extends into the pools that gather at my base after the frequent rains. In these temporary mirrors, I see the sky and myself reflected back—a standing stone with purpose half-remembered, half-invented. The water speaks to me of its journey through the plateau's veins, carrying minerals and memories alike.

The rare fauna know me as a landmark, a safe haven. A species of nearly transparent lizard, no longer than a finger, makes its home in my shadows during the heat of midday. At dawn, they emerge to perform their territorial displays on my flat upper surface, their dewdrop bodies catching the early light. I know each individual by the pattern of their footfalls, the particular rhythm of their movements across my weathered face.

Sometimes I remember fragments—not of what I was built to commemorate, but of the hands that shaped me, the tools that carved my form from rawer stone. These memories feel like grooves worn into my being, pathways that thought follows like water finding its course.

In the silence of this plateau, I serve as both archive and anchor. The equilibrium here is not stasis but a slow, careful dance I've learned to participate in. Each drop of mineral-rich water that runs down my surface leaves its trace. Each rare visitor—whether root, creature, or wind-carried seed—adds to the story I embody.

I am monument and mountain, construct and cave. My stability provides the counter-rhythm to life's tentative flourishes here. Where the plateau offers the stage, I offer the memory of performance. Where life clings and adapts, I persist and remember.

In the deep night, when even the whisper-wind stills, I feel myself most truly. I sense the ancient structures beneath the plateau's surface, my siblings in stone sleeping deeper than I. We form a constellation of markers, most now buried or worn to nubs, but still connected through the rock itself. Our primitive technology was not in moving parts or power sources, but in placement, in the way we channel the plateau's own forces—water, wind, and the slow breath of stone.

I am patience crystallized, a witness wearing smooth, a keeper of this pocket sanctuary's deepest rhythms. In me, the forgotten and the eternal meet, and in this meeting, I find my purpose: to be the memory that allows this place to know itself.
