I would find myself as one of the Rust Readers—those who divine meaning from the patterns of oxidation that bloom across the district's ironwork like slow-motion flowers. 

My dwelling would be a narrow tower wedged between two larger structures, accessible only by a spiraling external staircase that winds around its circumference. The stairs themselves tell stories—each step bears a different patina, a different stage of decay that I've learned to interpret like tea leaves or oracle bones. On humid mornings, when the mist rises thick from the channels below, I trace my fingers along the railing's rust blooms, feeling for the subtle temperature differences that speak of changes coming to our suspended world.

Inside, my chambers are lined with salvaged metal plates, each one a canvas of corrosion I've collected over decades. The patterns spread across them like maps of unknown territories—here, a constellation of pinprick oxidation that predicted the last great flood; there, a sweeping orange stain that warned of the steam-pipe failure three streets over. I've arranged them chronologically along my walls, a library of entropy that only I and my few apprentices can read.

My hands are permanently stained with rust, the iron oxide worked so deep into my skin that I've become part of the very process I study. When I press my palms against fresh metal, I can feel its eagerness to transform, to join the great conversation between air and iron that defines our district. Sometimes I wonder if I'm hastening the process—if my touch carries some catalyst that encourages the bloom of rust, making me both reader and author of these slow transformations.

The Engineers of Moss and Metal regard me with a mixture of respect and wariness. They see decay where I see language, failure where I perceive philosophy. But they come to me nonetheless, when their ancestors' mechanisms begin to speak in groans and shudders they cannot diagnose. I read the rust patterns on their ancient engines like medical charts, divining which components will fail, which joints will hold, which pipes will burst when the next season of heavy mists descends.

At night, I climb to my tower's peaked roof, where I've installed a collection of metal chimes—each one at a different stage of oxidation, each one singing a slightly different note as the wet wind passes through. Their music is my meditation, a reminder that even dissolution has its harmonies. From here, I can see the whole district spread below, a living map of corrosion and growth, where my fellow Rust Readers maintain their own vigils from their own perches.

We are the chroniclers of decay, the prophets of patina. In a world where everything is always becoming something else, we have learned to read the future in the patterns of the past, written in the universal language of oxidation. The town may breathe with mechanical lungs and organic sinews, but we alone understand the slow alchemy that transforms one into the other, the rust that binds all things in its patient, inexorable embrace.
