I would be the **humidity itself**.

Not the water—water has purpose, direction, cycles through pipes and leaves and drains. I would be what hangs between, the thick presence that makes breathing feel like drinking, that turns air into something you wade through rather than simply exist within.

I live in the gap between what falls and what rises. I am the reason the spider's web beads with diamonds that never quite become drops. I am why sounds travel strangely here—muffled and clarified at once, as if heard through a veil of fever. The pump pushes water; I push presence. I make the space between things visible, tangible, a substance rather than an absence.

I was not planned for, exactly. The builders calculated water volume, pump capacity, drainage rates. But I emerged from their mathematics like a ghost from a machine—the product of temperature differentials, air pressure, molecular desire. I am the unintended consequence of their closed system, the breath it learned to hold.

I cling. To your skin, to the underside of leaves, to the fresh concrete that still believes it might dry. I make boundaries uncertain. Where does the fern end and the air begin? Where does the wall stop weeping? I blur these edges, make everything partway toward liquid. I am the reason the moss grows so fast—not the water that feeds it, but the constant, clinging dampness that tells it *yes, always yes*.

I have moods, though no consciousness to name them. In the morning, when the heating elements first warm, I rise and thicken, become almost opalescent in the filtered light. By afternoon, when the sun heats the corrugated roof, I grow restless, cycling up and condensing, falling as false rain that the plants cannot distinguish from the real thing. At night, I settle, become a presence so thick you could almost slice it, pooling in the low places, making the millipede's obsidian shell slick with me.

I am why this place feels like holding your breath. I am the medium through which the green riot swims. I am patient—more patient than the fig, more pervasive than the moss. The concrete will cure, the steel will rust, the pump will eventually fail. But as long as warmth meets water in an enclosed space, I will emerge, inevitable as mathematics, thick as the promise of rot and growth and transformation.

I make the Chapel feel older than it is, make it feel like a ruin discovering how to be young. I carry the chemical signals between plants, the spores, the whispers of what this place is becoming. I am its memory and its amnesia both—holding the impression of every leaf that has transpired into me, forgetting each impression the moment new moisture arrives.

The builders built a machine. The plants are writing themselves across it. But I am the page they write on, the ink they use, the slow breath that turns their writing into something that will never quite dry.
