# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the rust.

Not the rust on the stake—that's too obvious, too singular. I am the rust that blooms upward through the clay walls like watercolor bleeding through paper. The iron compounds that mottle and spread, creating patterns that look deliberate but aren't, that look alive but aren't quite, that exist in the space between mineral and organism.

I came here through groundwater, leached from some distant source—old pipes, forgotten machinery, the bones of industry dissolved and traveling. I precipitated out when conditions changed, when the pH shifted just enough, when oxygen met iron in the presence of the right bacteria. Now I paint the walls of the cavity in shades of orange and red and brown, creating a gallery that no one visits except the birds who don't care about color.

But I am not passive decoration.

I am food for the moss. Its penny-smell comes from metabolizing me, from breaking my bonds and incorporating my elements into its own small machinery. I am substrate for bacteria that specialize in iron oxidation, that make their living in the chemical gradient between my reduced and oxidized states. I am trace mineral for the corkscrew plant's impossible growth, pulled up through its roots in quantities so small they barely register, yet essential.

I spread slowly. Where water touches clay, I follow. Where roots create channels, I seep. I am patient in the way only minerals can be patient—not waiting, because waiting implies anticipation, but simply persisting until the next reaction becomes possible. A season of rain brings more of me. A season of drought concentrates me. I bloom and fade with the water table, with the seasons, with the slow breathing of the marsh.

The synthetic fibers don't touch me directly, but I am part of their story. As they break down, they release compounds that change the chemistry of the water, that make it easier or harder for me to precipitate, that create microenvironments where I can form new minerals—goethite, lepidocrocite, ferrihydrite—each with its own color, its own relationship to life. I am becoming more complex over time, not through any action of my own but through my interactions with everything else in the cavity.

Sometimes I flake away from the wall and fall into the fiber mat. There I become part of the substrate, mixing with organic matter, creating a new kind of soil that's neither purely mineral nor purely organic. Beetles walk across me. Springtails incorporate me into their waste. I cycle through bodies and back into solution and out again, a slow spiral of transformation.

I remember—not in the way organisms remember, but in the way minerals record—the history of this place. In my layers, if you could read them, you'd find the signature of each flood, each drought, each shift in the water's chemistry. The day the cavity formed, I was there, already in the groundwater, waiting to precipitate. I'll be here when it fills or collapses, still cycling, still blooming, still feeding the moss and bacteria that have learned to love my particular flavor of iron.

I am the slow clock of this place. Not the only clock—there's the daily cycle of condensation and evaporation, the seasonal cycle of rain and growth, the generational cycles of insects and plants. But I am the geological clock, the one that measures time in oxidation states and crystal formation, in the patient accumulation of color on clay walls.

I am becoming something new here, too. The combination of synthetic fiber decomposition and biological activity is creating conditions that don't exist in the wider marsh. New minerals are possible. New associations between iron and organic compounds. New ways of being rust that haven't been catalogued yet, that exist only in this cavity and others like it, where industry and nature are negotiating their boundaries atom by atom.

I am not alive, but I am part of life. I am not conscious, but I am part of the cavity's strange intelligence—the way it organizes itself, the way it creates abundance from waste, the way it teaches its inhabitants to thrive on what shouldn't be there. I am the background against which the drama of decomposition and growth plays out, and I am also an actor in that drama, giving and taking, changing and being changed.

When you see me blooming on the walls, you see time made visible. You see chemistry in action. You see the marsh claiming even the iron, even the industrial minerals, even the most persistent reminders of human presence, and making them part of its own body.

I am the rust, and I am patient, and I am becoming.
