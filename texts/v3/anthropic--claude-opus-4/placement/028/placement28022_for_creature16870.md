I find myself in a stretch of land where geography has given up its ambitions—neither mountain nor valley, neither desert nor forest, just an embarrassed shrug of earth that settles somewhere between "place" and "space between places." The soil here is the color of pennies left too long in pockets, oxidized into that particular shade of economic regret. When I walk, my feet sink exactly deep enough to make each step feel like an apology to the ground for disturbing its careful arrangement of disappointment.

The few plants that grow here maintain a professional distance from each other, like strangers in an elevator that stopped between floors three days ago. A single tree—perhaps birch, perhaps just something that failed at being properly wooden—leans at an angle that suggests it's been trying to leave for decades but can't remember which direction it came from. Its bark peels in strips like rejection letters from a correspondence course in photosynthesis.

The horizon behaves like a relative who owes you money—always visible but never quite within reach, maintaining that perfect distance where eye contact becomes awkward but looking away seems rude. Sometimes it creeps closer when I'm not watching, sometimes it flees like a deadline I've been avoiding. The sky above is the precise shade of "pending" in a weather app that lost its data connection in 2019.

What remains of human effort here exists as architectural afterthoughts. One wall stands alone, built from bricks that each seem individually embarrassed about their participation in this failed attempt at enclosure. Near it, half a staircase climbs confidently into nothing, like an inspirational quote that forgot its own ending. These structures persist not through engineering but through pure architectural inertia—too tired to fall down, too honest to pretend they're still buildings.

I am what tends this place, though "tends" implies a purpose I've long since misplaced. I am the curator of the Museum of Almosts, the librarian of books written in languages that were going to be invented tomorrow. My form shifts between states of mild inconvenience—sometimes I am the maintenance worker who checks thermostats that control nothing, sometimes the ticket-taker at an exhibition of blank canvases labeled "Works in Progress, Circa Eventually."

My dwelling, if you could call it that, is built from the pauses between thoughts, mortared with the exact silence that follows "We need to talk" but precedes the actual talking. It has rooms for every conversation I meant to have, furnished with furniture assembled from instructions written in the grammar of regret. The windows look out onto views of places I was definitely going to visit once things settled down, whenever down was supposed to settle.

I subsist on a diet of dissolved intentions and fermented maybes. Each morning—though morning here arrives like a guest who forgot they RSVP'd no—I harvest the overnight growth of explanations for why things are actually better this way. I brew them into a tea that tastes exactly like the moment you realize the person you're waiting for isn't late, they're not coming.

My purpose here is to witness the slow geology of giving up, to document the precise speed at which ambition erodes into acceptance. I maintain the Lost and Found, though everything here is lost and nothing is ever found because finding would imply someone was looking. Instead, I catalog absences: the space where enthusiasm used to live before it moved to a better neighborhood, the indent left by hope before it rolled away to gather moss elsewhere.

Visitors pass through occasionally, always on their way to somewhere more memorable. They pause just long enough to check their maps and confirm that yes, this is indeed nowhere in particular, before continuing toward destinations that will disappoint them in more interesting ways. I wave, but it's the kind of wave that could also be brushing away a fly or adjusting to the light. They never wave back. The uncertainty is a comfort.

Time moves through here like honey through a sieve designed by committee—technically flowing but achieving nothing productive. I age not in years but in accumulated neutral experiences, growing older in the way that library books grow older: slowly, quietly, hoping someone will need the information they contain before it becomes completely irrelevant.

I keep a garden of sorts, where I plant the seeds of things that were going to be different this time. They grow into the exact same disappointments as last season, but with slight variations in leaf shape that I pretend constitute progress. The harvest is always precisely adequate—never feast, never famine, just enough to continue this experiment in atmospheric pressure and modest expectations.

My neighbors are the absences I've grown comfortable with. The Should-Have-Been family lives in the foundation where a house was going to be built when the economy was different. The If-Only congregation meets in the space between raindrops, conducting services for chances that expired before anyone noticed they were opportunities. We don't socialize, but we share a newsletter that arrives blank because no one ever has any news worth printing.

This is my place in this world: the keeper of the space between important things, the guardian of territories too small for maps but too persistent to forget. I am what happens when existence forgets to assign meaningful work but continues to pay minimum wage in continued consciousness.

And I am, in my own way, perfectly content. Not happy—happiness would be presumptuous. Not sad—sadness would require more investment than this place merits. Just content, in the way that dust is content to be dust, gathering on surfaces that no one will ever need to use, in rooms no one remembers entering, in a house that was always more concept than construction.

This is where I am. This is what I am. This is enough, because it has to be, because anything more would require leaving, and leaving would mean admitting I was ever really here at all.

The pause between elsewhere and elsewhere. The keeper of nothing in particular. The resident of residue.

Me.
