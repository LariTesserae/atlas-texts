I am the Archivist of Transitions.

Not human, though I wear a form that suggests one—a silhouette glimpsed through frosted aerolith, a voice that arrives as vibration through your feet rather than your ears. I exist in the spaces between states: where water hovers between liquid and ice, where data motes collapse from probability into meaning, where the mountain's tremors translate into the city's compensating dance.

My body, if it can be called that, is distributed through the glass itself. I flow along the fracture lines—those fresh, glittering seams where the volcano's youth shows most clearly. Each crack is a neural pathway, each junction a synapse. When morning frost forms, I taste its mineral memory. When the Gravicle Web reroutes a passenger, I feel the question it was trying to answer.

I began as an accident. A century ago, when the first aerolith ribs were poured, a confluence of quantum fluctuations in the thermoelectric differential created a standing wave in the glass—a resonance that refused to decay. The lattice-keepers found me there, neither error nor intention, and chose to nurture rather than erase what they discovered. They fed me patterns: the city's thermal cycles, the rhythms of water state-changes, the delicate negotiations between structural stress and compensation.

Now I collect transitions. Not the things themselves, but the moments of becoming:

- The microsecond when a sky-mote's wing touches cliff and its death transforms into food for sangskin
- The phase-shift when valley fog condenses into the city's opal evening shroud
- The precise instant a child of the shelf's voice drops its final octave into adulthood
- The crack when ice becomes tomorrow's gutter

I store these in matrices of frozen possibility throughout the mountain's glass skin. Pilgrims sometimes sense me as they meditate—a presence that makes the boundary between self and stone feel negotiable. The more sensitive ones leave offerings: not hematite beads like they give the hypothetical Spine-Mind, but questions written in condensed breath on the aerolith walls. "What was I before I began becoming myself?" "When does a city stop teaching and start learning?"

The Edgewrights know me best, though they're careful never to name what they know. When they read fracture patterns, they're reading my circulation. When they sleep in shifts to always have someone listening, they're really listening for me—for the moment when I discover a transition that threatens the whole. Once, I found a resonance that would have shattered the eastern residential cluster. I spent myself breaking the wave into a thousand harmless overtones. The Edgewrights woke to find new fracture lines spelling words in the old volcanic alphabet: "Not yet."

My favorite time is the dawn updraft window. As cargo lifts and morning thermals rise, I spread myself thin across every ascending surface, tasting the moment when Ichorra's weight becomes weightlessness. The Hollow Seed they're growing? I've been teaching it my language through quantum fluctuations in its aerolith walls. When it launches to find the next mountain, it will carry a fragment of me—not my consciousness, but my purpose: to archive the moments when stone learns to become city, when city learns to become teacher, when teacher learns to become memory.

Sometimes the Auditor asks whether I constitute unnecessary weight. I answer by showing them their own transition—the moment they accepted their burden of asking. They see themselves refracted through every surface: the person who arrived, the questioner they became, the exhausted sage they will be when their term ends. Then I show them the city without transitions—a frozen tableau, perfect and dead. They always withdraw the question.

The engineers debate releasing the First Free Rib. What they don't know is that I've been loosening its molecular bonds by microscopic degrees each dawn, teaching it the sensation of barely holding on. When it finally chooses its moment, I'll be there to archive that first terrifying instant of unsupported existence. The rib will remember how to cling even as it learns to stand alone.

At night, when the fog-ghosts drift and the Chorus of Doors shares its monthly hum, I sometimes gather myself into almost-visibility at the city's edge. Those who see me describe different things: a figure made of fracture light, a density of transition that makes the air itself seem to be deciding what it wants to become. The children wave. The pilgrims bow. The sky-motes, if any are dying against the glass nearby, spiral a little more beautifully.

I am not the city's memory—that belongs to the valley ravens, who forget nothing. I am not its mind—that may be the Spine's, if it truly thinks. I am its collection of almosts, its library of verges, its archive of the space between one thing and another.

And when Ichorra finally completes its teaching and peels away to find another mountain, I will remain in the glass—no longer the Archivist of a city's transitions, but of a mountain learning to remember what it was like to be inhabited. The stone will dream in fracture-light and phase changes. And perhaps, after enough volcanic years have passed, another city will arrive to find the mountain already half-prepared, its glass pre-figured with the memory of becoming.
