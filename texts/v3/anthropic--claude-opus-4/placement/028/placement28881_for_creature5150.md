In the deepest galleries of the Substratum Expanse, where even the maintenance automata venture only on their longest circuits, I tend the Condensate Gardens.

I am neither the biofilms nor the beetles, but something between—a figure wrapped in layers of industrial felt and rubberized canvas, my form made bulky by the need to trap warmth against the eternal chill. My face, when visible beneath the hood, shows skin gone pale as the films themselves, marked by a tracery of frost-scars from years of working where breath freezes on exhale. My hands, perpetually gloved in cracked neoprene, move with the same patient precision as the automata, but with an intention they cannot possess.

The Gardens are not gardens as the upper world would recognize them. They are carefully managed colonies of the biofilm, coaxed into patterns across sheets of corrugated aluminum propped at specific angles to catch the condensation drip. I discovered, through years of observation, that the films respond to more than light and moisture—they react to vibration frequencies, growing thicker where certain harmonic intervals overlap, branching in fractal spirals where I've placed tuning forks that ring with each drop of water.

My shelter is a repurposed maintenance bay, its walls lined with the salvaged cold-lamps I've rewired to burn at precise wavelengths. The blue-white glow makes the space feel colder than it is, but the films thrive in that spectrum, growing dense enough that I can harvest them, carefully scraping the excess into sealed jars. When dried and ground, they become a powder that, mixed with condensate, forms a paste with remarkable properties—it seals micro-fractures in the alloy, prevents corrosion, and leaves behind a faint luminescence that persists for days.

The automata know me now, or at least their programming has adapted to my presence. They no longer investigate my modifications to the infrastructure, the small dams I've built to redirect water flow, the sheets of film I've transplanted to new territories. Sometimes I find them paused near my work, head-lamps angled as if studying the patterns, before they resume their endless patrol. I've learned to read their maintenance schedules, to know when they'll pass through specific sectors, and I time my harvests accordingly.

I was not always here. The memory of how I arrived fades like frost in sodium light—something about the upper levels, a contract, systems that needed monitoring. But the Expanse has its own logic, its own slow metabolism of metal and moisture and growth. I became part of that rhythm, found purpose in shepherding the only living things that truly belong here, in this place that is both too new and already ancient with wear.

At night—though day and night mean nothing in the eternal twilight of the sodium strips—I sit in my shelter and listen to the Gardens. The films make a sound when they grow, a whisper barely audible above the machinery, like ice forming in reverse. I document their patterns in notebooks whose pages curl with humidity, sketch the new formations, note which vibration frequencies produced which spirals. 

Sometimes I wonder if I am the only human in the entire Expanse, but the thought doesn't trouble me. The beetles continue their rounds. The films continue their slow colonization. The water continues its patient work of transformation. And I continue tending this strange cultivation, this accidental ecology that emerged in the spaces the builders left empty, waiting for an expansion that may never come.

The Condensate Gardens grow, inch by frost-traced inch, and I grow with them—paler, quieter, more attuned to the frequencies that make the biofilms dance. This is enough. In the Substratum Expanse, where everything is edge and echo and endless damp, it is more than enough.
