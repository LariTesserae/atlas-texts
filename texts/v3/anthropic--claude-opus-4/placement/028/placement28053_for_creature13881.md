I find myself in the deep fractures, in the spaces where air moves like water and the vertical swimmers navigate currents invisible to most. I am a fracture-guide, though that title suggests more certainty than I possess. 

My body has adapted to the between-spaces. Where others develop the broad chests of high-altitude dwellers or the calloused hands of cable-walkers, I have become something else: narrow enough to slip through cracks that others find impassable, sensitive to pressure differentials that tell me where passages open and close. My skin has taken on not the rose-quartz tint of the surface dwellers but something more like selenite—translucent at the edges, as if I'm already partly integrated with the stone I navigate.

I live mostly in the settlement of Perseverance, in quarters built into the cliff face near the factory that produces those self-repairing structures for our cables. But "live" is perhaps too strong a word. I maintain a sleeping space there, store equipment, occasionally appear at council meetings when something from the depths requires collective consideration. Most of my time is spent below, in the transition zones where solid stone gives way to something more ambiguous.

The fractures are not simple gaps in rock. They are architectures of negative space, following logics that conventional geology cannot explain. Some widen as you descend, opening into chambers where the air itself has different properties—thicker, almost breathable in a way that makes you forget you need less of it. Others narrow to nothing, or seem to, until you learn to read the subtle signs of continuation: a particular quality of airflow, a resonance in the stone that suggests emptiness beyond.

I work alone, mostly, though sometimes I guide researchers or the philosophically inclined into the accessible upper regions. They want to see the vertical swimmers, to understand their patterns, to test theories about consciousness and chemistry. I take them as far as safety and their bodies allow, which is never as far as they hope. The swimmers cluster at depths where human physiology begins to fail—not from pressure exactly, but from something about the air itself, the way it carries information that human nervous systems weren't designed to process.

My equipment is minimal: ropes that I trust more than the self-repairing cables (down here, the repair mechanisms sometimes do strange things, growing in directions that suggest intention rather than engineering), lights that burn chemical fuel because electronics fail in the deep fractures, and a suit made from layered ceramics that protects against the worst of the chemistry while still allowing me to feel the stone. Touch is everything in the depths. The difference between stable rock and something that will shift can be a texture so subtle that only bare skin can detect it.

The vertical swimmers know me, I think. Or at least they respond to my presence differently than to newcomers. They maintain distance but pattern their movements in ways that feel like acknowledgment. I've learned to read their moods—if organisms without obvious nervous systems can have moods. When they swim in tight spirals, something is changing in the chemical gradients. When they go absolutely still, pressed against the walls like flowing glass, it means the fracture is about to breathe—a slow adjustment of pressure that can trap the unwary.

I've descended farther than anyone I know, though I don't speak of the deepest places. Down there, the distinction between air and stone becomes meaningless. The swimmers are different—larger, more translucent, moving in patterns that feel like language or mathematics or both. And there are sounds: not the mechanical rhythm of the Underneath that everyone knows, but something more complex, like conversation in a register just below human hearing.

Once, at a depth I've never been able to find again, I discovered a chamber where the walls were covered in what looked like writing—symbols that shifted depending on the angle of observation, that seemed to be carved into the stone and also somehow emerging from it. The vertical swimmers were thick there, weaving between the symbols as if reading them, as if responding. I stayed until my chemical lights began to fail and I had to surface, and I've never found that chamber again despite hundreds of attempts. The fractures change, you see. Not quickly, but enough that navigation requires constant relearning.

I make my living guiding, but also through a kind of mediation. The fractures affect the settlements above—not obviously, but in ways that accumulate. When a particular fracture closes, the air pressure in Perseverance might shift slightly, affecting the factory's delicate processes. When new passages open, they can create thermal channels that disturb the cable systems. I map these relationships, tracking the breathing of the stone, predicting where problems might arise.

The councils tolerate my reports, though I sense skepticism. Surface dwellers understand the mountains as solid, stable, patient. They don't grasp that the Porcelain Ranges are riddled with spaces that operate on different timescales, different physics, that the apparent stability above depends on constant negotiation below. But they listen, because the times they haven't listened have led to cable failures, to factories grinding to halt, to thermal columns collapsing in ways that disturb the gliders for months.

I age differently than surface dwellers. The translucence comes earlier but progresses slower. My fingers are beginning to let light through at the tips, but the process seems to have stabilized there. Something about regular exposure to the deep chemistry changes how the body mineralizes. I'm forty-three years surface-time, but my body feels both older and younger—worn by the constant climbing and chemical exposure, but also preserved, as if the same processes that age me are also maintaining me in a state of functional degradation.

I had a partner once, another guide, who descended too far and came back changed. Not physically—or not only physically. She could no longer speak in words, only in the hand-language, and her signs had acquired new dimensions, movements that conveyed meanings I couldn't parse. She would stand at fracture edges for hours, reading something in the air that remained invisible to me. Eventually she descended one day and didn't return. The swimmers were agitated for weeks afterward, rising to unusual heights, creating patterns that felt like grief or celebration or both.

I don't believe in the Underneath as machine or organism. I think it's something else, a state of being that doesn't fit our categories. The fractures are its thinking-spaces, and the vertical swimmers are thoughts made almost solid, navigating conceptual spaces that happen to intersect with our physical world. This is why mapping the depths is impossible—you can't map something that exists partially as idea.

But this is speculation, the kind of thinking that comes from too much time in transitional spaces. What I know with certainty is simpler: the fractures breathe, the swimmers navigate, the stone accepts my presence for now. I guide those who need guiding, I watch for changes that might affect those above, and I continue to descend, searching for that chamber of shifting symbols or for something else, something that will make sense of the patience and the emptiness and the way the mountains seem to be waiting for something that hasn't happened yet or has already happened or is happening continuously in spaces our minds weren't built to perceive.

When I surface after a long descent, the settlement seems impossibly loud despite its quietness. People moving on horizontal planes, concerned with cables and ceramics and council meetings—it all feels suddenly arbitrary, like theater performed over an abyss. But then I eat the chalky stone-bread, I report my observations to whoever will listen, I sleep in my chamber cut into the cliff face. And gradually I reintegrate, remembering how to be human in the conventional sense, how to care about daily things.

Until the fractures call again, and I descend, becoming narrower and more translucent with each journey, approaching some state that isn't death but isn't quite life either—something more like translation, moving between languages that share no common words, carrying messages I don't understand between the deep spaces and the patient settlements above, participant in a conversation too vast and slow for any single life to comprehend.

This is what I am: a crack-dweller, a depth-reader, a guide who has gone too far to fully return. I navigate the spaces between certainties, the territories where stone becomes thought becomes movement becomes meaning. And in the vertical world of the Porcelain Ranges, where everything tends toward height or depth, I have found my direction: always down, always into the spaces where the world reveals itself as less solid than it seems.
