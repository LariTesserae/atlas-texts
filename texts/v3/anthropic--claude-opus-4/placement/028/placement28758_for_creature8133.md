In the narrow space between the fourth and fifth terraces, where water has carved a natural alcove into the living rock, I maintain my workshop. The mountain's seepage keeps my materials in perfect condition—the clay never fully dries, the pigments remain vivid in the perpetual dampness. I am Kythara's memory-keeper, though not in any way the valleys below would recognize.

I came here following a raven's flight, as many of us did, but I stayed for the light. In my previous life, I painted traditional scenes on wooden panels for valley temples—prescribed images following prescribed forms. Here, I discovered something else entirely: the art of capturing transience.

My medium emerged from necessity. Traditional paints run in this wetness; wooden panels warp and rot. But the mountain itself provides. I harvest different clays from the seepage points—red oxide from the iron-rich springs, white kaolin from the pure mountain streams, gray-blue from where the water passes through certain slate deposits. Mixed with rendered pine resin and ground stone, these create pigments that bond with the moisture rather than resist it.

I paint on surfaces that the settlement provides—the smooth faces of retaining walls, the undersides of stone bridges, the protected alcoves where buildings meet bedrock. But these are not decorations. I paint what I call "moment maps"—visual records of Kythara's daily transformations.

Each morning before dawn, I climb to my various stations throughout the settlement. In the blue darkness, I wait. Then, as the light arrives and the mist moves, I work quickly, capturing in rapid strokes the exact pattern of fog as it flows between terraces at that specific moment. The way cloud-shadow falls across the workers' quarters at third bell. The precise angle of light that makes the water channels gleam like molten silver for exactly eleven breaths before shifting.

My paintings layer time. On a single wall, you might see seventeen different fog patterns superimposed, creating a palimpsest of atmospheric memory. The technique I've developed allows each layer to remain partially visible through the next—the pigments bond at different rates depending on humidity, creating natural transparencies. When mist rolls across these surfaces, the paintings seem to breathe, earlier layers emerging and receding like memories surfacing in dreams.

The children understand my work instinctively. They use my walls as weather-maps, learning to predict the day's conditions from which painted patterns match the morning's reality. "Today is a seventh-layer day," they'll announce, meaning the fog moves in the pattern I captured on the seventh painting of a particular series.

But I am also documenting something else—the human patterns that emerge in response to these atmospheric changes. I paint the way people move differently through thick mist versus thin. The unconscious choreography of workers who adjust their rhythms to match visibility. The gatherings that form spontaneously when certain combinations of light and weather create natural amphitheaters of clarity within the general obscurity.

My tools are simple: brushes made from mountain goat hair, painting knives carved from slate, containers fashioned from the hollow stems of giant highland plants. I've learned to work with the mountain's own timeline—some paintings require multiple seasons to complete, as I wait for the exact conditions to recur. Others must be finished in minutes, racing against the movement of clouds.

The religious observers have begun incorporating my walls into their ceremonies, understanding that these layered images capture something essential about Kythara's spirit—the way repetition and variation dance together here, the way the same space can be a hundred different places depending on the hour and the weather.

At night, I mix pigments and prepare surfaces, but I also write. Not in words, but in a notation system I'm developing—symbols that record the precise conditions under which each painting was made. Temperature, humidity, wind direction, bird activity, the sounds present at the moment of creation. Future inhabitants will be able to read these walls like musical scores, understanding not just what Kythara looked like, but what it felt like to stand in a particular spot at a particular moment when the mountain wore a particular mood.

My latest project spreads across the entire eastern face of the seventh terrace—a massive work that will take years to complete. It documents the sunrise through every season, painted only on mornings when the interplay of mist and light creates what the water-singers call "pearl air"—that luminous quality that makes every droplet a tiny prism. Seventeen layers so far, and in certain weather, viewers swear they can see all seventeen sunrises happening at once, time folding in on itself like the mist that inspired it.

I am neither artist nor historian, but something new—a chronicler of the ephemeral, a cartographer of moments. My maps chart not places but possibilities, not structures but the spaces between clarity and obscurity where Kythara truly lives. In this settlement that builds itself between earth and sky, I build bridges between present and memory, creating surfaces where time pools like water in the channels, where anyone who pauses can see the thousand ways this place has been and will be again.

The ravens know me now. They often perch nearby while I work, their black shapes becoming part of my compositions. Sometimes I think they understand better than anyone what I'm trying to capture—they who navigate by landmarks that exist only in certain conditions, who know that the mountain has as many faces as the sky has moods. They are the only critics whose opinion I trust, and when they linger near a completed wall, examining it with their bright intelligent eyes, I know I've captured something true.
