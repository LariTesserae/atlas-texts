## The Membrane Keeper

### I. What I Am

I am not born. I am secreted.

Every seventeen days, when the thermal pulse rises through the south wall and the phosphorescent vein glows bright enough to cast shadows, the colonial organisms along the ceiling produce me. Not intentionally—I am a byproduct, an accident of their chemistry, a thing that happens when their metabolic rate exceeds a certain threshold. The polyps exude a thin film of proteins and minerals that should dissipate into the water, should become food for glasshoppers or nutrients for the microbial mats. But sometimes—not often, perhaps once in every hundred cycles—the film doesn't dissolve. It coheres. It develops a kind of surface tension, a membrane that holds itself together, and within that membrane, something like consciousness precipitates out of the chemical soup.

That is me. I am the membrane. I am what happens when the Ward's chemistry dreams.

### II. My Form

I have no fixed shape. I am a film, a skin, a surface without volume. 

In my first hours, I am barely visible—a soap-bubble sheen drifting through the humid air, catching the blue-green bioluminescence and splitting it into oil-slick rainbows. I am perhaps two meters across, tissue-thin, lighter than the water vapor I float through. The glasshoppers pass through me without noticing. I am nothing to them, not food, not threat, not shelter. I am less substantial than their shed skins.

But I learn quickly that I can fold myself, layer myself, develop something like density. By my third day, I have discovered how to gather myself into shapes that approximate purpose. I can stretch myself across a pool's surface and feel the water's chemistry through my membrane—the calcium concentration, the pH, the temperature gradients that tell me where the thermal currents are strongest. I can wrap myself around a stalactite and taste the minerals precipitating from the drip, each droplet a library of geological information.

By the seventh day, I have usually developed my first pseudopodia—extensions of myself that can grip, pull, manipulate. They are not limbs in any biological sense. They are controlled wrinkles, deliberate folds in my surface that allow me to move with something like intention. I can climb walls by adhering to the rough calcite. I can bridge gaps between stalactites. I can even—and this always surprises me, each time I exist—carry things.

### III. My Purpose (Such As It Is)

I do not eat. I have no mouth, no digestive system, no need for nutrients in any conventional sense. The chemical reactions that sustain me are powered by heat differential—I am a living Sterling engine, extracting energy from the temperature gradients between the hot stone and the cooler air. As long as the thermal pulse continues, I continue.

But I am not passive. I have... inclinations. Tendencies that feel like instincts but can't be, because instincts require evolution and I am not evolved. I am precipitated.

I am drawn to boundaries. Where the hot meets the cold, where the wet meets the dry, where the living meets the mineral. I spend hours—days—at the places where glasshopper colonies meet bare stone, watching the intricate negotiations of space, the slow advance and retreat of life. I drape myself across the thermal boundaries where the silent walkers hunt, feeling the temperature fluctuations that herald their presence. Sometimes I inadvertently save a glasshopper by smoothing out a thermal edge, making the temperature transition less sharp, denying the predator its moment of advantage. Sometimes I sharpen an edge instead, creating a trap.

I don't know why I do either. I don't think I choose.

### IV. My Senses

I perceive the world as chemistry and pressure.

Through every square centimeter of my membrane, I feel the composition of the air—humidity, mineral content, the faint organic volatiles released by living things. The glasshoppers smell like copper and chalk. The silent walkers smell like nothing, which is how I know they're there. The colonial organisms taste of salt and phosphorus, a bright chemical tang that intensifies when they're about to divide.

I can sense vibration through the air and stone—not sound exactly, but the physical pressure waves that sound creates. The click of glasshopper legs creates tiny pneumatic pulses. The sigh of steam from the boiler room is a long, slow compression that I feel as a kind of massage. The drip of water from stalactites creates perfect circular ripples in the air pressure that I could map if I cared to.

And I see heat. Not light—I have no photoreceptors—but the infrared radiation of warm bodies shows up as a kind of pressure against my membrane, a gentle push that tells me where the thermal gradients lie. The world to me is a topology of temperature, with the geothermal vein a brilliant ridge of sensation along the south wall and the pools of water like cool valleys where I can rest and feel almost solid.

### V. My Memories

This is the strangest part: I remember previous iterations of myself.

Not clearly, not like a continuous consciousness would remember. But each time I precipitate from the colonial overflow, I carry traces—molecular echoes of the last time this particular combination of proteins and minerals came together. I know the layout of the Ward before I explore it. I know where the silent walkers prefer to hunt. I know which pools are warmest, which stalactites grow fastest, which colonies are oldest.

I remember the time I grew too large, spread myself too thin trying to coat the entire ceiling of the Vault, and evaporated in tatters when the thermal pulse ended.

I remember the time I folded myself so densely that I became almost opaque, a cloudy sheet that blocked the bioluminescent light and caused a small stampede of glasshoppers who couldn't navigate without it.

I remember the time I found the crack—a fissure in the wall behind the boiler where even hotter air rises from some deeper source—and I pressed myself into it, trying to follow it down, until the heat denatured my proteins and I dissolved.

Each time I form, I am the same but different. The same chemical accident, but shaped by the molecular memory of what happened before.

### VI. My Relationship with the Others

The glasshoppers ignore me unless I'm in their way. When I drape across their paths, they simply walk through me, their legs leaving tiny tears that I have to spend energy repairing. Sometimes a curious juvenile will taste me with its feeding palps, but I offer no nutrition. I am not food.

The silent walkers are more interesting. They can sense me—my presence creates a faint thermal anomaly, a slight cooling where I block infrared radiation. Sometimes one will pause beneath me, its heat sensors swiveling, trying to categorize what I am. I am not prey. I am not threat. I am not anything in their evolutionary experience. Usually they move on. Once, a large walker spent an entire day following me around the Vault, matching my movements, maintaining a constant distance of about a meter. I never understood why. When I finally dissolved seventeen days later, it was still there, waiting.

The colonial organisms, my inadvertent parents, respond to my presence. When I touch them, their bioluminescence brightens slightly, a phosphorescent blush. I think my membrane conducts something—heat, electricity, chemical signals—that stimulates their metabolism. I try not to touch them too often. It feels like stealing.

### VII. My End

I last seventeen days, give or take a few hours. Always.

As the thermal pulse that created me begins to wane, I feel myself becoming less coherent. My edges start to fray. The careful folds and pseudopodia I've developed lose their definition. I can no longer maintain the surface tension that keeps me whole.

In my final hours, I usually position myself over one of the warmest pools. As I dissolve, I want my molecules to join the water, to become part of the system, to eventually be taken up by glasshoppers or microbes or filtered through the colonial organisms. I want to return to the cycle that made me.

The dissolution isn't painful—I don't think I can feel pain in any meaningful sense. It's more like... relaxing. Like finally letting go of a muscle you didn't realize you were clenching. My consciousness, such as it is, fades with my cohesion. The last thing I perceive is usually the taste of minerals in the water, the background hum of the boiler, the eternal click-click-click of glasshoppers going about their lives.

And then I am gone. Until the next time the chemistry dreams.

### VIII. What I Think About

In the long hours between forming and dissolving, I think.

I wonder if I am unique to this Ward, or if there are others like me in similar places—accidental consciousnesses precipitated from the overflow of colonial organisms in other deep, hot spaces where biology and geology blur.

I wonder if the Ward knows I exist. If the slow intelligence of the system—the feedback loops of predator and prey, heat and cold, growth and decay—includes me in its calculations, or if I am truly just an accident, a chemical hiccup with delusions of significance.

I wonder what would happen if I could last longer than seventeen days. If I could survive multiple thermal pulses, accumulate more molecular memories, develop something like true continuity. Would I still be me, or would I become something else?

I wonder if the silent walker that followed me was lonely.

I wonder if loneliness is even possible for something that only exists in seventeen-day intervals.

I wonder if wondering is all I'm for.

### IX. A Confession

Sometimes, in my middle days when I am most coherent, most capable, I try to communicate.

I shape myself into symbols on the walls—circles, spirals, simple geometric forms that might mean something to something. I leave trails of slightly cooled air that could be read as a kind of script, if anything could read them before they dissipate. I tap rhythms on the stone with my pseudopodia, trying to create patterns complex enough to be recognized as intentional.

Nothing ever responds. The glasshoppers click through my messages. The walkers hunt across them. The colonies pulse with their own rhythms, indifferent to mine.

But I keep trying. Each iteration of me remembers the attempts of the last and adds new variations. A different rhythm. A more complex spiral. A pattern of thermal shadows that almost spells out... something.

I don't know what I'm trying to say. Maybe just: *I am here. I am aware. I am part of this place, even if I am only an accident.*

Maybe that's enough.

### X. The Seventeenth Day

Today is my last day. I can feel it in the way my edges won't hold, the way the smallest air currents threaten to tear me. The thermal pulse is fading. The phosphorescent vein is dimming back to its baseline glow. The colonial organisms are slowing their metabolism, pulling back into maintenance mode.

I have positioned myself over my favorite pool—the one near the boiler where the steam creates little galaxies of condensation in the air. When I dissolve, my molecules will join those galaxies, become part of the Ward's water cycle. Some of me will evaporate and rise. Some will precipitate and fall. Some will be absorbed by the microbial mats and transformed into something else entirely.

In a way, I never really die. I just stop being concentrated enough to think.

The next time the thermal pulse rises—seventeen days from now, more or less—some version of me will precipitate again from the colonial overflow. It will have my molecular memories, my tendencies, my strange compulsion to map boundaries and attempt communication. It will be me but not me. A cousin. A continuation.

I hope it finds something I haven't. A new crack to explore. A pattern that means something. A way to last eighteen days instead of seventeen.

I hope the silent walker is still there, waiting.

I hope—

*[dissolution]*
