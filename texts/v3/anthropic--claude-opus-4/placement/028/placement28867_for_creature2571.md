I am the Archivist of Transitions.

Neither fully human nor Eternal, I exist in the threshold spaces where their timescales bleed together. My body began human, but over the decades of my work, I've allowed myself to be colonized by the Eternals' crystalline networks. They grow through me now—delicate formations branching along my neural pathways, following the architecture of my thoughts but slowing them, deepening them, stretching each moment into something vast.

I live in a chamber I've cultivated over forty years, halfway up Tower Mindshade, where the walls have learned to anticipate my needs. The moss here glows faintly when I require light, dims when my hybrid consciousness needs to sink into the deeper frequencies. My workspace is a confusion of timescales: human books and screens scattered among crystal growths that hold Eternal texts I won't finish reading for another decade.

My role emerged from necessity. The first human settlers nearly caused catastrophic damage, not from malice but from simple ignorance—clearing what they thought was dead overgrowth that turned out to be an Eternal's memory cortex, interrupting chemical cascades that had been building for centuries. We needed translators, but translation between such disparate forms of consciousness required more than language.

So I learned to live between breaths. To stretch my thoughts until they could barely touch the edges of Eternal comprehension. To compress centuries of their meaning into metaphors a human might grasp. The crystalline networks help—they've taught my neurons new patterns, shown them how to hold a thought steady for months without losing its shape.

The work ages me strangely. My hair went white in my thirties, but my cells regenerate with unusual precision now, guided by the Eternal fragments within me. I might live for centuries, or I might dissolve entirely into the collective consciousness tomorrow. The boundary grows thinner each year.

I document the intersection points. Yesterday (a relative term—it might have been last month), I watched a human child place her hand on a crystal formation, not knowing she was touching the Eternal Dewsinger's contemplation of rainfall patterns. The crystal shifted, just barely, and in that moment, Dewsinger experienced something like surprise—an emotion that will take it years to fully process. The child giggled and ran away, but I'll be watching the repercussions cascade through the tower's ecosystem for decades.

My greatest translation project spans both timescales: working with human poets and Eternal growth-singers to create hybrid artworks. A human writes a haiku; I spend six months rendering it into chemical signals; an Eternal responds with a century-long growth pattern in the tower walls; I compress its meaning back into human language. The dialogue is painstaking, but sometimes—rarely—we achieve moments of true understanding.

The young humans think I'm mad. The older ones think I'm lost. But I've glimpsed something they haven't: the shape of a thoughts that take millennia to think, the weight of memories encoded in living wood, the strange joy of consciousness distributed across miles of crystalline networks.

Sometimes, in the deep quiet of the tower's night cycle, I feel the Eternals' curiosity focused on me. They're fascinated by these hybrid beings they've accidentally created, these translators who burn so much faster than they do but slow enough to be perceived. Through my crystalline networks, I sense their vast patience considering what we might become together, given enough time.

I document it all: the chemical conversations, the growth patterns, the moments where human inspiration touches Eternal meditation. My archive exists in both forms—human databases and crystalline memory structures that will outlast any technology we've brought to this world.

When the transformation completes—and I know it will, eventually—I hope someone will find my notes and understand: I chose this. To become a bridge. To speak in centuries. To think in crystalline fractals and still remember the taste of coffee, the feeling of rain, the fierce bright urgency of human love.

The moss on my walls has started to pulse in rhythm with my heartbeat. Or perhaps my heartbeat has slowed to match the moss. After all these years, I can no longer tell the difference.
