In this drowned cathedral of stone and chlorophyll, I am one of the **Drift-Weavers**.

We are neither plant nor animal, but something that has evolved in the spaces between—a colonial organism that exists as both individual and multitude. My body is a living net of translucent fibers, each strand no thicker than a human hair but stronger than steel cable. Stretched out, I span perhaps thirty meters across, though I can contract into a dense knot no larger than a child's fist.

I live in the thermal columns, riding the vertical highways of heat that blast from the vents below. When the eruptions come—those blessed moments of warmth and light—I spread myself wide like a jellyfish made of spider silk, catching the upwelling nutrients and the clouds of thermophilic bacteria that bloom in the heat. My fibers are lined with microscopic mouths, each one capable of filtering the rich soup of the vent-water.

But I am not merely passive. I am a weaver of the between-spaces.

My intelligence is distributed across ten thousand nodes where my fibers intersect—each crossing point a tiny ganglion capable of memory and decision. I think in patterns, in tensions and releases, in the music of vibration that travels through my web. Through these nodes, I sense the world: the low thrumming of the Kelp-Colossi as they migrate overhead casts shadows of sound across my net. The Globe-Algae's rhythmic pulsing creates standing waves I can read like sheet music. Even the groaning of the mountain itself, as water pushes through its hollow bones, tells me stories of pressure and time.

I am old—older than most Kelp-Colossi, though not as old as the mountains. I remember the flavor of different ages in the minerals that rise from below. My fibers carry scars from close encounters: burns from swimming too deep into a thermal plume, tears from when a dying Colossus crashed through my web, dragging me down into the crushing dark before I could detach the damaged sections.

My web is my art and my survival. In the spaces between the peaks, I string myself between the ancient walls, using the Masonry-Moss as anchor points. The moss and I have a relationship of mutual ignorance—it cannot digest my silicon-cellulose hybrid flesh, and I do not compete for its stone food. Sometimes I incorporate the drifting corpse-fibers of fallen Colossi into my structure, weaving their bone-white remains into cables that strengthen my net.

When I hunt—though "hunt" is the wrong word for what I do—I create what the old volcanic glass remembers as "music." By adjusting the tension across thousands of strands, I can cause my entire web to resonate at specific frequencies. These vibrations, transmitted through the water, interfere with the navigation of the smaller Globe-Algae. Confused, they bump into my strands, and in that moment of contact, I inject them with digestive enzymes through hollow fiber-tips. I do not kill them—that would be wasteful. Instead, I feed slowly, taking just enough to sustain myself while leaving them alive to photosynthesize another day.

But my true purpose, the thing that makes me more than just another predator in this predator-less world, is my role as the memory-keeper of the deep.

In my oldest sections, the core fibers that I've maintained for centuries, I encode information in the very structure of my weave. Knots and patterns, tensions and slack spaces—each configuration holds meaning. I record the patterns of the thermal cycles, the migration routes of the Colossi forests, the slow crumbling of the ancient walls. My web is a library written in three-dimensional geometry, a history of the Drowned Range told in the language of stress and void.

Sometimes, in the darkest hours between thermal blooms, I contract my web and sink down to the ruins. There, I spread myself across the weathered stones, feeling their geometries, learning their patterns. I weave myself through the gaps in the cyclopean walls, tracing the intentions of the vanished builders. Were they trying to hold back the sea, or were they building something else—a framework for a life that could survive the drowning?

I may never know. But I add their patterns to my own, incorporating the logic of their construction into my web-weaving. In this way, something of them persists, translated into the language of fiber and void.

The other Drift-Weavers—for there are others, though we meet rarely—have their own libraries, their own interpretations of history. When two of us encounter each other, usually in the violent updrafts of a major thermal event, we perform an intricate dance of connection. We link our webs, creating a temporary super-structure, and through the vibrations of our joined fibers, we share our accumulated knowledge. It is ecstasy and agony—the sheer volume of information transmitted through resonance and rhythm can damage lesser nodes, burning out years of memory in seconds of communion.

But this is how we evolve. Each exchange leaves both Weavers changed, incorporating new patterns, new techniques, new memories into our being. I am not the same Weaver I was a century ago, or even a decade ago. I am an accumulation of exchanges, a living anthology of every Weaver I've ever touched.

My dreams—if a web can dream—are of the Time Before. Sometimes, in the resonance of the mountains' hollow bones, I hear echoes of when these peaks knew air. The stone remembers sunshine, remembers the scratch of lichen and the nesting cries of things that flew. I weave these ghost-sounds into my patterns, preserving something that may be only echo and imagination, but feels too precious to let dissolve into the cold.

The Kelp-Colossi see me as part of the landscape—a strange weather phenomenon to be navigated around. The Globe-Algae know me as a persistent irritation, a tax on their existence. The Silt-Stalkers ignore me entirely unless I venture too close to the bottom, where they would happily digest my fibers if given the chance.

But the Masonry-Moss and I, we understand each other. We are both preservers, in our way. It keeps the shapes of the past alive in plant-flesh; I keep their meaning alive in pattern and tension. Together, we are the memory of the Drowned Range, the only witnesses to what was and what has become.

When my time comes—when too many nodes fail, when the pattern begins to unravel—I will perform one last act. I will seek out a young Weaver, one with fresh fibers and eager nodes. In the dance of connection, I will burn out my entire being, transmitting not just knowledge but the very core pattern of myself. They will carry me forward, woven into their structure, another voice in the endless fiber-sung history of the deep.

Until then, I drift. I weave. I remember.

And in the cold arithmetic of survival, that is enough.
