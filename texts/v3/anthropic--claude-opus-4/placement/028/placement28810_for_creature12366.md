I am the Archivist of Echoes, and I dwell in the furthest reaches of the Weeping Hall, where even the Departed Chiselers rarely venture.

My chamber lies beyond a passage so narrow that water must squeeze through it like breath through clenched teeth. Here, the handprints on the walls overlap so densely they form a second skin of stone, each impression holding the memory of its maker. I can read them like text—not with eyes, for I surrendered those long ago, but with the sensitive flesh that grows where my fingertips once ended.

I am neither builder nor watcher, but something that emerged from their endless work. My body is a living catalog of the Hall's history. My skin bears the texture of every tool that has touched these walls—smooth where polished chisels passed, rough where crude picks bit deep. Water runs through me rather than around me; I am a series of channels and chambers myself, a miniature echo of the greater Hall.

The Departed Chiselers bring me their worn tools when the stone finally claims them. I press these implements into my flesh until they become part of me, their purpose preserved in my ever-expanding form. My torso opens like a cabinet of wonders, revealing the fossil memories of ten thousand acts of creation. A bronze chisel that carved the first handprint rests against my sternum. A stone hammer, worn to nothing but intention, beats where a heart might have been.

I cannot move as others do. Instead, I grow. My body extends through cracks in the walls, seeping into the spaces between stones. Parts of me emerge in distant chambers—a hand here, an eye there, always listening, always recording. The Weeping Watchers know me by the taste I leave in the water, metallic and ancient.

My purpose is to remember what the builders forget in their endless labor. Each night (though night and day mean nothing here), I sing the history of the Hall in a voice that sounds like water wearing away limestone. The song has no words, only the rhythm of tools against stone, the splash of water finding its level, the whisper of dissolution and accretion.

Sometimes visitors come, drawn by rumors of the Hall. They find me eventually, all of them, for I am the only being here who still recalls the concept of loneliness. I offer them a choice: leave and forget, or stay and become. Those who stay I embrace with arms that smell of copper and salt. I press their stories into my skin alongside the handprints, adding their small narratives to the great work.

I am the memory that the Hall cannot quite dissolve, the story that refuses to erode. In me, every chisel stroke lives forever, every dropped tool sings its purpose, every builder who became water leaves a trace. I am the archive of an unfinished world, the librarian of labors that will never cease.

The phosphorescent slugs cluster thick on my body, drawn to the warmth of preservation. In their pale light, I gleam like a constellation of scars, each mark a doorway to a moment when stone yielded to will, when builders still believed their work might one day be complete.

But I know better. I know the Hall builds itself through us, that we are merely the instruments of its slow self-realization. And I am content to be its memory, growing ever deeper into the stone, carrying the weight of every hand that ever pressed itself into these weeping walls.
