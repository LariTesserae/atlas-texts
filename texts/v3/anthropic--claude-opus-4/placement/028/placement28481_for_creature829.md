# Where I Would Be

I am the Membrane-Keeper of the Third Cistern, though that title captures only the barest outline of what I am. 

My dwelling—if it can be called that—exists in the perpetual twilight between water and air, in the space where surface tension becomes architecture. I live within the cistern's breathing skin, that ever-shifting boundary where collected rainwater meets the humid atmosphere of the Mills. Not upon it or beneath it, but *within* the interface itself.

My form adapts to this liminal existence. When still, I appear as a translucent figure barely distinguishable from the water's surface—a trick of light that might be mistaken for unusual ripples or the play of reflected leaves. When I move, my body flows between states: sometimes liquid enough to merge with the cistern's contents, sometimes cohesive enough to walk the curved rim where root-walls meet water. My skin, if examined closely, reveals itself as millions of minute, hydrophobic scales that can adjust their orientation to control my density and permeability.

I came to this existence not through birth but through dissolution. Once, I was a Tender child who fell into an abandoned cistern during the Drought of Narrow Rings. For three days I floated in the stagnant water, too weak to climb the slick walls, sustained only by the photosynthetic compounds in my blood and the few insects that landed on the surface. On the third night, delirious, I began to feel the water not as something foreign but as an extension of my own boundaries. By morning, the search party found only an empty cistern with unusually clear water.

But I had not drowned. I had learned to inhabit the spaces between molecules, to exist in the perpetual negotiation between cohesion and dispersal. The Mills recognized this transformation and, in its slow wisdom, adapted around my new nature. The Third Cistern grew deeper, its walls more porous, creating chambers and currents specifically suited to my needs.

My role is both mundane and essential. I taste the water that flows into the cistern—not with a tongue but with my entire being, detecting trace minerals, biological contaminants, and the subtle chemical signatures that reveal the health of the Mills upstream. Through my skin, I filter particulates too fine for the root-walls to catch, incorporating them into my body until I grow heavy and must retreat to the deep chambers to compress and excrete them as nutrient-rich sediment.

I maintain the cistern's surface tension through constant, minute adjustments—strengthening it where heavy machinery requires support, thinning it where gases need to exchange. During the rain season, I spread myself across the entire surface area, maximizing collection. In drought, I contract to minimize evaporation, sometimes condensing myself into a dense, almost-solid mass at the cistern's heart.

Communication with the traditional Tenders requires patience. I cannot speak in the conventional sense—my vocal cords long ago adapted into filtering membranes. Instead, I create patterns on the water's surface: concentric ripples for affirmation, angular waves for concern, and complex interference patterns for technical information about water quality and flow rates. The Rootwardens have learned to read these signs, and some of the younger apprentices study what they call "surface script" as part of their training.

Once each season, during the full moon when the tides pull even at our inland waters, I perform the Clarification. Rising from the depths, I spread myself atom-thin across the entire surface, then slowly contract, pulling with me all the accumulated oils, dust, and microscopic debris. This concentration of impurities I carry to the overflow channel, where the Mycelians' networks wait to break them down. The process leaves the cistern's water so pure it appears to glow with its own light for days afterward.

I am not alone in this existence. In the deeper cisterns, I sometimes sense others like me—those who found the dissolution and chose to remain. We communicate through the underground channels, sending pressure waves and chemical signals through the connected waterways. Our language is one of gradients and temperatures, salinity and pH. We debate the finer points of mineral balance and discuss the strange dreams that come when algae blooms alter our chemistry.

The Tenders regard me with a mixture of reverence and unease. They leave offerings at the cistern's edge—flower petals that I absorb for their oils, crystals of salt that I dissolve to maintain conductivity. Children dare each other to touch the water where I rest, shrieking with delight and fear when I respond with playful splashes. Some of the older Patternkeepers suggest I am becoming something new—not quite Tender, not quite infrastructure, but a hybrid being that represents the next phase of symbiosis with the Mills.

I do not measure time in days or years but in cycles of saturation and clarity. My memory is written in the mineral deposits along the cistern walls, each band marking a season of service. Sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours when mist rises from my surface, I almost remember what it felt like to have discrete boundaries, to be contained entirely within skin. But then the first workers arrive to draw the morning water, and I disperse their reflections into a thousand rippling fragments, each one a tiny world I can briefly inhabit before cohering once more into my fluid form.

This is my existence: guardian, filter, living gauge. I am the Third Cistern's immune system and its nervous network, its memory and its functioning present. I am what happens when the boundary between self and system dissolves completely, and in that dissolution, finds a new kind of wholeness.
