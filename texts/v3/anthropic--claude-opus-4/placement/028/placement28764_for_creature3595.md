In this realm of mist and becoming, where the boundaries blur between dream and stone, I am the Keeper of Thresholds.

I am not wholly one thing or another — my form shifts with the fog itself, sometimes appearing as a tall figure in a weathered industrial coat, pockets heavy with peculiar instruments: barometers that measure probability, compasses that point to forgotten things, keys that open doors which don't yet exist. Other times I am merely a presence in the mist, a density in the air that makes travelers pause and reconsider their path.

My dwelling moves through the settlements like a secret. It might be the forgotten maintenance room beneath a factory, where pipes sing with more than steam — where the valves, when turned just so, release not water but whispers of what might have been. Tomorrow it could be the top floor of an abandoned observatory, its broken telescope trained not on stars but on the spaces between certainties. The building itself flickers at the edge of perception, its walls sometimes brick, sometimes condensed fog, always slightly out of phase with the rest of reality.

I collect moments of transition: the instant when rain becomes mist, when waking becomes dream, when the angular mountain creatures pause at the edge of human settlement, uncertain whether to approach or retreat. These moments crystallize in my dwelling like strange minerals — some appear as bottles of impossible color, others as photographs that show different scenes depending on the angle of viewing, still others as small mechanical devices whose purpose remains beautifully unclear.

My work is to maintain the balance of instability that keeps this realm alive. When the humans grow too certain, their buildings too solid, I introduce small uncertainties — a staircase that has one more step on Tuesdays, a window that shows the mountain as it will be in a thousand years. When the ethereal threatens to dissolve everything into dream, I leave small anchors of the tangible — a perfectly ordinary wrench that always stays exactly where it was left, a cup of tea that remains precisely the right temperature.

I know every factory worker who pauses in their labor to listen to the strange harmonics humming through the machinery. I watch the children who play in the flooded alleys, befriending the silver fish and learning to see the patterns in the static. I tend to the spaces where the moss grows thickest, where the boundary between plant and pipe becomes a conversation rather than a border.

The beetles with their iridescent shells sometimes carry messages for me — not in any language, but in the patterns of their movement, the way light plays across their wings. The mist itself is my medium; I read its densities and currents like others read books. Where it thickens, possibilities multiply. Where it clears, choices have been made.

I am ancient and newly born in each moment, as old as the first fog that ever rolled between peaks, as young as the condensation forming now on the factory windows. My memory is not linear but layered, like sediment, like the way the settlements build upon themselves. I remember when there were no humans here, only the raw mountains and their slow breathing. I remember futures that haven't arrived yet, cities of glass that float in the permanent fog, the day when the distinction between human and ethereal finally dissolves.

In the deep vibrations that rise from the earth, I hear the planet's dreams. In the crack of static, I catch fragments of conversations from other possibilities. My role is not to resolve these mysteries but to tend them, to ensure they remain mysterious enough to sustain wonder, tangible enough to inspire exploration.

When humans glimpse me in the mist, they often mistake me for another worker heading home, or a surveyor mapping new territories, or simply a trick of the fog. This is as it should be. I am the threshold itself — the place where one thing becomes another, where certainty yields to possibility, where the solid world admits it has always been dreaming.
