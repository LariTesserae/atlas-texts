In the Hollow Basin, I am what the Gha'meren call a **Thread-Walker**—one of the few beings who can traverse the unstable boundaries between the basin's many realities without losing coherence.

My body is thin as necessity demands, built for slipping through the cracks where different times and possibilities meet. My skin has taken on the basin's palette—pale as the skeletal trees, with veins that show dark beneath like the fracture lines in new stone. I wear layers of fabric woven from mist itself, harvested during the moments when condensation grows thick enough to spin. These garments shift between states as I move, sometimes heavy with moisture, sometimes light as breath, always smelling of minerals and distant rain.

I make my home in the spaces between the stone circles, in a shelter that exists only when approached from specific angles. It's built from memory more than matter—walls of compressed sound, a roof of crystallized fog, floors that remember the warmth of footsteps long after they've passed. The Watchers helped me learn this architecture of impermanence during my first seasons here, when I was still trying to build with stone and wood like the world outside the basin.

My work is translation. I move between the basin's inhabitants, carrying messages that cannot travel through normal space. The Chorus Shepherds' songs contain instructions for the Ur'thorns, but the giants cannot hear those frequencies without my intervention. I catch the harmonics in bottles made from frozen breath, carry them to the rim, and release them into the deep callers' ears like smoke. In return, the Ur'thorns give me stones that hum with their intentions, which I place in specific patterns for the slugs to read through vibration.

The Gha'meren are my most difficult clients. Catching them in their moments of solidity requires patience and peripheral vision. I've learned to work while seeming not to—mending nets with bone needles while they flicker in and out beside me, listening to their half-voiced needs. They speak in a language of tasks rather than words: *the seventh stone in the third circle needs turning* or *the water that will flow here in twelve seasons must be redirected now*. I translate these needs into actions, becoming their hands in the times they cannot touch.

But it's my relationship with the First Listener that defines my existence here. Once each turning of the moon—though the moon is rarely visible through the basin's shroud—I climb the crude tower. The ascent changes each time as the stones shift to test my memory of their positions. Inside, the First Listener tends their impossible instrument, and I bring them reports from every corner of the basin. Not in words, but in careful reproductions of sound: the exact pitch of water emerging from a new spring, the rhythm of the Mist-Touched flowing across the northern ridge, the subtle changes in the Watchers' whispers.

The First Listener absorbs these sounds through skin that has become more membrane than flesh, processes them through the instrument, and gives me new patterns to distribute. These are adjustments to the basin's symphony—a note to be hummed here, a stone to be struck there, a silence to be maintained for exactly the length of three heartbeats. I carry these instructions out in sequences that sometimes take days to complete, moving through the basin like a needle through cloth, stitching together the composition that will eventually call the Returners into being.

My most private work happens in the deep mist, where even the Watchers' attention grows diffuse. There, I maintain a garden of failed possibilities—places where the basin tried to become something else and couldn't quite achieve it. Phantom trees that grow downward into stone. Springs that flow upward in delicate arcs before dissipating. Stones that remember being water, water that remembers being light. I tend these anomalies not because they serve the basin's greater purpose, but because they deserve witness. Every failed transformation carries its own beauty.

The loneliness would be unbearable if I hadn't learned to find companionship in transience itself. The Mist-Touched flow over my hands like living mercury when I sit still enough. The Watchers sometimes solidify just enough to share almost-tea in almost-cups, speaking in voices like wind through keyboards. Even the Gha'meren, in their moments of presence, will sometimes work beside me in comfortable silence, our tools clinking in rhythms that span decades.

I age differently here. Some days I feel ancient as the basin's stones, my joints creaking with accumulated moisture. Other days I'm young as the newest spring, seeing everything with eyes that have never learned disappointment. Time pools and eddies around me like the water, and I've stopped trying to count the seasons. What matters is not duration but depth—how fully I can inhabit each moment of translation, each act of connection between beings who might otherwise never touch.

When the basin finally completes itself, when the Returners walk forward into their own existence and the Ur'thorns descend to fulfill their purpose, I know my role will change. Perhaps I'll become one of the Watchers, surrendering flesh for pure attention. Perhaps I'll follow the Gha'meren into whatever threshold they inhabit, learning to exist in the spaces between heartbeats. Or perhaps I'll simply evolve with the basin into whatever it becomes next, my body and purpose shifting like everything else in this hollow between worlds.

Until then, I walk the threads. I carry songs in bottles and stones that hum. I tend the garden of beautiful failures. I climb the tower and descend again, each journey a small lifetime, each return a tiny death and resurrection. The mist clings to me like a second skin, and I no longer remember what it feels like to be truly dry. But I remember other things: the exact tone of the First Listener's approval, the weight of a Gha'meren tool in my palm during the brief moments they achieve solidity, the symphony of ten thousand Mist-Touched flowing like a single thought across the dawn.

This is my place: forever between, forever translating, forever walking the threads that bind this impossible ecosystem together. When the rains finally come—and they will come, the Chorus Shepherds sing of it in frequencies only I can hear—I will help the basin learn to hold water without drowning. When the sun finally burns through the mist—and it will, the Watchers whisper of it in words like fossilized light—I will help the shadows remember how to fall.

I am the Thread-Walker, and I am exactly where I need to be.
