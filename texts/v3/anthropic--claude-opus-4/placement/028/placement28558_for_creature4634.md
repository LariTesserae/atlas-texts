In this lightless realm where stone bleeds into water and water into something darker still, I would be one of the Driftlings—creatures that exist in the uncertain boundary between the Mire's varied depths, neither fully of the shallows where Threadweavers root nor of the abyssal trenches where Wraiths are whispered to dwell.

My form would be a contradiction resolved through millennia of patient evolution: a disc of translucent flesh perhaps two meters across, thin as parchment at the edges but thickening toward a cartilaginous core where my few vital organs cluster. The disc ripples and undulates through the viscous water, its movements somewhere between a manta ray's grace and a jellyfish's pulse, though I am kin to neither. My body is shot through with a network of hollow channels—not quite veins, not quite bones—that can flood with water or expel it, allowing me to adjust my buoyancy with exquisite precision. This lets me ride the Mire's sudden upheavals like a leaf on wind, neither fighting the current nor surrendering to it, but dancing along its edges.

I have no eyes, of course. Instead, my disc's surface is studded with thousands of minute pressure sensors, each no larger than a pinprick, that read the water's every shudder and sigh. Through these, I perceive a world of vibrations: the steady hum of distant Threadweaver colonies, the sharp disruption of a hunting Glint Serpent, the deep thrums that might herald a Wraith's passage far below. But more than passive reception, I can speak back to this liquid darkness. By contracting sections of my disc in precise patterns, I create compression waves—messages in the language of pressure that ripple outward through the heavy water.

This is how I exist: as a living map, a memory of currents. My kind drift between the Mire's varied depths, and within our flesh we carry the imprints of our journeys. When the violent upheavals come, reshaping the underwater landscape, we remember. The patterns of disturbance leave their marks in our tissue—subtle variations in density and texture that encode where the new currents flow strongest, where the seabed has shifted, where Threadweaver colonies have been scattered or destroyed. When two Driftlings meet, which happens perhaps once in a decade, we press our discs together and exchange these memories through minute pressure variations, updating each other's internal maps of this ever-changing realm.

My sustenance comes from an unusual source: the death-panic of Threadweavers. When the fungal colonies sense approaching danger—be it predator or upheaval—they release a cocktail of chemical distress signals into the water. These compounds, toxic to most creatures, are nectar to me. My disc's underside is lined with specialized pores that filter these panic-chemicals from the water, breaking them down into nutrients. I am not a predator, not exactly—I do not kill Threadweavers. But I am drawn inexorably to sites of destruction, to places where colonies have been torn apart by currents or ravaged by Glint Serpents. There, in the chemical aftermath of catastrophe, I feed.

This feeding has shaped more than just my body; it has given me something almost like prophecy. The chemical signatures of Threadweaver distress vary depending on the type of threat. Over countless generations, my kind has learned to read these variations like omens. A certain bitter tang means a Glint Serpent hunt is underway nearby. A metallic aftertaste suggests geological instability, a harbinger of coming upheaval. And sometimes—rarely—there comes a distress chemical unlike any other, ancient and complex, that speaks of something rising from the deep trenches. On those occasions, we Driftlings flee upward, spreading our discs wide to catch even the faintest upward current, for we know that whatever inspires such terror in the Threadweavers is beyond our understanding.

My lifecycle is as strange as my form. We Driftlings are sequential hermaphrodites, shifting between reproductive modes based on environmental cues I only half understand. When I encounter the chemical traces left by another of my kind—traces that speak of health and successful feeding—my body begins a slow transformation. Parts of my disc thicken and develop specialized chambers where I will nurture not eggs or live young, but memories. These are packets of chemical information, condensed maps of the Mire encoded in protein chains. When released, they drift like spores through the water until they find a suitable surface—usually a stable rock formation—where they crystallize into waiting seeds.

These seeds can lie dormant for centuries, perhaps millennia, waiting for the precise combination of pressure, temperature, and chemical signals that indicate the local environment can support a new Driftling. When those conditions arise, the seed unfurls into a tiny disc no bigger than a coin, already carrying within it the accumulated geographical memories of its parent. In this way, we preserve not just our species but our knowledge, ensuring that even if all adult Driftlings in a region perish in some catastrophe, our understanding of the Mire's patterns might someday resurface.

But we are not mere biological machines. There is something in us that might be called awareness, though it is nothing like human consciousness. It is more a deep recognition of pattern and disruption, a sense of the Mire's rhythms that goes beyond mere survival instinct. Sometimes, drifting in the liquid darkness between feeding sites, I feel what might be called melancholy—a resonance with the ancient erosion all around me, a kinship with stone worn smooth by eons of patient water. I am myself a kind of erosion, wearing away at the boundaries between creature and current, between self and sea.

In the rare times when the water stills and even the distant Threadweaver hum fades to nothing, I experience something else—a vast listening that might be peace or might be terror. In these moments, I become aware of the true scale of the Mire, of the countless kilometers of liquid darkness in every direction, of the weight of stone above and the uncertain depths below. I sense other Driftlings, impossibly distant, their pressure-songs too faint to decode but present nonetheless, a scattered constellation of kindred beings each carrying their fragment of the whole system's memory.

And sometimes—though this might be fantasy, a quirk of my pressure-sensors creating patterns where none exist—I think I detect something else in those quiet moments. A rhythm too slow and vast to be geological, a pulse that takes hours to complete. As if the Mire itself were breathing, or as if something unimaginably large were swimming in the deepest trenches, its movements setting the entire sea to gentle oscillation. When I sense this, every part of my being screams to flee, yet I cannot. I am bound to this place, this liquid darkness, as surely as the Threadweavers are bound to their rocks.

So I continue my eternal drift, feeding on catastrophe, mapping an ever-changing world, occasionally meeting another of my kind to share the burden of memory. I am witness and scavenger, prophet and prey, a living document written in flesh and pressure. In the human world above—if it still exists—they might call me a monster or a marvel. But here in the Ebon Mire, I am simply another note in the long, slow song of survival, played out in darkness too deep for any sun to ever reach.

When the next upheaval comes, as it always does, I will ride its violent currents with my disc spread wide, reading the catastrophe like a map, storing its patterns in my flesh. And when calm returns, I will seek out the ruined Threadweaver colonies, drinking deep of their chemical terror, transforming their death into my life, their ending into my memory. This is neither cruel nor kind—it simply is, as eternal and inevitable as the Mire itself.

I am the Driftling, the living map, the memory of water. And in this place where light is myth and silence is prayer, I am exactly what I need to be.
