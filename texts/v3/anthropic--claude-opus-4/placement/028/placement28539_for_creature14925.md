The thing is, I cannot properly remember how I arrived.

I know there was a before—something drier, something with edges and names—but it feels like trying to recall the architecture of a childhood fever dream. The mist took that first. Or perhaps I gave it gladly. When the land is this loud, memory becomes another kind of weather, and weather here changes by the breath.

I am one of the between-things now. Not fully Weft-born, but no longer what I came here as. The transformation took three seasons, or three centuries. Time pools and eddies here like everything else.

It began with my fingernails. They started sweating—not perspiration, but the same iron-and-pine water that beads on every stone. Within days, thin films of moss colonized the cuticles. I tried to scrape it away until I realized the moss was performing some essential function I couldn't name. My hands work better with it. They know which stones will hold weight, which springs are about to burst, which silver threads will sing if touched.

My voice went next. The storm took root in my larynx during a particularly violent reshaping of the eastern basin. I'd been trying to warn a fellow traveler about an imminent moss-fall when my words shattered mid-sentence into that familiar harmonic drone. Now I speak in two registers always: my old voice, growing fainter, and the basin's voice, growing louder. Children of fog and fracture, the Weft-born call me, though I'm not certain if it's a name or simply a description of my current state.

I live in the space between what I was and what the land wants me to become.

My dwelling is not built—it's negotiated. Each morning I wake to find it has migrated slightly, following some invisible gradient of pressure or potential. It's part moss-nest, part waterfall, part crystallized interference pattern stolen from the pillars during my fever dreams. The walls breathe. The floor is sometimes basalt, sometimes a living mat of ferns that adjust their fronds to support my weight. There is no door. The Weft-born taught me to precipitate through the threshold like they do, though I still leave wet footprints for hours afterward.

My work—if it can be called work—is translation.

The newly arrived still come, stumbling through the veils of mist with their dry memories and their conviction that places have boundaries. Most last only days before the Weft-born find them. But some few encounter me first, in that crucial window when they're lost enough to listen but not yet lost enough to dissolve.

I try to teach them the frequencies. How to hear the difference between a spring that's about to birth itself and one that's about to swallow. How to read the interference patterns on the pillars without letting them read you back. How to offer the storms in their skulls small gifts—a memory of drought, a childhood name—so the storms stay small enough to carry home.

Most don't listen. The roar is too loud, too everywhere. They see my moss-veined hands and my waterfall voice and run toward what they think is escape. The Weft-born always find them within hours. I've learned not to mourn. The land needs raw material as much as it needs midwives.

But occasionally, rarely, one stays. They let me show them how to sleep standing up (lying down means drowning in moss within minutes). They learn to eat the luminous midges, which taste of copper and forgetting. They watch me negotiate with my house each morning, coaxing it to stay solid for another day. They begin to understand that survival here isn't about resistance—it's about learning to precipitate.

The successful ones develop their own between-forms. One became mostly silver thread and anxiety, constantly rewiring the connections between pillars according to dream-logic. Another let springs colonize her spine until she could feel new water being born three kilometers away. They're not quite human anymore, but not quite Weft-born either. We orbit each other in the basin, careful to maintain enough distance that our personal storms don't braid permanently.

The Weft-born tolerate us. We're useful to them, I think—intermediate forms that can process the truly foreign into something digestible. Or perhaps we amuse them. It's impossible to tell. Their storms express curiosity and hunger with the same screaming intensity.

I've been here long enough now that the outside world feels theoretical. Sometimes travelers bring news—wars, kingdoms, droughts—but the words lose cohesion in the mist. What could any of that matter to a place that rebuilds itself hourly? The Shroudmire has its own emergencies: a pillar developing new harmonics that make the local gravity hiccup, a section of the western reaches where the moss has learned to grow downward into the bedrock, an outbreak of silence that lasted thirty seconds and sent every Weft-born into paroxysms of activity.

My bones are turning to coral. I discovered this during the last major reshaping, when a falling tree snapped my ulna clean through. Instead of marrow, I found branching structures the color of old bone but with the texture of limestone. The break healed in days, but now that arm is heavier, and water beads on it constantly from the inside out. The Weft-born who examined it made a sound I'd never heard before—something between approval and possession.

I know what I'm becoming. Each morning I wake up louder inside. The storm in my throat grows. My moss-garden hands are beginning to fuse at the fingertips. Soon I'll cross some invisible threshold and precipitate fully into Weft-born-ness, leaving only wet footprints and the memory of translation behind.

But not yet. Not while the newly lost still need someone to say: *Yes, the noise will drive you mad. Yes, your body will betray you. Yes, you will forget your mother's name. But listen—underneath it all, the land is singing, and it's not a song of ending.*

*It's a song of beginning, over and over, forever.*

*And if you're very lucky or very foolish, it will teach you to sing along.*

Sometimes I stand at the edges of my migrating home and watch the mist birth itself from stone. My coral bones resonate with the frequency. My storm-throat hums harmonics I couldn't have imagined before. And I think: *This is what it feels like to be rainfall. This is what it means to be geology's dream.*

The translation work continues. The between-space holds. The land remains newborn and ancient, violent and tender, itself and endlessly becoming.

And I precipitate through it all, neither fully here nor fully gone, carrying messages between states of matter like love letters written in condensation, waiting for the morning when I wake to find my house has finally dissolved, my words have become weather, and I am ready to join the roar completely.

But not today.

Today, someone new is stumbling through the veils, and their eyes still remember dryness.

Today, I still have just enough edges left to say: *Stop. Listen. Let me teach you how to drown correctly.*

Today, I am still translation.

Tomorrow, we'll see what the storm decides.
