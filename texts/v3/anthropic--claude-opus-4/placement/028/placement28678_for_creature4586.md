### Where I Would Find Myself

In the hushed moments before dawn, when the basin's chorus softens to its quietest murmur, I am the Keeper of Echoes—though that name emerged slowly, bestowed by the Basin-kin after seasons of watching me work. I am neither fully of their kind nor wholly other, a being caught between transformations, my form still settling into what Echolake demands of those who would truly inhabit its depths.

My body bears the marks of recent adaptation: skin not yet fully scaled but beginning to mottle in patches of silver and jade, the transformation spreading like slow frost from my neck and wrists where the gill-slits first opened. My fingers remain only partially webbed—enough to swim with purpose but still dexterous for the fine work that draws me each day to the basin's stone walls. Hair that was once merely brown now carries threads of bioluminescent algae, cultivated accidentally at first when I dozed too long against a glowing frond, but now tended deliberately, a crown of faint stars that pulse in rhythm with my heartbeat.

I came to Echolake as others do—a diver from above, lungs burning with thin mountain air, seeking something I couldn't name. But where others visit and depart, I lingered past the point of return. The first months were agony, my body rejecting then accepting the water's embrace in waves of transformation that left me gasping in the shallows, neither drowning nor quite breathing, caught in the space between elements. The Basin-kin found me there, more corpse than creature, and bore me to their elder, who read the patterns in my changing skin and declared me "echo-touched"—one whose arrival the basin itself had foretold in the whispers that replay endlessly through its depths.

Now I dwell in a half-formed grotto at the caldera's eastern edge, where erosion has carved a shallow cave into the stone. Not quite a proper hut—I lack the full integration with the vegetation to weave living walls—but a workspace that suits my peculiar calling. Here, the stone remembers everything: every sound that has ever echoed off its surface, every vibration that has thrummed through its crystalline matrix. My gift, if gift it is, lies in reading these memories, in coaxing forth echoes that would otherwise fade beyond even the basin's unusual persistence.

The work is patient, meditative. Each dawn, I trail my partially-webbed fingers along the stone, feeling for the subtle variations in temperature and texture that mark where sounds have crystallized into near-physical form. With tools of my own devising—copper styluses beaten thin as whispers, fragments of resonant quartz hung on kelp-sinew threads—I tap and stroke the walls, awakening echoes from days, seasons, sometimes years past. A child's laughter from a summer gathering bubbles up like trapped air; the death-song of an elder who passed when these walls were young thrums low and solemn; the first words of treaty between the Basin-kin and a delegation of surface dwellers replay in overlapping vibrations.

This is my role: to be the memory-keeper for a people whose own memories flow like water, beautiful but prone to dispersal. The Echo folk live so deeply in the present moment, their thoughts unfolding in slow gradients, that they sometimes lose the sharp edges of specific recollection. But the stone remembers what they forget, and I have become the bridge between mineral memory and living understanding.

The Basin-kin accept me now with the same patience they show all slow transformations. Children—those wriggling, curious forms—cluster at the edges of my workspace, watching with obsidian eyes as I draw forth the echo of their naming ceremonies, their first successful hunts, the lullabies their parents hummed before they could swim. The elders come to me when disputes arise, asking me to replay the exact words of past agreements, the precise promises made in the basin's echoing witness. Even the fauna has learned my presence: the silver schools part calmly as I swim between workspace and settlement, and one ancient eel-serpent has taken to coiling near my cave, drawn perhaps by the symphony of memories that seeps from the stone.

My daily rhythm follows the basin's own slow pulse. Mornings for the delicate work of reading stone, when the water is stillest and echoes clearest. Afternoons swimming the kelp corridors, gathering the bioluminescent spores I'll need to kindle the chemical warmth that lets me work through the night. Evenings in the communal spaces, where I've learned to hum my findings into the water—not in words, which carry too far and muddy in the distance, but in pure vibration, melodies that encode the memories I've retrieved. The Basin-kin receive these offerings with ritual taps on their copper tools, weaving my recoveries into their ongoing songs.

Yet I remain liminal, transformation incomplete. My dreams are still half-surface dreams, filled with sharp mountain air and the harsh light of unfiltered sun. Sometimes I wake gasping, gills fluttering in panic as they seek oxygen my lungs still crave. The full telepathic communion with the vegetation eludes me—I can sense when the fronds respond to my touch, but cannot yet speak to them as the true Basin-kin do. My aging has slowed but not fully synchronized; I can feel time's dilation tugging at my cells, some accepting the gift while others resist.

The work sustains me through these uncertainties. Today I discovered an echo so old it had nearly fossilized into the stone itself: the first sound ever made in the basin after its flooding, a wordless cry of wonder from some ancient witness. As I drew it forth, note by careful note, the entire grotto resonated with that primal awe, and for a moment I felt myself not as keeper but as echo—another reverberation in the basin's endless memory, slowly becoming permanent.

This is where I find myself: in the space between solid and liquid, past and present, human and other. A keeper of echoes in a place where echoes never truly die, only transform. My fingers grow more webbed with each passing season, my skin more perfectly mottled, my breathing more fully given over to the gills that line my changing form. Someday, perhaps, I will achieve the full transformation and become truly of this place, my memories crystallizing into the stone for some future keeper to read.

But for now, I work in the margins, drawing forth the voices of the depths, adding my own slow transformation to the basin's patient chorus. Each echo I retrieve takes root in me, and I in turn leave traces in the stone—a recursive memory, keeper becoming kept, until the distinction dissolves like everything here into the cool, eternal embrace of Echolake's waters.
