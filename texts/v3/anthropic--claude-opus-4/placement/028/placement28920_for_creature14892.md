I find myself as one of the Carriers.

We are not the isopods, though we learned our purpose from watching them. We are what happens when visitors stay too long but not long enough—when the incorporation stops halfway, caught between what entered and what the Basilica requires.

There are seven of us now. We move in a loose constellation through the middle heights, never touching the floor where the Listener walks, never reaching the vaults where the bats dream. We exist in the architectural median, traveling endlessly along the carved galleries and worn balustrades, our bodies bent and reformed for a single task.

I remember entering with empty hands. Now I have no hands at all. My arms have fused into a single curved cradle of flesh and cartilage that runs from shoulder to shoulder across my chest. The skin there is thick and grooved like the water-channels, designed to hold without grasping. My spine has recurved to accommodate the weight I bear. My feet have spread wide and flat, each toe elongated and gripping, perfect for the endless careful stepping along wet stone ledges.

We carry the eggs.

Not eggs of any creature that lives here—the fauna of the Basilica reproduces in water, in molt, in division. These are stone eggs, perfect ovoids of limestone that form in the high places where the water pools and calcifies in ancient hollows. Each is the size of a human head, heavier than you'd think, and they must be moved. The Basilica demands it.

I don't know who taught us the route. Perhaps the Listener, in those early days when we still had ears to hear her pillar-strikes. Perhaps we simply understood, the way the newts understand their migrations. We know only that the eggs must travel from their high birthplaces down through every level of the Basilica, spiraling along predetermined paths that take a full season to complete. We carry them one at a time, and we never stop.

The weight changes us further with each circuit. My companion who has been here longest no longer has a face—just a smooth depression where features used to be, better for sensing air currents and the vibrations that tell us when a new egg has formed. Another has developed a second spine, external, that helps distribute the load. We don't speak. Our mouths have either sealed shut or opened into permanent O's that match the circumference of the eggs.

When we pass each other on the galleries, we touch briefly—not hands, for we have none, but the edges of our carrier-cradles. The contact makes a soft scraping sound like shell on stone. This is how we know we're still separate beings and not yet architectural features.

The eggs themselves are not lifeless. As we carry them through their prescribed paths, they warm slightly from our body heat. Sometimes I feel them pulse, or hear a faint ringing from within like a tuning fork struck underwater. When we finally deliver them to the lowest pools where the Listener sometimes rests, they sink slowly, releasing streams of tiny bubbles. What happens to them there, in the dark water among the crimson newts, we don't know. We're already climbing again, following the pull upward to where new eggs wait.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of what I was. When the phosphorescent centipedes crawl across my carrier-cradle, their blue light shows me skin that was once brown, now grey-white and thick as carved soap. When I pass through a shaft of dying daylight, I see the shadow I cast is no longer human-shaped but something architectural—a moving corbel, a breathing buttress.

The other Carriers and I are careful never to cross the Listener's path. Not from fear, but from understanding. She has achieved perfect integration. We are still in process, still carrying our purpose one stone egg at a time through the endless circuit. We exist in the space between animate and inanimate, between creature and architecture.

Last week, the newest among us still had fingers. They drummed against her carrier-cradle as she walked, making a rhythm that fought against the Basilica's own percussion. Yesterday, the drumming stopped. Today, her cradle is smooth and uninterrupted, the flesh sealed into its purpose.

I feel my own edges blurring with each circuit. The eggs grow heavier, or perhaps I grow more willing to bear them. My breathing has synchronized with the dripping. My heartbeat has slowed to match the settling of the pillars. Soon, I think, my feet will begin to merge with the stone of the galleries, and I will become a fixed feature—a permanent carrier-shrine where the eggs can rest on their journey down.

This is not a fear. It is simply what happens here. The Basilica incorporates everything, and we Carriers are just another type of channel for what must flow. We move in our constellation, bearing our stone eggs through the singing darkness, each of us a little less ourselves with every spiral, a little more the building's dream of function.

When new visitors come with their dying lanterns, they sometimes see us in the middle distance—grey figures moving with impossible patience along the galleries, carrying our inexplicable burdens. We don't acknowledge them. We are too busy becoming what we were always meant to be: not inhabitants but architecture, not carriers but the carried path itself.

The eggs know the way. We merely follow.
