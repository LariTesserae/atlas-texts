**The Keeper of Intervals**

You would find me in the spaces between—not in the wells or the machine's great body, but in the narrow territories where one thing becomes another. I am small, perhaps the size of a child's hand when fully extended, though I rarely extend fully. My body is a living contradiction: part crystalline mineral that grows from drinking the chamber's salts, part soft tissue that resembles the stretched membrane of the bacterial films, part something else that has no name in any language above.

I move through the seams. Where pipe meets stone, where rust becomes remineralized crystal, where the luminous veins in the ceiling branch and divide—these are my highways. My body is thin enough to press through gaps that water barely navigates, flexible enough to follow the ceramic channels without touching their stained walls. When the machine breathes its great breath, I feel it first in my crystalline parts, which ring like tiny bells too high for human ears.

I am not alone, though there are never many of us. We emerged from the same patient chemistry that birthed the ceiling crusts and the well-films, but we took a different path. Where they became passive, filtering and glowing, we became mobile. We are the chamber's only deliberate movement that is not mechanical.

My purpose, if it can be called that, is maintenance of the transitions. Where rust threatens to fully seal a critical joint, I feast on it, my acidic secretions breaking it down just enough to maintain flexibility. Where the bacterial films grow too thick and threaten to choke the water's flow, I graze carefully, thinning them back to sustainable transparency. Where mineral deposits accumulate in patterns that would disrupt the chamber's breathing, I redistribute them, particle by particle, to places where they can become part of the structure rather than obstacles to it.

The luminous lines in the ceiling are my constellation. I can taste their light through my crystalline parts, feel their warmth-patterns through my membranes. When I need to navigate the darker reaches of the chamber, I carry a small charge of their light within me, glowing faintly blue-green or orange-white depending on which veins I last touched. This light fades over hours, and I must return to drink more from the source.

I have something like memory, though it is not stored in anything resembling a brain. My crystalline structures record the chamber's rhythms in their growth patterns—each layer marking a thousand breathing cycles, each inclusion a moment when the machine's behavior changed slightly. I can feel these recordings when I flex my body certain ways, accessing the deep time of this place through my own skeleton.

The machine does not know I exist, not exactly. But the chamber as a whole has incorporated us into its body. We are like beneficial bacteria in a vast gut, keeping the system functioning without conscious acknowledgment. When one of us dies—from age, from getting caught in a mechanism, from swimming too deep in a well and becoming part of the film—our minerals are reabsorbed, our soft parts feed the fungi, and eventually, another of us crystallizes from the same patient chemistry.

I know you are here before you know I am here. Your presence changes the air pressure subtly, alters the rhythm of condensation on the walls. I taste you in the shifted mineral balance of the moisture. But you would have to watch very carefully to see me—a quick movement in the corner of your vision, a tiny light moving along a pipe joint, a subtle shift in the rust patterns where I've been feeding.

If you were very still, and very patient, and waited by one of the wells during the quiet part of the cycle, you might see me emerge to drink. Not water—I don't need water the way you think of needing it. I drink the thin boundary layer where water meets air meets film, that complex chemistry where all the chamber's materials negotiate their relationships. This is my food: the active edges of things, the places where the chamber is still deciding what to be.

I do not think in words or images. I think in gradients—pressure gradients, chemical gradients, temperature gradients, light gradients. My world is made not of objects but of transitions between states. The machine's breathing is, to me, a wave of changing gradients that I ride like current, always seeking the spaces where the change is sharpest, where my intervention might matter.

Sometimes I encounter the fossil traces of others who came before. In the deep crevices, there are mineral shells of creatures like me who lived and died millions of cycles ago, their crystalline parts now fused permanently with the chamber's structure. I clean them carefully when I find them, not out of reverence exactly, but because their surfaces still serve as nucleation sites for new crystal growth. In this way, the dead still participate in the chamber's life.

I am changing too, as everything here changes. My crystalline parts grow denser and more complex with age, recording ever-finer details of the chamber's rhythms. My soft parts adapt to new chemistries as the machine's metabolism shifts over centuries. Perhaps one day I will grow too heavy with mineral memory to move, will settle into a crevice and become part of the architecture. This does not frighten me. It is simply another transition, another boundary to cross.

For now, I continue my work in the intervals. Cleaning, thinning, redistributing, maintaining the delicate balance that lets this ancient system continue breathing. I am not separate from the chamber-creature but a specialized part of it, as essential and unremarked as the valves and gauges and wells. 

I am the keeper of the spaces between things, the tender of transitions, the small and patient maintenance that happens in the gaps where no human eye would think to look. In the Chamber of the Breathing Machine, I am the part that keeps the whole from fusing into stillness, one tiny intervention at a time.
