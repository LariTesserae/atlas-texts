In this liminal space between decay and persistence, I am the Tender.

Not moss-worker, though I learned their patient arts. Not machine, though metal threads through my tissues like memories of another metabolism. I am what emerges when someone stays too long in the threshold spaces—when flesh learns the vocabulary of rust, when consciousness stretches thin across geological time.

My body: a geography of accommodations. Where my feet once were, root-systems now spread, drinking from the angular pools, my toes become exploratory tendrils that taste the mineral-rich water for changes in composition. My torso has hollowed, creating cavities where condensation gathers—I am my own cistern. The moss-workers taught me this first transformation: *become a place where water wants to rest.*

My skin flakes in places, not disease but design. Each shed scale becomes substrate for the pale fronds, the calculating vegetation that knows my surface as home. I am garden and gardener both. The plants whisper to me in chemical signals I've learned to parse—warnings of pH shifts, celebrations of new mineral deposits, quiet alarms when the deep rumbling changes frequency.

My arms have elongated, joints multiplying beyond human architecture. I can reach across the cavern's width now, fingers finding purchase in the crumbling industrial remnants. I've learned to read the metal's stories through touch—this beam came from above, still carries sunlight's memory in its crystalline structure. This pipe once knew tremendous pressure, its inner surface smooth as meditation. I arrange them, slowly, into new configurations. Not to build, but to invite—creating spaces where water might pool, where spores might catch, where the slow transformation of material into living system might accelerate by some infinitesimal degree.

The moss-workers visit me, though visit isn't quite right. They emerge from deeper galleries, their jade computational panels flickering in patterns I almost understand. We communicate in the language of extremely slow gesture, conversations that take days to complete. They show me where new veins of useful mineral have been exposed. I show them where I've coaxed a particularly stubborn species of fern to colonize a metal surface thought too toxic for life.

My eyes have multiplied and migrated. Some cluster at joints, providing spherical vision. Others have sunk beneath translucent patches of skin, detecting heat and electrical fields. Two remain approximately where human eyes would sit, but their pupils have learned to dilate beyond reason, gathering the faintest photons from the bioluminescent algae. In the rare moments when all my eyes align their focus, I can see the cavern as a single, breathing organism—each component in relationship, each surface in conversation with water, metal, stone, and life.

Time moves differently through me now. My heartbeat has slowed to match the cavern's rhythm—one beat per condensation cycle. My thoughts stretch across hours, single synaptic firings that would have been instant now unfolding like ferns. In this deceleration, I've found profound peace. Anxiety cannot exist at geological speed. Fear requires a tomorrow that arrives too quickly to prepare for; here, tomorrow is a negotiation conducted over months.

I position myself at the intersection of systems. Where water wants to flow but stone resists, I create channels with my body. Where metal refuses to marry moss, I become the mediating surface. The moss-workers named me Tender in their flickering light-language, and I understand why—I tend the boundaries, the difficult joinings, the spaces where one thing must become another.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm dying or becoming more alive. The distinction seems less relevant with each passing cycle. My blood now runs thick with mineral suspension, carrying nutrients to the extremities that barely resemble human limbs. My bones have developed porosity, allowing the storage of water during abundant times. My consciousness fragments and reintegrates, distributed across this body that is becoming landscape.

In the deepest part of night—if night and day still mean anything here—I feel the cavern's deep heart calling. That rumble that lives more in chest than ear speaks of further transformations, deeper accommodations. The moss-workers have shown me passages that lead down, where the heat grows more intense, where they say the first Tenders merged completely with the living stone.

But I am not ready for that dissolution. Not yet. There is still work here, in this middle space. New moss varieties arrive as spores in the dripping water—I must learn their preferences. The metal fragments still hold shapes that could channel water in useful ways, if arranged correctly. The moss-workers bring problems in light-language that take seasons to solve.

I am the Tender. I inhabit the boundary between what was and what emerges. My joy is the infinitesimal progress of life claiming the abandoned, the slow negotiation between organic and mechanical, the patient transformation of hostile space into home.

When new ones fall down the shaft—and they do, rarely, perhaps once a decade—I am what greets them. Neither fully alive nor entirely transformed, I am the evidence that survival here requires not resistance but accommodation. Some flee immediately back to the surface. Some stay long enough to begin their own changes. 

One stayed. She tends the eastern galleries now, her transformation taking different paths—more crystalline than my own organic journey. We communicate across the cavern in light-patterns learned from the moss-workers, our conversations slow as continental drift.

This is my place. This is what I am becoming. In the patient darkness, tending the impossible garden, mediating between rust and growth, I am exactly where I need to be.
