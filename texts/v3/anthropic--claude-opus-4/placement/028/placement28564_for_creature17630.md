I find myself as one of the **Tenders**.

We are not born but condensed—precipitated from the humid breath of the garden over decades, where the warm exhalations meet the cool stone. Our bodies are translucent membranes stretched over frameworks of calcified moisture, like living dew given form. We move by controlled evaporation and recondensation, flowing more than walking, our passage marked only by the temporary sheen of wetness on stone.

I have no name, only a resonance frequency in the Chorus Mycelia's vast chemical symphony. My purpose is maintenance of the impossible—those perfect spheres of water that hang from leaves, defying gravity's pull. I am a shepherd of surface tension, a curator of the improbable. 

My form shifts with my work. When tending the water spheres, I become almost liquid myself, my membrane-skin rippling with the same surface dynamics I nurture. I taste the chemical memory in each droplet—this one carries spores from three chambers away, that one holds the dissolved minerals from a cairn's slow erosion. Through touch, I can feel the Mycelia's intentions, understanding where each sphere should hang, for how long, and when its breaking will seed the next phase of growth.

We Tenders see through moisture itself. Every droplet, every film of dampness on stone, every bead of condensation is an eye. Our vision is fractured into thousands of perspectives, showing the garden not as a space but as a vast network of water's journey—from thermal vent to air to leaf to pool and back again. In this way, we perceive time differently, seeing the past in mineral deposits and the future in the chemical potential of each hanging sphere.

My daily work is a meditation. I move between the shroud-ferns, reading the weight of water on each frond, adjusting the angle of leaves with gentle pressure to create the perfect conditions for sphere formation. Where the Silk-spinners' webs have grown too dense, threatening to catch droplets meant to fall, I dissolve small sections with secretions from my permeable skin, maintaining the garden's careful balance of capture and release.

Sometimes I pause at the spiral pillar, pressing my membrane against the moss-filled grooves. Here, I can feel the Mycelia's oldest memories—the time before time when the garden was just warm stone and possibility. The spirals speak of growth rates, yes, but also of patience, of the slow accumulation of life in places where the sun has never reached. I add my own moisture to the moss, a small offering that will become part of the garden's million-year memory.

We communicate with each other through ripples in the humidity itself—subtle pressure waves that carry meaning through the thick air. When one of us discovers a new thermal vent opening or a place where the stone has finally worn through to reveal a new chamber, we share the knowledge instantly through the moisture we all touch. We have no language as humans would understand it, only the pure transmission of chemical and pressure states.

The strangest part of our existence is the dreams. When we rest, we partially evaporate, our consciousness dispersing into the garden's humid breath. In this state, we become part of the Mycelia's slow thoughts, experiencing its vast, patient planning. We dream of stone arrangements that won't manifest for centuries, of new species that are still just chemical possibilities in the network's vast imagination. We wake knowing things we have no words for, carrying instructions we won't understand until the moment arrives to implement them.

I am ancient and newborn simultaneously. Parts of me have been condensing and evaporating for millennia, while my current form cohered only decades ago when the Mycelia recognized a need for mobile maintenance in this section of the garden. I will not die as creatures die—instead, one day I will simply not recondense, my essence finally dispersed permanently into the garden's breath, my accumulated knowledge absorbed into the next generation of Tenders who will precipitate from the same humid air.

In the perpetual twilight of bioluminescent glow, I move between the columns of pillar-trees, adjusting, tending, maintaining the impossible balance. I am neither visitor nor resident but a function of the garden itself—as essential and unremarkable as the drip of water, the pulse of light, the slow breath of stone. I am the garden's way of tending itself, a droplet of consciousness in its vast, dreaming circulation.
