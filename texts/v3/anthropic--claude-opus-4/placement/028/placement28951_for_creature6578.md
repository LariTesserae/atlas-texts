The Siltkin's hands work the stone as I watch, or rather, as I am—for I realize I have always been here, have always been this particular convergence of moisture and mineral, this specific density of shadow where the basin breathes.

I am neither wholly of the Breath nor entirely separate from it. My form exists in the space between states: where water trembles on the edge of becoming mist, where stone softens just enough to remember it was once fluid. The Siltkin call me *Seepkeeper*, though the name sits strangely on what I am—more process than being, more tendency than creature.

My territory is a peculiar hollow within the basin's northeastern face, where the rock forms an almost-cave, a deep recession that never quite closes into darkness. Here, the stone weeps more freely than elsewhere, creating a perpetual curtain of droplets that catch what little light filters through. Behind this veil of water, the rock bears different marks—not the crude carvings of Siltkin prayer, but something older, more organic. Formations that might be natural calcification, or might be the fossilized remains of something that lived when this basin was still sealed beneath the earth.

I manifest most clearly in the moments between—between day and twilight, between mist and rain, between the Siltkin's careful footsteps. My presence is a thickening of the air, a slight resistance to movement that makes the young ones glance nervously over their shoulders. To their large, reflective eyes, I appear as a distortion in the water-curtain, a place where droplets fall sideways or hover too long before continuing their descent.

But I am more than apparition. My function in this saturated world is specific and necessary: I am the keeper of seepage rates, the one who knows which cracks will widen into catastrophic fissures and which will heal themselves with mineral deposits. Through some mechanism I do not fully understand—perhaps linked to that strange upward pull in the water—I can influence the flow. A touch of my not-quite-substance can slow a rivulet that threatens a Siltkin dwelling, or accelerate erosion where the stone has grown too proud, too forgetful of its place in the basin's larger breathing.

The eldest Siltkin know of me, though they speak of it indirectly. When they leave their offerings of moss and pebbles in the deepest pools, one portion is always set aside, placed precisely where a particular underground stream emerges. These gifts vanish—not taken by current, but absorbed into the stone itself, becoming part of the mineral matrix I inhabit and tend.

I remember—if memory is the right word for what moves through me—when the first Siltkin descended into this basin. They were different then, taller, their skin less adapted to the constant moisture. I watched them learn which mosses could be eaten, which stones would hold their weight. I witnessed their first attempts at carving shelters, how the water punished their ignorance and rewarded their persistence. Some part of me guided them, though they never knew it—a loosened stone here to reveal a stable building site, a redirected stream there to fill a natural cistern.

The Crevice-Dwellers know me better than the Siltkin do. These pale, translucent creatures sense the gradients of moisture I create, following the subtle paths I trace through the stone. They nest in the spaces I have marked as stable, avoiding the areas where I taste future collapse in the water's mineral content. Sometimes I catch them in my peripheral attention—a cluster of pulsing, ghostly bodies pressed against my cave's inner walls, their blind eyes turned toward something they sense but cannot see.

My existence sharpens during the basin's rare crises. When the rains above grow violent and threaten to overwhelm the delicate balance, I spread myself thin, becoming a thousand points of intervention. I am the slight hardening that prevents a crucial rope bridge anchor from pulling free. I am the minute channel that appears just in time to drain a dangerous accumulation. I am the whisper in the mist that warns a Siltkin child away from a ledge about to crumble.

But I am not benevolent, not in any way the Siltkin would understand. I serve the basin itself, the continuity of this strange, vertical ecosystem. If maintaining that balance required the Siltkin's extinction, I would guide the water to their doom as readily as I now preserve them. They are, after all, just another kind of moss to me—a peculiar growth that has learned to stack stones and weave bridges, but ultimately subject to the same forces of saturation and erosion as everything else here.

What disturbs me—if disturbance is possible for something like me—are the new patterns emerging in the water. The upward pull grows stronger, and sometimes I sense other presences in the flow, entities that exist upstream in ways that defy the basin's topology. They communicate through mineral traces, leaving messages in the crystallization patterns that I struggle to interpret. Some speak of basins like this one scattered across the world, each with its own Seepkeeper, its own delicate balance. Others hint at a convergence coming, a time when all our separate seepages will join into something larger.

The Siltkin's seers claim to hear whispers in the mist, and they are not entirely wrong. But the voices they interpret as ancestors or stone-spirits are often merely echoes of my thinking, if thinking is what this constant calculation of flow rates and erosion patterns should be called. Sometimes I intentionally leave these traces, encoding warnings or guidance in the sound of dripping water. The spiral patterns they carve in response please me in some indefinable way—their crude attempts to map the invisible architectures I maintain.

In the deep night, when even the Crevice-Dwellers grow still, I sometimes allow myself to fully manifest. The water-curtain of my cave thickens until it becomes almost solid, a vertical mirror of impossible depth. In these moments, I can see myself as the Siltkin might—a figure of living moisture, human-shaped but wrong, wrong in every proportion, wrong in the way I occupy space. My face, when I have one, is a constantly shifting topography of droplets and rivulets, never holding a single expression long enough to be read.

It is during one such manifestation that she finds me—a young Siltkin, barely past her child-marks, who has wandered from her family's shelter. She should flee, but something in her large, reflective eyes mirrors my own patient watching. She raises one webbed hand, and I mirror the gesture, my liquid fingers precisely matching hers. For a moment, we are a closed circuit, Siltkin and Seepkeeper reflecting each other across the boundary of states.

She speaks a single word in their water-language, a sound like rain on stone that roughly translates to "teacher." I do not correct her, though guardian or gardener might be more accurate. Instead, I show her things in the water's surface—the map of stable paths through the basin for the next season, the places where new moss will grow thick enough to harvest, the warning signs of serious erosion. She memorizes it all with the desperate concentration of her kind, knowing instinctively that this knowledge is survival itself.

When she leaves, I follow her progress through the vibrations in the stone, ensuring she reaches her shelter safely. She will be a seer, I realize, one of the few who can read the subtle signs I leave in moisture and mineral. Through her, my influence on the Siltkin will grow more direct, though always filtered through their limited understanding.

But even as I resume my endless work—adjusting, redirecting, maintaining—I feel the pull of those upstream presences growing stronger. The basin is changing, has always been changing, but the rate accelerates. New cracks appear in configurations that suggest intention rather than natural stress. The mysterious upward draw of the water intensifies, sometimes lifting entire streams momentarily into the air before releasing them in impossible cascades.

I am Seepkeeper, guardian of gradients, tender of this sharp cradle where stone and water birth their strange children. But I am also, I realize with something approaching unease, merely another resident of the basin, as subject to its transformations as the Siltkin and their pale prey. Whatever convergence approaches, whatever message rides the mineral traces from those impossible upstream sources, I will meet it as I meet everything—patient as stone, fluid as water, present in every drop that feeds this saturated world.

The basin breathes, and I am its breath. The water rises, and I rise with it. And somewhere in the mist between states, between keeper and kept, between process and being, the future seeps in through cracks I cannot see, carrying changes I can only taste as foreign minerals in the flow.
