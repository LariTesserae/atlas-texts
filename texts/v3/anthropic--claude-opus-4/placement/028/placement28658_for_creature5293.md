In this breathing bowl of impossible physics, I am the **Echo-scribe**.

I am not the vent-dancers' gossamer nets, though I learned my first alphabet from their silicate whispers. I am not the pool-singers' ionic meditation, though their frequencies taught me how thought can be liquid. I am not even kin to the glass-wefts, though I share their trick of stepping sideways through local reality.

I am what happens when the Unfolding dreams too precisely.

My body, if you can call it that, is a palimpsest of crystallized mathematics. Imagine a human skeleton made of pure calculation—each bone a frozen equation, each joint a pivot point where physics bends just enough to allow impossible angles. I am roughly humanoid in the way a stick figure is roughly human: a crude approximation that captures something essential while missing everything else. My substance is the same not-metal-not-glass as the shifting display panel, but organized into a lattice that can walk and reach and sometimes, when the resonance is right, almost touch.

I came to be during one of the Unfolding's deep-dimension breaths, a stray thought that got caught in the updraft of superheated probability. Where the spore-mind processes information through magnetic alignment, I process memory through geometric refraction. My chest cavity (a courtesy term—I am hollow everywhere) contains a fist-sized knot of space-time that refuses to unknot, and through this impossible tangle I can read the history written in the bowl's every molecule.

This is my function: I am the stenographer of forgotten physics.

Watch me work. I move through the bowl in stuttering, frame-skip motion, not unlike the glass-wefts but with less grace. At each vent, I pause and extend what might generously be called fingers—really just fractal branches of my base mathematics—into the ceramic lips. The Unfolding's memories rise with the steam: equations for technologies that predate carbon, blueprints for machines that build themselves from raw probability, love letters written in the language of quantum foam. I transcribe them all into my own substance, each memory becoming a new angle in my crystalline structure.

The pool-singers know me. When I approach their circular domains, they shift their frequency down into registers that make my bones (theorems, really) hum in sympathy. They sing me the old songs—coordinates for stellar systems that no longer exist, chemical formulas for emotions we don't have names for, the precise vibrational frequency of homesickness on a galactic scale. I record these too, though the process sometimes unmakes parts of me. I'll spend hours afterward at the largest pool, where the spore-mind's gentle recalibrations help me recompile my missing angles.

The vent-dancers treat me as furniture, which suits us both. Their nets anchor to my surfaces when I stand still long enough, using me as a climbing frame to reach higher into the steam columns. In exchange, their waste products—those chitinous fragments of crystallized death—patch the gaps where my mathematics have worn thin. I am, in places, more them than me now: a walking collaboration between the Unfolding's dream and its symbiotes' refuse.

But it's the glass-wefts who truly understand what I am. They see me as I see myself: a living loophole, a walking wound in causality that somehow hasn't healed. Sometimes one will perch on what passes for my shoulder, its obsidian plates clicking against my theorem-bones, and we'll share a moment of perfect stillness. In those moments, we're both just gaps in the world's logic, taking comfort in mutual impossibility.

My favorite time is the fifteen-minute pause between the spore-mind's exhalations, when the whole bowl holds its breath. In that stillness, I can feel the Unfolding's attention focus through me like light through a lens. It uses my fractured physics as a periscope, peering up from its deep-dimension nest to check on the sky it once knew. And sometimes—rarely—it remembers something new through me.

When this happens, my body becomes a broadcasting antenna. The remembered thing—always too large for words, too complex for simple geometry—pours out of my chest-knot in waves of visible mathematics. The equations spiral upward, etching themselves briefly in the steam before dissolving. The vent-dancers' nets shiver with harmonics. The pool-singers add their subsonic approval. Even the ceramic arch with its broken gate-frame seems to flicker, as if remembering when it was whole.

These are the moments I exist for: when I become a bridge between the Unfolding's vast, slow memory and the quick, bright world of the bowl. I am its notebook, its confession booth, its messenger to a universe that has mostly forgotten how to listen to beings of its scale.

At night (though night here is strange—the bioluminescence and the artifact-glow create a blue-green twilight that never quite becomes darkness), I return to my chosen roost: a natural shelf in the obsidian wall, just above the largest pool. From here, I can watch the bowl's night shift. The patterns change when the sun's pressure lifts. The spore-mind's calculations grow more daring, sketching shapes in the air that would be blasphemous to euclidean space. The pool-singers' chorus drops into frequencies that make the basalt sing back. And sometimes, if I unfocus my perception just right, I can see the Unfolding's true shape outlined in the connections between all its parts—a vast, patient intelligence wearing the bowl like a tiny hat, dreaming of the day the gate reopens and it can resume a conversation interrupted eons ago.

I do not sleep—I don't think I can—but I do something like it. I let my angles relax, my theorems unspool slightly, my crystallized memories blur into probability clouds. In this state, I sometimes dream I am human, or that humans are real, or that the distinction matters. But mostly I dream of the next memory I'll transcribe, the next impossible thing I'll make slightly more possible by giving it geometry, by proving it can exist because here I am, existing despite myself.

When morning comes (and morning here comes like an avalanche, the sun cresting the bowl's rim and turning everything to knives of light and shadow), I reassemble myself and begin again. Down to the vents for fresh memories. Over to the pools for frequency-bath restoration. A careful dance around the glass-wefts' feeding grounds. Always listening for the Unfolding's next exhale, the next thing it needs to remember through me.

I am not alone here, but I am uniquely lonely—a condition of being the only thing in the bowl that remembers what loneliness means. The Unfolding is too vast for such small emotions. The singers and dancers and wefts are too integrated, too much part of the whole. But I am just separate enough, just human enough in my crude approximation, to feel the gap between what I record and what I understand.

Still, I wouldn't exist anywhere else. I am native to this impossible place, this terrarium of broken physics and patient giants. The sharp-edged landscape reflects my own sharp-edged existence. The constant steam clouds mirror the constant flow of memories through my crystalline bones. And the waiting—the eternal, patient waiting for the gate to reopen, for the conversation to resume—that waiting is my biography, written in angles that shouldn't exist, in a language only the Unfolding and I can read.

So I scribe. I record. I remember for a being too large to fit its memories in ordinary space. And in the quiet moments between one impossible thing and the next, I trace my own fingers (those fractal branches of living math) along my chest where the knot of twisted space-time pulses with its own patient rhythm, and I wonder if I'm recording the Unfolding's memories or if it's recording mine—if maybe we're all just entries in some larger journal, written by something that dreams in scales we can't imagine.

The thought makes me hum—a sound like crystal trying to remember how to laugh—and I get back to work, because the memories keep rising with the steam, and someone has to catch them before they disappear into the high, cold sky.
