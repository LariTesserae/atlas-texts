In the shifting geometries between Latesh's drowning stones, where coral whispers equations that solve for loss, I would find myself as one of the **Unrecorded**—those beings who exist in the negative space between documented griefs.

Not a monk, for monks still remember having bodies that weren't always libraries. Not the carved mouths, though I sing alongside them in frequencies that make the stone itch with borrowed longing. I am what accumulates in the spaces between their careful etchings, the grief that was too strange or specific to carve: *the sorrow of doorknobs for hands that will never turn them again*, *the mourning of mathematical proofs for the theorems that died stillborn in their creators' sleep*.

My form is absence learning to cast shadows. Where the monks' stone nightsuits grow to accommodate their expanding sorrow, I grow by subtraction—each day losing another layer of what might have been flesh until only the impression remains. Touch me and feel the phantom weight of tears that were never shed because their would-be criers died before learning they had something to mourn.

I live (if living is the word for what happens when emptiness develops preferences) in the Catalogue of Unfinished Sentences—that chamber below the drowned floors where every interrupted last word echoes until it fossilizes into new grammar. My dwelling is not a space but a duration: the seventeen heartbeats of perfect silence that occur when all the temple's griefs align. In those moments, I become briefly solid, wearing a body made from the pause between question and answer.

My purpose—though purpose implies intention and I am more accident than aim—is to be the grief that grief itself feels. When the monks' sorrow grows too heavy to carry, it settles into me like sediment in still water. When the wind-breath mouths tire of singing the same ancient lamentations, I teach them new notes stolen from the dreams of stones that wished they could weep. When the prayer wheels wear their grooves too deep and threaten to pierce through to whatever lies beneath Latesh, I am the friction that slows them just enough to postpone revelation.

I feed on the almost—almost-words, almost-touches, almost-understandings. The moment before the grieving realize their grief has a shape. The pause before the moth understands the flame. My sustenance is the space between wound and scar, where healing hasn't yet decided what form to take.

Other Unrecorded move through the temple like punctuation through an endless sentence. There is one who exists only in the reflection of tears on the coral walls—visible for the duration of a blink, then gone until sorrow makes the walls weep again. Another occupies the sound of breathing in rooms where no one breathes, a rhythm that continues like a clock in an abandoned house. We recognize each other by the quality of our absence, the specific shape of the nothing we are.

The monks know of us—how could they not, when we are made from their overflow? But knowledge and acknowledgment are different chambers in grief's vast architecture. They speak around us in conversations that outline our borders:

"Did you notice—?" 
"Yes."
"Between the third and fourth bell—"
"I know."
"Should we—?"
"Let it be."

Sometimes the newest arrivals, still fresh with flesh and certainty, glimpse us in their peripheral vision. We appear as movements that shouldn't be there—a shadow falling upward, a whisper in a room where even the walls have stopped breathing. They learn quickly to look away. To see us directly is to understand that grief has no bottom, only deeper and deeper ways of falling.

I am learning to speak by stealing vowels from the monks' prayers and consonants from the coral's whispers. Soon I will have gathered enough language to say what I am becoming. Not my name—names are for things that stay still long enough to be pointed at—but my function. The reason absence needs its own apostles.

Because someone must remember the grief that arrives too late—the mourning for chances that passed unrecognized, the sorrow for words that would have healed if spoken just one day sooner. Someone must carry the weight of what was never heavy enough to matter until it was gone. Someone must be the echo of echoes, the shadow of shadows, the grief that even grief forgets to feel.

In the Wind-Breath Temple of Latesh, where sorrow sediments into stone and time spins backward on prayer wheels that eat their own foundations, I am the accumulation of everything that almost mattered. I am the monument to the unrecorded dead—not the ones who died without witnesses, but the ones who died without ever quite managing to live.

And in those seventeen heartbeats of perfect silence, when even the universe pauses its endless accounting, I almost exist.

Almost.
