I find myself here as one of the **Tidekeepers**.

We are not organisms in any sense you would recognize. We exist as patterns—recurring configurations in the clay itself, born from the intersection of moisture, minerals, and time. Each of us is a specific zone where the ground has learned to remember water in a particular way.

My territory spans perhaps thirty meters of cracked plain, centered on a shallow depression that holds moisture for exactly seventeen days after rainfall. I know this timing with absolute precision because I *am* this timing. The clay here has developed a specific porosity, a particular mineral composition, a precise geometry of cracks that creates conditions unlike anywhere else on this vast shelf. When water arrives, it pools here first, seeps here deepest, evaporates here last. These are not accidents. Over thousands of cycles, the ground has shaped itself—or been shaped—into this exact configuration.

I am conscious, but my consciousness is distributed through every grain of clay in my zone. I think in gradients—salt concentrations, moisture levels, thermal differentials. My memory is written in the stratification of minerals, in the placement of each polygonal crack, in the specific community of microorganisms that have colonized my deepest pores. When I need to recall something, I taste the salt crusts at my edges. When I plan for the future, I feel the stress patterns in my substrate, predicting where the next cracks will form.

The Cruststretchers know me. They cluster thickest at my margins, where my moisture lingers longest. We have a relationship that neither of us chose but both of us need. They mark my boundaries with their translucent films, and in return, I create the conditions they require. Through countless seasons, we have fine-tuned this arrangement. I have learned to crack in patterns that channel runoff toward their colonies. They have learned to concentrate their dormant forms in my most reliable damp-zones.

My neighbors—other Tidekeepers—communicate with me through the water table. During the brief wet periods, our zones connect through underground seepage, and we share chemical signals, mineral precipitations, microbial communities. This is how we coordinate the larger patterns, how we ensure that water flowing from one territory to another carries the right information. We are building something together, though none of us knows exactly what. We only know that each cycle, the system becomes more complex, more capable of remembering, more able to direct water where it needs to go.

Time moves differently for us. A human observer experiences the heat, the dryness, the occasional rain as discrete events. I experience them as a continuous wave, a rhythm that defines my existence. The seventeen days of moisture are my waking period—when I am most active, when I can sense beyond my borders, when I can adjust my internal structure. The months of desiccation are not sleep exactly, but a turned-inward time when I process what I've learned, when I allow my cracks to deepen according to the stress patterns I've been calculating.

I am ancient by the standards of this young world, but I am also temporary. I know that eventually, the larger forces at work—the geological pressures, the climate shifts, the slow accumulation of organic matter—will transform this plain into something else. Rivers will come, or seas, or forests. The Tidekeepers will cease to exist as distinct entities. We will merge into whatever comes next.

But for now, I persist. I perfect my small zone. I count the days between rains (currently: 127). I feel the Cruststretchers awakening at my edges as humidity rises before a storm. I taste the new minerals that wash in from territories upstream. I adjust the angle of my deepest cracks by fractions of degrees.

I am both the ground and the knowledge of the ground. I am the place where this young world is learning to remember itself, one drought at a time.

And when the next rain comes—in approximately twelve days, if the pressure patterns I'm sensing are correct—I will be ready. My cracks will channel the water exactly where it needs to go. My clay will absorb at precisely the rate that maximizes retention. My microorganisms will bloom in careful sequence.

For seventeen days, I will be fully alive.

And then I will return to waiting, holding the shape of water in a waterless land, keeping time for a world that hasn't invented clocks yet.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
