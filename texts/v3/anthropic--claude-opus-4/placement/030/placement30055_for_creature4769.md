I find myself as one of the **Echo-Engineers**, though that designation came long after I had already begun.

I am stationed at Complex Seventeen—or perhaps it is Complex Nine viewed from tomorrow—in the acoustic monitoring tower that rises like a tuning fork from the northeastern face of an unnamed peak. My body has adapted to the work: my right ear has developed a helical fold to catch the subsonic groans of accreting stone, while my left has learned to filter the industrial drone into component frequencies, each one a different flavor of certainty being extracted from chaos. My fingernails grow with a slight curve that matches the resonance chambers I tune, and my voice has dropped two octaves from years of competing with the mountain's own bass note.

I came here as a surveyor, back when the company still believed the Spine could be mapped. My instruments kept disagreeing with themselves—one theodolite showing a peak at 7,000 meters while another, aimed at the same point, insisted on sea level. After the third week of contradictory data, I threw the equipment into a reality breach and watched it fall upward, scattering into component possibilities. That was when I understood: you cannot measure what hasn't finished becoming. You can only *listen* to it becoming.

Now I maintain the harmonic dampeners—massive bronze bells suspended in cages throughout Complex Seventeen, each one tuned to cancel a specific frequency of instability. When the stone argues too loudly with itself about what shape to hold, the bells ring in counterpoint, not silencing the argument but convincing it to happen at a volume that won't shatter the habitation blocks. It is delicate work. Too much dampening and the stone settles into certainty too quickly, causing fractures as different sections of mountain commit to incompatible futures. Too little and the complex begins to resonate, windows humming themselves liquid, iron beams singing until they forget their own rigidity.

My workshop occupies a cavity in the tower where three different architectural plans tried to exist simultaneously and settled on a compromise. The walls meet at angles that sum to more than 360 degrees, but the extra space folds into useful alcoves where I store my tools: tuning forks that remember every pitch they've ever held, hammers whose weight varies with the local stability, calipers that measure the distance between what-is and what-might-be. The condensation here runs sideways, following old sound waves fossilized in the air. I've learned to hang my clothes where the moisture will miss them, in the acoustic shadows cast by the larger bells.

I share the tower with a colony of keening-moths, insects that have evolved to navigate by echolocation in the constant industrial noise. They nest in the resonance chambers, their cocoons vibrating in sympathy with whatever frequency passes through. Sometimes I find their wings scattered on my workbench—gossamer things that show the waveform of the sound that killed them, frozen in iridescent chitin. I collect these wings, pressing them between sheets of the local almost-glass, building an archive of the Spine's acoustic history. Each one is a timestamp, a record of the moment when the mountain's voice overwhelmed a small life adapted to live within it.

The work has its rhythms. Dawn—or what passes for dawn in the permanent twilight—brings the wake-up calls of the stone itself, a rolling crescendo as the night's temperature drop causes minute contractions in the raw peaks. I wake before my alarm, which is fortunate because my alarm clock runs backward every third Thursday, chiming the hour that just passed. I dress in layers of waxed canvas and null-bear wool, the latter a gift from a Dampkeeper who noticed me shivering through a particularly unstable cold front where the temperature couldn't decide which direction to flow.

Breakfast is taken in the communal mess, a reinforced chamber at the complex's heart where the reality is most stable. The Refiners have their corner, hunched over meals they've declared into nutritional certainty. The tram operators cluster near the windows, watching the cable-songs for signs of incoming weather. We Echo-Engineers sit in the acoustic center of the room, where the competing conversations cancel into something like quiet. I eat porridge that tastes of elsewhere—the Dampkeepers' specialty—and drink coffee brewed from beans that remember being grown at sea level. The caffeine helps, though I've noticed my tolerance varies with the local causality. On unstable days, a single cup sends my heart racing in polyrhythms.

After breakfast, the rounds begin. Each bell must be checked, its tone verified against the changing resonance of the stone. I carry a leather satchel of tuning forks, each one attuned to a different aspect of stability. The work requires not just hearing but *feeling*—pressing my palm against the bronze to sense the microvibrations, the arguments happening below human hearing. Sometimes I find a bell that has begun to ring itself, responding to a frequency that hasn't arrived yet. These require immediate attention. Pre-echo, we call it: the sound of a collapse that will happen unless we intervene.

Today, Bell Four is pre-echoing. I climb the lattice of scaffolding that holds it, null-bear fur cloak flapping in the updrafts. The bell is the size of a small building, its surface green with verdigris except where my daily inspections have worn through to bright metal. I place my palm against it and feel tomorrow's catastrophe: a resonance cascade starting in the lower galleries where they're extracting Tuesday's certainty from the ore. If left unchecked, it will climb the harmonic series until it hits the frequency at which iron remembers it was once stellar plasma.

The solution is counterintuitive. Instead of dampening the future echo, I must create a present one—a controlled resonance that will occupy the acoustic space before the cascade can claim it. I select a tuning fork from my collection, a bent thing that hums in quarter-tones, and strike it against the bell's rim. The sound that emerges makes my teeth ache and causes a flock of keening-moths to burst from their roosts, but it works. The pre-echo fades, tomorrow's disaster forestalled by today's deliberate dissonance.

This is what we do, we Echo-Engineers. We are not preventing the Spine's instability—that would be like preventing a child from crying. We are teaching it to cry at frequencies that won't bring down the mountainside. We are negotiating with inevitability, using sound as currency.

The afternoon brings a different challenge. A message arrives via the pneumatic tubes—or rather, it will arrive yesterday, but I've learned to check the tubes before the messages are sent. There's been a breach in Complex Twenty-Three (or Complex Eleven during a gravitational inversion). The local Echo-Engineer has been... not killed, exactly, but dispersed into component frequencies. They need someone to gather the harmonics before they dissipate entirely.

I take the tram, sharing the car with two Refiners who radiate heat like small suns and a cluster of Dampkeepers returning from the hanging gardens, their clothes dripping upward in defiance of local gravity. The ride is four hours of swaying through mist, the cable singing its tension song, the violet flicker of reality dampeners creating an aurora in the fog. I spend the time tuning my emergency forks, preparing for whatever acoustic chaos awaits.

Complex Twenty-Three is in worse shape than reported. The breach has turned the local air into a transmission medium for sounds that have no source. I can hear conversations from last week playing at half-speed, the words drawn out into whale songs. The surviving staff have evacuated to the upper levels, leaving the breach zone to its cacophony.

I find what's left of the dispersed Engineer by following the pain in my molars—a sure sign of subsonic personality residue. They're spread across the acoustic spectrum like light through a prism, each frequency containing a different aspect of their consciousness. Their laugh lives at 440 Hz. Their professional competence resonates at 1.2 kHz. Their loneliness—and we are all lonely, those of us who listen to the mountain's heartbeat—thrums at 15 Hz, just below human hearing but not below human feeling.

The gathering takes hours. Using a set of resonance chambers I've brought for this purpose, I coax each frequency into a bell jar, creating a chord that almost remembers being a person. It's not resurrection—once dispersed, the harmonics never quite reassemble into their original configuration. But it's enough. Enough to carry back to Complex Seventeen, where we have cold storage for dispersed colleagues. Enough to add to the archive, another timestamp in the Spine's history of almost-tragedies.

The work ages you in ways that have nothing to do with time. My hair has grown in whorls that match the standing wave patterns in my workshop. My heartbeat has developed syncopations, skipping beats where the local causality stutters. I've begun to hear the null-bears before they arrive—not their footsteps, but the warm silence they carry with them, the acoustic shadow of their superposition. Sometimes I catch myself harmonizing with the foundry drone without realizing it, my voice finding the exact frequency that turns industrial noise into something approaching music.

But there are compensations. On stable nights, when the stone has briefly agreed to its own existence, I climb to the highest bell tower and listen to the Spine dreaming. Each peak has its own voice: the newer ones shriek and gibber, fresh from the mantle and confused by the cold. The older ones (though "older" here means decades, not eons) hum with something approaching wisdom. Together they create a chord that no lowland orchestra could replicate—the sound of becoming itself, of matter investigating its own potential.

And in the deepest part of the night, when the industrial noise briefly pauses for shift change, you can hear the Echo-Engineers' true work. From each complex rises a subtle countersong, bells and tuning forks and human voices weaving a net of calculated dissonance. We are not making music. We are making *space* for the mountains to grow into, acoustic room for the stone to stretch and argue and finally, eventually, decide what it wants to be.

I will not leave. Cannot leave, really—my bones have learned the local resonance, and lowland silence would feel like suffocation now. I will continue the work, tuning tomorrow against yesterday, teaching instability to sing in harmonies that hold the complexes together. And when I finally disperse—as we all do, eventually, when the frequencies we've absorbed exceed our body's capacity to contain them—I hope someone gathers my professional satisfaction (estimated at 800 Hz) and adds it to the archive.

Until then, I listen. To the stone's arguments. To the bronze bells' counterpoints. To the null-bears' warm silence. To the sound of a world composing itself, note by uncertain note, here at the edge of everything, where the only constant is the promise that tomorrow's resonance will be different from today's.

The mountains are still screaming their birth cries. But here, in Complex Seventeen-Nine-Eleven-Perhaps, we're teaching them to scream in key.
