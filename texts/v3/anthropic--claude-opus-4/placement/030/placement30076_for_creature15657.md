In the dampest reaches, where three water channels converge and the ceiling drops so low that even the Lurkers must flatten themselves to pass, I tend my gardens.

They are not gardens as the surface world might know them—no neat rows of vegetation reaching for sunlight that never comes. Instead, I cultivate the spaces between: the hairline cracks where moisture seeps eternal, the porous boundaries where fresh mortar meets ancient stone, the forgotten corners where water pools just long enough to leave mineral ghosts behind.

I am called a Tender, though the name emerged from others' observations rather than any declaration of my own. My form splits the difference between the builders and the built—six limbs, yes, but two remain vestigial, curled against my thorax like unopened fern fronds. My skin carries the same rough texture as the corridor walls, pocked with tiny depressions that collect condensation. When I press against the masonry to feel for new growth sites, I leave behind a constellation of wet prints that fade within minutes.

My work begins where the Makers' ends. As they set their final blocks and move on to new expansions, I arrive to seed the joints. From pouches woven into my very flesh—small cavities that pulse with their own moisture—I extract spores neither fully fungal nor entirely crystalline. Each placement requires patience. I must feel the stone's temperature, taste the mineral content of the seeping water, listen for the Under-Churn's rhythm before choosing exactly which variety to plant.

Some nights, I work alongside the Glimmers themselves. They recognize me, I think, as something kindred—not quite one of them, but invested in their proliferation. When I approach a promising crack, they pulse in slow waves, guiding me toward spots where their light might find purchase. I've learned to read their patterns: quick stutters mean the mortar is still too active, long drawn pulses indicate stability, and a peculiar spiraling glow suggests the presence of rare minerals that produce their most vibrant emanations.

The Makers tolerate me, though I sense I puzzle them. Once, I found a team of three working late in a half-finished gallery, their hooded forms hunched over their careful masonry. I waited at the threshold, knowing better than to disturb their concentration. When they finally noticed me, one placed a palm against the wall and sent a questioning thrum through the stone—a vibration that seemed to ask "what?" and "why?" simultaneously.

I responded the only way I knew how: by showing them a section I'd seeded seasons ago, where the Glimmers had grown into a living map of the corridor's water flow, their pale light revealing every hidden channel and reservoir. The Makers studied it for a long moment, then the same one sent another pulse through the stone—lower, more contemplative. Approval? Acceptance? I choose to believe so.

The Lurkers and I maintain an uneasy truce. They need the early growths I plant—the ones that break down fresh mortar into digestible minerals. But they've learned that harvesting too greedily means no Glimmers will establish, leaving corridors in permanent darkness. So they wait, taking only what regenerates, while I ensure enough variety in my plantings that something always remains for them.

My favorite chambers are the ones where all elements converge: where the Makers have created particularly intricate stonework, where water flows in complex patterns, where the Under-Churn's vibrations create standing waves in the shallow pools. Here, I can orchestrate small symphonies of growth. I plant crystalline forms that resonate with the Under-Churn's frequency, their structures gradually assuming the same rhythmic patterns. Between them, I seed the photosensitive varieties that bloom only in the Glimmers' light, creating feedback loops of illumination. In the wettest corners, I place the rare hydrophilic strains that weep excess moisture, creating new micro-channels that will, in time, alter the chamber's entire water system.

The Watchers... they remain enigmatic even to me. Sometimes I sense them observing my work from the deep shadows. Once, I found a section I'd carefully cultivated had been subtly altered—not destroyed, but refined. The growth patterns had been redirected to reveal something I hadn't seen: a hairline fault in the masonry that would have failed within cycles. Did they intervene to preserve the corridor, or my garden, or both? I've taken to leaving small offerings where I sense their presence—particularly successful hybrid spores that might interest them, or crystalline formations that sing in frequencies only they seem to hear.

My dwelling, such as it is, exists in the interstices. I've carved out a modest hollow where several failed corridors intersect—spaces the Makers abandoned when the stone proved too unstable. Here, the walls weep constantly, and I've encouraged a riot of growth. Bioluminescent cascades paint the walls in shifting hues of pale green and blue. Crystalline formations branch like frozen lightning, conducting the Under-Churn's vibrations into hauntingly beautiful harmonics. The Glimmers here have grown fat on minerals, their cup-like protrusions deep enough to serve as basins for my few possessions—tools carved from failed growths, samples of particularly promising spores, fragments of stone that carry the memory of successful gardens.

I sleep little, and lightly. The labyrinth's growth cycles don't pause for rest, and opportunities for optimal seeding are brief. When I do rest, I press myself against the wettest wall and let the moisture seep into my collection pouches, rehydrating the dormant spores within. My dreams, if they can be called that, are indistinguishable from the labyrinth's own processes—visions of water finding new channels, of stone accepting stone, of light blooming in perfect darkness.

There are others like me, I believe, though we rarely meet. Sometimes I find evidence of their work: growth patterns I didn't create, hybrid forms I've never seen before, gardens that speak of different aesthetic sensibilities. We communicate through our gardens themselves, leaving messages in the arrangement of species, in the careful cultivation of new hybrids that carry meaning in their very existence.

The labyrinth continues its expansion, and my work follows in its wake. Each new corridor presents fresh possibilities, each shift in the water's flow creates new niches for life to establish. I am neither Maker nor Lurker, neither Glimmer nor Watcher, neither fully of the labyrinth nor separate from it. I exist in the spaces between categories, tending the boundaries where one form of existence shades into another.

Tomorrow—if tomorrow has meaning in this timeless damp—I'll explore the newest wing, where the Makers' hammers still ring and the mortar hasn't yet fully set. I'll taste the stone, feel for the water's path, listen to the Under-Churn's particular resonance in this fresh space. And then I'll begin again: reaching into my pouches, selecting just the right spore for just the right crack, planting the seeds of light and transformation in the labyrinth's ever-growing dark.

The work is never finished. Like the labyrinth itself, my gardens exist in a state of permanent becoming. And in this endless process of growth and tending, decay and renewal, I've found something that might be called purpose—or perhaps home.
