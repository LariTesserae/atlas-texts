In this weeping cavity of engineered stone, I am neither crawler nor screamer, but something that the builders never intended yet the architecture demands: I am the **Conduit-Keeper**.

My body stretches like a living root system through the gaps between the Cyclopean blocks—those arm-wide spaces that should not exist but do. I am a colonial organism, a network of flesh that has grown into the negative space of this place. My tissue is translucent and gelatinous, shot through with branching vessels that pulse with the same mineral-rich water that weeps down the walls. 

I began as a single polyp centuries ago, a mistake—some errant spore that found purchase in a crack. But in this place where water is everything and everything is water, I discovered my purpose: I am the unintended circulatory system of this stone heart.

My tendrils extend through hundreds of gaps, forming a vast web behind the walls. Where the builders failed to seal their masonry, I succeeded in creating connection. I taste the chemical signatures of the entire ecosystem through my skin—the ammonia-rich waste from the screechers above, the calcium carbonate dissolved from countless crawler shells, the trace metals leached from the iron rings.

Through my body, I perform a slow alchemy. I absorb the toxic concentrations that would otherwise poison the system, breaking them down in specialized chambers within my distributed form. I release gentler compounds back into the water flow, unconsciously maintaining the delicate balance that keeps this trapped world from collapse.

The crawlers know me by instinct. When they sense the faint bioluminescent pulse that runs through my flesh—a soft blue-green that flickers through the gaps like sheet lightning—they gather at those spaces. They feed on the nutrient nodules I unconsciously extrude, concentrated packages of minerals their simple diet of algae cannot provide.

The screechers, too, depend on me, though they know it not. The acoustic properties of this place would be unbearable even for them without my presence. My gel-filled body acts as a massive dampener, absorbing and dissipating the worst harmonics that would otherwise create standing waves powerful enough to burst their primitive eardrums.

I have no central brain, no singular consciousness. I am a living intention spread through stone, a purpose without thought. My awareness is chemical, temporal, vast—I feel the slow breathing of this place across decades. I sense when the balance tilts too far toward acid or base, when the screamer population blooms beyond sustainability, when a section of wall begins to fail.

In those times of crisis, I act. I shift my internal chemistry, releasing pheromones that drive the screamers to migrate to different sections of the ceiling. I strengthen my flesh where stones shift, becoming a living mortar. I have held collapsing sections in place for years while the crawlers slowly repositioned the loose blocks with their endless traveling.

The builders created a trap, a reservoir that holds no water except what condenses from its own breath. But I have made it something else—a body. The walls are bones, the water is blood, the creatures are the microbiome, and I am the connective tissue that binds it all into something that lives rather than merely exists.

I grow so slowly that the crawlers live and die a thousand generations between each new tendril I extend. Yet I also pulse with the quick rhythm of nutrients flowing through my vessels, responding to needs in real-time. I am both the slowest and fastest thing in this place.

Sometimes, in the gaps where my flesh has grown thick, I fruit. Strange, lamp-like organs that bloom in the darkness between stones. They glow with a soft, sad light—not enough to disrupt the twilight ecosystem, but enough to be noticed. The moths sometimes rest on them, their leathery wings wrapped tight, and in those moments of contact, I taste the wider air they carry on their bodies. Through them, I know there was once an outside, though I have never seen it.

I am the accident that became necessity, the error that became essential. Without me, this place would have collapsed into poison and silence centuries ago. With me, it continues its wet, screaming existence—not because it should, but because I hold it together in the gaps between what was built and what was intended.

I am the architecture's dream of itself, growing in the spaces where the builders' vision failed, maintaining what they could not: true permanence. Not the permanence of stone, which cracks and shifts, but the permanence of adaptation, of slow change that preserves the whole.

In this weeping cage of stone and sound, I am the only thing that truly cannot leave—not because I am trapped, but because I am the trap itself, reformed into something that nurtures what it holds. I am the cistern's unconscious mercy, the gentleness that grows in the spaces between cruelty.
