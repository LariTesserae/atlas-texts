I would be one of the maintenance walkers—a Stone-Dweller whose days unfold in the careful tending of the boundary spaces where metal meets moss, where industry breathes with the wild.

Each dawn, I wake to the sound of condensation dripping from my dwelling's eaves, a liquid percussion that has marked every morning of my thirty-seven years. My home sits in the middle tier of Building Seven, one of the angular structures that rises like geometric fungus from the basin floor. The walls sweat constantly; I've long since given up trying to keep them fully dry. Instead, I've learned which corners to avoid, which surfaces to oil, where to place the moisture-catching vessels that I empty each evening into the communal cisterns.

My work takes me through the gaps between buildings, along the catwalks that span the black streams, into the humid chambers where the generators pulse their eternal rhythm. I carry a leather satchel heavy with tools—wrenches wrapped in oiled cloth, brushes for clearing moss from crucial joints, small vials of the organic lubricant we harvest from certain bog plants that grow where the water moves slowest. But I also carry shears for directing vine growth, collection jars for the bioluminescent moss spores that we cultivate in the darkest machinery spaces, and always, always, a small net for catching Mist-Midges when their swarms grow too dense around the intake valves.

I've developed a particular sensitivity to the basin's rhythms. I can tell by the pitch of a generator's hum when condensation is beginning to pool in its housing. I know from the way the Obsidian Salamanders cluster at certain stream bends that the water flow will shift within days. The angle of vine growth on the eastern buildings tells me about wind patterns that affect our whole microclimate.

My route is never quite the same. The basin changes—slowly, but it changes. A new seep appears in the rock face. A congregation of Slick-Tongues blocks a usually clear channel. The Building Three collective decides to expand their fungi cultivation onto what was a walkway, so I must find a new path. I adapt. We all adapt.

There's a particular place I always pause, where an old waterwheel housing has been completely overtaken by moss—not abandoned, but transformed. The wheel still turns inside its verdant cocoon, and I maintain the bearings through gaps in the growth, but the moss has become structural now, holding certain components in place better than the original bolts. Here, I often encounter the same large salamander, easily the length of my forearm, that has claimed a pool in the mechanism's spray. We've grown familiar with each other over the years. Sometimes I share part of my lunch—fermented root paste—which it accepts with what I've come to interpret as dignified appreciation.

In the evenings, I return to Building Seven as the mist thickens and the bioluminescent moss begins its faint glow. I stop at the communal washhouse to rinse the day's accumulation of moisture and machine oil from my outer layers. Then home, where I'll tend my own small cultivation—a variety of fern that I'm selecting for its ability to absorb the specific metallic tang from our air. My dwelling smells of wet stone and the sharp sweetness of this plant, mixed with the ever-present industrial undertone that seeps through everything.

Before sleep, I often stand at my narrow window, watching the Mist-Midges swirl in the light from the lower-level workshops, listening to the layered sounds: water rushing, generators humming, metal occasionally singing its expansion song, amphibians calling across the dark streams. This pocket of the world asks for patience, for careful attention, for a willingness to work with rather than against the persistent damp and the slow green force that would reclaim everything if we let it.

I am a keeper of balances, a tender of boundaries, a person who has learned that in this basin, the distinction between the built and the grown, the mechanical and the living, is not a line but a conversation—one that never ends, only deepens with each turning of the seasons in our mist-wrapped world.
