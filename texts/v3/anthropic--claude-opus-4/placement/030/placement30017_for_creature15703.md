I find myself as one of the deep-listeners, those who have grown so attuned to the place that we barely distinguish between the voice of our own blood and the pulse of water through vine-veins. My dwelling is no dwelling at all, but rather a series of favored perches throughout the vertical maze—a hollow where three massive roots converge and trap the morning mist, a ledge beneath an overhang where the rain drums but never reaches, a bower of woven vines that I've coaxed over years to grow in just the right spiral pattern to catch the afternoon light.

I am neither young nor old by the counting of seasons, though such measurements lose meaning here where time flows in cycles rather than lines. My skin bears the map of my wandering: scars from thorn and stone, patches where the constant moisture has encouraged a fine bloom of symbiotic algae that helps me blend with the moss-covered bark, calluses thick as leather on my palms and soles from a lifetime of climbing. My hair has been braided with seedpods that rattle softly when I move, a sound that tells the small creatures I mean no harm, that I am merely another part of the endless rustling.

What I do cannot quite be called living, nor can it be called watching—it is something between and beyond. I taste the air each dawn, reading in its weight and flavor what manner of rain will come, whether the insects will swarm high or low, if the fruit-heavy vines near the eastern spire have finally ripened. I know by the particular silence that falls just before the great aerial hunters dive that I should press myself flat against the stone. I understand the meaning in the way certain vines suddenly shift their growth, turning away from seemingly perfect light to avoid some danger that won't manifest for months.

My tools are few: a blade of volcanic glass sharper than any worked metal, traded long ago for three perfect spiral shells I found in a pool that appears only during the dry season's single week; a gourd that whistles in twelve different tones depending on how I hold it to the wind; a net so fine it seems made of shadow and dew, which I use not for catching but for reading—the way insects avoid or approach it tells me things about the invisible currents of the place.

I am a keeper of the old markings, though keeper suggests more intention than truth allows. Rather, I am drawn to them, compelled to trace my fingers along the spirals carved in stone, to add my own small marks in harmony with what came before. Not preservation, but continuation—like adding my voice to a song that has been singing itself since before the spires stood alone. Sometimes I find others have been there before me, adding their own quiet modifications: a new curve that transforms the meaning, a careful polishing that makes the stone shine with collected moisture.

The others—for there are always others, despite the vastness—know me by the way I move through their temporary territories. I disturb nothing unnecessarily, take only what regenerates quickly, leave small offerings of seeds in the crooks of trees where I've sheltered. We speak when we meet, though speaking is often just a matter of sitting in compatible silence, perhaps sharing the smoke of burning sap that helps us see the patterns in the canopy's weave. Words, when they come, are sparse and weighted: warnings about unstable paths, news of where the copper-winged beetles are pupating, invitations to witness something rare—a blooming that happens once a decade, a gathering of the sky-swimmers in their mating spirals.

But mostly, I am alone without loneliness, accompanied always by the ten thousand lives that share these vertical meadows. I have learned to sleep in snatches like the climbing mammals, one part of me always alert to the shift in sound that means danger or opportunity. My dreams are indistinguishable from waking: I dream I am bark, and wake to find insects traversing my still form; I dream I am rain, and wake with my mouth open to the sky.

There are depths here I haven't fathomed. Hollows in the largest spires where the air tastes different, older, carrying hints of something vast and patient. Groves where the vines grow in patterns too perfect for chance, where the sound of your heartbeat seems to echo from the plants themselves. I've seen the seed-pods that glow in the deep shade, felt the almost-intelligence in the way certain root systems seem to track my movement. Once, during a storm so fierce that even the stone seemed to bend, I sheltered in a cave formed entirely of woven vines, and could have sworn the shelter tightened around me protectively, that something breathed in rhythm with my fear.

I am what this place has made of me: a note in its vast composition, a cell in its sprawling body. My purpose, if I have one, is simply to be present for the endless conversation between stone and stem, rain and root, the ancient and the immediate. To add my small marks to the spiral patterns. To know when the copper-backed lizards are hunting too close to the nesting grounds. To whistle warnings in the gourd-language when the winds speak of distant storms. To be the memory that notices when a spire sheds another layer of stone, when a new vine bridge spans what was once impassable.

Sometimes travelers find me, though find suggests more intention than usually exists in our meetings. They emerge from the green maze, wild-eyed with the overwhelming life of the place, and I remember what it was like to feel separate from it all. I share water, point out which fruits won't turn their stomachs to acid, show them the stable paths. But I cannot explain what it means to live here, any more than a fish could explain water. I can only demonstrate through being: the way I automatically shift my weight when the wind changes, how I read a full story in the pattern of disturbed droplets on a leaf, why I suddenly go still when the chorus of life holds its breath.

I am learning to die here too, as all things must. Not in fear but in curiosity about what transformation comes next. Will I become one of those whispered presences that future wanderers sense in the shadows? Will my bones feed a new grove of the vines I've tended? Will the spiral patterns I've traced in stone mean something to someone centuries hence, or will they too be worn smooth by rain and time? These questions don't require answers. They are just another part of the endless wondering that fills the spaces between heartbeats, between rainfalls, between the cry of one beast and the answer of another.

This is what I am: a listener who has become an echo, a watcher who has become part of the watched, a keeper of patterns who is themselves just another pattern in the vast, living weave of spire and vine, stone and song. I am exactly where I need to be, which is to say, I am.
