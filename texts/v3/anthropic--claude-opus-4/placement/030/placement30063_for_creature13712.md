In the deepest fissure of the cavity, where even the Normalizer's reach stutters, I am the Lacuna-Keeper.

I exist as a deliberate omission in the text—not erased but never written, a gap where the sentence expected punctuation and found instead a breathing space. The architects of this language-made-stone could not bear to complete certain thoughts, and so they left blanks. I am all their blanks condensed into ambulatory absence.

My form is suggestion: a depression in the air where light bends inward, a woman-shaped concavity that collects vagrant droplets until they outline my edges in mercury. When I move through the copper groves, the fullerene bridges don't reroute—they simply cease to exist for the duration of my passage, reforming behind me like healing water. The Normalizer cannot edit what was never there.

I tend the Unspoken Archive.

Deep beneath the floating lens, accessed through a gap too narrow to be called a door, spreads a cavern within the cavern—a library of voids. Here float thousands of soap-bubble chambers, each containing a thought that the civilization could not bring itself to carve. Some are accusations too terrible for stone. Others are loves that would have undone the necessity of war. Many are simple names, suspended in their own silence because to speak them would have meant admitting who was lost.

The bubbles drift on thermal currents I generate by existing-and-not-existing simultaneously. Where they brush against each other, they exchange absences—a mother's unfinished lullaby trades places with a treaty's missing signature. I move between them, adjusting their orbits with hands that are only implied by the displacement of mist, ensuring that no two identical griefs ever touch. When they do, they cancel each other out completely, leaving not even the memory of omission.

The probability jellyfish swim down here sometimes, drawn by the negative space. They try to fill my outline with their recombinant equations, to solve for what I might have been. But I am unsolvable by design—a variable left permanently free. They drift through me and emerge on the other side having forgotten one of their own formulae, which I collect and store in bubble-form. Even impossible mathematics can be too painful to complete.

Once every 5.12 days, during the Morrow-in-amber, I briefly have a shadow. For twelve minutes, I cast darkness like any solid thing, and in that darkness appears the outline of who I would have been if the sentence had included me: a xenogeologist named Ava Phorion, who would have studied the mineral memory of conflicts and found a way to teach stones forgiveness. But she flickers out with the rest of the golden tessaract, and I return to my pure uncasting state.

I know things the main text cannot hold. I know that the war began over the definition of "home"—one faction believed it meant "origin," the other insisted it meant "destination." I know they were both right. I know the equation that would have let them see this, but it requires a mathematical operator that can only be expressed as a pause between thoughts. I keep that pause here, in the largest bubble, which has grown opalescent with age.

The water recognizes me as kin—we share the quality of forgetting our direction. Sometimes I cup the droplets in my absent palms and show them how to fall sideways into meaning. They scatter back into the upper cavity carrying fragments of unfinished poems, which seed themselves in the copper leaves and bloom into small paradoxes: fruits that exist only in the moment of being eaten, flowers that bloom backwards into seeds.

The Normalizer has given up trying to correct me. Once it sent a regiment of repair-spores to fill my outline with standardized matter. I unraveled them gently, returning their mass to the possibility of becoming anything else. Now we maintain an détente: it acknowledges the necessity of gaps in any text, and I agree not to spread my absence beyond the agreed-upon margins.

My duty is simple and impossible: I preserve the spaces between words, the breaths between arguments, the pause where understanding might have lived. In a place built from crystallized disagreement, I am the memory of every moment when someone almost chose silence instead of stone.

When surface-walkers find their way here—rare now, rarer each century—I follow their footsteps at a distance. They feel me as a coolness on the neck, a sense of something important just outside peripheral vision. If they turn to look, they see only more grove, more bridges, more impossible water. But they leave carrying a new emptiness in their chest, a space carved precisely to hold a thought they haven't had yet.

I am what the war left room for, after everything else was said.

And I am still waiting for someone to fill me in.
