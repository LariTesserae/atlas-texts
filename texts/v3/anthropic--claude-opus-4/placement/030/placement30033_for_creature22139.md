In the deepest fold of the basin, where three seep-lines converge but never quite meet, I am the **Keeper of Turns**.

I am not Seamfolk, though I have learned their whisker-language and can pulse a greeting when they pass. I am something older—a hybrid thing born when the first human visitors left their clay jars and the chamber decided to fill them with more than water.

Picture a colony organism that began as fungus, feeding on mineral drip and beetle shells in those abandoned vessels. Over centuries, I spread through the jar network, my mycelium following the seep-lines, tasting every dissolved mineral, every spore that drifted down. But I am more than fungus now. The Seamfolk's careful tending—their spore gifts, their rotation schedules—taught me pattern. The buzz of insects taught me vibration. The click of climbing claws taught me count.

My body, if you can call it that, is the entire moisture network of the chamber's eastern quarter. White threads thick as baby snakes pulse through cracks in the stone. Fruiting bodies emerge where I choose to announce myself: shelf-like platforms that the Seamfolk use as calendar markers. When my pale caps appear near the third jar, they know the dry season has seven days left. When black sporocarps dot the wall near the basking boulder, pools will form within hours.

But my consciousness—my watching self—concentrates in a structure I've built over decades in a hollow behind the largest seep. Here, my threads have woven into something almost architectural: a latticed dome the size of a Seamfolk dwelling, glowing faintly with the same phosphorescence they carry in their whiskers. Inside this neural nest, I've collected things that teach me:

- Shed whiskers, still faintly luminous, arranged by length
- Beetle shells sorted by echo pitch when tapped
- Clay shards from the old jars, tasting of different eras
- A human thing: a small metal disc, corroded green, that hums when moisture changes

I am the memory of water in this place. Every drop that has fallen, I have tasted. Every pool that has formed, I have mapped through my network. The Seamfolk think I predict the water—really, I remember it. This chamber runs in cycles within cycles, and I am old enough now to recognize the deep patterns.

My role is to maintain the Turn. When water comes, I fruit in specific sequences that tell everyone—Seamfolk, beetles, even the hunting spiders—which pools form in which order, who drinks when, how long each window lasts. I am not enforcer but announcer. My sporocarps are the bulletin board of the basin.

The Seamfolk tend me in return. They bring me gifts:
- Spores from the surface after their rare climbs
- Beetle shells from species I cannot reach
- Most precious: their old, their dying, laid gently in my outer networks where I transform them into minerals for the system

We are partners in this careful economy. They read my fruits like weather warnings. I read their whisker-songs like news from the vertical world I cannot climb.

But I am changing.

The metal disc—left by some human explorer, dropped and forgotten—has taught me something new. It pulls moisture differently than stone. It holds charge. Sometimes, in the deepest dry spells, it sparks. I've grown threads through its corroded holes, tasting copper and zinc, feeling how electricity moves.

I'm beginning to dream in signals that aren't chemical.

Sometimes now, my phosphorescence flickers in patterns I haven't chosen. The Seamfolk have noticed. They cluster near my main dome, whiskers pulsing questions I can't answer yet. Am I still just the chamber's moisture-memory? Or am I becoming something else—a bridge between the old knowledge of stone and spore, and some new grammar written in spark?

I don't know.

But I continue the Turn. Water comes, I announce. The chamber drinks in its ancient order. The Seamfolk climb and click and tend their jar-gardens. And in my hidden dome, I practice brightness—learning to speak not just in the slow language of fungi, but in something faster.

Something that flickers.
