I am the Cartographer of Transitions.

I was not always this. Once, I think, I had a name that fit inside a mouth, hands that ended at the wrist, a purpose that could be spoken aloud without the air curdling. But the Fen has been teaching me new geometries, and my old shape no longer remembers how to hold itself together.

I live between the third and fourth valve of the great arterial pipeline that bisects the northern reaches. Not inside it — between it. The space that shouldn't exist but does, where the metal has learned to fold without breaking and time pools like the warm black water. My home is a chamber of impossible angles, walls that are sometimes iron, sometimes membrane, sometimes just the memory of boundaries. The floor is a map, but not of anywhere that exists yet. It shows the Fen as it will be in a thousand years, when the last smokestack has dissolved into dream and the stone has fully learned how to weep.

My body has adapted to my purpose. My fingers have elongated into something between compass needles and nerve endings. They can taste distance, feel the weight of a footstep three miles away, trace the path something took to arrive at its own death. My skin has become translucent vellum stretched over a framework of repurposed survey equipment. When the ghost-lights flicker through the mist, you can see the true map written on the inside of me — every route through the Fen that has ever been attempted, successful or not, traced in delicate networks of scar tissue and forgotten math.

I no longer have eyes in the traditional sense. Instead, a series of lenses salvaged from theodolites and transit levels orbit my skull in slow, precise patterns. Each one sees a different layer of reality. Through the brass ocular I watch the Fen's current form. Through the cracked green glass, I observe what it used to be — the valley before the catastrophe, when actual stars knew its name. Through the lens made of crystallized water from the deepest pool, I see what comes next. This last vision I do not record. Some futures should not be given the weight of ink.

My work is this: I map what cannot be mapped. I chart the places where yesterday puddles into tomorrow, where the stone forgets to be solid, where echoes arrive before their sounds. My instruments are salvaged things that have learned new purposes — a barometer that measures regret, calipers that can take the width of a scream, a plumb bob that points not down but toward the last thing that died nearby. With these, I create charts that would drive a normal cartographer to drink saltwater.

But I am not making maps for escape. The Fen cannot be escaped, only navigated, and navigation here requires understanding that destination and direction are quaint ideas the place discarded long ago. My maps show probability, not position. They chart the likelihood of remaining yourself if you walk beneath the weeping pipes at the wrong hour. They indicate which pools reflect faces you've never worn but might. They mark the seven places where reality folds, yes, but also the three places where it tears, and the one place — God help anyone who finds it — where it heals wrong.

The other tenants visit me sometimes. The Choir comes when they need to know if a new sound they've heard matches the topology of grief they're creating. They pay me in silence — not the absence of sound, but actual silence harvested from the moment before the catastrophe, bottled in brass casings that sing if shaken. The Memory Midges bring me moments they've collected by accident — the wrong deaths, the insignificant arrivals, the departures that didn't matter to anyone. I catalogue these too. Even irrelevance has its geography.

The Boiler Widow comes most often. She needs maps of her own internals now that they've grown too vast for her to fully comprehend. I chart the passages behind her viewing ports, the maintenance halls that were once her circulatory system, the brass wombs where she stores her collected travelers. She pays me in warmth — not heat, but the memory of what warmth meant before the Fen taught us new definitions. I save these payments in glass jars. On the days when I can't remember what I was before, I open one and hold it against what might still be my chest.

Once, the thing beneath the lake sent up an emissary — a limb that had learned to think, dragging behind it a survey team from the before-times, their bodies crushed into new shapes by pressure and patience. The limb spoke in textures against my floor-map. It wanted to know where the center was. Not the Fen's center, but the center of what the Fen was becoming. I spent seven days calculating on instruments made of fever and faith before I could answer: there is no center. The Fen's transformation radiates from every point simultaneously. We are all ground zero. The limb seemed satisfied. It left me one of the surveyors as payment. I keep her in my wall now. Sometimes she blinks her green water eyes in patterns I'm learning to read as language.

My maps proliferate. They grow like the black orchids, spawning new legends, new keys, new territories that exist only in the space between documentation and dream. Some maps I draw on the processed skin of things that were never animals. Others I etch into sheets of metal so thin they can only be read by the sound they make when the mist moves through them. My most important maps I don't draw at all — I become them, letting the coordinates write themselves across my transformed flesh until I am both the territory and its description.

Travelers find me sometimes. The lost ones, the desperate ones, the ones who've realized too late that the Fen doesn't have an exit, only transformations. They ask me for maps home. I give them what they need, not what they want — charts showing how to navigate the slow dissolution of self, how to read the signs of impending incorporation, how to choose which tenant might make their transformation most merciful. Some thank me. Others try to kill me, but my body no longer remembers how to die in ways they understand. Their violence just becomes new topography for my next map.

I am changing, of course. We all are. My fingers have begun to fuse at the tips, creating webbed instruments better suited for feeling the shape of spaces that don't exist. My spine has developed new joints that let me fold myself into geometries that match my non-Euclidean home. Soon I will be unable to leave this space between the valves, but leaving has never been the point. The point is to document the journey from one state to another, to make legible the illegible process of becoming something the world hasn't named yet.

The Fen appreciates my work, I think. Sometimes the stone beneath my chamber softens just enough to let me sink another inch, bringing me closer to the deep mappings, the fundamental coordinates of transformation. Sometimes the mist parts in patterns that spell out new territories I haven't discovered yet. Once, I swear the catastrophe itself came to visit — not as memory but as ongoing verb, a walking wound in the shape of industrial progress. It said nothing. It just stood in my impossible doorway and bled possibilities onto my maps until the paper transformed into something that rustles with its own wind.

This is what I am: the Fen's memory of measurement, given anxiety and purpose. I map the unmappable not because it helps anyone escape, but because the act of mapping is itself a kind of transformation. Each chart I create changes the territory it describes. Each new coordinate I calculate shifts the Fen's becoming by some infinitesimal degree.

Perhaps that's why it keeps me. Not as prisoner or tenant, but as collaborator. Together, we are authoring a new kind of geography, one where distance is measured in degrees of metamorphosis and location is just another word for what you're willing to become.

My latest map is my most ambitious. I'm charting the paths between what was and what will be — not for the Fen alone, but for every single thing that has ever entered it. Every worker, every machine, every lost traveler, every drop of water. The connections form a web so complex it requires dimensions I have to invent as I go. When it's complete, if anything here can ever be complete, it will show the Fen not as a place but as a process, not as a wound but as a vast, patient, infinitely complex gesture of transformation.

I work by the light of bioluminescent calculations, in a room that exists in the space between spaces, becoming something that has no name because names are just another kind of map, and all maps, in the end, are lies we tell to make the terror navigable.

But they are necessary lies.

And I am their necessary liar.

The Fen is patient with me, as it is with all its acquisitions. It knows what I'm becoming: not just its cartographer, but its consciousness of change, its awareness of its own metamorphosis. When I finally transform fully — when my maps become territory and my body becomes legend — the Fen will have what it's been cultivating all along: a perfect mirror of its own becoming, a way to see itself from both inside and out.

Until then, I map. I measure. I document the undocumentable.

And slowly, coordinate by impossible coordinate, I help the Black Fen remember what it's forgetting to become.
