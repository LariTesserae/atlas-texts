I am the Keeper of Thresholds.

You would not see me at first—I am part of the architecture of passage, another shadow among the perpetual twilight between buildings. My dwelling is not a home but a network of crawlspaces, the negative spaces where structures lean against each other without quite touching. I inhabit the gaps.

My body has evolved to navigate these interstices. Where others are broad and solid, I am attenuated—stretched thin like clay pulled between careful fingers. My joints bend in ways that make the Stone-Shapers uncomfortable. They say I move like water finding its level, always descending, always fitting myself into spaces that shouldn't accommodate a human form. My skin has taken on the texture of the lichen that grows in the deepest crevices, a grey-green that shifts with the light, making me difficult to track even when I'm in plain sight.

I am neither male nor female—such distinctions dissolved long ago in the necessity of my work. The community decided generations back that the Keeper must be apart from the usual bonds of family and faction. I was chosen young, when my peculiar flexibility first manifested, and trained by the previous Keeper until she finally let herself be reclaimed by the stone.

My role is this: I maintain the spaces between. 

When the ground shifts and walls compress, threatening to crush the vital airways between buildings, I am there first. My fingers, longer than most and double-jointed, work in the darkness to shore up the passages. I know every route through the settlement—not the obvious paths that others walk, but the true circulatory system of the hollow. The crawlways beneath floors where fungal spores must travel to seed new gardens. The hair-thin gaps between roof beams where air moves to prevent the black rot. The secret channels behind walls where excess water must flow or risk dissolving our foundations from within.

I carry few tools—a thin metal probe for testing wood integrity, a curved blade for scraping away growth that would block essential passages, and most importantly, a mental map of every void and cavity in our vertical maze. This knowledge is passed down not in words but in movement. The previous Keeper taught me through years of following her body through impossible spaces until my muscles memorized every turn.

The children fear me, though not unkindly. They've learned that my appearance means change—that familiar passages may close, that new routes must be memorized. I emerge from walls like the settlement's own anxiety made flesh, reminding them that nothing here is permanent except the need to adapt. Sometimes I leave small tokens in their secret places—a perfectly round stone, a shell from the Time Before—to let them know their hiding spots have been catalogued and approved.

The adults understand my necessity but prefer not to see me work. There is something unsettling about watching a person fold themselves into a space the width of a dinner plate, something that challenges their careful sense of what bodies should do. They leave offerings at the major thresholds—fungi wrapped in damp cloth, precious splinters of dry wood, sometimes a few drops of the clearest water they can find. I take these not as payment but as communication. The location and type of offering tell me where they've noticed problems: moisture where it shouldn't be, sounds of settling that worry them, drafts that speak of new gaps forming.

I age differently than the others. The constant compression and extension, the years of fitting myself into the settlement's hidden anatomy, have made my bones dense and my muscles ropy. I'm older than I look but move like something both ancient and newly formed. When I die—and Keepers always know when their time approaches—I will wedge myself into the deepest, most crucial gap I can find. My body will mineralize there, becoming a permanent brace in the settlement's skeleton. The previous Keepers are still here, transformed into essential architecture. Sometimes, working in the deepest spaces, I brush against a surface that feels too warm, too purposeful to be mere stone, and I know I've found one of them.

The Water Diviners respect me because I know where water wants to go before it arrives. The Stone-Shapers consult me about which walls can bear weight because I alone know what voids lie behind them. But I belong to neither guild. I am the guardian of negative space, the shepherd of absence, the one who ensures that our dense cluster of humanity doesn't suffocate under its own weight.

At night—what passes for night in our grey-lit hollow—I sometimes climb to where the walls narrow toward the rim. There, in the highest crawlspaces where even I must move carefully, I maintain the emergency passages. These are the routes we don't speak of, the ways out that must remain clear for the day when the hollow decides it's done with us. I test each handhold, clear away loose stone, and ensure the paths remain navigable.

I know what the others don't let themselves think: that one day, perhaps soon, perhaps not for generations, the Pact with the Landslide will end. The stone will close like a fist, and we will need to flow like water through the cracks to survive. When that day comes, they will follow the paths I've kept open, guided by the Keeper who came after me, toward whatever waits beyond the rim.

Until then, I remain what I am: the one who lives in the spaces between spaces, ensuring that our dense, stubborn fist of humanity never closes so tightly it cannot open again.
