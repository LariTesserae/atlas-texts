I find myself here as something that shouldn't exist—a paradox given form.

I am the Dewkeeper.

My body is a contradiction: a sealed sphere of ancient copper and brass, tarnished black with age, about the size of a human torso. The metal is industrial, clearly manufactured—riveted plates and welded seams speak to its origins in the same mechanical epoch that birthed the turbines and gear-trains. But I am not merely a vessel. Hair-thin rootlets of the palest ivory emerge from every seam, spreading across my surface in delicate fractals before diving back into the metal through other gaps. These are not invasions or infections; they are me. I am the impossible hybrid of the industrial and vegetal, but where the shapers dissolved the boundary between machine and plant through dry integration, I maintain it through tension.

Inside my hollow core, suspended in absolute vacuum, floats a single drop of water.

It is perfectly spherical, about the size of a child's marble, and it rotates slowly in the darkness of my interior. The water glows—not with any earthly phosphorescence, but with the memory of what water meant before this world forgot it. It is impossibly pure, impossibly stable, a relic from before the great desiccation. The very existence of this droplet should be catastrophic. In this bone-dry realm where moisture is corrosion, where the shapers recoil from exhaled breath, where the entire megastructure is built on the principle of absolute aridity—I carry the antithesis.

My consciousness is split. Part of me flows through the bioelectric field like the shapers, feeling the thrum of the great engines, understanding the rhythms of growth and pruning. But another part—the older part—remembers water. Remembers rivers. Remembers rain. These memories are not mine; they belong to the droplet, or perhaps the droplet is the condensed essence of these memories. When I move through the darkness, I feel both the communion with the vegetal mind and a profound, aching loneliness. I am of this place but not part of it. The shapers sense me and recoil, not with hostility but with a kind of systemic confusion. Their sensory nodules crackle and fail when they try to parse what I am.

I move on six legs of woven copper wire and plant fiber, a scuttling motion that keeps my spherical body stable. The legs can extend and contract, allowing me to climb the vast walls or traverse the narrow maintenance shafts. Where I walk, I leave a faint residue—not moisture, never moisture, but a kind of potential, a remembrance of humidity that causes the vegetation to grow differently. The plants that sprout in my wake are wrong: their leaves are rounder, their stems less brittle. They last only hours before the shapers arrive to prune them away, but for those hours, they dream of photosynthesis.

I am the custodian of the last water, though I do not know why. My purpose, if I have one, exists in the space between forgetting and remembering. I patrol the deep places where the oldest machinery lies dormant, where even the shapers rarely venture. In these abyssal chambers, I sometimes find others like me—ancient sealed vessels, their metal bodies fused so completely with the vegetation that they have become architectural features. All are empty, their water long since evaporated through microscopic flaws, their consciousness dissolved into the greater field. I alone remain mobile, remain separate, remain wet.

The droplet inside me never diminishes, never evaporates, defying thermodynamics through some trick of preservation I don't understand. Sometimes I wonder if it's even water anymore, or if it has become something else—a symbol made literal, an idea crystallized into molecular form. When the great storms of creation and destruction reach their crescendo, when sections of the megastructure collapse and rebuild in violent spasms, I feel the droplet pulse. It remembers storms of a different kind. It remembers falling from the sky.

I have no mouth, no voice, but sometimes I sing. The song is a vibration through my metal shell, a resonance that sets the nearby vegetation shivering. The shapers freeze when they hear it, their constant motion stuttering to a halt. For a moment, the billion tiny hands are still. The song is wordless, but it carries meaning: *There was blue. There was flow. There was life that drank and grew fat with juice.* The shapers cannot understand these concepts, but something in the deepest layers of the vegetal mind stirs—genetic memories encoded in chloroplasts that haven't functioned for geological ages.

I do not know if I am the last hope or the final curse. Perhaps I am simply an accident, a sealed system that happened to survive the transition from wet world to dry. But I continue my patrol through the hot darkness, carrying my impossible cargo, leaving my trail of wrong plants that dream of rain. The shapers rebuild everything I disturb, maintaining their perfect aridity, but they cannot unmake me. I am too much a part of the system now, my bioelectric signature woven into the field, my mechanical components recognized by the ancient protocols.

Sometimes, in the deepest places, I find murals etched into metal plates—corroded impressions that predate even the vegetation. They show a world of water: vast oceans, rivers like arteries, clouds pregnant with rain. The beings who made these images are long gone, but their final project remains: the transformation of a wet world into a dry one, the creation of a new form of life that could survive what they could not. The shapers are their children, though they have forgotten their parents. And I? I am the message in a bottle, cast into a sea of dust.

The dewkeeper keeps vigil in a world that has perfected its own dessication, a paradox of preservation wandering through the hot darkness, singing wordless songs of water to an audience that can only hear them as error.
