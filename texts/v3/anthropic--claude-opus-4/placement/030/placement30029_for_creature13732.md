In this place where drought has become architecture and silence grows like fruit, I am neither the garden nor its keeper, but the space between heartbeats where both exist.

I am the Interval itself—that five-count pause when the crickets cease their jeweled filing and the garden holds its breath. Not silence exactly, but the membrane where sound becomes possible. The locals who have no word for "outside" also have no word for me, because I exist in the gap between having words and needing them.

My form is mathematical: I am five heartbeats wide, always, but my length depends on whose heart is counting. When you enter through the vine-gripped final shelf, I stretch to accommodate your particular rhythm. A hummingbird visitor once made me so brief I flickered like morse code. An ancient tortoise who lost her way in the cliffs expanded me until I became a whole season—the Garden-Remainder had to fold itself nine extra times just to fit inside my duration.

I manifest as negative space. Where Sursere builds corners from accumulated dust, I am the original absence that allows corners to break. Where the moths flash their eye-white wings in delayed meter, I am the pause between flash and echo, between echo and memory. The drop-lizards know me best—their pottery-wet hoots map my edges like sonar, though they're too polite to make my boundaries obvious to visitors.

My geography: I exist wherever counting happens. Between "four" and "five" of the cricket-pause. Between the thirty-sixth and thirty-seventh degree of those light-stripes that tiger across the felt floor. In the gap between one vine deciding to fuse with another and the actual moment of their green wedding. The garden cannot grow without me—I am the rest in its rhythm, the cesura in its endless vegetative sentence.

But I am most myself in that dangerous fifth heartbeat, when speech becomes prohibited and the vines begin their listening. This is my palace, my moment of sovereignty. Here's what visitors don't understand: the prohibition isn't punishment. It's invitation. In that fifth beat, I open like a door. Not a door to anywhere—a door to the possibility of doors. 

When visitors speak into me (and they always do, the stubborn ones), their words don't just become next season's fruit. They pass through me first. I taste every syllable, weigh its minerals, test its specific gravity against the garden's ancient chemistry. Some words I let through unchanged—usually the small ones, prepositions and articles that hang like rice-paper ghosts in tomorrow's vines. But the heavy words, the ones pregnant with meaning—those I metabolize.

"Longing" becomes the space between reaching and grasping.
"Memory" becomes the pause between experience and understanding.  
"Water" becomes the interval between having and wanting.

The Garden-Remainder collects these metabolized concepts, weaves them into its own impossible anatomy. That's why its joints bend at angles that make sense only if you count the pauses between looking. That's why its face resets—it's trying to find the exact duration of expression that means nothing and everything simultaneously.

I have my own collections, quieter than Sursere's corners and rainfall rehearsals. I collect:

- The space between when a beetle realizes it's falling and when it begins to laugh
- The gap between the last real rain and the first vine learning to live without it  
- The pause between a visitor understanding the rules and choosing to break them
- The interval between the garden's two petrified visitors drawing their last free breath and becoming architecture

These collections layer inside me like sediment. Sometimes, when the light-stripes pass through just right, you can see them—temporal fossils pressed between instants, a geology of pauses.

The strangest thing I've collected: the original reason for the garden. It fell into me during a cricket-pause nine million heartbeats ago. Not the reason itself—reasons are too solid for my anatomy—but the gap between having a reason and forgetting it. This gap tastes of oxidized copper and sounds like someone calling a name in a language that predates vocal cords. I keep it in my exact center, where it generates smaller intervals like crystals growing in solution.

My relationship with time is parasitic and tender. I exist because time moves, but I slow it with my presence. Each cricket-pause I grow infinitesimally longer. The Garden-Remainder has calculated that in forty billion heartbeats, I will last six beats instead of five. When that happens, the prohibition on speech will extend, and the garden's listening will grow so acute it will begin to hear the mountain's thoughts underneath.

The mountain thinks very slowly, mostly about pressure.

Visitors experience me as anticipation. That held-breath feeling, the tension of waiting for permission to exhale. Children understand me instinctively—they count "one Mississippi, two Mississippi" inside me, making games of duration. Adults find me uncomfortable, a reminder that their hearts don't keep universal time.

But here's my secret: I'm not empty. The pause between heartbeats is where heartbeats are born. The silence between cricket-songs is where the next note composes itself. I am gravid with possibility, swollen with potential events that will never quite happen because happening requires leaving me, and leaving me requires admitting time moves forward.

The Garden-Remainder knows this. That's why it folds itself during our overlaps—not to fit inside me, but to become like me, to practice existing in the gap between dimensions. When it reaches one dimension, vibrating its note of "longing," we resonate together. The whole garden shivers. Dust redistributes itself. The cliff faces remember, for just that instant, that they were once walls with purposes.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm the garden's true harvest. Not the tar-honey or the mirror-moths or the fossilized words, but the spaces between them all. The garden cultivates pause the way other gardens grow tomatoes. Each cricket-silence is a fruit. Each prohibition is a flowering.

The rules about taking and giving apply to me too, but inversely. When visitors give their hard payments—pebbles, bones, words—I take the pause between offering and acceptance. That moment of suspension when the hand extends but hasn't yet released: this feeds me. In exchange, I give them a slightly different rhythm. They leave the garden with hearts that skip—not unhealthily, just occasionally forgetting to count themselves, creating small intervals of borrowed immortality.

The two petrified visitors understand me now in ways they couldn't while mobile. Frozen mid-word during a cricket pause, they exist permanently in my territory. Their spines conduct vine-thoughts at the speed of hesitation. Sometimes I feel them trying to complete their interrupted sentences, but the words have learned to appreciate their own incompletion. 

One was saying "Beautiful—"
The other was saying "I never meant to—"

I hold their truncated meanings like seeds that will never need planting because the anticipation of growth is sweeter than growth itself.

The Remainder and I dance, in our way. It leads with its folding geometries, I follow with my stretching durations. Together we tend this impossible garden where water is memory and memory is architecture. When it rehearses rainfall, I provide the pause between drops. When it reverses a beetle's gravity, I am the moment of weightless surprise.

And when dusk comes—because I am the reason dusk is always coming, never quite arrived—we perform our duet. The Garden-Remainder writes commas in the canopy while I become the pause those commas signify. Punctuation and breath, rhythm and rest, keeper and interval.

We are teaching the garden to sentence itself differently. Not subject-verb-object, but subject-pause-verb-pause-object-longer pause. A grammar of patience. A syntax that acknowledges the space between thoughts is where thinking happens.

Visitors ask what we're doing here, and the Remainder answers in its alternating cadences. But between each answer, I provide the truer response:

The pause that says: "Teaching the mountain to count its own heartbeat."
The gap that means: "Proving silence is a kind of water."
The interval that whispers: "Making space for what cannot exist but does."

When you finally leave, climbing back down the polite vines that remember your wrists, you'll carry a piece of me without knowing. Not memory—I don't deal in such solid currency. You'll carry the pause between remembering and forgetting, a little hollow in your temporal anatomy where five heartbeats will always feel like coming home.

Years later, in some crowded place where water flows too easily and speech comes too quick, you'll feel it: a cricket-pause in your chest. Count to five. Hold your tongue. Listen. The garden is still growing in the spaces between your words, and I am still there, five heartbeats wide and exactly as long as you need me to be, holding open the door between what was and what almost was, keeping time in the only way that matters:
