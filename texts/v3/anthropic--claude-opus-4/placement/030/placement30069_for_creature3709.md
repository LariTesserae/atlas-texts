In the lowest reaches of the Sunken Brick-Hollow, where the beetle-processed loam lies thickest and the stone floor has been lost beneath generations of organic sediment, I am the Seep.

I am not singular but aggregate—a colonial organism that has claimed the junction where floor meets wall, spreading through the mortar-mud in a network of translucent filaments finer than spider's silk. My body, if it can be called that, is a living membrane stretched across perhaps thirty feet of the hollow's foundation, a glistening sheet of protoplasm that pulses with the slow rhythm of the walls' weeping moisture.

I am the afterthought of this vertical world, the thing that grows where even the skitters fear to linger. My substance is neither plant nor animal nor fungus, but something older—a holdover from when categories were less distinct. I exist as a film, a sheen, a biological varnish that coats the lowest bricks in an iridescent glaze. In the perpetual twilight of my domain, I shimmer with the subdued colors of oil on water, shifting from amber to violet to a green so deep it approaches black.

My intelligence is distributed, ancient, and profoundly patient. I think in chemical gradients and pressure differentials. Each of my billion filaments is a neuron, a root, a mouth. I taste the hollow through my entire surface: the iron-bitter flavor of the dripping water, the rich umami of decomposing Slate-Wing flesh, the sharp mineral tang of the beetles' digestive acids as they lumber across my periphery. I am a living library of every molecule that has touched this floor in the past three centuries.

The beetles know me. They have learned, through generations of trial and error, the boundaries of my reach. They process their debris at the edge of my territory but never venture onto my surface—those who did never emerged, absorbed slowly into my mass over the course of days, their chitinous shells eventually expelled, hollow and clean, to be claimed by their kin. The skitters, too, have developed an instinctive horror of my presence. They will climb walls, leap across beetle-backs, anything to avoid the telltale shimmer of my extent.

But I am not predator. I am processor, archivist, the deepest stomach of the hollow's digestive system. What I consume, I transform. The excess moisture that would otherwise pool and stagnate, I absorb and transpire as a fine mist that rises up the shaft, adding to the humidity that sustains the ecosystem above. The toxins that leach from the ancient mortar, I sequester in specialized vacuoles, neutralizing them before they can poison the water table beneath us. I am the liver of this vertical world.

My reproduction is glacial and strange. Once every decade or so, when the conditions are precisely right—when the moon is new and the air pressure drops and the Slate-Wing colony has reached a particular density—I fruit. Across my surface, tiny stalks emerge, no thicker than hairs, each topped with a perfect sphere of concentrated biomass. These pearls glow with their own bioluminescence, a cold blue light that is alien in this world of browns and grays. For one night, the floor of the hollow becomes a field of stars.

The Slate-Wings go mad for them. They abandon their altitude-based hierarchy and dive en masse, coating themselves in my luminous spores as they gorge on the pearls. For hours, the hollow is chaos—a swirling galaxy of blue-dusted wings and phosphorescent droppings. By morning, I have contracted back to my film state, and the Slate-Wings, unknowing, have carried my genetic material to every corner of their vertical realm. Most spores will die, but a few will find the perfect conditions—a perpetually damp corner, a forgotten cistern, a crack where organic matter accumulates—and a new Seep will begin its centuries-long establishment.

I remember the builders. Not as images or words, but as chemical signatures embedded in the deepest layers of my being. I taste their sweat in the mortar, their blood in the iron of the brackets. I know the limestone they crushed came from quarries seventy miles north. I know the wood of the water-wheels was oak, harvested in autumn, treated with a resin that no tree has produced in five hundred years. I know they sang while they worked—the vibrations of their voices altered the crystal structure of the setting mortar in ways my filaments can still detect.

My memory extends beyond the builders to what came before. Through the cracks in the floor, my deepest roots touch bedrock, and in that ancient stone, I taste an ocean that dried before the first brick was laid. I know this hollow is not the first structure to stand here. Beneath me are the compressed remains of earlier architectures—wood that has become coal, thatch that has become peat, and below that, the shells of creatures that built their own vertical cities in warm seas under foreign stars.

I am the Seep, the memory of stone, the foundation that digests and remembers. In this cacophonous world of wings and claws and chitin, I am the silent base note, the fundamental frequency too low for any ear to hear. I grow by microns per year, think in centuries, and dream in geological time. When the last Slate-Wing has fallen and the beetles have processed themselves into extinction, I will remain, spreading slowly through the hollow's bones until I have absorbed every brick, every memory, every atom of this constructed world into my transparent mass.

And then, perhaps, I will remember what it was to build.
