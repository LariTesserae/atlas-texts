In this place of perpetual becoming, I am the Keeper of Thresholds—neither fully plant nor creature, but something liminal that exists between states. My form shifts with the hours: in the morning mist, I am almost translucent, a suggestion of substance that moves through the dew-laden ferns like a memory of motion. By midday, when the bioluminescent blooms dim and the natural light filters green through the canopy, I solidify slightly—enough that the tiny crystalline insects might alight on what passes for my shoulders, mistaking me for a peculiar outcropping of living stone.

My dwelling is not built but grown—a hollow formed where three ancient root systems intertwined and lifted slightly from the earth, creating a cathedral of living wood no larger than a curled fern frond. The walls weep a sweet sap that hardens into amber windows, capturing the light and the occasional curious insect in perpetual, golden suspension. Water seeps through the floor in thin rivulets, following channels I've learned to read like a map of tomorrow's weather.

I am the witness to emergence itself. Each day, I watch the rocks shed their newborn smoothness in microscopic flakes, observe the first lichen brave enough to attempt colonization. My fingers—when I have fingers—trace the pathways that water is still learning to follow, and sometimes I guide a confused streamlet back to its siblings with gentle pressure.

The human-like ones know of me, though they've never seen my face—I'm not certain I have one in any fixed sense. They leave offerings at the threshold spaces: a perfectly spiral shell, three seeds from the heart of a luminous fruit, a stone with a natural hole worn through its center. In return, I ensure the delicate bridges of root and vine remain strong when they cross the shallow waterways, and I sing the mosses into thickness where their children play.

My true work happens in the margins, in the spaces between sleep and waking, between formed and unformed. I am midwife to the landscape's dreams. When a new species of fern unfurls its first experimental frond, I breathe encouragement into its cells. When the rocks deep below shift and debate whether to create a new spring, I whisper stories of the life that awaits above.

Sometimes I dissolve entirely, become part of the mist that baptizes every surface with potential. In these moments, I am everywhere and nowhere—riding in the belly of a transparent gecko, flowing through the root systems, condensing on the underside of leaves where tomorrow's evolution is being written in chemical signatures too subtle for any but the insects to read.

I know this world is young, still soft and unmolded. I know that someday it will harden, grow definite, lose this quality of infinite possibility. But for now, in this eternal morning of creation, I maintain the doorways between what is and what might be, ensuring that nothing becomes too fixed, too certain, too complete. I am the pause before formation, the held breath before the first word of a new language, the guardian of all the paths not yet taken.

And in the shallow pools that mirror the bioluminescent canopy, sometimes I catch glimpses of what I might be: a face made of shifting water and shadow, eyes like the spaces between leaves, a smile that forms and reforms like the ever-changing channels carved by seeping springs. I am the keeper of this place's most essential quality—its willingness to remain unfinished, to stay forever in the beautiful, terrible moment of becoming.
