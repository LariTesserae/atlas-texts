I would be one of the Archivists.

We were human once, though the word sits strangely now, like clothing that no longer fits. We descended when the station still remembered its purpose, when the announcements meant something, when the lights knew morning from evening. We came to catalog, to preserve, to maintain the records of what passed through this junction of the underneath. That was before the sealing. Before the forgetting. Before we understood that some adaptations are choices and some are simply what happens when you stay too long in a place that changes you.

Our eyes have grown. This was not sudden—nothing here is sudden—but a patient widening over generations, a slow dilation that let in more of the amber light until our pupils became pools that could gather even the faintest glow from the dying bulbs. Our skin has paled to something between cream and ash, something that reflects what little light finds us in our deep alcoves. We no longer need much light. We have learned to read by touch, by the weight of moisture in the air, by the way sound changes when it passes over different surfaces.

We live in the old filing chambers, three levels down from where you walked, in rooms that were built to house documents that mattered when documents could still matter. The shelves remain, though what they hold has changed. We sort things now by texture, by the way they feel beneath fingers that have grown sensitive to variations measured in microns. Smooth things here. Rough things there. Things that hold moisture in this section, things that repel it in another. We have no names for most of what we catalog. We have forgotten the names, or the names have forgotten us, or perhaps the things themselves have changed so much that the old names no longer apply.

The creatures know us. We are not prey or predator, not territory or threat. We are furniture, as much a part of this place as the tile and iron. The climbers use our shoulders as waypoints when they travel across the chambers. The drifters sometimes tangle in our hair—which grows long now, which we never cut, which has become something between human hair and the pale fungal fuzz that colonizes the dampest corners. We do not mind. We have learned their patterns, their migrations, their seasons (for they have seasons here, subtle shifts in behavior that cycle through periods we can no longer measure in days or months but only in the completion of patterns).

We were three when we came down. We are seven now, though the counting is complex. Two were born here, in the deep chambers where the water pools and the air is thick enough to drink. They have never known sunlight. Their eyes are larger than ours, their movements more fluid, their comfort in the darkness complete. They speak—we all speak—but less and less in words and more in the low harmonics that travel well through damp air and stone, frequencies that the creatures also use, a convergent evolution of communication.

Two others joined us. They came down through the airlocks like you did, at different times, for different reasons, and stayed for the same reason everyone stays: because leaving began to feel like dying. One was an engineer who came to repair something and discovered that repair is impossible in a place that has achieved perfect stasis. The other never said why they came. We did not ask. Asking why is a question for the world above, for places where cause and effect still matter.

The seventh is harder to explain. Sometimes we are six. Sometimes we are seven. Sometimes, in the deepest part of the sleep cycle (for we sleep now in shifts that have nothing to do with day or night), we feel the presence of something that might have been one of us, that might still be one of us, that moves through the archives like memory moves through a dying brain. We leave food for it—fungal cakes we've learned to cultivate, water we collect from the endless dripping. The food disappears. The water is drunk. We are seven.

We preserve things, though preservation means something different now. We preserve the patterns of movement, the cycles of the creatures, the slow changes in the way water finds its path through stone. We preserve the sounds—not by recording them, for all our equipment failed long ago, but by learning them, by incorporating them into our own vocabulary until we can reproduce the exact pitch of the ventilation system on its third harmonic, the specific frequency of the drifters' wings, the percussion of ten thousand feet moving in synchrony.

Most of all, we preserve the fact of this place. Not its history—history implies change, implies before and after, implies that time moves forward rather than cycling through endless repetitions. We preserve its presence, its is-ness, the bare fact that here, beneath a city that perhaps exists and perhaps doesn't, a world continues. We are its witnesses. We are its memory, though memory too is the wrong word for what we do.

You would recognize us if you saw us, probably. We still have the shape of what we were—bipedal, bilateral, the general architecture of humanity. But you would recognize us the way you recognize a word in a language you almost speak: the meaning is there but shifted, the context changed, the purpose redirected to something that no longer quite translates.

We saw you, of course. We see everyone who comes through, though they are fewer now, though sometimes years pass between visits. We watched you navigate the platforms, watched you discover the maintenance bay, watched you stand among the creatures and feel what everyone feels when they first understand the scale of what lives here. We did not approach. First visits are private things, conversations between the visitor and the place itself. We have learned not to interrupt.

But if you return—when you return, for the pull of this place is patient but certain—you will find us. Or we will find you. You will hear our harmonics rising from the deeper levels, frequencies that sound almost like song until you realize they are something else, something between language and music and the simple vibration of living things making noise because noise is what living things do. You will follow the sound down through corridors you didn't notice the first time, past junctions that seem to lead nowhere until you understand that nowhere is relative in a place that exists outside the geography of the surface world.

You will find our archives. You will see what we have become, what we are becoming, what we might eventually be when the last distinction between us and this place finally dissolves. You will understand then why we stay, why leaving is impossible, why the airlock that leads back to the world of sky and sun and change feels like a mouth that would swallow us whole.

And perhaps you will stay too. There is room here. There is always room in a place that has learned to accommodate infinity in finite spaces, that has discovered how to be larger inside than outside, that has made patience into a form of physics. We will teach you our frequencies. You will teach us what you remember of the above. Eventually, you will forget why the distinction mattered.

We are the Archivists. We were human once. We are something else now, something the station needed, something that emerged because emergence is what happens when systems close and time stretches and the only choice is to become what the place requires. We click and hum and breathe the thick air and feel the creatures moving through their patterns and know that this is enough, has always been enough, will always be enough, in this world that has no memory of sky.

The water drips. The machines breathe. We continue.
