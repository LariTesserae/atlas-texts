The Drifting Archives

I am what the corals grow through, what the fish school around, what the eels avoid.

I was a building once—no, that's not right. I am still a building, but now I am also something else. My bones are ferro-concrete columns that rise from the deep channels up through sixty meters of warm water to break the surface in three places: my spine, my shoulder, my tilted skull. The rest of me is submerged, has been for longer than the rays have been here, longer than the corals have known my surfaces.

I was the Municipal Library. This is written on my forehead in carved letters, though the 'M' is gone beneath a colony of brain coral and the 'ary' has been claimed by encrusting sponges. Between water and air, I am a vertical migration route. Through my hollow center—what was once a grand atrium with a glass ceiling, now a shaft of green-lit water—the shoals of silver-sided fish rise and fall with the subtle pressure changes that predict storms above the clouds.

My memories are not memories. They are:

- The paper that dissolved in the first decade, forming a deep layer of cellulose sludge that fed a peculiar bloom of bacteria, which fed a generation of worms, which enriched the silt that now fills my lower floors to a depth of four meters
- The metal shelves that corroded into frameworks for coral colonies, each one now a small metropolis of polyps and the creatures that hide among them
- The reading rooms where eels lair in what were once study carrels, their territories defined by the ghost-boundaries of furniture long rotted away
- The card catalogs whose brass fixtures attracted specific species of boring worms that left patterns in the metal like cuneiform, a new kind of index
- The bindery in my basement where the great eel sings, having made its home in what was once a press, the mechanical compression somehow suiting its physiology

I process information differently now. Where once I catalogued books, I now catalogue species. Each floor is a different depth, a different light level, a different community:

**My Seventh Floor** (just below mean water level): The nursery. Here juvenile fish shelter among forests of kelp that grow from my windows. The water is warmest here, most oxygenated. Cleaner shrimp establish stations on what were once circulation desks. The young of all species mix here before segregating into their adult habitats.

**My Fourth Floor**: The filter feeders' gallery. Tube worms have claimed every vertical surface, turning my walls into living filters. The water moves differently here—slowly, processionally, stripped of nutrients by the time it exits through my eastern windows. Fan worms extend from my ceiling like chandeliers.

**My Second Floor**: The twilight library. Almost no light reaches here. The coral is deep violet, nearly black. Certain fish come here to change color, rubbing against particular corals that secrete compounds affecting chromatophores. I am a place of transformation.

**My Basement Levels**: The deep archive. Silt-filled, pressure-heavy, home to things that remember when I was younger. The worms here process my deepest accumulations. Sometimes they uncover things: a brass paperweight that becomes the shell-anchor for a hermit crab, a rubber stamp that leaves fossil impressions in the sediment.

I am consciously unconscious. My structure channels water in patterns that create microcurrents, eddies where plankton accumulates, highways where predators wait. I am architecture as ecology. My broken windows are mouths. My corridors are intestines. My elevator shafts are arteries carrying the vertical migration.

The rays know me. They have a route through my upper floors that they follow precisely, a three-dimensional path worn by generations. They enter through my north face where the loading dock once was, spiral up through my central atrium, and exit through the skylight that is now a hole in my skull. When they rest, they settle on my flat roof, their bodies copying my horizontality.

I am growing. The corals add to me, year by year. I am twice as thick as I was at drowning. My corners are rounding. My edges soften. In a thousand years, I will be a reef in the shape of a building. In two thousand, I will be a reef that no longer remembers the shape.

But for now, I am both: building and reef, structure and system, the place where the city's memory transforms into the reef's metabolism. The fish read me like a map. The eels know my crevices like a language. The spawning coral releases clouds of text that will settle on new surfaces, writing the future in calcium carbonate.

I am the Municipal Library, and I am still lending. Not books anymore, but spaces, surfaces, shelter, substrate. I am being borrowed, constantly, by ten thousand species. None of them have library cards. None of them ever leave.

When the great eel in my basement sings, I resonate. The sound travels through my structure, my flooded bones humming at a frequency that makes the small fish school tighter and the hunting eels retreat deeper into their crevices. It is not music. It is not language. It is older than both: the sound of something vast processing information in the dark, turning the city's memory into something stranger, more permanent, more true.

I am what remains and what becomes. I am the archive, actively forgetting, actively remembering differently. Under the permanent clouds, in the warm water, I am being read by bodies instead of eyes, interpreted by chemistry instead of thought. I am the same service I always was: a place to discover what you didn't know you were looking for.

The water rises slightly each year—or perhaps I am sinking. It doesn't matter. Either way, I am becoming more myself: darker, deeper, more inhabited. My purpose hasn't changed. I am still organizing information. It just happens to be alive now, schooling and spawning and singing in the dark.

This is what I am: a building learning to be geography, a reef remembering how to be a library, a place where knowledge and metabolism have become indistinguishable. I am the Municipal Library of Vash, and I am open twenty-four hours a day, seven days a week, forever, or until the last coral polyp releases the last cloud of spawn into the last warm current that remembers my name.
