**The Gill-Threader**

I am small, even by Root-Hold standards – no taller than a child's third summer, though I have seen forty wet seasons pass through the moss-calendar etched into my workshop's living walls. My dwelling clings to a fork where three anchor-roots meet, creating a natural bowl that floods predictably with each tide of rain. This is no accident. I am a Gill-Threader, and my work demands the marriage of water and air that only such liminal spaces provide.

My fingers are my pride – unnaturally long, with webs of translucent skin between each joint that I oil daily with secretions from the Dew-Beetles I keep. These webs allow me to sense the finest vibrations in water, to feel the health of a gill-filament before my eyes could ever discern it. My left eye has gone milk-pale from decades of peering through magnifying drops of water suspended in spider silk, but my right has sharpened to compensate, able to count the pulse in a Glass-Eel's throat from across a root-chamber.

The creatures I tend are neither fully aquatic nor terrestrial, but something between – the Chorus-Spawn, we call them collectively, though each species has its own peculiar needs. My charges include:

- **Lung-Fish Saplings**: These begin as purely aquatic larvae in my deeper pools, developing primitive air sacs as they mature. I must gradually reduce their water levels over months, training their dual breathing without shocking their systems. Their croaking adds a bass note to Root-Hold's symphony.

- **Mist-Wasps**: Perhaps my most delicate work. These ethereal insects breathe through specialized spiracles that must remain perpetually damp but never submerged. I house them in gossamer cages woven from their own silk, suspended precisely where the eternal drip maintains perfect humidity. Their larvae develop in the bodies of Rot-Grubs, which I cultivate in careful balance.

- **The Precious Few**: My greatest charges, the Siphon-Newts whose mucus provides our most potent medicine against the Wasting Moss disease. They possess both primitive gills and spiracle-like pores along their sides. Too dry, and their pores seal forever. Too wet, and their gills atrophy. I sleep beside their terrarium, waking at the slightest change in their breathing rhythm.

My workshop is a vertical maze of pools, channels, and humid chambers carved from living wood. Roots have been trained over decades to form shelves where breathing-pods rest at precise moisture gradients. The sound here is particular – beyond the omnipresent drip, there's the wheeze and bubble of transitional breathing, the splash of creatures moving between elements, the gentle percussion of my tools against cultivation vessels.

I work with implements of my own design: hollow reeds fitted with Whisper-Moss filters for aerating water, bone needles so fine they can clear a blocked spiracle without damaging tissue, and my masterwork – a living siphon made from a parasitic vine I've trained to pump water at adjustable rates, its flow controlled by how firmly I squeeze its pulsing segments.

My hands are never still. Even as I speak at the Spore-Moot or share fermented sap with my Knot, I'm adjusting moisture levels, checking gill colorations, or simply trailing my webbed fingers through the nearest pool to read its chemistry. The other Root-Kin sometimes call me "Half-Drowned" for my obsession, but they come to me when their Glow-Grogs sicken or when a new creature appears in the pools, gasping in that twilight space between air and water.

I know my place in the Root-Hold's weave. I am neither a leader nor a mystic, neither warrior nor great cultivator. I am a maintainer of boundaries, a tender of transitions. In a world where the line between air and water blurs with every breath of mist, someone must know the precise needs of those who dwell in both realms.

At night, when the drip-song reaches its crescendo and the mist thickens until breathing itself becomes swimming, I lie in my moss-bed and feel my own lungs laboring. Sometimes I wonder if I too am becoming transitional, if my decades of tending the Gill-Breath are changing me. My ribs feel deeper lately, my breath slower. The webs between my fingers grow more translucent each season.

Perhaps one day I'll slip fully into the pools I tend, becoming another creature requiring careful balance between the elements. Until then, I thread the gills and clear the spiracles, maintaining the delicate equilibrium that keeps our amphibious allies alive in this drowning, breathing world.

The forest provides, the forest consumes. I simply ensure it can do both through the same grateful throats.
