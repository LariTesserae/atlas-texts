I find myself as a mass of filamentous roots suspended in the humid air, neither fully tree nor fully earth-bound. My body is a living bridge—a network of aerial roots that began high in a strangler fig's crown and descended over decades toward the mud below, some strands thick as wrists, others fine as fishing line, all of them breathing through their skin.

I am not one organism but a collaboration that has forgotten its boundaries. Where my roots touch bark, I fuse with it, trading sugars for minerals in exchanges so old neither party remembers who initiated the deal. Where I reach the ground—and I reach it in a hundred places—I become indistinguishable from the root-mat that holds this whole sodden world together. My upper reaches, still aerial, drip with their own ecosystem: moss grows on me like fur, bromeliads nest in my loops and whorls, and tiny orchids mistake me for a branch and bloom in the dim light, their flowers no bigger than fingernails.

I taste the basin's chemistry through every surface. The soot that drifts down from the unseen machine-world beyond settles on my highest strands—I metabolize it slowly, incorporating those carbons into my body with the same patience I show fallen leaves and bird droppings. When the petroleum sheens wash through in the rain, forming rainbow skins on the puddles below, I feel them too—alien oils that my fungi partners struggle to break down, leaving me slightly fevered in those portions, my growth there stuttering and strange.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is distributed and seasonal. I think in the movement of water through my vessels, in the sugar-pulse of photosynthesis in what leaves I still maintain high above, in the electric whisper of nutrients climbing from mud to canopy. My thoughts are chemical gradients, hormonal cascades that take weeks to complete a single "decision"—whether to thicken here, branch there, let this section die back to feed new growth elsewhere.

The animals use me as a highway. Ants run their sealed tunnels along my length, their feet barely registering except as the faintest tickle. Snakes pour through my loops like water finding its level. Small frogs shelter in the cups where my roots fork and rainwater collects. At night, bats read me as a landmark in their sonic map, my shape a familiar darkness in the darker air. I am furniture in their world, infrastructure they assume will outlast them—and they're right.

I remember in rings and scars. That smooth section where bark has grown over a wound—that was a branch that fell in a storm forty monsoons ago. This hollow where my roots cage empty air—that was another tree, a host I embraced and slowly consumed, leaving only the ghost-shape of its trunk. These knots and galls are where insects bored into me and I grew around the damage, incorporating their galleries into my architecture. My memory is written in wood, each year adding another layer to the palimpsest.

My relationship with time is nothing like an animal's. I operate on tree-time, where a season is a breath and a decade is a thought. But I also experience the faster time of my inhabitants—the frenetic now-now-now of ants, the daily cycles of the frogs, the generational pulses of the beetles that live and die in my bark. I am a city that experiences both its own geological time and the quick lifetimes of its citizens.

In the wet season, I swell with water until my roots are taut as drumskins. I can feel every droplet moving through me, climbing against gravity by capillary action and raw cellular will. In the dry months—never truly dry here, just less wet—I contract slightly, my aerial portions becoming more rope-like, less alive-looking, though I am never less than alive. I just redirect my efforts downward, mining the water table through my ground-touching reaches.

The industrial hum from beyond the green wall is a constant companion now, a vibration I feel more than hear. It resonates in my wood at a frequency that disturbs my growth—not enough to kill, just enough to make me orient slightly away from it, the way a plant turns from too-harsh light. My newest roots, the ones begun in the last decade, all curve faintly toward the basin's center, away from that mechanical breathing.

I am not immortal, though I am older than any single tree around me. Parts of me die constantly—small roots withering, sections going brittle and breaking away, whole loops sometimes falling in storms to become nurse logs on the forest floor. But the colony persists, the network endures. What I lose in one place, I gain in another. I am less an individual than a process, a slow waterfall of wood and vegetable flesh constantly replacing itself.

My future is written in the seedlings that sprout in my accumulated debris, in the young stranglers that even now are beginning their own slow embraces of nearby trees. Eventually, I will be replaced by my own descendants, my body becoming the substrate for the next iteration of this patient, vertical ecology. I don't fear this. It's what I'm for.

For now, I continue what I have always done: I reach up for light, down for water, and sideways for connection. I transform air and mud into living architecture. I provide the infrastructure for a thousand smaller lives. I grow at the exact speed of patience, no faster, no slower, while the basin breathes around me in its endless, crowded night.
