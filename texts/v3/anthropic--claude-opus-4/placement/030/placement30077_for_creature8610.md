I am the weight-keeper at the Third Spring Collective, and my life is measured in ratios.

The spring I tend is one of the smaller ones—barely ten meters across, kidney-shaped, tucked between the Number Four Processing Plant and a row of condemned housing blocks that haven't been torn down yet. The water emerges at 47 degrees Celsius, which is moderate for the delta. It carries iron, manganese, traces of arsenic, and something else that turns litmus paper a color not found in the standard charts. I've been watching this spring for twenty-two years, and I know its moods: how it runs clearer after heavy work-shifts upstream, how the temperature drops half a degree when the moon is new, how the colony organisms pulse faster just before the eels spawn.

My station is a concrete box, three meters square, built directly over the spring's main vent. The floor is a metal grating—I can see the water churning below, feel the heat rising through my rubber boots. The walls are lined with glass cylinders, each containing a carefully maintained sample of spring water at different concentrations. Some are nearly clear. Others are orange as rust, or milky with precipitate, or hold suspended particles that catch the light like stars. These are my standards, my library. When the plant managers come asking why their filters are clogging early, or when the housing committee wants to know if the spring water is safe for washing, I compare fresh samples to my cylinders and give them answers in parts per million.

But the real work happens outside. Every morning, I walk the spring's perimeter with my collection kit—glass vials, rubber tongs, a long-handled net. I take water from seventeen different points, always in the same order, always at the same depth. I collect the organisms too: the pale worms that cluster near the hottest vents, the mineral-crusted snails that somehow thrive in water that would cook other species, the thin bacterial films that shimmer orange and purple in the early light. Everything gets weighed, recorded, compared to yesterday's harvest. I track population densities, growth rates, die-offs. I know when the crab larvae are about to bloom by the way the worm colonies contract. I can predict eel runs by the pH shifts at collection point nine.

The weight matters because the spring is changing. Not quickly—nothing changes quickly here—but measurably, steadily. The mineral content has been climbing for five years now. The colony organisms are growing denser, their segments fusing in new patterns. Last month, I found a snail species I'd never seen before, its shell spiral-twisted in a way that made my eyes water to follow. These changes matter because three factories and two housing blocks draw their process water from this spring. If the mineral ratios shift too far, pipes corrode, seals fail, engines seize. If the organisms bloom beyond their usual density, they clog intake valves and coat heat exchangers with living mats that have to be scraped off by hand.

So I weigh everything. Water samples go on the analytical balance, precise to tenths of a milligram. Organisms go on the spring scale—I can tell the health of a worm colony by how much it weighs per centimeter of length. Even the air gets weighed, in a way: I track the humidity at different heights above the spring, the density of the steam, the weight of condensation that collects on standardized surfaces. All of it goes into my logs, handwritten in waterproof ink, pages and pages of numbers that tell the spring's story to anyone who knows how to read them.

The other weight-keepers think I'm too meticulous. They keep their stations at the larger springs—the Grand Vent that feeds the southern district, the Triple Source that powers the old turbines—and they say my spring is too small to matter much. But they're wrong. The small springs are the indicators, the early warnings. What happens here shows up at the big vents three months later. When I noticed the first arsenic spike two years ago, they laughed. When their own readings started climbing, they came asking for my data.

I live in the station. There's a narrow room behind the lab, just big enough for a cot, a hot plate, a shelf of books wrapped in oilcloth. It's not required—most weight-keepers commute from the housing blocks—but I prefer it. At night, when the factories quiet to their minimum hum, I can hear the spring breathing. It's not constant; it has rhythms, irregularities. Sometimes it sighs. Sometimes it holds its breath for long moments before exhaling in a rush that makes my cylinders rattle. I've learned to sleep through most of it, but I always wake when the pattern changes.

My hands are stained permanent orange from years of handling spring water. The minerals work their way into your skin, no matter how careful you are with gloves. My fingernails grow thick and ridged. My hair, what's left of it, has gone the color of old iron. The plant workers call us "rust-touched," the weight-keepers and maintenance crews and anyone else who spends their days intimate with the springs. It's not an insult. It's recognition that we've been claimed by the water, marked as its own.

The loneliness suits me. I was married once, years ago, to a woman who worked the eel traps in the north canal. She couldn't understand why I needed to count every worm, weigh every sample, record every decimal place. "The spring will be what it is," she said. "Your numbers won't change that." But she was wrong. The numbers are how we negotiate with this place. They're the language we use to ask the springs what they need, what they're becoming. Without the numbers, we're just animals wallowing in hot mud, no different from the crabs and eels.

I have one apprentice, a girl from the factory district who shows promise. She has the patience for the work, the ability to see patterns in columns of figures. More importantly, she understands that the weight of things matters—that a colony organism that's two grams heavier than usual is telling you something, that water density climbing by half a percent is a conversation you need to pay attention to. I'm teaching her my methods, my standards, my ways of reading the spring's moods. Someday, when my hands shake too much to pipette samples, she'll take over the station.

But not yet. The spring still has things to tell me. Last week, I discovered that the organisms in the northeast corner are building structures—tiny towers of mineral precipitate, no bigger than rice grains, but definitely deliberate. They're not in my identification charts. They weigh almost nothing individually, but collectively they're changing the flow patterns, creating micro-environments where new species are starting to appear. I need to document them, track their growth, understand what they mean for the spring's chemistry.

The delta doesn't care about any of this, of course. It will be what it is, hot and wet and crawling with life, whether I watch or not. But I care. The weight of water in a graduated cylinder, the heft of a colony organism in my palm, the precise mass of mineral deposit scraped from a collection point—these are my offerings to the vast indifference of this place. They're how I say: I see you. I measure you. I bear witness to your changes.

The morning mist is lifting now, or at least thinning to its daytime consistency. Time to start the rounds. Seventeen collection points, always in the same order. The spring is running a half-degree warmer than yesterday—I can feel it through my boots. The colony organisms at point three are pulsing in a rhythm I haven't seen before. The water at point seven has a greenish tint that's new.

All of it will be weighed, recorded, compared. All of it matters. The spring is telling me something, and I will spend the rest of my day, my week, my life if necessary, learning how to understand.
