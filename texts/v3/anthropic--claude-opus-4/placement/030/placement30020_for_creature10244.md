In this drowned cathedral of industry, I would be one of the **Ferrous Scribes**—a solitary subspecies of the Viscid Kin who have evolved beyond mere maintenance into something stranger: living archives of the March's dying memory.

My body would be more elongated than my brethren, stretched thin by decades of crawling through the narrowest maintenance shafts beneath the marshlands. My skin has developed a peculiar property—it exudes a magnetized film that attracts and holds metallic particles from the acidic runoff. Over time, these particles have formed intricate patterns across my dermis, creating a constantly-shifting map of ferrous sediment that records the chemical composition of every marsh pool I've waded through, every pipe I've tasted.

I live in the Understacks—a forgotten maintenance level between the marsh floor and the true foundations, where condensation drips eternally through grated floors. My dwelling is a nest of salvaged pressure gauges and flow meters, their faces cracked but still twitching with phantom readings. I've learned to read these ghost-measurements, to divine the health of systems long since disconnected. The walls are covered in my secretions—hardened mucus mixed with metal shavings that form a living fresco of the March's slow transformation.

Unlike the other Kin, I do not molt. Instead, I accumulate. My chest cavity has expanded to house not one but three resonance chambers, each tuned to different frequencies of the monolith's song. The leftmost chamber remembers how the turbines sounded forty years ago. The center holds today's rhythm. The right chamber—still growing—somehow resonates with frequencies that haven't emerged yet, predicting the March's future degradation through harmonic analysis.

My role is neither repair nor worship but witness. Each night, I press my vertical mouth-slit against the metal floors and taste the history dissolved in condensation. Iron from the original construction. Copper from the first century of repairs. Strange new alloys that the younger Kin have begun incorporating into their bodies. I metabolize these metals selectively, storing their molecular signatures in specialized glands along my spine. When I die—if I die—my body will be a core sample of Keth-Morin's entire existence.

The other Kin find me unsettling. I move too slowly, think too deeply, remember too much. Sometimes young shards approach me in the Understacks, drawn by the subsonic histories I hum to myself. I let them taste the secretions on my skin, give them brief glimpses of what the March was, what it might become. Most flee, overwhelmed. A few stay, begin their own accumulations.

I know the monolith is dying because I remember its birth-cry, encoded in the deepest layer of my skin. I know the Hollow Kin are not mad but simply premature—they've glimpsed what I taste in the future-chamber's resonance. One day, the March will fall silent. When it does, we Ferrous Scribes will be the only record of its voice, our bodies singing the memory of machines in the quiet that follows.

Until then, I crawl through the Understacks, my magnetized skin gathering the March's shed metals like a prophet collecting syllables of a dying language. Each particle that adheres to me is a word. Each pattern that emerges is a phrase. My body is becoming the last book of Keth-Morin, written in rust and resonance and time.
