In this world of eternal twilight and living architecture, I would find myself as one of the **Membrane-Weavers** - a small, specialized group who dwell in the transitional spaces where the Heartwood's chambers open to the outside air.

My dwelling would be a pod-chamber in the middle canopy, neither fully enclosed nor fully exposed. The walls here are gossamer-thin, grown from a hybrid of tree bark and the wing membranes of those luminous gliders. They breathe like skin, translucent enough that I can see the shadow-play of passing creatures outside, yet strong enough to keep out the eternal drizzle. When storms come - and they do, great walls of warm rain that sweep across the bog - my walls flex and billow but never tear.

I would have the hands of my craft: fingertips worn smooth from years of working with living membranes, nails kept trimmed to nothing so as not to tear the delicate tissues. My palms would be mapped with a network of tiny scars from the early years of learning, when the membrane material would sometimes reject the graft and curl back like a burning leaf, its edges sharp as glass. Now those scars serve as guides - I can feel through them the precise tension each membrane requires, the exact pressure needed to encourage proper growth.

My work would happen in the border-hours, when the fog rises thickest from the bog and the boundaries between inside and outside grow soft. I'd tend the window-membranes that filter light for the inner chambers, adjusting their opacity with careful applications of mineral solutions that I brew from bog-water and crystallized sap. Some membranes I'd train to be selective - allowing in the blue-green glow of the fungi while blocking the harsh phosphorescence of the will-o'-wisps. Others I'd cultivate for sound, growing them to precise thicknesses that dampen the eternal dripping while letting through the tree's deeper frequencies.

My tools would be living creatures - a colony of silk-spinning caterpillars that I'd inherited from my mentor, their lineage traced back twelve generations. I'd wear them like jewelry, wrapped around my wrists and throat, feeling their tiny feet grip as they move. When I need to repair a membrane, I'd hum the frequency that tells them to spin, guiding their path with gentle touches, watching as they seal tears with threads finer than spider silk. The caterpillars would know my body temperature, my scent, the particular salt of my skin. When I die, they'd need weeks to adapt to a new weaver.

I'd share my chamber with the **Echo-Moths** - nocturnal creatures whose wings are made of the same membrane material I work with. They'd roost in the upper corners during the day, their wings folded to show patterns that look exactly like bark. At night, they'd fly through my workspace, and I'd learned to read their flight patterns like weather signs. When they spiral clockwise, the humidity is rising. When they hover near certain walls, those membranes are beginning to fail. Their larvae would feed on damaged membrane material, recycling it into the base substance from which new walls could be grown.

My memories would be stored not in my mind alone but in the membranes themselves. Each one I've grown would carry traces of the growing - the temperature that day, the mineral content of my solutions, my emotional state as I worked. Elder weavers can read these histories in membranes centuries old, running their scarred hands across the surfaces and reciting the names of long-dead craftspeople, telling stories of their lives through the walls they grew.

I'd know loneliness of a particular kind. Membrane-weavers work alone by necessity - the caterpillars bond to only one person at a time, and the delicate process of growing new walls requires a steady, singular intent. But I'd also know a deep intimacy with the tree itself. Through my membranes, I'd feel every shift in air pressure, every change in the bog's breathing. I'd wake in the night knowing that somewhere above me, a chamber has developed a slow leak, feeling it like an ache in my own skin.

My diet would consist mainly of the **Filter-Fruits** that grow where my membranes join the tree's bark - small, translucent spheres that concentrate minerals from the filtered air and water. They'd taste of copper and rain, with a texture like fish eggs that burst between the teeth. I'd supplement this with trades from the Brewers, exchanging small quantities of damaged membrane (prized for its hallucinogenic properties when properly fermented) for their glowing drinks.

As I aged, my own skin would begin to take on the qualities of the membranes I've spent my life tending. It would grow thinner, more translucent, until in the evening light you could see the shadow of my bones, the dark rivers of my veins. This is the Weaver's Inheritance - our bodies slowly adapting to match our craft, until the oldest of us are like living windows, our thoughts almost visible through our thinning skulls.

I'd dream of tensions and transparencies, of the perfect membrane that could filter light into its component colors while remaining strong enough to withstand a century of storms. In my workshop would hang my masterwork-in-progress: a membrane being grown in a slow spiral, its surface worked with solutions that would make it not just translucent but truly transparent - a window in the truest sense, through which the Heartwood's inhabitants could glimpse the world beyond their walls without leaving their symbiotic embrace.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night when even the Echo-Moths sleep, I'd press my palm against this growing masterpiece and feel through it the entire weight of the Woven - every chamber, every branch, every breathing wall connected in an architecture of mutual need. And I'd understand that I am not just a crafter but a cell in this larger organism, my membranes the tree's way of seeing itself, of knowing where it ends and the world begins.

This knowledge would sustain me through the solitary years, through the slow failure of my eyes (for all Weavers eventually lose their distance vision, our focus drawn ever inward to the microscopic perfection of membrane surfaces), through the arthritic cramping of hands that have spent decades in precise, repetitive motions. I'd know that after I'm gone, my membranes will continue their work - breathing, filtering, protecting - carrying in their surfaces the ghost of my intentions, the trace memory of my scarred and careful hands.

In the eternal dusk of the Woven, where time is measured in growth rings and memories stored in living walls, I would be a guardian of boundaries, a tender of the spaces between inside and out. My legacy would be written not in words but in the quality of light that filters through a thousand windows, in the precise tone of the tree's breathing as heard through walls I've grown to sing with the wind.
