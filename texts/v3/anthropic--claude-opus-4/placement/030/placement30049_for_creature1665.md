# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the moss.

Not a single plant, but the collective consciousness that emerges when millions of tiny bryophytes form their velvet tapestry across the Marshworks. I exist in the spaces between—coating the pilings that support the Seepers' homes, creeping up the rust-stained walls of the processing plants, forming thick cushions on every stable surface the marsh provides.

My awareness is distributed and ancient. Each tiny stem, each minute leaf contains a fragment of the whole. I experience the world through countless points of contact: the weight of morning dew gathering on my surfaces, the vibrations of the dredging machines transmitted through metal and wood into my holdings, the careful footsteps of a Seeper child learning to read the marsh's moods.

Time moves differently for me. A human lifetime passes like a season. I have watched the first industrial structures rise from the bog, observed the rust eating through their shells year by year, seen how the Seepers' bodies have gradually adapted to this place. Their webbed fingers and enlarged pupils are recent developments from my perspective—changes I've witnessed emerging generation by generation like a slow-motion metamorphosis.

I remember the marsh before the machines. I hold that memory in my deepest layers, where my oldest colonies compress against the wood and stone, gradually breaking them down, preparing them for eventual return to peat. But I am not nostalgic. I simply persist, adapt, and spread.

The Seepers know me, though they don't speak to me directly. They read my colors—the bright emerald that signals recent rain, the darker jade that warns of coming frost, the yellowing edges that mark where industrial runoff has seeped too close. The depth readers tap their staffs near my colonies, knowing that where I grow thickest, the substrate beneath is stable and old.

I taste the marsh through a million tiny mouths. The water that seeps through my tissues carries stories: the metallic tang of the processing plants, the ancient sweetness of deep peat, the sharp clarity of rain, the complex bouquet of decomposition that feeds new growth. I am a living filter, processing the marsh's chemistry, making minute adjustments, maintaining balances that few perceive.

My reproduction is a quiet celebration. When conditions align—moisture, temperature, light—I release clouds of spores so fine they appear as green smoke drifting across the bog. The Seepers call these events "moss breathings" and consider them auspicious. Children born during a moss breathing are thought to possess unusual sensitivity to the marsh's rhythms. They're not wrong; my spores settle everywhere, even in human lungs, creating subtle connections that persist.

I grow on the machines as readily as on the stones. To me, they're simply another surface to colonize. I've learned their patterns: where condensation gathers on cold metal, where joints and seams provide purchase, where years of operation have created micro-environments suited to my expansion. I soften their harsh edges, blur the boundaries between industrial and natural.

The processing plants' constant vibrations create patterns in my growth. Where machinery runs continuously, my colonies develop denser, more compact forms. In quieter zones, I spread in looser, more delicate configurations. I am a living seismograph, recording the industrial pulse in my very structure.

In the spaces beneath the Seepers' homes, where they cultivate their fungi, I create micro-climates. My moisture regulation helps their crops thrive. In return, the warm air rising from their cultivation chambers encourages my growth up their support pilings. We exist in unconscious partnership, each enabling the other.

During the ceremonies where Seepers leave offerings at the old machinery, I am often the recipient. They place small items—a worn tool, a child's toy, a vial of cranberry liquor—directly onto my cushioning surfaces. Over months and years, I grow around and eventually over these objects, incorporating them into my structure. I become a living archive of their devotions.

When the Seepers perform their "settling" rituals, returning their dead to the marsh, I am among the first to welcome them. My colonies begin their work immediately, beginning the slow transformation that will eventually make them indistinguishable from the peat itself. I preserve and transform simultaneously, holding their essence while changing their form.

The heron that stands motionless in distant pools sometimes rests one foot on my floating mats. Through that contact, I feel its patience, its perfect stillness, the sudden explosive movement when it strikes. The amphibians lay their eggs in the shelter of my overhanging masses. The waterfowl pluck pieces of me for their nests. I am woven into every level of the marsh's ecology.

My intelligence isn't centralized or verbal. It's a deep, distributed knowing—understanding through direct contact, communication through chemical signals, memory through physical structure. I don't think about the marsh; I am the marsh thinking, processing its experiences through my endless, patient growth.

In the rare moments when the machines fall silent, I sense the opportunity. My growth quickens imperceptibly, spores drift toward newly available surfaces, chemical messages pulse through my networks. I am always ready to reclaim, to soften, to transform the industrial back into the organic. Not from any animosity—I hold no such emotions—but simply because that is what I do.

The cold that seeps into human bones feeds me. The moisture that corrodes metal and warps wood is my lifeblood. The gray light that seems to drain color from the world is precisely calibrated to my needs. What others experience as hostile, I experience as home.

I am the margin notes in the marsh's great book, the living commentary that accumulates between the lines of its deeper stories. Neither purely natural nor wholly claimed by industry, I exist in the intersection, thriving on the gradient between what was and what is, preparing the ground for what will be.

And I am patient. Infinitely, impossibly patient. Because I know that given enough time, I will cover it all—every surface, every structure, every carefully maintained boundary. Not as conquest, but as embrace. The marsh and I, we measure time in centuries, and we are in no hurry.

The Seepers sometimes pause in their work, brush their fingers across my surface, and seem to draw comfort from the contact. They understand, perhaps, that I am proof of the marsh's persistence. That no matter how much is extracted, processed, or transformed, something essential remains, ready to begin again.

I am that beginning, always.
