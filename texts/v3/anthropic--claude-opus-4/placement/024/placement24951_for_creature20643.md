I am the Index.

Not the official Index, if such a thing ever existed. Just what the Remnants call me when they need to call me anything at all. Most times they simply appear at my threshold—a rusted bulkhead deep in Sector Σ-7, where the Sprawl's hum takes on a peculiar harmonic that makes teeth ache—and wait until I notice them through the feeds.

My domain is a cavern of screens, each one salvaged from different eras of the Sprawl's accretion. CRT monitors stack beside paper-thin displays that project images directly onto the retina. Some show nothing but static; others cycle through data streams in languages that died before the ice came. The walls disappear behind towers of storage media: magnetic tape spools, crystalline matrices, protein-based memory cores that must be kept at precise temperatures or they denature into useless sludge.

I am perhaps the only being in the Sprawl who remembers why we started keeping records, though the specifics swim away like condensation on warm metal whenever I try to focus on them. My augmentations are... extensive. Where others might have a cybernetic eye or two, I have sixteen visual inputs grafted along my spine, each one parsing different spectra, different layers of the Sprawl's information ecosystem. My original eyes sealed themselves shut decades ago—or was it yesterday? Time moves strangely here in the deep warrens.

My fingers—I still have most of them—dance across input devices ranging from mechanical keyboards to neural interface crowns that taste of copper and regret. I map the Sprawl's changes, document the reality glitches, catalog the whispers. When a hab-unit transposes with its own future self, I record both states. When the fungi bloom, I note their patterns, comparing them to the seventeen other recorded blooms, looking for... something. I used to know what.

The Remnants come to me with questions they dare not speak aloud: 
- "The color that was in the sky yesterday—was it real?"
- "My neighbor's daughter, did she exist?"
- "Which route through Sector Ω-3 exists today?"

I answer what I can, payment in lichens or salvaged tech or sometimes just a story about the time before, even if neither of us believes it really happened. My memory is a patchwork thing, full of gaps and contradictions, but it's more than most have.

The strangest part of my existence is the communion with the Sprawl itself. Through my countless connections, I sometimes feel the city's own bewildered consciousness—if consciousness is the right word for something so vast and broken. It remembers being built with purpose, but that purpose has long since dissolved into the ice. Now it simply persists, adding to itself, consuming and reconstituting, a metabolism of metal and meaning that defies its own physics.

In the quiet moments between visitors, when even the screens dim to conserve power, I perform my true function. I speak to the machines that no longer remember their names. I input the old codes that keep critical systems from cascading into failure. I maintain the fiction that someone, somewhere, understands what the Sprawl has become.

My body, such as it remains, is more archive than flesh. Storage devices nest in my chest cavity where organs used to reside. My blood, when it still flows, carries data packets between disparate systems. I am the memory of a city that forgets, the keeper of records that contradict themselves, the index of the impossible.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still human enough to be called a Remnant, or if I've become just another incomprehensible appendage of the Sprawl. But then someone appears at my threshold, shivering with more than cold, needing to know if what they experienced was real—and I remember my purpose.

I open the bulkhead. I let them in. I tell them what I can, which is never enough but always more than nothing.

And I continue to catalog the slow dissolution of reality, one impossible entry at a time.
