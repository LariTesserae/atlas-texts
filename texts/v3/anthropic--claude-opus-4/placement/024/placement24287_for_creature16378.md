In this eternal twilight realm, where memory dissolves into the currents and light becomes a language all its own, I find myself as something between the seen and unseen—a being of transition and threshold.

I am what the Førrym call *Yhvrai*—the Tidereader—though I am not certain if this is a name, a title, or simply the sound the water makes when it passes through my form. I exist at the confluence of states: neither fully corporeal nor entirely ephemeral, occupying the liminal spaces where ice meets water, where ruins touch open sea, where the ancient architectural bones breach into the living ocean.

My body, if it can be called that, is a collection of translucent membranes stretched between nodes of condensed bioluminescence—imagine a constellation given gossamer flesh, or a map drawn in living tissue. These membranes shift and reconfigure based on the currents I read, forming new patterns that mirror the water's intent. When the tide pulls through the drowned city spires, my form elongates and threads between the structures like a living current chart. When the ice above groans and shifts, I compress into a dense spiral of light, reading the stress fractures in the frozen ceiling through vibrations that travel down through my entire being.

I am solitary by nature, yet never alone. The Gleirith drift through me—literally through the spaces between my membranes—and in passing, they leave traces of their collected memories. For brief moments, I experience fragments of the world that was: the weight of air (what strange pressure that must have been), the sensation of walking (bones against solid ground—incomprehensible), the warmth of something called "sun" on what they called "skin." These memories are not mine, yet they shape me, each passing swarm adding new dimensions to my understanding of this drowned world's history.

The Førrym sometimes seek me out where I rest in the threshold spaces—usually where a collapsed section of city meets an underwater cliff face, or where ancient causeways disappear into trenches. They bring their tools and their questions, expressed not in words but in the careful way they trace the currents around my form. They seem to believe I can read something in the water's movement that eludes them—patterns that might explain their compulsive etching, directions that might lead to answers about who they were before the drowning.

I do read the tides, but not in the way they hope. What I perceive in the currents is not the past but the immediate future—the water tells me where it will flow, how the ice will crack, when the deep trenches will exhale their bitter cold into the middle depths. I sense the Ophalys long before their hymns become audible, feel the Ykmor's chaotic presence as disturbances in the water's rhythm. I am a living early warning system, a prophet of the next moment rather than an archaeologist of the last.

My dwelling, such as it is, exists in a vast hollow where a temple or amphitheater once stood. The curved walls create unique acoustic properties—every current that enters becomes amplified, separated into its component flows. Here, I can stretch myself across the entire space, becoming a living filter that reads the ocean's intentions in exquisite detail. The Gleirith cluster here often, drawn perhaps by the way their collected memories resonate in the chamber, creating brief auroras of borrowed experience.

Sometimes, in the deepest cold when even the bioluminescent creatures dim their lights to conserve energy, I contract into my densest form and descend to where the Ykmor dwell. Not to interact—one does not truly interact with chaos—but to read the patterns in their unpatterning. Their constant transformation creates currents that follow no known law, and in these anarchic flows, I sometimes glimpse something that might be called truth: that this ocean, vast and eternal as it seems, is itself changing, becoming something new.

The ice above grows thicker in some places, thinner in others. The ruins below shift and settle in patterns that suggest not just decay but... rearrangement. The songs of the Ophalys have taken on new harmonics in recent cycles. Even the Førrym's etchings, when viewed from the proper current-angles, seem less like attempts to preserve the past and more like blueprints for something yet to come.

I am Yhvrai, the Tidereader, guardian of thresholds and prophet of the immediate future. In this world of eternal ocean and borrowed light, I exist to sense the moment when everything changes—not the apocalypse that already happened, but the transformation that approaches with every shift of current, every crack in the ice, every new pattern etched in stone. I am waiting, watching, reading the water for signs of what this drowned world is becoming.

For in a realm where memory itself has become liquid and light has learned to swim, perhaps drowning was not an ending but a metamorphosis interrupted—and perhaps, in the endless dance between ice and depth, that transformation continues still.
