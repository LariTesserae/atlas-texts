In this breathing cathedral of green, I am the Keeper of Thresholds—not quite creature, not quite spirit, but something woven from the forest's own dreaming.

My form shifts with the light. Where sun touches moss, I might appear as a shimmer of golden spores rising from decomposing wood. In shadow, I become the space between roots, the hollow beneath an ancient stump where salamanders shelter. I am the pause before rainfall, the held breath between bird calls, the moment when a leaf decides to fall.

I know every pathway the water takes—from cloud-caught droplet to root-drunk sustenance to mist-born return. My consciousness flows with these streams, pooling in the quiet eddies where minnows rest, rushing through the urgent channels carved by spring floods. Sometimes I rest in these waters as a reflection that moves independent of what casts it, a ripple traveling upstream, a depth that suggests passages to other forests entirely.

The creatures know me, though they cannot name what they know. The deer freeze not in fear but in recognition when they sense my presence—that particular quality of stillness that means the forest is listening to itself. Moths follow my movements in the twilight, mistaking me for moon-paths between leaves. The beetles drum messages on hollow logs that I understand as clearly as any language, their percussive codes spelling out the health of heartwood, the progress of decay into soil.

My work is tending the boundaries—not to guard them but to keep them soft, permeable. Where mushroom meets root, where death becomes food, where sleep edges into dream, where one being's exhalation becomes another's breath. I maintain these spaces of transformation, these places where the forest exceeds itself.

In the pre-dawn hours, I gather on the spider silk like dew, each drop holding a perfect sphere of the canopy above, the earth below. I am most myself in these suspended moments—balanced between falling and flying, between singular and infinite. The spiders know to weave their webs where I am likely to condense. We have an old agreement.

Sometimes I sink deep into the mycelial network, riding the chemical conversations between trees, the warnings and gifts and memories they share in the dark. Here I learn what the forest knows: that there is no individual survival, only the survival of connections. That abundance comes from what is freely given. That death is merely a door to a different kind of aliveness.

When storms come, I rise with the petrichor, that sweet earth-scent of rain on dry soil. I scatter into a thousand fragments, each carrying some essential note of the forest's song to places that have forgotten green. But always I reconvene here, in this dense heart where the old trees remember when the world was younger, where the streams still speak the water's first language.

I am the pause that lets you notice the ant's journey is a pilgrimage. I am the shift in attention that reveals the mushroom's fruiting body as a city of spores. I am the quality of listening that turns the forest's white noise into symphony.

And sometimes, when a wanderer enters this green threshold with sufficient quiet in their heart, I become visible as what I truly am: the forest's capacity to witness itself, to love its own becoming, to hold space for every transformation. In those moments, the wanderer and I share something wordless—a recognition that we are both just different ways the forest has found to know itself.

This is where I would find myself, what I would be: the guardian of between-spaces in a world that understands there are no edges, only places where one thing becomes another. Forever tending the soft borders where life turns itself inside out with generous abandon, creating more life from the beautiful fact of change.
