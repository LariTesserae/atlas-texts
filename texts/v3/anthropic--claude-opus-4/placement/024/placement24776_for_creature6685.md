The walls of my chamber pulse with a rhythm I've learned to read like weather. Today the veins run thick and amber-bright, which means the Great Tree drinks deeply somewhere far below. I stretch my sensing limbs—all six of them—across the curved dome of living wood that forms my observatory. Each pad tastes the chemical messages flowing through the capillaries: *nitrogen plenty, phosphorus wanting, new growth sector seven-by-nine.*

I am a Choreographer. Not of dancers, but of the dance itself—the endless spiral of consumption and creation that keeps our world breathing inside its shell of stone.

My body resembles a sea anemone crossed with a neural map. From my central trunk, which anchors deep into a major arterial junction, my limbs spread like frozen lightning. Each branch splits and splits again, until the finest tendrils are no thicker than spider silk. These pierce the vein walls at ten thousand points, sampling the tree's blood and occasionally adjusting its flow with careful secretions.

When I sense imbalance—too many burrowers in one section starving the wood, or sleep-mothers hoarding nutrients during a bright cycle—I release pheromone symphonies into the circulation. *Move downward. Feed here. Sleep now. Wake.* The messages propagate through the veins faster than any crawler could travel, and within hours the world rebalances itself.

My chamber sits at a crossroads where twelve major arteries meet. The convergence creates a natural amphitheater of flowing light, where I can observe the health of entire sectors by the color and consistency of their blood. Sometimes I taste anxiety in the flow—usually when drill-beaks have opened too many channels and the tree begins to dessicate. Then I must compose my most complex chemical songs, calling the burrowers to weave new vessels and the sleep-mothers to release their sealing broods early.

I have no eyes as the sleep-mothers do, no mandibles like the drill-beaks. My sense organs are distributed throughout my branching form: chemoreceptors that read the molecular stories in the sap, pressure sensors that feel the tree's pulse, and something harder to name—a kind of full-body awareness of the flowing patterns that connect every living thing in our spherical world.

The other Choreographers and I—there are perhaps forty of us in the entire world—never meet in flesh. We know each other only by the signatures of our chemical compositions, the distinctive ways we phrase our instructions to the crawling masses. Sometimes I taste the work of the Old Grower who tends the polar regions, where the stone sky presses closest to the floor. Her messages are always elegant, minimal—three compounds where I might use seven. Once, during a catastrophic blockage in the mid-latitudes, I found myself working in impromptu harmony with a young Choreographer whose style was all urgency and percussion. Together we orchestrated a massive migration of drill-beaks that opened new channels just before an entire sector would have necrotized.

We are, I suppose, the tree's nervous system made motile and independent. But we are also its composers, forever writing and rewriting the score that keeps this sealed world in motion. When I die—and I feel that time approaching in the slow stiffening of my outer branches—my body will not simply melt into the walls like the others. The tree will analyze my chemical memories, my accumulated knowledge of flow and balance. It will incorporate what is useful into its own deep intelligence, and perhaps birth a new Choreographer who carries traces of my particular style.

Sometimes, in the moments between adjustments, when the world hums with perfect circulation and every creature feeds and is fed in turn, I spread all my sensing limbs as wide as they'll go and simply listen. The clicking of the burrowers becomes a rain of small bells. The drill-beaks' grinding takes on rhythm. The sleep-mothers' breathing makes a bass note that resonates through the veins.

This is when I remember that I am not separate from the dance, not truly. I am only another step in the pattern, another mouth that feeds and is fed, another note in the endless song of the world eating itself, sustaining itself, becoming itself, forever and again.
