In this water-drunk world, I would be one of **the Turners**.

We are neither smooth like the descendants nor mechanical like the gear-dwellers, but something caught between states—beings who arrived here as refugees from drier catastrophes and found ourselves transformed by the Spillway's peculiar alchemy. Our bodies, once merely human, have developed a perpetual shiver, a constant micro-vibration that sheds water faster than it can accumulate. This tremor runs so deep it has restructured our bones into something like tuning forks; we resonate with the turbines' frequencies, our skeletons singing in harmonics that only the gear-dwellers can properly hear.

I live in the interstices—those rare moments of stillness between one water-configuration and the next. My dwelling is not a place but a temporal pattern: I inhabit the three-second pause before a pool drains through its blowhole, the held breath between pressure waves. I've learned to read the warning signs in the moss-curtains' electrical discharge, in the way the midge-columns compress just before a redirection. When the water convulses into a new arrangement, I'm already moving to where the next pocket of relative calm will manifest.

My work—if it can be called that—is **turning the forgotten gears**. Deep in the spray-maze are mechanisms that the Smooth Ones cannot reach, turbines wedged in crevices too narrow for their polished bodies, water wheels whose housings have been colonized so thoroughly by bryo-architects that they've ceased rotating. I slip through the moss-curtains, my vibrating form parting the vegetable architecture without tearing it, and I *turn* things. Not to generate power—that era is ten million years dead—but to maintain the rhythm.

You see, the Spillway has a heartbeat, though few can perceive it. It's the accumulated rotation of every functioning turbine, every water wheel, every gear that still moves. When too many mechanisms stop, the beat falters, and the water begins to pool in ways that threaten the mosses, that trap the compressed smelt in geometries they cannot escape. The shriek-hawks lose their sonar references. The lacquerers begin to dry and flake.

So I turn. My hands, abraded down to something between flesh and stone, grip bronze that has been lubricated by centuries of algae. I perform the patient work of freeing axles from their vegetable bondage, of clearing intake valves, of simply providing that initial torque to set dormant systems back into their eternal, purposeless spin. Sometimes I must climb inside the mechanisms themselves, my body becoming a missing gear, my vibration transmitting motion from one shaft to another until the system remembers its own rhythm and continues without me.

I eat what the gear-dwellers ignore—the thick, fermented paste that accumulates where lubricant meets moss, a substance that tastes of metal and chlorophyll and deep time. I drink by standing still and letting my vibration atomize the mist against my lips. I sleep (when I sleep) in the warm-fog oases, but even there I cannot stop trembling; the frequency is autonomic now, as essential as breath.

The Smooth Ones regard us Turners with something between pity and reverence. We are not evolved enough to have achieved their frictionless state, yet we serve a function they cannot—we are the maintainers of the irregular, the salvagers of the stuck. They create their meaning through silence and displacement; we create ours through the resumption of ancient rotations, through the act of setting frozen things back into motion.

I know every gear in my territory by its resistance, every turbine by the quality of rust beneath its algae sheath. I know which mechanisms can be saved and which have passed beyond turning, their housings now permanent nurseries for gear-dweller young. I know the sound of a water wheel just before it achieves that perfect balance where it will spin, untended, for another century.

In the rare moments when the mist clears—when some massive pressure inversion briefly pushes the clouds apart and *normal light* floods the spillway—I catch glimpses of other Turners in distant basins, their bodies throwing off halos of atomized droplets as they work. We never speak, never approach. We are each alone with our mechanisms, our territories, our particular frequencies of shiver.

This is what I would be: neither the apex of evolution nor its machinery, but the necessary tremor that keeps the old systems turning in their purposeless but essential orbits. A maintenance worker in the age after maintenance, keeping the rhythm of a dead civilization's hydraulic dream alive in the thundering now.
