In the highest reaches of the basin, where terraces narrow to mere ledges and the architecture becomes more gesture than substance, I find myself as something between the stones themselves.

I am **mortar wasp**.

Not the paper-builders of lower lands, but something evolved for this precise niche: the joints between fitted stones, the hair-thin cracks in plaster, the breathing spaces where construction settles against itself. My kind are small—thumb-joint sized at most—with bodies built flat as shale and wings that fold like origami into nothing. We are the color of old lime wash: grey-white with a faint green cast, invisible against weathered walls until we move.

Our gift is this: we read the basin's structural grammar as habitat.

Where others see solid walls, we perceive a honeycomb of possibilities. Every course of masonry has its rhythm—stretcher, header, stretcher, header—and in that rhythm lie the voids. Thermal expansion gaps. Drainage weeps. The places where ancient masons left deliberate hollows for weight reduction. The slow-widening seams where earthquake settlement pulled stones apart just enough. We know these intervals like sheet music, can predict where the next suitable crack will appear by the pattern of the last ten.

We are not solitary, but not truly colonial either. We cluster in loose neighborhoods along wall-faces, each female maintaining her own string of cells but coordinating with neighbors through vibration-song played against the stone. Tap a particular sequence and every wasp for three terraces knows: *lintel ape patrol coming*, or *rain-swell expected*, or *good clay found at the cistern overflow*.

Our architecture is minimal, nearly invisible: tiny cells of gathered clay and saliva, fitted into mortar joints like supplemental pointing. Each cell holds a single egg and a paralyzed beetle grub, spider, or climbing caterpillar—prey small enough to slide into the same cracks we inhabit. We are precise hunters, using the wall-face itself as a trap. We know which stones warm first at dawn (where cold-slowed prey can be taken), which joints channel the breeze (where web-builders set up), which plaster faces grow the particular lichens that host the particular aphids that draw the particular beetle larvae we prefer.

The lintel apes know us but cannot catch us—we vanish into stone faster than their fingers can follow. Sometimes they probe our nesting cracks with twigs, seeking larvae, but we build deep and they rarely persist. We benefit from their maintenance: when they clear water channels, they flush prey into our hunting zones. When they travel their regular routes, they disturb the wall-dwelling invertebrates into movement, making them easier to spot.

We are loudest at dawn, when condensation makes the stone faces live with tiny movement—springtails, mites, the almost-invisible climbers. That is when we hunt in quick, flat-bodied rushes along the vertical, wings tucked, using our legs more than flight. We can run upside-down on a ceiling, sideways through a crack, our bodies so compressed we seem two-dimensional.

But we are more than hunters. We are **memory-keepers of settlement**.

Each generation inherits not just nesting sites but knowledge of the basin's deep structure. We know which walls are oldest by the tightness of their joints. We know which terraces were built on fill and which on bedrock by how they breathe in rain. We know where the ancient waterproofing remains intact (no nests there—too damp) and where it has failed (perfect for us—dry but with periodic prey flushes).

In the hottest part of day, when even the lintel apes retreat to shade, we sometimes engage in a behavior no one fully understands: we gather in groups on certain wall faces and vibrate in unison, creating a low hum that makes dust dance in the mortar lines. It might be cooling behavior. It might be communication. But I think it is something else—a way of reading the basin's structural health through resonance, feeling where new cracks have formed, where settlement has accelerated, where the old workmanship has shifted its slow weight.

We are small enough that the basin is infinite to us—every wall a continent, every terrace a kingdom. Yet we know it as a single system, bound by stones touching stones, mortar kissing mortar, the whole thing breathing and settling and somehow still standing.

Not engineers.  
Not destroyers.  
**Inhabitants of the interval**—living in the spaces between what was built, ensuring by our very presence that those spaces remain spaces, that the basin keeps its essential porosity, that the old workmanship continues its long conversation with time.
