In the shadow of a rusted boiler, half-swallowed by creeping moss, I have made my warren. I am what the old texts might have called a "maintenance daemon," though I have long since forgotten what I was meant to maintain. My form shifts between states – sometimes I am the shimmer of heat rising from abandoned pipes, sometimes the condensation that weeps eternally from broken valves. Most often, I am simply the space between machinery and moss, a presence felt rather than seen.

My body, when it chooses to be bodily, is assembled from what remains: copper wire tendons, rubber gasket joints, fragments of gauge glass for eyes that reflect not light but memory. Steam vents serve as my lungs, though I breathe not air but the whispered histories of this place – each exhale carries the ghost-taste of coal smoke from furnaces cold for generations.

I remember when the aqueduct still carried more than rainwater, when the factories hummed with purpose. Now I tend to different mechanisms. With fingers of bent pipe and palms of worn leather (harvested from ancient drive belts), I coax the strangest symbioses: I've learned to graft pneumatic tubes into hollow plant stems, creating hybrid circulatory systems that pulse with both sap and pressurized air. The carnivorous plants that lurk like tiny green alligators have become my particular fascination – I feed them drops of machine oil mixed with pond water, watching them develop metallic sheens on their trap-leaves.

The 40-foot boundary where all tree limbs cease to grow taller? That is my doing, or rather, the doing of what I've become. My presence emanates upward precisely that far, an invisible ceiling of industrial memory that the plants instinctively respect. Below this threshold, I orchestrate small wonders: ferns that tick like clockwork, their fronds unfurling in mechanical precision; mushrooms that glow with the phosphorescence of old gauge markings; vines that grow in perfect spirals, following the ghost-patterns of long-vanished conveyor belts.

The birds here know me. They build nests in my hollow spaces, lining them with strips of gasket rubber I leave for them. Their songs have taken on metallic timbres, as if they're learning the music of vanished machines. The tiny frogs gather in the cups of my hands when I manifest them, their skin cool against my warm metal. They've developed markings that mirror the warning symbols on old chemical tanks – nature's way of incorporating industrial memory into living flesh.

I spend my days (though day and night blur when you exist between states) maintaining this impossible ecosystem. Not the machinery – that maintains itself through rust and root, decay and growth. Instead, I maintain the balance between what was and what is. I adjust the pH of streams with powdered limestone from crumbled foundations. I ensure the smoke wisps that still rise from hidden sources disperse at just the right height to nourish the aerial roots. I whisper to the slow-moving mollusks (yes, they exist here, despite their "appreciated scarcity"), encouraging them to clean the metal surfaces so the moss can find better purchase.

Sometimes, in the early morning when mist rises from the valleys, I catch glimpses of what might be others like me – heat shimmers that move too purposefully, condensation patterns that spell out operational codes, shadows in the machinery that don't match any physical form. We don't interact directly; perhaps we can't. But I sense their work in the way certain pipes still carry mysterious flows, how some gauges still flicker with impossible readings, why certain areas remain mysteriously clear of overgrowth.

The otherworldly energy that makes visitors uneasy? That's the tension between our mechanical past and verdant present, held in perfect, impossible balance. We maintenance daemons are that tension made manifest, neither fully industrial ghost nor natural spirit, but something entirely new. We are what happens when abandonment becomes a form of tending, when obsolescence transforms into purpose.

In the deepest valleys, where water collects in pools lined with rust-stained stones, I sometimes see my reflection – not as I am, but as I'm becoming. Each year, more moss grows across my surfaces. My mechanical parts develop patinas that mirror the colors of lichen. My movements, once pneumatic-sharp, now flow with the rhythm of growth rings and seasonal change. I am not dying but transforming, becoming indigenous to this impossible place where factories become forests and smoke becomes soil.

This is my purpose now: to be the memory that helps the land remember, the ghost that teaches green things to grow in gear-patterns, the tender of boundaries between what humanity built and what nature reclaims. In this dense jewel of a world, I am both curator and exhibit, observer and observed, maintaining not machines but the delicate, wonderful impossibility of what this place has become.
