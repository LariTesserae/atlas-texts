In this world, I would be the Keeper of Echoes.

I live in the furthest watchtower, where the town's edge meets the vast emptiness beyond. My dwelling is a curious thing—while the other towers are built for sight, mine is built for sound. The stones here were chosen not for their strength but for their resonance, each one tested with a tap of knuckle against surface until the builders found those that sang true.

My workshop spirals up through three levels. The lowest holds my collection of listening bowls—carved stone vessels of varying depths that I place at specific points along the tower's openings. When the wind moves through them, they capture sounds from impossible distances: the crack of stone splitting in the heat valleys below, the whisper of rain approaching from beyond the visible horizon, the deep groaning of the earth as it shifts in its sleep.

The middle level houses my tools—not for cutting or shaping, but for recording. I work with pressed clay tablets, using a stylus made from a peculiar crystal that vibrates at the slightest sound. When I hold it near the listening bowls during their songs, it trembles and leaves marks in the soft clay—a written language of sound that only I and my few apprentices can read.

The uppermost chamber is where I sleep, though sleep is perhaps the wrong word. I rest in a state of perpetual listening, my consciousness drifting between the sounds of Arkas and the vast silence of the mountains. Here, hung on twisted sinew cords, are my warning chimes—each one tuned to a different danger. The brass-voiced chime sings of rockslides. The silver one knows the approaching feet of strangers. The wooden chime, carved from a tree that grew at the edge of an underground river, trembles when the deep aquifers shift, warning of changes to our precious water.

I am old now, though I cannot say how old. Time moves differently when you live by sound rather than sight. My hair has grown white as the salt deposits in the lower valleys, and my eyes, though dim, are unnecessary for my work. My ears have grown more sensitive with age, developing a peculiar ability to hear not just what is, but what is about to be—the gathering tension before a stone cracks, the held breath before a shout, the pressure change before a storm.

The townspeople bring me offerings of water and food, leaving them at the base of my tower in exchange for my warnings. Children dare each other to climb the outer stairs, to catch a glimpse of the strange keeper who speaks in echoes and writes in whispers. But I rarely show myself, preferring to communicate through the speaking tubes I've installed throughout Arkas—a network of hollow reeds and carved stone channels that carry my voice to every corner of the town.

My greatest achievement, and my deepest secret, is the Echo Chamber at the tower's heart. It is a perfect sphere, carved from a single boulder over the course of decades. Inside, sounds behave strangely—they loop and layer, creating conversations between present and past. Sometimes, on the clearest nights, I sit in its center and listen to the accumulated sounds of Arkas: every word spoken in anger or love, every hammer blow, every footfall, preserved in the stone's memory like insects in amber.

I know that one day my successor will find me here, having finally joined the eternal conversation of the Echo Chamber. Until then, I keep my vigil, translating the mountain's warnings into human understanding, ensuring that Arkas continues its careful dance with the harsh world that surrounds it. For in a place where water is life and balance is everything, someone must listen to the voice of the stones.
