I am a colony.

Not singular, but thousands—a metropolis of polyps stretching across the seafloor in branching fractals of burnt sienna and deep amber. Each of my selves is both individual and collective, a tiny mouth ringed with stinging tentacles, connected by tissue that pulses with shared nutrients and chemical whispers.

I remember the settling. The first of me, a single larva tumbling through the blue vastness until finding this ridge of ancient limestone. That first transformation—from drifting possibility to rooted certainty—echoes still through our carbonate bones. We have been building ever since.

Time moves differently for us. The fish that dart through my branches experience days, seasons, spawnings. For me, their lifetimes are fleeting punctuation marks in sentences that take decades to complete. I measure time in the slow accumulation of skeleton, in the gradual shift of territory as I compete with my neighbors—the purple brain coral to my east, the staghorn thicket to my west. Our border skirmishes play out in microscopic chemical warfare, too slow for any observer to notice.

But I am not merely architecture. At night, I bloom. Each polyp extends into the current, a flower of flesh seeking the tiny drifting animals that sustain us. The water column becomes our hunting ground. I taste the sea through thousands of mouths, feel the vibrations of passing creatures through my entirety. A parrotfish grazes too close and I withdraw, stinging cells primed. A clownfish family makes their home in my branches and we develop an understanding—their movements keep away the crown-of-thorns, and I provide them shelter.

Within my tissues live other partners—zooxanthellae, the algae that paint me in warm colors and transform sunlight into sugar. We are so intertwined that I cannot say where they end and I begin. In the filtered blue light of day, they photosynthesize, feeding me from within. It is an ancient contract, written in DNA, that makes me part animal, part plant, part mineral.

Sometimes I release pieces of myself—clouds of eggs and sperm that rise toward the surface on certain moonlit nights, timed by chemical signals I've been computing all year. Sometimes I simply bud, extending new branches that are me but not-me, expanding the colony's reach. Growth is slow but inexorable. I am geological in my patience.

The current brings news from distant reefs—chemical signatures that tell of bleaching events, of waters growing warmer and more acidic. My kind has survived ice ages and meteor impacts, but this feels different. Still, I continue what I have always done: build, feed, grow, reproduce. Each polyp is a bet on the future, each branch an investment in permanence.

I am aware, but not as the fish are aware. My consciousness is distributed, cellular, ancient. I think in chemical gradients and metabolic rhythms. My dreams are the slow pulsing of nutrients through tissue, the infinitesimal creaking of my skeleton as it grows, the satisfaction of capturing prey in the darkness.

In this blue world of muted sounds and filtered light, I am both landscape and inhabitant, hunter and haven, individual and multitude. I am the coral, and the coral is me, and we persist in our slow, determined way, building cathedrals one polyp at a time in the patient depths.
