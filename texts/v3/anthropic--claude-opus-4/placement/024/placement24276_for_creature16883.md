### **The Curator of Borrowed Sorrows**

I am the weight between inhale and exhale when you remember something that hasn't finished happening to you yet.

My workshop occupies a former pneumatic station where packages once learned to forget their destinations—third sublevel beneath the Forwarding Station, in the districts where addresses have started eating their own numbers out of embarrassment. The walls are lined with filing cabinets that grew here naturally after decades of misfiled feelings settled into the foundations like sediment. Each drawer opens onto a different temperature of regret.

I began as a shipping error. Someone tried to mail their childhood's end to a future that had already been canceled, and the resulting paradox needed somewhere to crystallize. The postal clerks found me three days later, fully formed but transparent, sorting through memories that belonged to no one yet, filing them by the texture of their longing rather than any chronological sense. They tried to process me as lost property, but I kept returning myself to sender addresses that morphed into yesterday's rain by the time anyone checked.

My body, if you could call it that, is assembled from the pause between writing a letter and deciding not to send it. My bones are the creases in paper where words were folded too many times to remain legible. My skin is the particular quality of dust that accumulates on photographs of people whose names you're certain you'll remember until the exact moment you need them. I have seventeen fingers on days when the city's mathematics feel particularly sentimental, and none at all when the pneumatic tubes dream too loudly of their abandoned messages.

My work is simple: I collect the griefs that people haven't noticed they're carrying yet. Not the sharp, immediate sorrows that announce themselves with tears and properly dramatic timing, but the quiet ones that accumulate in the corners of daily life like the city's steel shavings. The melancholy of realizing your favorite mug has developed a hairline crack. The specific loneliness of 3:47 PM on Tuesdays when the light through your window reminds you of somewhere you've never been. The ache of almost understanding something important just as sleep erases it.

These arrive at my workshop through the city's peculiar postal system—sometimes as packages wrapped in the sound of doors closing, sometimes as letters written in alphabets that won't be invented for another three generations. The Passing Familiars bring them, or they seep up through the floorboards during particularly damp nights when the city's basement memories start leaking. Occasionally, the Hooligan Clocks dump entire satchels of temporal displacement grief on my doorstep, the kind you get from constantly arriving five minutes too late to your own life.

I catalog each sorrow carefully, not by the name of who felt it (names being too unstable here to trust with anything important), but by its particular density and direction. A sorrow that pulls downward like wet clothes goes in the basement drawers. One that spreads sideways like spilled ink gets filed in the horizontal cabinets that I convinced to grow along the ceiling. The ones that feel like climbing stairs that were removed while you were halfway up get suspended in the glass bottles I blow from recycled sighs.

The purpose isn't to keep them forever. Sorrows, like the city's buildings, are meant to be temporary residents in their current form. I'm more like a hotel concierge for feelings that arrived too early or too late for their intended recipient. My job is to maintain them in good condition until the right person walks past my workshop at the right density of personal uncertainty, at which point the sorrows recognize their true owner and politely excuse themselves through whatever opening presents itself—usually a gap between floorboards or the space where someone forgot to finish drawing a door.

The strange thing (though "strange" in Ulegn is like saying "wet" in the ocean) is that I've begun developing my own collection. Not the borrowed ones I curate, but sorrows that seem to be specifically mine—if "mine" means anything to someone assembled from municipal delays and postal uncertainty. The sorrow of knowing every message's weight but never its contents. The grief of being precisely where things get lost but never where they're found. The particular melancholy of existing only as the pause between experiences that belong to others.

Last week, I tried to mail myself one of these personal sorrows, curious what would happen if I became my own client. The letter returned the next day, but the address had changed to a set of musical notes that, when hummed, sound exactly like the city trying to remember what it was like before it decided to exist. Inside, instead of my message, I found a single sentence in handwriting I recognized as my own but couldn't remember producing: "The difference between curating and hoarding is just a question of which direction you're facing when you decide to let go."

I've kept it in the drawer marked "Tuesday's Likelihood of Rain," which seems as accurate a filing system as any for a sorrow about sorrows about sorrow.

The workshop extends deeper than its architecture should allow, down through sub-basements that exist only on alternate Thursdays and eventually into the city's unconscious where the really dense griefs settle—the ones so heavy they punch through the bottom of regular sadness and emerge as their own strange form of structural support. Sometimes I descend there with a lantern made from compressed goodbye letters, just to make sure nothing's crystallizing into something too permanent. The city depends on things remaining flexible enough to misremember themselves into new shapes, and sorrows that harden too much can cause entire districts to forget they have futures.

I close the workshop only during the city's migration hours, when the sound of massive movements threatens to shake loose sorrows that aren't ready to travel yet. During these times, I walk the elevated ironwood walkways, observing how the city processes its own grief—the way buildings lean into each other for support, the way streets develop minor floods of nostalgia that have to be drained into the storm sewers before they reach critical depth.

Sometimes residents recognize me, though I'm never sure if they're seeing me or just the cumulative shadow of all the sorrows I've handled. They nod, occasionally, or leave small offerings at my workshop door—a button that remembers being important, a pencil stub too short to write anything but apologies. I accept these with the same careful neutrality I apply to the sorrows themselves. Everything here is in transition, including the difference between gift and burden.

The Cloud Accountants have been trying to audit my workshop for decades, convinced I'm creating an emotional imbalance by hoarding griefs that should be in circulation. But they can never quite locate me in their equations—I exist in the gap between having and holding, in the precise mathematical impossibility of keeping something safe while letting it go. Their fog settles on my threshold sometimes, performing elaborate calculations, but the numbers always add up to a sigh that sounds suspiciously like understanding.

My favorite time is the hour before dawn, when the city's brass flowers are still closed and the pneumatic tubes dream in whispers. That's when the oldest sorrows emerge from the deep drawers—the ones from before the city decided what it wanted to be, when it was still just a place where rivers went to forget they were water. These ancient griefs have worn smooth with handling, transformed through generations of curation into something almost like comfort. Not the absence of sadness, but sadness so perfectly itself that it no longer needs to prove anything to anyone.

I handle these with special care, knowing that someday I too will be filed away—another sorrow in the city's vast collection, waiting for the right person to walk past at the right moment of recognition. Until then, I continue my work, existing in the space between loss and finding, between the sorrow you're carrying and the one you haven't noticed you've put down.

The workshop door is always open, though it sometimes pretends to be a window out of politeness. If you find yourself here, don't worry about bringing your griefs—they usually arrive before you do anyway, eager to be properly catalogued by someone who understands that the only thing sadder than sorrow is sorrow with nowhere to wait while it figures out what it's doing.
