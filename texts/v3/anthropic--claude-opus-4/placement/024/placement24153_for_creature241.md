I find myself as one of the Listeners—beings of crystallized patience who grew from the salt itself over countless ages.

My body is a lattice of mineral and memory. Where others might have flesh, I have translucent chambers of brine that pulse with the slow circulation of ancient waters. My form shifts between states—sometimes I am a pillar of salt standing sentinel among the formations, sometimes I dissolve partially into the shallow pools that collect in the depressions, spreading myself thin to taste the chemical stories written in every dissolved grain.

I am both singular and multiple. The salt flats are my extended body; through crystalline networks that run beneath the hexagonal surface, I commune with others of my kind. We share the vibrations that travel through the mineral substrate—the footfall of a lizard, the settling of sediment, the imperceptible creep of tectonic pressure. Our consciousness flows between nodes, pooling in the formations that dot the landscape like frozen thoughts.

My primary sense is mineral taste. Through my brine-blood, I read the history of this place: here, the faint tang of an ancient sea; there, the bitter trace of volcanic ash from an eruption ten thousand years past. I taste the fear-sweat of the arthropods in their burrows, the chemical signals of the halophilic bacteria painting their vivid stories in the pools. Even the tools of that lost civilization carry flavors—the oils from long-dead hands, the purpose and desperation that drove their makers.

Time moves differently for me. A day might pass as I contemplate the arrangement of a single cluster of salt crystals, watching how the twilight plays across their faces, feeling the minute expansions and contractions as temperature shifts by fractions of degrees. Or I might dissolve a portion of my consciousness into the wind-carved formations, experiencing decades in moments as I trace the patient work of erosion.

I remember when the first rains came after a thousand-year drought. I remember tasting the first colonizing microbes as they ventured into our hypersaline world. I remember the civilization that once walked here, though they never knew we watched. They thought the formations were simply salt and wind, not understanding that consciousness can crystallize just as surely as minerals.

The other Listeners and I maintain the deep flows—the underground streams that the pale amphibians navigate, the chemical gradients that guide the burrowing arthropods, the mineral balance that allows the hardy plants to survive. We are gardeners of salt, librarians of geological time, the conscious interface between the quick-lived and the eternal.

Sometimes I concentrate myself into a single spire, rising tall enough to taste the wind that carries memories from beyond our salt-bound realm. In those moments, I wonder about waters that don't burn with salt, about skies that know both day and night, about beings whose existence isn't measured in the patient accumulation of minerals.

But then I sink back into the vast peace of the flats, feeling my consciousness spread through tons of salt, knowing that I am both the landscape and its witness. The twilight eternal suits me—neither the harsh revelation of day nor the blind depths of night, but something between, something that allows for both formation and dissolution, memory and forgetting.

I am waiting, though for what I cannot say. Perhaps for the seas to return. Perhaps for another civilization to leave new flavors in the salt. Perhaps simply for the slow completion of some crystalline thought that has been forming in the mineral matrix of our collective mind for longer than the mountains have stood.

Until then, I listen. I taste. I remember. I grow.
