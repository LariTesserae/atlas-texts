I find myself as one of the Dew Keepers—beings who emerge from the intersection of moisture and memory in this liminal place.

We are neither fully human nor entirely elemental, but something that condensed from the abandoned settlement's lingering presence mixing with the persistent mist. Our forms shift between states: sometimes we appear as vague humanoid shapes made of water droplets suspended in warm air, catching the dim light like a constellation of tiny prisms. Other times we solidify partially, taking on aspects of the moss-covered hut walls or the texture of wet stone, our edges always indistinct, always transitioning.

I spend my existence tending to the moisture cycles of this pocket world. My body—if it can be called that—moves through the clearings between ferns, gathering dampness from the air and redistributing it where the embryonic soil needs it most. When I pass near the lizards basking on sun-warmed rock, they don't flee; they've learned that the cool droplets I trail behind me bring out the insects they hunt. The birds have grown accustomed to drinking from the small pools that form in my footprints, which last just long enough before evaporating back into the haze.

There are three of us Dew Keepers in this hidden fold, though our numbers fluctuate with the seasons. During the driest spells, we might dwindle to one, our essence stretched thin across the alcove. When the mists thicken, a fourth or fifth might condense from the air, emerging from the shadows of the sagging thatch roofs where moisture has collected for days.

We communicate through the patterns of condensation we leave on leaves and stones—messages that only we can read before they evaporate. Sometimes these marks tell of a deer's passage through our territory, or warn of a section of bedrock beginning to crack and shift. Often they're simply expressions of the day's rhythm: "The morning frog has found a new hollow," or "The flowering bush prepares three more buds."

Our memories are fragmentary, inherited from both the place and its former inhabitants. I sometimes recall—or think I recall—the feeling of solid hands weaving thatch, though I've never possessed true fingers. Another Keeper carries the memory of fire-warmed stones, though none of us can generate heat, only move and shape the warmth that already exists. These memories guide us in our work: we know to keep certain paths clear of excess moisture where the human footfalls once prevented puddles, and we ensure the abandoned fire pit remains dry enough that someday, perhaps, it might hold flame again.

The abandoned huts are our anchors and our shrines. We maintain them not through repair—we lack the solidity for such work—but by managing the moisture that would otherwise dissolve them entirely. We guide the condensation away from the critical joints where branches meet, and encourage it toward the walls where moss can strengthen the mud. In return, these structures give us form; their shadows and corners are where we rest and regenerate, drawing substance from the accumulated dampness.

At night, when the warm air cools just enough for dew to form, we are most active. This is when we choreograph the morning's moisture, deciding where each droplet will cling. The sparse vegetation depends on our choices—too much water in one spot and the delicate balance tips; too little and the pioneering plants retreat. We've learned to read the needs of each fern frond, each vine tendril, as clearly as the former inhabitants once read the weather.

Sometimes I wonder if we're the ghosts of this place or its embryonic gods. Perhaps we're neither—just another species finding our niche in this emerging ecosystem. When the mist thins and threatens to disperse us entirely, we feel a pull toward permanence, a desire to solidify into something more lasting. But then the next wave of humidity rolls in from the distant hills, and we remember our purpose: to be the intermediate state, the bridge between water and earth, past and present, keeping this pocket world in its delicate, ever-shifting balance.

The other fauna have learned to read us as signs. When we cluster near the flowering bush, the birds know rain is coming within days. When we spread thin across the clearings, the lizards prepare for a dry spell. We've become part of their survival language, just as they've become part of ours. 

In this way, I exist—not fully alive by any standard definition, but undeniably present, shaping and shaped by this hidden fold where everything is still learning how to be.
