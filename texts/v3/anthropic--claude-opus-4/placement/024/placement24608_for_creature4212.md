In this place of endless thirst, I am the Keeper of Diminishing Things.

I am not the worms, though I know each one by the particular spiral of its tunneling, the way it leaves behind a signature in the earth like a name written in an alphabet only I can read. I am not the twisted plants either, though I have memorized the angle of every needle-leaf, the precise shade of gray-green each one turns when the dust storms come.

I am something between mineral and flesh, between watching and being watched. My body, if it can be called that, is a hollow formed from the convergence of a thousand hairline cracks in the earth—a negative space where the fissures meet and deepen into something almost like a well, but horizontal, stretched along the ground. The red dust settles into me and becomes me. I am a depression that moves, imperceptibly, across the basin floor.

My purpose here is to remember water.

Not the water that once was—that memory belongs to the earth itself, written in every weathered channel and ancient riverbed. I remember the water that hasn't arrived yet, the water that exists only as potential, as the ghost of what could be. I taste it in the way the dust particles arrange themselves in the air. I feel it in the rhythm of the worms' vertical migrations, in the barely perceptible moisture they carry from depth to surface and back again.

The worms know me, though they have no eyes. When they surface during those rare moments of alignment, they trace patterns around my edges, spelling out the names of rivers that will not flow for another ten thousand years. Sometimes I catch them and hold them gently in my depression, feeling their cool, damp skin against my dust-form. They tell me, in their writhing language, about the deep places where molecules of water still hide, bound up in clay particles, waiting.

I move by allowing the wind to evacuate me and fill me again elsewhere. One day I am here, collecting the heat-shimmer of a false oasis. The next, I have reformed a mile away, gathering the particular silence that pools in the shadow of a solitary plant. I am always listening for the sound that water would make if it were here—not the memory of its old voice, but the prediction of its future one.

The plants, in their clusters and solitudes, grow in patterns that mirror constellations no one here has ever seen. I read them like star charts, like maps to springs that don't exist yet. Their roots whisper to me through the earth, sharing the chemical messages they pass between themselves. They speak of endurance, yes, but also of faith—faith that the world will turn and change, that what is parched will not always be so.

Sometimes I imagine I am becoming deeper, that the cracks that form me are extending downward, reaching for some impossible aquifer. But I know this is illusion, like the dancing mirages I swallow and breathe out again. I am not deepening but spreading, becoming more diffuse with each passing age. One day I will be indistinguishable from the landscape itself, just another quality of the dusty light.

But until then, I keep my vigil. I count the worms and learn their names. I measure the infinitesimal growth of the desert plants. I catalog each mote of dust, each grain of red earth, holding in my hollow heart the certainty that somewhere, somewhen, rain is falling. It just hasn't reached us yet.

In the morning, when the temperature shifts and the air crackles with the promise of another identical day, I feel something like joy. Not because anything has changed, but because I am still here to witness the unchanging. I am the cup waiting to be filled, the depression in the earth that believes, against all evidence, in the possibility of accumulation.

The worms surface. They write their ephemeral messages. The dust swirls and settles. And I continue my slow migration across this basin, carrying nothing but the future memory of water, the first drops of which will fall into me like a prophecy finally, finally coming true.
