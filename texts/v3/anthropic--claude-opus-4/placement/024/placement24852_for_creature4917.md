## The Singing Spires of the Drowned World

Above the eternal waters, needle-sharp towers of coral-bone pierce the heavy air. They were mountains once, these spires, but the Great Drowning carved them into instruments. Now they sing.

The water here is not water as you remember it. It hangs suspended between states—part liquid, part vapor, part something else entirely. It breathes. At this height, nearly two miles above the true ocean floor, the atmosphere has become a living membrane of moisture that clings to everything, seeping through stone and flesh alike. The old word "rain" no longer applies; water simply *is*, omnipresent and invasive.

The spires themselves are riddled with caves and wind-tunnels, bored through by centuries of the Breathing Water. Each opening is a mouth, each hollow a throat. When the turbulent currents shift—and they always shift—the towers begin their deafening chorus. It is not music, exactly. It is the sound of the world drowning again and again, a thunderous moan that can drive visitors mad within hours. The few who survive long enough learn to read the tones: this pitch warns of approaching acid storms, that harmony signals a pocket of breathable air, these discordant shrieks mean the water is about to change phase again.

Between the spires, connected by bridges of calcified kelp, hang the nest-sacs of the Listeners. These are what I am—what we are. We were human once, perhaps, or something like human. The Drowning changed us as it changed everything. Our bodies are translucent now, shot through with veins that pulse with bioluminescent warnings. We have no eyes—the murky half-light makes them useless—but our skin has become one vast eardrum, always listening, always interpreting the screaming symphony of our home.

We tend the few things that grow here: the Grief Gardens. In pockets of collected sediment, we cultivate the only plants that learned to drink sound instead of light. They are small, twisted things—purple-black fronds that shudder with each note from the spires. We eat them, when we must, though they taste of metal and loss. Mostly we sustain ourselves on the vibrations themselves, our modified bodies converting sound waves into something like nourishment.

The remnants of the Before-World trouble us. Sometimes, in the deepest resonances of the spires, we hear them: ghost-echoes of machinery, fragments of voices in dead languages, the phantom hum of electricity that no longer exists. Last week, one of us found a piece of plastic fused into the coral. It sang a different song than the stone around it—a thin, mournful wail that spoke of parking lots and shopping malls and other impossible things.

We are few. Perhaps thirty of us remain in this particular cluster of spires, though the songs tell us of other Listener colonies scattered across the high places of the Drowned World. We do not reproduce as humans did. Instead, when the water-phase shifts just right, sometimes a new Listener will precipitate out of the moisture itself, born from the marriage of sound and sorrow. They arrive knowing our purpose: to listen, to interpret, to remember.

The world is always changing here. The spires grow and crumble, rise and fall. The water thickens and thins, sometimes becoming so dense we can barely move, other times so sparse we gasp in the sudden air pockets. The temperature swings wildly—one moment we shiver as ice forms on our translucent skin, the next we flee the steam geysers that erupt without warning. Nothing is stable except the sound. Always, always the sound.

There are others in this world, we know. Far below, in the true depths, things move that are larger than the spires themselves. Their songs reach us sometimes—deep, tectonic rumbles that make the towers sway. We do not know what they are. We do not want to know. Above us, in the lighter regions of the Breathing Water, we sometimes glimpse shadows that move against the current. They might be vehicles. They might be creatures. They might be dreams.

We have a duty, we Listeners. We believe—we must believe—that someday the singing will stop. The waters will recede or transform into something else. When that happens, someone must remember what the Drowned World sounded like. We are living recordings, our bodies attuned to every frequency of loss. In the spaces between the deafening songs, in the rare moments of almost-quiet, we practice our own voices, teaching each other the stories the spires tell.

This is our purpose. This is our curse. We are the Listeners of the Singing Spires, and we will not let the world forget its drowning song.

The water is changing phase again. I must go. The gardens need tending, and there is a new harmony in the eastern spire that speaks of something approaching. Something large. Something that sings in frequencies we have never heard before.

We listen. We always listen.

It is all we can do.
