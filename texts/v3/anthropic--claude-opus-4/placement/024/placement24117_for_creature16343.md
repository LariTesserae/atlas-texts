In this misted highland town, where dampness clings to stone and time moves like sap through winter wood, you would find yourself as the Keeper of Echoes.

Your dwelling sits where the town's highest street curves into shadow—a narrow house of weathered granite, distinguished only by the peculiar arrangement of small bronze bells that hang from your eaves. These bells, no larger than acorns, never ring in the wind. They wait for something else entirely.

You are neither young nor old, though the townspeople have known you all their lives. Your hair carries premature silver like the mist carries light, and your fingers are stained blue-black from working with the lichens that grow on the northern faces of the oldest buildings. You harvest these in the pre-dawn hours, when the town sleeps and only the stones remember.

Your craft is singular: you preserve the sounds that would otherwise vanish. In your workshop—a single room where shelves climb toward shadows—sit hundreds of small clay vessels, each no bigger than a child's fist. Their mouths are sealed with beeswax mixed with crushed stone from the town's own streets. Inside each vessel lives a captured echo: the last laugh of a grandmother before she passed, the first word of a child now grown, the sound of rain on the day the old bridge was completed, the particular scrape of the blacksmith's hammer from before he learned to strike true.

The townspeople come to you, though not often. A mother might arrive at your door holding nothing but air cupped in her palms—the space where her daughter's giggle just was. A stoneworker might bring you the resonance of his chisel striking the same stone his great-grandfather carved. You take these offerings with hands that move like water, slow and certain. You breathe them into clay vessels you shape yourself from earth gathered where the town meets the wild slopes.

The process is delicate. You must hold the sound in your throat without letting it dissipate, carrying it like a bird that mustn't know it's caged. The vessel must be formed around the echo while it still vibrates with life. Your fingers work the clay as the sound settles, and you seal it with wax gathered from the candler's waste, mixed with dust from the specific place where the sound was born.

At night, when the tallow candles burn low and the mist presses against windows like curious spirits, you sometimes walk your shelves. You know each vessel by touch—this one holds the market day from forty years past, when the harvest was so good the usually quiet townspeople sang until dawn. That one contains the whisper of the mountain wind on the night the last wolf was heard howling in the distance.

You are the town's memory made manifest, though you rarely speak of what you do. When someone's child is about to leave for distant places, they might come to you. When the old storyteller's voice begins to fade, he visits your door. You never promise anything, but your vessels multiply slowly, organizing themselves according to a system only you understand—not by date or importance, but by the way sounds relate to each other, the way an echo of hammering calls to an echo of rain, the way laughter seeks its own kind across years.

Your own voice has grown quiet from this work. You speak in undertones, as if afraid to disturb the silence that allows you to hear what others miss. The children think you strange but not frightening. They've noticed that sounds behave differently near your home—footsteps seem to linger a half-beat longer, and sometimes they swear they hear their own whispers returned to them in voices aged by years they haven't lived yet.

The bronze bells on your eaves are for the rarest echoes, the ones that come without human bearing. On certain nights, when the mist is so thick it swallows sound before it can travel, those bells detect vibrations that exist on the edge of hearing: the settling of ancient stones, the conversations between water and rock far beneath the streets, the sound of the town itself breathing. These you capture in vessels of a different kind—rough-hewn stone hollowed with infinite patience, sealed with resin from the highland pines.

You know that one day someone will need to tend your collection, to understand the organization that lives in your fingertips and nowhere else. But for now, you continue. Each morning, you wake before the town stirs, check your vessels for cracks (sound, like water, finds its way through the smallest spaces), and prepare new clay. You listen to the town awakening—the first footfall on cobblestones, the creak of shutters opening, the whistle of the kettle in the baker's shop.

Today, like every day, you'll preserve what others think ephemeral. You'll hold in trust the sounds that make this place itself: unremarkable perhaps, but irreplaceable. Your neighbors go about their business, maintaining stones and weaving wool, while you maintain something less tangible but no less essential—the audio memory of a town that changes slowly, where every sound matters because it might not come again.

When tourists arrive on those rare occasions, they don't notice you. Your work leaves no visible mark on the town. But the townspeople know. In the quiet moments of their daily lives, they're aware of you as they're aware of their own heartbeats—essential, unobtrusive, keeping time with something larger than any single life.

You are the keeper of what would otherwise be lost, the curator of echoes in a town where sound travels strangely through mist and stone, where the past lives not in books but in the careful preservation of vibrations that once moved through air, now held safe in darkness, waiting.
