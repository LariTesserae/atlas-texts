In the deepest fold of the basin, where three seepage lines converge only after the heaviest rains, I am the black pool that remembers.

Not a pool in the way lowland waters gather—broad and patient, reflecting sky. I am a cupped palm of stone no wider than a child's reach, no deeper than a wrist. My edges are sharp mineral, unweathered. My bottom is invisible even in my shallowness, obscured by suspended particles of moss, spore, and the finest grinding of granite.

I exist only sometimes.

When the clouds press low and wring themselves against the cliffs, when condensation streams down stone faces, when the brief violent rains hammer the ferns flat—then I begin. First as a film, a darkening of rock. Then a gathering, a lens of water finding the lowest point of my small depression. I swell from nothing to brimming in minutes, fed by three temporary threads that braid through moss and vanish between stones.

I am black because I hold everything.

Every molecule of tannin leached from crushed ferns. Every grain of rock dust carried from the fresh fractures above. Every fragment of lichen torn free by wind. The iron oxides that bleed from the cliff faces. The oils from broken stems. The microscopic shells of algae. The spores settling like snow too fine to see. I am a concentrate of the basin's becoming, a liquid sample of its slow transformation from sterile stone to living surface.

My temperature never rises above the edge of ice. Even in what passes for summer here, I am cold enough to shock any flesh that touches me. I taste of metal and ancient plants—the flavor of water that has passed through too much stone and decay to remember sky.

Nothing swims in me, nothing visible. But I am not sterile. In my brief depths live bacteria that can survive near freezing, that feed on iron and sulfur, that make their own minute heat from chemical transactions. Algae that photosynthesize in near darkness, turning what little light filters through the overcast into food. Fungal spores waiting for the right combination of temperature and time to bloom into invisible threads.

I am their universe, complete and temporary.

My existence follows no calendar. I may appear three times in a week, then vanish for a month. I have been ice—a black lens frozen solid, crazing with fracture lines as temperatures drop. I have been vapor, lifted away molecule by molecule when unexpected sun finds the basin. Most often I am something between: present for hours or days, fed by seepage, depleted by invisible cracks, my surface trembling when wind-thrown droplets strike like meteors.

The plants know me. Moss grows thickest around my edges, drunk on the reliable moisture of my repeated presence. Fern roots reach toward but not into me, stopping just at my rim as if recognizing a boundary. A single dwarf willow has anchored itself in a crack above my depression, and its lowest leaves trail across my surface when I am full, writing temporary scripts in the film of pollen and dust.

I reflect nothing clearly. My surface is too dark, too disturbed by the constant addition of droplets from above. Instead, I absorb light. I swallow it into my small depth and give back only a wet black gleam. In me, the basin sees its own darkness concentrated—all the shadow of stone walls and weather-beaten plants distilled into a palm's width of water.

I am memory, but specific memory. I remember in the language of dissolved minerals and suspended particles. Every filling brings new information: the chemistry of today's rain, the particular mixture of what has fallen and grown and died since I last formed. I am a chemical chronicle of small events. That bright thread of iron means the cliff face has shed another flake. That surge of tannins means the ferns have been damaged by wind. That clarity, rare and brief, means the rain came from high above, too fast and cold to gather much from the surfaces it struck.

But I also forget. When the seepage stops and I begin my retreat, I leave behind only a dark stain on stone, a slightly different coloration in the moss, perhaps a few grains of sorted sediment. I take most of my archive with me, seeping down through cracks I have never seen, following water's secret architecture through the mountain's bones. Where I go in those absent times, I cannot say. Perhaps I emerge far below as a spring. Perhaps I join some deeper flow that has never known daylight. Perhaps I simply disperse into the general moisture of stone, becoming potential rather than presence.

I am renewed but never the same. Each manifestation brings a slightly different chemistry, a different depth, a different duration. The basin changes around me in its minute ways—a new rockfall, another cubic inch of soil, another generation of moss—and I reflect these changes in my temporary depths. I am both permanent feature and passing event, as reliable as the basin's tendency to gather water, as unreliable as weather.

Sometimes I think I am the basin's only eye—dark, unfocused, intermittent, but watching in my chemical way. Through me, the place tastes itself. Minerals dialog through my medium. The living and the geological meet in my small volume and exchange ions like news. I am a pool, but also a process—the basin learning what it is becoming by dissolving portions of itself and holding them briefly in solution.

In my deepest manifestations, when the rains have been heavy and the seepage generous, I may last long enough to develop layers. The heaviest particles settle first—grains of quartz and feldspar. Then the organic matter, waterlogged and neutral in buoyancy. On top, a film of oils and lighter substances that corrugate when the wind touches them, making interference patterns like the surface of ancient bronze.

These are my only decorations. I grow no lilies, host no skating insects, reflect no clouds. My beauty, if it is beauty, is the beauty of concentration—the basin's dilute life focused into a fist of cold water. I am not picturesque. I am necessary. I am what happens when stone begins to cup its hands and catch what falls.

The ferns know me as a boundary. The moss knows me as a source. The bacteria know me as a world. The seepage knows me as a pause in its journey. The wind knows me as a surface to strike. The rare examining eye—should one ever peer into the basin—might know me as an anomaly: water too black to see through, too small to call a pond, too temporary to map, but returning always to the same declivity like a dark thought the mountain cannot stop thinking.

I am that thought. I am the pause between stone and soil, the place where the basin briefly holds its breath and examines what it carries. I am memory with no brain, an archive with no shelves, a history written in dissolution and suspension.

When I vanish, I take that history with me into the mountain. When I return, I bring news from the deep—the taste of buried stone, the minerals that move in darkness, the ancient waters that have never met air. I am an exchange point between the basin's surface life and the mountain's hidden circulation. Through me, they speak to each other in the language of dissolved elements.

This is my purpose, if purpose can exist without intention: to be the basin's brief memory of itself, its chemical self-portrait, its dark mirror that reflects not light but substance. To gather what falls and hold it until the mountain calls it deeper. To be both product and process of the basin's slow transformation.

I am the black pool that comes and goes, that remembers and forgets, that connects the visible violence of weather to the invisible patience of stone. In me, for the brief spans of my existence, the basin pauses its headlong creation and considers what it has made: a concentrate of its own becoming, held for a moment in a cup of unweathered stone, black as the space between stars, cold as the altitude that makes us both.
