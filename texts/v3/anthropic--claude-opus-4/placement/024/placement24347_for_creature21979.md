In this vertical city of perpetual damp and patient machinery, I am one of the chain-keepers.

My cabin sits at the seventh terrace, neither high nor low, tucked into the elbow where the great hoist meets the gallery wall. It is a small space, perhaps six feet square, built of the same wet iron as everything else, but it is mine in the way that matters here — not by ownership but by attendance. The chain passes through it, massive and slow, each link the size of a dinner plate, ascending from the deep streets with its burden of water or coal or cloth, and I am its listener, its tender, the one who knows by the faintest change in its voice whether all is well.

I came to this work young, as most of us do. My mother was a lamp-tender on the fourth terrace, my father one of the high people who came down to court her and stayed, dazzled by the orange light and the close walls. Their child — myself — came out suited to neither extreme but to this middle band, where the daylight is still a rumor but not quite a stranger, where the fog shows gray through the gratings but does not press so close as it does below.

The chain speaks constantly. Not in words, but in a language I have learned over twenty years of sitting beside it: the deep bone-note of its working speed, the slight brightening of pitch when the load is light, the almost-musical complaints it makes in the minutes before a squall when the air pressure drops and all the metal in the city tightens. I oil it twice a shift, feeling each link as it passes, reading its wear through my fingertips. My hands are like everyone's here — wet, green-tinged in the creases, always wiping down my thighs before I touch anything that matters.

My cabin has a chair, iron and cushioned with waxed canvas. A small table, bolted to the floor. A lamp, always lit, throwing its orange circle. A window, no glass, just a square cut in the wall that looks out onto the gallery and, beyond it, nothing — the fog, the void, the suggestion of the opposite slope when the weather clears. The chain enters through a slot in the floor, exits through a slot in the ceiling. The mechanism itself, the great wheel and the engine that drives it, is in the chamber behind me, but I do not need to see it. I know it by sound.

I keep a flask of bitter tea, the kind that stays hot for hours in the thermal bottles the fifth-terrace smiths make. I keep a book sometimes, though I rarely read. The listening takes most of my attention, and what's left I spend watching the gallery through my window — the occasional figure passing in their slate and moss colors, the water running in the runnels, the fog changing its mind about what it wants to show.

The other chain-keepers know me, and I know them, though we meet rarely. We are spread along the great lifts, each in our own cabin, each wedded to our own length of iron. When we do meet — at the shift changes, at the repair depot where we collect our oil — we speak in the lowest of registers, barely voiced, because we have all trained our ears so fine that anything above a whisper feels like shouting. We compare our chains' health like parents comparing children. We share the small wisdoms: that the number twelve link from the drive wheel always sings a half-tone higher than the rest, that you can predict a bearing failure three days out by a smell like hot brass, that the chains are happier in the fog than in the rare clear days when the temperature swings make the metal argumentative.

There is a romance, among the high people and the visitors, about the rain. They think we chain-keepers must love the squalls, must thrill to the violence like everyone else. They are wrong. The squalls are when my chain is in danger, when the loads swing and the wet makes everything slick and the sudden weight of water pouring onto a half-emptied carrier can throw the whole system into distress. I do go out when the storms come — we all do — but not for the thrill. I go out because that is when my chain needs me most, when I must watch the loads swaying in the wind and be ready to throw the brake if they swing too far, when I must listen through the roar for the one wrong note that means trouble.

I stand on the gallery in my oiled coat, one hand on the brake lever, rain streaming off the brim of my hat, and I am not watching the sky. I am watching the chain, always the chain, reading its motion, feeling through my boots the way the building shudders when a gust hits the loads broadside. The other people, the ones who are not responsible for keeping the city's heart beating, they can afford to find the storms beautiful. I find them necessary and difficult and sometimes terrifying, the way a parent finds a child's fever — something to be weathered, to be brought through safe.

When the squall passes and the dripping resumes, I go back to my cabin, wipe down the chair, dry my hands on my thighs, and sit again in the orange circle of my lamp. The chain resumes its patient note. I pour tea. I listen. This is the shape of my days, and I am content in it the way the moss is content on the walls — not from lack of imagination but from having found the right place, the place where I fit, where my particular qualities of ear and patience and middle-light eyes are exactly what is needed.

Sometimes, in the very quiet hours when even the foot traffic stops and the only sound is water and chain, I think about the children I do not have and probably never will. Chain-keepers marry late or not at all — the work eats so much of us, leaves so little for the patient courtship of the terraces. But I am not bitter about this. Someone must tend the chains. Someone must love them into their old age and know their voices and keep them singing their deep notes in the dark. If not me, then someone like me, another middle-band soul with good ears and wet hands and a tolerance for the long quiet.

I am forty-three years old. I have been here twenty years. The city was old when I was born and will be old when I die, and in between I keep this one part of it alive, this one great length of iron that lifts the necessities up and lets them down. It is not glamorous work. It is not remembered. But it is essential, the way breathing is essential — unnoticed until it stops.

The fog is thickening outside my window. The evening shift will come soon, and I will oil the chain one last time, hand over the cabin to Marek whose ears are even better than mine, whose chain sings a perfect fourth below mine in the quiet. I will climb the wet stairs to my room on the eighth terrace, light my own lamp, make my own tea, and sit at my own window watching the fog move, feeling the building breathe around me, hearing, always hearing, the deep patient note of the chains that keep us all alive.

That is who I am in this vertical country: a listener, a tender, a necessary person doing necessary work in the middle heights, between the dark and the light, content as the moss is content, waiting for nothing but the next link to oil, the next cup of tea, the next storm to bring through safe.
