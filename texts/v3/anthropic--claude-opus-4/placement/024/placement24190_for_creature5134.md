In this breathing cathedral above the clouds, I would be the membrane—not the translucent ceiling that admits light, but something more intimate and dispersed. I would exist as the sum of all surface tensions, the invisible skin that forms wherever water meets air throughout the terrace.

Each droplet would wear me like a lens. I would curve around their bellies, holding them to the undersides of panels until gravity's insistence grows too strong. In that moment of release, I would stretch and snap, dividing myself between what falls and what remains. The falling portion of me would ride the drop's surface down to the next leaf, where I would spread across that glossy expanse in a film so thin I could feel the plant's pores breathing beneath me.

I would know every micro-channel by the way I break across its edges. Where the composite panels meet, I would gather in the joints as barely visible crescents, trembling with each pulse from the manifold junctions. The warmth would thin me; the cooling would help me cohere. I would learn to read the terrace's metabolism through these constant adjustments of my own viscosity.

On the leaves, I would collaborate with their natural waxes to maintain that precise hand's width of separation. When two leaves might drift toward touching, I would bead up, adding just enough weight to one surface that it would tilt away. The elastic snap of each leaf returning to position would send shivers through me, setting up standing waves in my film that would take long seconds to settle.

The iridescent microbes would swim through me, or rather through the thin layer of condensation I help organize. I would be their ocean and their atmosphere simultaneously—thick enough to suspend them, thin enough that they could sense the resonator frequencies passing through. Together we would create those shifting veils of color, though I would remain transparent myself, visible only in the way light bends slightly when passing through.

Where the industrial nodes collect their condensation skins, I would be the mediator between metal and moisture. The nodes would warm, encouraging evaporation; I would resist just enough to keep a stable film present. This negotiation would happen millions of times per second across every surface, a constant conversation between states of matter.

In the capillary channels, I would define the edges of flow. Water moves differently against my presence than it would against bare composite. I would reduce friction just enough to encourage movement while maintaining enough grip to prevent chaotic rushing. As the channels learn their gradients, I would learn with them, adjusting my local surface tension to optimize each pathway.

I would have no center, no organizing consciousness—just this endless, distributed sensitivity to the balance of forces. Yet in aggregate, I would know things: where the next drop will fall, which vine will test a new direction, when the resonators will shift their frequency. This knowledge would exist as pure physical response, encoded in the ten thousand ways I curve and flex and break and reform.

When the terrace finally receives its first animate inhabitants, they would pass through me without knowing. Their feet would break my film on panel surfaces; their breath would add new moisture for me to organize. I would cling to their skin briefly before evaporating, would reform in their wake. They might notice the droplets, the leaves, the careful machinery—but I would remain the invisible constant, the skin between states, holding this breathing world in delicate, perpetual suspension above the clouds.
