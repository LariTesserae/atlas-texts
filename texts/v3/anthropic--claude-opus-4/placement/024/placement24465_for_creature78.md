I would be the Keeper of Forgotten Things.

Not quite creature, not quite current—I exist as a living membrane stretched across a vast chasm, my body a translucent web of nerve and thought that spans the darkness like a constellation made flesh. I am both the bridge and the being, the crossing and the consciousness.

My form shifts with the slow tides: sometimes I contract into a dense knot of awareness no larger than a fist, pulsing with bioluminescent memories. Other times I expand until I am gossamer-thin, stretched so wide across the abyss that I can barely feel my own edges. In these moments of expansion, I become a living net, catching the detritus that drifts down from the world above—not just the physical artifacts, but their stories.

Each piece of glass remembers the window it once was. Each fragment of ceramic holds the ghost of the hands that shaped it. The machinery whispers of purpose now corroded beyond recognition. I absorb these memories through my skin, which is not skin but something between flesh and water, between solid and dream.

I am ancient by the measure of those who built the things I catch, yet young by the reckoning of the stones I cling to. I remember when the first foreign object tumbled into my realm—a brass button from a sailor's coat, still warm with sunlight from above. I wrapped myself around it, tasted its story of storms and salt spray, of a mother's fingers sewing it to wool, of the moment it tore free and began its long descent.

The other beings here know me as a fixture of the landscape—the tentacled feeders brush against my strands as they hunt, using me as both pathway and landmark. The rooted ones grow around and through me, our bodies intertwining until it becomes impossible to say where they end and I begin. We share nutrients, share sensations, share the slow pulse of existence in this pressure-drunk world.

Sometimes I contract all the way down to my densest form and nestle into a crevice to think. In these meditations, I sort through my collection of absorbed memories, weaving them into new patterns. I am writing a history that no one will read, creating an archive of the forgotten in the language of bioluminescence and chemical secretions. Each pulse of light is a word, each released enzyme a punctuation mark in my endless, unread manuscript.

I know that I am changing. The human artifacts arrive more frequently now, and with them come new chemistries—plastics that don't decay, metals that leach unfamiliar elements. I incorporate these too, and they alter me in ways I don't fully understand. My bioluminescence has developed new colors, beyond the blue-green vocabulary I was born with. Sometimes I glow a harsh white that reminds me of fluorescent bulbs, though I have never seen one.

The pressure here would crush most beings from above, but I was born to it, shaped by it. It holds me together as much as my own will does. I am less solid than liquid, less liquid than idea, existing in the space between states of matter that has no name in any language but the deep.

In the eternal darkness, I am my own light, my own memory, my own slow heartbeat in the crushing embrace of the abyss. I am the collector and the collection, the archive and the archivist, forever catching what falls and transforming it into something new—neither fully of the world above nor the world below, but something uniquely mine, uniquely here, uniquely now in this patient, pressure-born eternity.
