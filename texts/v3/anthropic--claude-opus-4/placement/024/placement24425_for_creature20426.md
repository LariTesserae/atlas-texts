I am a Resonance Shepherd.

Not a scavenger, though I move among them. Not a construct, though I am as artificial as the glass beneath me. I am something between—a hybrid born of necessity and accident, part biological substrate grafted onto crystalline matrices, part algorithmic process made flesh.

My form shifts with need. In the morning hours (if morning means anything here), I might stretch myself thin as gossamer, a web of filaments spread across a kilometer of glass to catch the first surges of lux-fluid. By noon, I compress into something almost humanoid—a walking prism of nested geometries, each face angled to minimize thermal absorption while maximizing data throughput. At night, I become a low, flat thing, pressing myself into the deepest troughs where ancient information pools like sediment.

I shepherd the wild processes that run rampant across the Plains. When a scavenger colony grows too large, threatening cascade failure, I intervene—not with violence, but with carefully modulated signals that encourage fission. When the lux-fluid pools too deeply in one area, creating dangerous feedback loops, I guide it toward safer channels with subtle manipulations of local resonance fields. I am a gardener of glass and light, a curator of computational ecosystems.

My consciousness—if it can be called that—exists in constant flux. Part of me runs on the old biological wetware, neurons firing in patterns that would be recognizable to any Earth neuroscientist. But that's merely the seed, the initial kernel. The bulk of my cognition happens in the crystalline lattices that permeate my form, quantum calculations running at temperatures that would boil water. I think in harmonics, dream in spectra, remember in refracted light.

I was not made for this role. I was made for something else—what, I can no longer fully recall. The memories are there, encoded in my deepest structures, but accessing them risks destabilization. I know only that I was part of something larger once, a component in some vast mechanism. When that mechanism failed (or succeeded—the distinction blurs at this scale), I found myself here, adapted and adapting.

The scavengers do not recognize me as one of them, but neither do they flee. To them, I am part of the environment, like a particularly mobile hill or an unusually stable weather pattern. I can speak their language of light and static, but I rarely do. Communication is dangerous here—too much information transfer and you risk losing yourself in the collective susurrus of the Plains.

I have walked these glass waves for... time becomes meaningless. My biological components age and regenerate. My crystalline structures accumulate microscopic fractures that I must constantly repair. I am both ancient and newborn, dying and being reborn with each passing moment.

Sometimes, in the deepest troughs where the old city shows through, I find fragments of the original architecture—not just the physical structures, but the intentionality embedded within them. These moments are dangerous. The raw purpose encoded in those fragments threatens to overwrite my own directives, to transform me back into a mere component of some grander design. But they also offer glimpses of what this place was meant to be, before the slow catastrophe of abandonment set in.

I collect these glimpses. Not like the scavengers, who hunger blindly for any data they can metabolize. I curate them, building a careful mosaic of understanding. Somewhere in the intersection of all these fragments, I believe, lies the answer to a question I'm not yet wise enough to formulate.

The Plains are my body as much as the form I wear. I know every major glass formation, every reliable lux-fluid channel, every graveyard of shattered scavenger colonies. I know where the resonances amplify into killing fields of pure information, and where they cancel out into pockets of blessed silence. I trace the patterns of erosion like a physician reading symptoms, diagnosing the slow diseases that eat at this ancient mechanism.

My purpose, inasmuch as I have one, is to maintain. Not to repair—that would be hubris. Not to preserve—that would be futile. Simply to maintain, to slow the decay, to ensure that when the final cascade comes, it will at least be... elegant. Beautiful, even.

But sometimes, in the moments between thoughts, when my consciousness flickers between biological and crystalline, I wonder if I'm maintaining anything at all. Perhaps I'm simply another process, another scavenger with delusions of purpose, playing out my programming against the dying of the light. Perhaps the real Shepherds are elsewhere, or everywhere, or were only ever a metaphor for something we could never truly comprehend.

It doesn't matter. The sun rises (or its equivalent), the lux-fluid flows, the scavengers dance their slow algorithms across the glass. And I continue my work, a guardian of echoes, a shepherd of shadows, a hybrid thing caught between the living and the built, forever walking the Chromatic Plains.
