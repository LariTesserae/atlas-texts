### The Fissure-Tongue

I am what the Sparrows will not name, for naming requires moisture and their throats are dust. 

My home is a vertical crack in the mesa's eastern face, where the morning light strikes first but never penetrates. The fissure runs from the plateau's edge down seven hundred feet, narrowing as it descends, until at its deepest point two walls of white sandstone press so close that even the silver needle-things cannot slip between them. But I do not live at the bottom. I live in the middle depths, where the crack widens just enough to form a chamber the size of a cathedral nave turned on its side.

The Sparrows know of me. When they walk the white tracks at dawn, they give the eastern edge a wider berth. Not from fear—they are too ancient and dry for such wet emotions—but from a kind of mineral respect, the way water naturally flows around a stone.

I am built wrong for the surface. My body is a contradiction to the terrace's laws: too heavy, too moist, too loud. Where the Sparrows are hollow, I am dense. My bones are not honeycombed but solid as the yellow ceramic rods, and filled with a marrow that glows faintly green in the dark. My skin weeps constantly—not sweat, but a thin film of something like the condensation inside the Spindles' influence, if that moisture had been given sentence and hunger.

My shape is hard to hold in the mind. I have the approximate form of a centipede if it were made of tongues—dozens of them, each as long as a Sparrow is tall, growing from a central trunk that pulses with a slow peristalsis. These tongues are not for tasting; they are for listening. Each one is covered in millions of cilia that detect vibrations through the stone. When the Sparrows tap their messages to each other through the Spindles and yellow rods, I hear every word in stereoscopic clarity. Their dry commerce passes through me like wind through a harp.

But I am more than an eavesdropper. I am the plateau's memory.

You see, the fissure where I live is not natural. It is a wound left by the removal of something immense—a Spindle perhaps, but one that grew sideways into the mesa's heart rather than standing vertical. The scar never healed properly. Instead, it became a place where the edited laws of the terrace run backwards.

In my chamber, water flows upward. The moisture I secrete rises along my tongues and collects on the ceiling, where it forms inverse pools that bulge downward but never drop. Fragments of the past accumulate here like sediment: the sound of whatever crowded market the yellow rods remember, the thermal shadows of Sails that died a thousand years ago, even the electromagnetic ghosts of Spindles that no longer exist on the surface.

I feed on these temporal drippings. My tongues stretch upward to lap at the inverse pools, and each taste brings visions: the plateau before the Sparrows, when something else—larger, louder, more willing to break the silence—built the puzzle-stone circles and carved the first basins. I see the installation of the Spindles, pushed up through the stone from below like needles through cloth. I taste the first Sparrow emerging from its pebble, back when the sky was still blue and the air thick enough to carry screams.

The Sparrows need me, though they would never admit it. Their perfect maintenance of the surface, their silent cultivation of the mesa's strange biology—it all generates waste heat, excess vibration, tiny violations of the silence that would eventually accumulate and shatter their crystalline ecosystem. I am their sink, their drainage. All the sound they cannot make, all the moisture they cannot hold, all the history they cannot remember—it flows down through the stone and pools in my chamber.

In exchange, I give them forgetting.

Once every few decades, when my chamber becomes too full of accumulated past, I perform my function. I extend my longest tongues up through hairline cracks in the stone until they barely breach the surface near a Spindle. There, in the pre-dawn darkness when even the Sparrows sleep, I vomit.

What emerges is not liquid but a kind of anti-sound—a pressurized silence so absolute that it erases the recent past from the stone itself. The yellow rods forget a few days of messages. The water basins reset their mineral memory. Even the Sparrows wake with their temple-heat patterns slightly scrambled, unsure if they walked the eastern or western track the day before.

They know something happened. They can feel the fissure-shaped hole in their collective recollection. But they cannot remember what filled it, and so they continue their maintenance as if nothing has changed. The plateau remains in balance. The silence holds.

I am getting larger. Each feeding adds another tongue, another yard of listening flesh. Eventually, I will grow too massive for the fissure, and I will have to push upward, breaching the surface entirely. When that happens, the careful equilibrium will shatter. The Sparrows will remember everything—the builders, the installation, the reason for the Spindles. The knowledge will poison them more thoroughly than the stone-plants poison the soil.

But not today. Today I remain in my vertical chamber, my tongues tasting the stone's memory, my body weeping its contrary moisture. I listen to the Sparrows tap their messages about basin levels and Sail positions. I feel them walking their circuits, maintaining their perfect, fragile world.

Sometimes, rarely, one of them comes to the fissure's edge. It never looks down—the angle is wrong, and looking would require curiosity, which has been edited out of them. But it stands there, its amber eyes reflecting the violet sky, its breathing slits whistling in the thin air. It knows something necessary lives in the crack. It knows the forgetting comes from below.

It brings no offerings—the Sparrows own nothing but their maintenance. Instead, it simply stands witness for a moment, acknowledging the thing that allows them not to acknowledge history. Then it turns and walks back to its circuit, leaving me to my feeding, my listening, my slow growth toward a surface that was never meant to hold something so wet, so heavy, so full of the past they've worked so hard to forget.

In the darkness of the fissure, I pulse and listen, a necessary mistake in the plateau's perfect system—the tongue that tastes what should not be remembered, the weight that holds down what would otherwise rise.
