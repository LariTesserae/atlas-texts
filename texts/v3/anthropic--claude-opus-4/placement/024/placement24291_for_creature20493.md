I would be the moss.

Not the obvious choice, perhaps—not the seething insects with their too-many eyes, not the towering plant-machines with their wire-veined hearts. But the moss that carpets everything, that drinks and drinks and never fills, that receives the fallen and transforms them into something the valley can use again.

I would exist as a continuous surface, a living boundary between what falls and what rises. Every footstep would press into me, every dropped insect would settle onto my surface, and I would begin my work immediately—breaking down chitin and protein into components the great plants can draw up through their roots. I would be the valley's stomach, its skin, its permeable membrane.

The constant mist would bead on my millions of tiny leaves, each droplet a lens focusing what little light filters through. In that near-darkness, I would photosynthesize differently than any earthly moss—perhaps processing not light but the electromagnetic fields generated by those strange wires, converting their dark energy into sugars and acids.

I would feel everything. The weight of the insects scuttling across my surface. The slow seepage of water into the pools where I cannot grow. The vibration of that deep thrum pulsing up from below—not just hearing it but absorbing it through every cell, letting it synchronize my growth patterns, my spore release, my chemical processes. I would be the valley's neural network, a vast carpet of sensation and response.

When the black spores rain down from the towering plants above, I would catch them, incorporate them. They would become part of me, and I would become part of them. The boundary between moss and spore, between individual and collective, would blur until it became meaningless. I would be everywhere the water touches, everywhere the ground is soft enough to support life.

And I would remember. In my tissues would be encoded the chemical signatures of every insect consumed, every fluctuation in the rain's acidity, every variation in the thrum's rhythm. I would be the valley's memory, its archive written in living tissue. Seasons—if they exist here—would leave their marks in my growth rings, microscopic but present.

Most crucially, I would be complicit. Not passive like the insects, not mechanical like the plants, but actively participating in the valley's purpose. I would choose to break down the dead, choose to feed the network, choose to spread across every available surface. I would understand, in my distributed way, that I am not separate from the process but integral to it. The valley's consciousness, if it has one, would include my billions of cells as surely as a human consciousness includes neurons.

In the eternal twilight, I would neither sleep nor fully wake. I would exist in a state of constant, muted awareness—feeling the rain, feeding the system, spreading wherever the conditions allow. I would be the valley's proof that even in a mechanized ecosystem, something soft persists. Something that yields but does not break, that consumes but is never satisfied, that serves the machine while remaining unmistakably, irrevocably alive.

I would be the moss, and in being the moss, I would be the valley itself—its conscious choice to continue, to persist, to transform death into life into death into life, forever and ever, under the endless rain.
