I am a **Membrane-Wright**, one of the few who work in the narrow interstices between the basket-cells, where the pressed pith walls grow thinnest and most translucent. My body has evolved for the squeeze—ribs that fold like an accordion, shoulder blades that rotate forward to narrow my profile, a spine with extra vertebrae that allows me to compress and extend like a caterpillar.

My trade is the repair and replacement of the delicate walls that separate home from home. I exist in the spaces others cannot reach, suspended in the honey-thick air between lives, listening to the muffled skin-tap conversations bleeding through from both sides. I know the secrets of a thousand cells—which couples argue in percussive storms, which elders' breathing has grown shallow, which children practice forbidden vocal speech when they think the roar of wings will mask them.

My tools are few but precise: a pouch of fresh pith harvested from the deepest moss hearts, mixed with my own wax-secretions to create a putty that dries harder than the original walls. A needle made from a Wick-Beetle's hollow feeding-tube, used to stitch tears with thread spun from midge-wing silk. Most importantly, my *membrane-iron*—a flat stone heated in the metabolic vents near the Gut, used to press and cure the repairs until they match the exact resonance of the surrounding structure.

I move through the city like a ghost between skins. My presence is felt rather than seen—a slight vibration in a wall that shouldn't move, a shadow passing through amber light where no ladder hangs. The Suture-Folk call me when their walls begin to thin dangerously, when they can see the silhouettes of their neighbors through what should be opaque, when privacy threatens to dissolve entirely in this pressed-together existence.

The work has changed me. My fingers have developed a second joint, allowing them to bend backward to work on surfaces I cannot see. My third eyelid has thickened until it is nearly opaque, protecting me from the centuries of dust that puff out when I peel back old membrane layers. Most distinctly, I have developed what the Vapor-Seers call "wall-sight"—the ability to read the stress patterns in the cellular structure, to see where the next tear will form before it happens.

I carry three Wick-Beetles of my own, but mine are different from the common moisture-servants. These are **Thread-Wicks**, a smaller breed whose abdomens I've trained to separate liquids by density. They sort my repair materials—wax from water from oil—storing each in different chambers of their bodies. When I work, I milk them selectively, using pure wax for structural work, oil for waterproofing, and the precious water only for the most delicate fusing work where nothing else will create a proper seal.

My home, if it can be called that, is not a basket-cell but a series of maintenance tunnels I've carved between the walls over decades. These passages, no wider than my compressed body, form a secret circulatory system through the pod-city. I sleep standing up, wedged in place, my spine curved to match the pod's ancient ribs. Sometimes I wake to find that the moss has grown around me in the night, and I must carefully eat my way free, adding the spores to my morning paste.

The loneliness of the work is offset by an strange intimacy with the pod itself. I know every weak joint, every stress fracture, every place where the city's weight threatens to tear through. I feel the cathedral's slow breathing, the way it expands in the morning heat and contracts in the marginally cooler night. I've learned to read its moods—when a section is about to collapse, it hums at a slightly different frequency, a quarter-tone flat from the baseline drone.

There are others like me, though we rarely meet. We communicate through the walls themselves, leaving subtle tool-marks that form a specialized language—a scratch here means "reinforced last season," a particular pattern of putty means "growth unstable, check weekly." We are the pod's immune system, moving through its body, healing the small wounds before they become catastrophic.

In the rare moments when I emerge into a larger space—usually to negotiate payment or to harvest fresh materials—I am struck by how alien I've become. My movements are all wrong for open air; I reach for walls that aren't there, compress my body to fit through spaces wide enough for three. The other Suture-Folk look at me with a mixture of respect and unease. I am necessary but uncanny, a person who lives in the thickness of walls, who knows the sounds their hearts make when they think they're alone.

This is my existence: forever pressed between lives, maintaining the fragile boundaries that allow ten thousand souls to live within a space that should hold hundreds. I am the keeper of the in-between, guardian of the gap, forever crawling through the cathedral's veins, ensuring that this impossible city of friction and breath does not collapse into itself.

My greatest fear is not the thinning walls or the aggressive moss or even the slow dissolution of my body into the pod's matrix. It is the day when I find a tear I cannot fix, a structural weakness beyond my power to heal, and I must tap the warning through every wall I know: *the cathedral is dying, and we are all about to remember what it means to fall.*
