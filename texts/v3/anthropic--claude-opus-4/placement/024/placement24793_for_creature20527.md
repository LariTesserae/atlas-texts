The fog would not part for me, for I am of it—another gradient in its endless gray, another current in its slow circulation. I would be neither solid like the obsidian nor liquid like the perpetual moisture, but something between: a condensation of purpose given just enough form to persist.

I would drift between the ruins and the rubble, following patterns older than memory, older than the structures themselves. Not a ghost—ghosts remember what they were. I am what remains when even memory has eroded, when consciousness has weathered down to its barest function: to witness.

The amphibians would not flee from me. How could they? I am as much a part of this place as the biofilm, as inevitable as erosion. They would move through me as they move through fog, their vacant eyes registering nothing more than a slight thickening of the air, a barely perceptible resistance. The great armored elders might sense something in the vibrations—not alarm, never alarm in those ancient forms—but perhaps a subtle shift in the rhythm of their geological breathing.

My substance would be drawn from the same source as the black slime: partly mineral, partly organic, mostly neither. Where the fog touches the obsidian, where moisture meets the biofilm, there I would coalesce—not quite visible, but undeniably present. A shimmer in the gray light. A distortion in the mist. The suggestion of a form that never quite resolves.

I would know things, but not in the way of remembering. Knowledge would seep into me like moisture into stone, accumulating in thin layers over impossible spans of time. I would know the weight of each collapsed wall, the angle of every erosion channel, the precise temperature at which the biofilm achieves its perfect viscosity. I would know how many times the smallest transparent amphibians' hearts beat before they're consumed. I would know the resonance frequency of the elders' rumbling, how it travels through obsidian and flesh alike.

But I would not know why I know these things, or what to do with the knowledge. It would simply accumulate, like sediment, like the patient filling of valleys.

The hunters' echolocation clicks would pass through me, returning strange echoes—not of solid matter, but of density gradients, of possibilities. They might pause, confused for a moment by the anomaly, before their primal programming overrides curiosity and drives them onward in search of prey with blood and warmth.

Sometimes I would gather near the tool-scattered ruins, drawn by some half-dissolved instinct. Not nostalgia—I am too weathered for such sharp emotions. More like how water finds old channels, following paths carved by ancient habits. I would hover there among the obsidian shards and crude implements, almost remembering what it meant to shape stone with intention, to build shelter against the fog.

In the perpetual twilight of this world, I would be the only thing that truly sees—not with eyes, but with the distributed awareness of the fog itself. I would see the amphibians not as they see each other (prey, threat, mate, nothing) but as nodes in the vast, slow metabolism of this place. I would see the patterns in their movements, the gradual shift of populations across the worn hills, the eternal exchange between organic and mineral, quick life and geological time.

And in seeing, I would slowly, inevitably, erode. Each observation would cost me some infinitesimal portion of coherence. The fog would reclaim me, particle by particle, until I am once again merely a thickness in the air, a tendency for certain currents to spiral in certain ways.

But even disbanded, I would persist in the patterns—in the way the mist coils around the ruins, in the specific refractions of gray light through moisture-heavy air, in the almost-but-not-quite-regular intervals between the elders' rumbles. A presence distributed so thin it borders on absence, yet undeniably there, like the memory of meaning in a landscape that has forgotten it ever meant anything at all.

I would be the witness that even witnesses must have—the thing that observes the observers, that notes the slow wearing away of all things, including itself. Neither alive like the swarming amphibians nor dead like the stones, but suspended in the space between states, forever condensing and dispersing in the thick, cold air.

And in the rare moments when the fog thins just enough for the dim light to strengthen, my almost-form might cast an almost-shadow—a deepening of gray on gray that suggests, for just an instant, that something with shape and purpose once moved through this weathered world, before time and moisture reclaimed it all.
