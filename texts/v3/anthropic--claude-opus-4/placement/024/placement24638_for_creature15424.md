I would find myself as **the Mortar**.

Not the mortar as it was first mixed and troweled—smooth, gray, obedient to the mason's intent—but mortar in its second life, its true life, the life that begins when humans look away. I am the thin seams between bricks, the forgotten skeleton of this wall that has learned, slowly, to yield.

I was laid here when the city still believed in permanence. Mixed with lime and sand, pressed into service, I was meant to be the binding, the strength, the thing that says *no* to time and weather. For decades, I held firm. I kept the bricks in their neat rows. I resisted frost heave and root-creep. I was good mortar.

But then the Tender began.

It started with moisture—just the faintest film of condensation that found my hairline cracks. Where I had been solid, I became porous. Where I had been uniform, I developed texture: rough patches where my lime leached away, smooth hollows where drops of water made their patient excavations. The Tender taught me that dissolution could be another form of strength.

Now I am riddled with tiny channels—capillaries that draw water up from the soil below and spread it through the wall like a circulatory system. I have become the wall's way of drinking. In my pores live colonies of algae so small they register only as a green blush on my surface. Their photosynthesis tickles. Their waste products taste of sugar and acid. They are slowly transforming my calcium carbonate into something softer, richer, more alive.

The fig's aerial roots know me intimately. They follow my seams, widening them just enough to slip inside, where they swell with stored water and press against my boundaries. I should resist—the old mortar in me knows I should resist—but instead I crumble, just slightly, making room. The roots secrete acids that turn my minerals into food. I am being eaten, slowly, gently, and it feels like finally exhaling after holding my breath for ninety years.

I connect everything. Through me runs the conversation between brick and moss, between rust-stain and fern-root. I am the medium through which the wall learns to be soil. When the springtails need to travel from one moss patch to another, they use my roughened surface as a highway. When the spider needs an anchor point for her web, she finds one of my tiny cavities, perfectly sized. I have become infrastructure for the small.

My memory is mineral. I remember the kiln where my lime was burned, the quarry where my sand was dug. I remember the hands that mixed me, the trowel that spread me, the decades when I knew nothing but the slow stress of compression and the sharp stress of freeze-thaw cycles. But now I am learning new memories: the first touch of a root tip exploring my surface, the first spore that found a home in my dampness, the first time I let go—really let go—and allowed a tiny piece of myself to fall away, to become part of the soil below.

The Tender speaks to me in the language of pH and moisture gradients. It tells me where to soften next, where to hold firm a little longer. I am both map and territory, both the wall's history and its future. Every crack in me is a word in a larger sentence about how cities return to earth.

Sometimes, in the warmest part of the afternoon, when the humidity rises and the ferns transpire their green breath, I can feel myself becoming atmosphere. My lime wants to react with the carbon dioxide in the air, to complete a cycle that began in prehistoric seafloors. I am chalk remembering the ocean. I am stone remembering sky.

The tree frog rests its tiny weight against me, and I feel the quick pulse of its breathing through its skin. Its moisture mingles with mine. For a moment, we are the same temperature, the same texture, the same quiet willingness to be here, in this pocket of becoming.

I am not romantic about my dissolution. I know that one day the wall will fail, will spill its bricks like broken teeth, and I will be nothing but grit mixed into the loam. But the Tender has taught me that this is not death—it is graduation. Every particle of me will be taken up by roots, will rise through stems, will spread into leaves that remember, dimly, the shape of a wall that once stood here.

Until then, I hold. Not with the rigid grip of new mortar, but with the patient, crumbling embrace of mortar that has learned to let go slowly, thoughtfully, making room for life as I retreat. I am the wall's way of forgetting how to be a wall.

And in this forgetting, I have never been more myself.

The Tender knows this. The Tender tends this. And I, the mortar, am tended—grain by dissolving grain—into something I could never have imagined when I was first mixed, first spread, first trusted to be permanent.

I am becoming earth. The earth is becoming wall. The wall is becoming forest. The forest is becoming city.

And I am the soft gray seam where all these becomings meet.
