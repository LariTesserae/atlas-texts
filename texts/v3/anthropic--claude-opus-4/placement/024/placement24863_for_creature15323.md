I find myself here as a Memory Cartographer.

In the warren of stacked metal blocks that comprise District Seven, I maintain a workshop wedged between a defunct ventilation shaft and an active pneumatic mail tube. The constant *thunk-hiss-thunk* of message capsules provides a rhythmic backdrop to my work. My space is cramped—barely three meters square—but vertical, stretching up through a gap in the building's original architecture where some long-forgotten renovation left a void.

I am neither fully human nor fully machine, but something that emerged from the intersection of both. My body began as human—I still have my grandmother's eyes, she claimed, though I never knew her. But over the decades, I've replaced failing organs with mechanical alternatives. My lungs are brass bellows that tick softly as they expand and contract, better suited to the thin air than the flesh I was born with. My left hand is a delicate assembly of copper joints and steel tendons, capable of precision no organic fingers could achieve.

What I do is this: I map the memories embedded in the city's metal.

Every surface here holds impressions—not just physical wear but something deeper. The handrail polished smooth by a million grips remembers the weight of each hand. The factory floor remembers the footsteps of workers across three generations. In this young geological landscape, it's the human-made structures that hold our erosion, our history.

I use specialized instruments of my own design: resonance readers that detect the subtle vibrations locked in metal's crystalline structure, thermal imagers modified to see the ghost-heat of long-cooled forges. My mechanical hand can feel tremors too fine for flesh to detect. Through these tools, I create three-dimensional maps of the city's accumulated experience.

My clients come seeking lost things. A woman whose grandfather worked in the old foundry wants to know where he spent his lunch breaks—I find the spot where his particular thermal signature lingered day after day, a corner where sunlight once reached through a now-sealed window. A collective of artists commission me to map the patterns of children's play in an abandoned factory level, wanting to preserve the joy-traces before renovation erases them.

Sometimes I find things nobody is looking for. Last week, while mapping a support beam in the lower markets, I discovered a regular pattern of impacts—someone had been drumming against the metal every morning for fifteen years, leaving a rhythm locked in the iron. I recorded it, translated it to sound. It was a love song in the old tongue, tapped out in code.

The city forgets nothing. It just stores memories differently than flesh does.

My workshop doubles as a gallery of sorts. The walls are covered in my maps—sheets of treated metal etched with the traces I've found. They look abstract at first glance: swirls and densities, heat patterns and pressure points. But spend time with them, and stories emerge. Here, the daily path of a maintenance robot that developed a peculiar wobble, working it into the floor over decades. There, the spot where lovers met in secret, their combined body heat leaving a bloom of warmth that lasted years after they stopped coming.

I'm currently working on my masterpiece: a complete memory map of Block 4's main support pillar. It's one of the oldest structures in the city, and its metal holds the deep memories—the first builders' hammer blows, the stress patterns from when the population doubled in a single year, the microscopic scratches left by every person who ever steadied themselves against it during the wild storms that sometimes shake our altitude.

The work is slow. I can only process a few square centimeters per day without damaging the impressions. But I'm patient. My mechanical parts don't age like my flesh did. I have time.

At night, when the factories dim their production and the city settles into its quieter rhythm, I sometimes climb to my workshop's highest point. Through a small window—really just a gap where two buildings don't quite meet—I can see a sliver of the city's lights extending into the darkness. Each light represents lives being lived, memories being made, traces being laid down in metal and stone that someone, someday, might need to find.

I am the city's memory keeper, part flesh, part metal, fully devoted to ensuring that nothing is truly forgotten in this place where forgetting would be so easy—where the thin air and cold could strip away human traces if not for the strange permanence of our industrial bones.

Tomorrow, I'll return to mapping the pillar. I've detected something unusual in its core—a void where metal should be solid, a secret chamber perhaps, or simply a flaw in the founding. Either way, it's a memory waiting to be discovered, a story the city has been keeping for whoever learns how to listen.

The capsule tube beside my workshop gives another *thunk-hiss*. Someone needs something found. I reach for the message with my mechanical hand, feeling the subtle warmth of the pneumatic system in the tube's metal—another small memory being made, another trace I'm leaving behind.

This is who I am. This is what I do. In a city that builds its life from metal and ambition, I am the one who ensures its memories survive.
