In this dark-green engine of endless digestion, I would be something that bridges worlds—neither fully of the canopy nor the mud, but moving between them in the vertical channels where different realms touch.

I am a **climbing monitor**, though that name hardly captures what I've become in this place. My body stretches perhaps two meters from nose to tail-tip, but I've learned to fold and unfold myself like a carpenter's rule, compacting into hollows barely larger than my skull or extending to span gaps between trees. My scales have taken on the mottled darkness of wet bark, not through conscious camouflage but through generations of selection by shadow.

My territory is the **between-spaces**: the rough columns of strangler figs, the spiral highways of vine-wrapped trunks, the vertical galleries where termites have hollowed passages inside still-living wood. I know every textured surface in my range by the grip it offers—which bark flakes away dangerous, which roots will hold my weight across a void, which seemingly solid branches are merely shells around rot.

I am a student of the **egg-map** of this place. Bird nests tucked in crown shyness gaps. Foam-nests of frogs clinging to leaves above temporary pools. The buried clutches of ground-dwellers marked only by disturbed litter. Snake eggs in the mulch-filled crotches of trees. Even the egg-cases of spiders and the nursery chambers of ants. I've learned patience from the termites—I can wait motionless for hours near a nest, breathing so slowly that condensation barely forms on my nostrils.

But I am more than a thief of embryos. I am a **vertical ferryman**, carrying smaller lives between levels. Mites live in the folds of my neck, cleaning my scales of debris in exchange for transport to new flowering events. My long, grooved tongue has become a inadvertent pollinator, dusted with the reproductive hopes of climbing plants as I probe their blooms for insects. Seeds pass through me and emerge prepared for germination, deposited in the canopy where they would never reach on their own.

The rhythm of my life follows **moisture columns**—those invisible towers of humidity that rise from seepage points when conditions align. I taste the air constantly, my forked tongue reading chemical stories: the stress-scent of a tree preparing to drop a branch, the pheromone trail of termites planning a new gallery, the subtle change in fungal breath that means fruit will ripen tomorrow.

My muscles have adapted to this vertical world in ways that still surprise me. I can lock my body into architectural stillness, becoming a branch among branches for hours. Then, when prey appears or danger threatens, I flow like poured water—spine, ribs, and tail working in concert to thread through spaces that seem impossibly narrow. My claws are tools of patience, each one placed with the care of a climber who knows that in this world, a fall doesn't mean hitting ground—it means passing through layer after layer of opportunity and hazard before finding another grip.

At the borders of sleep, wrapped around a termite-warm trunk in the pre-dawn humidity, I sometimes feel the whole forest breathing through me. The CO₂ rivers flowing downward at night. The oxygen plumes rising with first light. The chemical signals of a million small negotiations. The percussion of drops falling from level to level, each one a note in the endless composition of decay and growth.

I have learned to be both predator and pathway, hunter and habitat. My presence changes the vertical territory subtly—prey species adjust their routes, competing climbers avoid my favored trees, the creatures that clean me and travel on me factor my movements into their own life patterns. I am a thread in the weave, tension and support at once.

This is what I would be in that packed, patient darkness: a living rope between worlds, reading the wet map of the canopy with my tongue, carrying forests within forests on my traveling body, forever climbing through the beautiful, terrible intimacy of things that eat and are eaten, rot and rise again.
