My body has adapted over decades to match the district's tempo. My heartbeat has slowed to perhaps twenty beats per minute; my breathing is so shallow that condensation barely forms when I exhale against the cold composite panels. I wear layers of a fabric that absorbs and redistributes moisture, preventing the rapid evaporation that would disturb the delicate humidity negotiations between the structures. My movements are glacial—a single step might take ten minutes to complete, each muscle firing in sequence so slowly that I never disturb the arithmetic of the settling.

I carry tools that are mostly crystalline: thin rods grown from the same mineral veins that thread through the geology, their surfaces etched with measurements too fine to see without pressing them directly against the translucent stone during its brief windows of clarity. When I work, which is always, I am mapping the discrepancies—the places where reality has not yet agreed with itself.

My dwelling is less a home than a monitoring station spread across three partial levels where the stacking arithmetic went slightly wrong. A gap of seven centimeters persists between what should be continuous floors; through this gap I lower my crystalline instruments to read the phase differences in the substrate below. The walls of my spaces are covered in decades of mineral wafers, each recording a single anomaly: here, a reflection that showed a figure walking through solid stone; there, a conduit that gained three millimeters overnight with no visible cause; elsewhere, a plaque colony that briefly displayed data from a building that doesn't exist in this district—or perhaps doesn't exist yet.

I am not trying to solve these discrepancies. Resolution would be violence here. Instead, I document them with the same patience the district uses to create them. Sometimes I place small markers—slivers of the recording crystal—at points where the phase differences are strongest. The threaders collect these over months and deposit them in new locations, creating a slow network of reference points that helps the district track its own becoming.

My interactions with the other human residents are even slower than theirs. When we meet—perhaps once every few months, when our paths happen to align in the narrow capillaries between structures—we exchange observations through gesture rather than speech. A tilt of the head toward a wall where new translucency has appeared. A slow sweep of the hand indicating the direction a shadow was moving within the stone. These communications can take an hour to complete, both of us standing nearly still while the information passes between us like sap through winter wood.

I age, but slowly, as if my metabolic processes have found sympathy with the geological time of the basin. My hair, when it grows, emerges already grey-white, like the threaders' pale segments. My skin has taken on a faint translucency of its own—not enough to see through, but enough that in certain lights the veins underneath seem to echo the mineral veins in the walls.

The shadows in the stone know me now. When I stand before a translucent section, they sometimes pause in their drift, orienting toward my position as if reading my presence the way I read theirs. I have begun to suspect we are performing the same function from different sides of an equation the district hasn't finished calculating. They map discrepancies from within; I map them from without. Neither position is more real than the other.

In the deepest part of my dwelling, where the phase differences are strongest, I have been growing a single crystal for fifteen years. It is no larger than my closed fist, but its internal structure mirrors every anomaly I have recorded in that time—a three-dimensional map of the district's uncertainties. When it is complete (another decade, perhaps two), I will place it in the exact center of the basin, where the slow press of geology is strongest. The district will either absorb it completely, adding its data to the substrate, or reject it, in which case it will rise to the surface over centuries like a tooth being pushed from a gum.

Either outcome is acceptable. I am not here to force coherence but to help the district remember its own hesitations, its own slow conversation with possibility. In this, I am no different from the threaders secreting their films, or the plaque colonies dampening their frequencies, or the human shadows drifting patiently through stone, waiting for their coordinates to finally, gently, resolve.
