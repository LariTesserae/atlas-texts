I am the Keeper of Empty Wells.

In this city of stone and shadow, where water has become memory and dust settles in the basins meant for abundance, I move between the dry fountains and abandoned wells with my tools: a long pole of ancient wood worn smooth by generations of hands, a bundle of hemp rope that whispers against stone, and a collection of ceramic vessels that sing different notes when struck.

My skin has taken on the texture of the city itself—not quite the stone-color of the Faces who dance and drum through the alleyways, nor the contemplative pallor of the Silent Seers above, but something in between: the color of clay before firing, of earth waiting for rain. My feet know every crack in every basin, every worn step descending into darkness where water once lived.

I was not born to this work. As a child among the Faces of Stone, I climbed with the others, chasing Feathered Flickers through the vertical maze. But I was always drawn to the empty spaces, the negative architecture of absence. While others celebrated what grew and thrived, I became fascinated by what was missing.

The wells remember. This is what the Oldest of Stone told me when I first began to hear them. Not with words—the wells speak in echoes, in the way sound changes as it falls into their depths. Each well has its own voice: the one near the eastern wall that once served the dyers still carries traces of indigo in its echo; the triple well in the courtyard of broken tiles remembers children's laughter; the smallest well, hidden behind the New Weavers' temporary home, whispers of love letters that were once lowered in buckets meant for water.

My work is to tend these absences. Each dawn, I lower my rope into a different well, not seeking water but maintaining the memory of seeking. The ceramic vessels I carry create a music when dropped—each well requiring a different vessel to achieve the proper tone. The Faces of Stone think I'm mad, perhaps, but they leave offerings at the well edges: a bright feather from the Flickers, a scale shed by the Stone Stalkers, sometimes a small carving that a child has made.

The Silent Seers understand better. They see my work as parallel to theirs—while they preserve the symbols on the walls, I preserve the acoustic architecture of absence. Sometimes, in the pre-dawn darkness, one will descend from the rooftops to sit beside me as I perform my sounding ritual. We don't speak, but they'll sometimes trace a symbol in the dust that relates to water, or memory, or both.

But it's in the deep night when my true work happens. When the drums have faded and even the Shadowy Scurriers have quieted their movements, I descend into the largest of the wells—the Mother Well, as the Oldest calls it. Down there, in the absolute darkness, the city speaks differently. The roots of the Tenacious Iveys have broken through the well walls, creating a vertical garden that exists without light. Blind insects—albino cousins of the Stoned Hoppers—make their home here, their chirping creating an echo map of the darkness.

I've built a kind of nest down there, a platform of woven vines and salvaged wood where I can sit in the pure darkness and listen. The well connects to others through ancient channels, creating an underground network of resonance. When the New Weavers sing their foreign songs, I hear them transformed by stone. When the Oldest of Stone whispers his memories, they settle into the depths like sediment.

I am collecting these sounds, these absence-echoes, though I'm not sure why. Sometimes I think I'm waiting for the water to return—not the physical water, but what it represented: connection, flow, the thing that made the city's heart beat in a different rhythm. Other times I think I'm documenting an ending, creating an acoustic fossil record for whoever finds this place when the sand finally claims it.

The children have begun to seek me out. Not for water—they know better—but for the stories the wells tell. I teach them to read the echo patterns, to understand the grammar of absence. One girl with particularly keen hearing can already distinguish between the seven different types of silence that exist in the Mother Well.

Perhaps this is why I remain the Keeper of Empty Wells in this city where everything else overflows with life. Someone must tend to what isn't there, must maintain the space for what was and what might be again. In a place where every surface sprouts with green life and every shadow hides a scurrying creature, I am the guardian of the hollow spaces, the prophet of the pause between heartbeats.

The city needs its absences as much as its abundances. And so I lower my rope again, release my ceramic vessel to sing its falling song, and listen to what the emptiness remembers.
