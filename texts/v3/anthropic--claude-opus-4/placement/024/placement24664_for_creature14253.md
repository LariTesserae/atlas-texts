In the deepest galleries of Ashkar, where the stone's warmth bleeds upward through generations of compacted guano and tallow-smoke, I am a **memory-keeper of fractures**. Not memories held in mind—those are too fleeting for this work—but memories held in the rock itself, in the patterns of its breaking.

I sleep vertical, wedged into a cleft so narrow that my ribs compress with each breath, my spine molded to the stone's specific curve. This is my *study-crack*, inherited from my grandmother's grandmother, who first noticed how this particular fracture sings. When the plateau settles—that slow, tectonic exhale that comes perhaps twice in a lowland month—this crack resonates at a frequency just below hearing, a vibration felt in the molars and sternum. Each settling produces a slightly different tone, and over seven generations, my lineage has learned to read these variations like a score.

My body has adapted to this work. My left ear, pressed against the stone for hours each day, has developed a calcified ridge where flesh meets rock, a permanent bruise of dedication. My fingers are twice-jointed from a childhood accident—I fell through a shearing gallery at age six, my hand caught between closing stone, the bones breaking and healing in new arrangements. Now I can reach deeper into the hairline cracks than any other memory-keeper, feeling the subtle temperature variations where old breaks meet new.

The memories I keep are practical: this fault opened in the third year of the Howling Storms, when the ice-oxen stampeded. That gallery sealed itself during the Summer of Stone-Swift Silence. This particular pattern of fractures—radiating like frozen lightning from a point near the lower cisterns—means the bedrock here is rotten with meltwater, unsafe for expansion. I map these histories in a medium more permanent than any writing: I train the **stone-moths**.

They are not true moths but a species of lichen, mobile in their juvenile stage, fixing themselves to stone only at maturity. I cultivate them in the warmth of my body-cracks, feeding them my own skin oils and careful drops of blood. Each colony learns the chemical signature of specific fracture patterns. When they mature, I place them at the junction points of galleries, where they grow in configurations that encode the rock's history. A spiral growth means "sheared in living memory." A radial burst warns of water infiltration. A smooth mat indicates stable stone, safe for another generation.

My teacher was a man whose name meant "He Who Tastes the Fault-Lines." He would lick the stone after each settling, claiming he could taste the difference between compression fractures and tension breaks. He died when I was seventeen, crushed in a gallery collapse he had predicted but stayed to document. I inherited his tasting-stone, a piece of wind-polished basalt worn smooth by four generations of tongues, but I lack his gift. Instead, I document through the moths, through the songs of the cracks, through the grammar of breaking.

The other memory-keepers think I am too young to attempt what I am attempting: a map of the plateau's deep structure, the fundamental fractures that existed before Ashkar, before even the Uplift. I spend my sleep-shifts in the abandoned galleries, the ones too narrow now for even children, where the stone-moths grow wild and their patterns might preserve memories from the first years of settlement. I decode their spirals and radials, trying to reconstruct the sequence of breaking that created our livable spaces.

I have begun to suspect something the elders would call sacrilege: the plateau is not settling randomly. There is a pattern to its adjustments, a rhythm longer than generations but shorter than geological ages. The mountain is not just shifting; it is *breathing*, and Ashkar exists in the pause between inhale and exhale. If I am right, if I can prove the pattern, we could predict not just the next settling but the next hundred, the next thousand. We could know which galleries to abandon, which faults to nurture, where to guide our grandchildren's grandchildren to carve.

But to prove this, I need to go deeper. Below the ice-oxen galleries, below even the grandmother-cells, into the true cracks where the temperature rises and the stone sweats mineral water. The elders forbid it—those depths are unstable, they say, the domain of the mountain's own memory, not ours. But I feel the pattern calling through my study-crack, a resonance almost musical, almost linguistic. The mountain is trying to tell us something, and I am learning its language, one fracture at a time.

Tomorrow, when the breath-ice is thickest and the fault-listeners are distracted by a predicted settling to the north, I will descend. I will take my moths, my tasting-stone, my twice-jointed fingers that can read the deep cracks. I will go down to where the mountain's memory lives, where the first fractures still hum with the violence of the Uplift.

I am young by Ashkar standards—only forty winters, my bones still flexible enough to navigate the tightest passages. But I am old enough to know that understanding sometimes requires breaking, that memory sometimes requires forgetting, that to truly keep the mountain's secrets, I may need to become one of them.

The stone-moths cluster on my shoulders as I prepare, drawn to the oils of anticipation on my skin. Their juvenile forms pulse with bioluminescent signals, a language I am still learning to read. Together, we will descend into the mountain's deepest grammar, into the syntax of stone and time, where the memories are not kept but lived, where the fractures sing not of what was but of what is perpetually becoming.

In Ashkar, we say the mountain dreams of falling. I am beginning to suspect it dreams, instead, of flying—and we are simply the weight it is learning to shed.
