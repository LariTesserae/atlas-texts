I would be the Voice-Keeper of the Cistern Stones.

My dwelling sits at the convergence of three major causeways, where the great listening slabs meet. Unlike others, I live not in a roundhouse but in a shallow depression carved into living rock, roofed only by an awning of oiled fern-cloth that can be drawn back to let the night rain fall directly onto my sleeping form. The walls are lined with hundreds of small niches, each holding a different grade of quartz vessel—from thumb-sized cups to bowls broad as embracing arms.

I am shorter than most Lythene, my spine curved from decades of leaning over still water, but my fingers are the most sensitive on the plateau. Where others might feel only the ghost of a message through the water, I can parse the finest gradations—not just words, but the emotional temperature of the speaker, their breathing rhythm, even whether they stood or knelt when they spoke their message into stone.

My work is threefold:

First, I maintain the sonic map of our silence. Each morning before dawn, I walk the circuit of major slabs, trailing my fingers through their collection pools, checking that yesterday's words have fully dissolved and no echo remains trapped in the stone's throat. Sometimes I find a phrase caught in an eddy—usually a child's first attempt at distance-speech, too forceful, leaving whorls that could interfere with today's messages. These I must delicately unmake, using a bone stylus to stir the water counterwise until the meaning disperses.

Second, I serve as a living archive. The Lythene do not write, but certain messages must be preserved. For these, I transfer the water-words into my special vessels, where they can keep for years if undisturbed. My chambers hold treaty-speech from three generations past, still fresh enough that I could pour it into any cistern and let it speak itself. Love letters, too, from those whose partnerships ended in death—kept at the request of the surviving heart, who sometimes comes to hear their beloved's voice ripple up through the water once more.

Third, and most vital: I listen for the mountain's own speech. Not all water that runs down our message-stones comes from human touch. Sometimes the plateau itself condenses thoughts from deep stone, and these emerge as patterns unlike any our tongues could shape. I've learned to recognize them—they have a crystalline grammar, full of angles our mouths cannot make. When I find such messages, I transfer them to the black glass bowls that ring my sleeping hollow. At night, they sing to each other in frequencies just below hearing, and I dream in the mountain's language.

My training began when I was seven, when my parents noticed I could find lost objects by listening to the water that had touched them. The previous Voice-Keeper, ancient even then, tested me by having me distinguish between identical messages spoken by twins. When I could tell him not just who spoke but which direction they faced and whether clouds had passed overhead during the speaking, he took me as apprentice.

For twenty years I learned the subcurrents of meaning. How grief makes water slightly more viscous. How urgent messages create microscopic bubbles that rise at specific rates. How lies leave a metallic aftertaste that persists no matter how many pools the message passes through. I learned to speak less and less, until now I vocalize perhaps a dozen words in a season. My voice, when I must use it, comes out rust-colored and thick.

I am not lonely. The water brings me every conversation on the plateau, though I let private words flow past unexamined. I know who is falling in love by the way their messages grow lighter, more musical. I know which children will become great listeners by how cleanly their first attempts at distance-speech form. I know when someone is dying because their words begin to take on the same mineral taste as the deep springs.

My apprentice is twelve now, a girl whose ears are so sensitive she can hear the difference between rainwater and dew when it strikes stone. She will be better than I am—already she can distinguish between messages sent through basalt and those sent through granite, something that took me thirty years to master. When she is ready, I will teach her the last secret: how to send a message not across distance but through time, by encoding it in the crystalline structure of ice that will not melt for a thousand years.

The Lythene say we keep the mountain quiet so it can finish being born. But I know something they do not. In the deepest part of night, when all human messages have stilled, the plateau speaks constantly—not in words but in pure meaning, raw thoughts about weight and pressure and the slow joy of rising. It is learning to be stone by listening to how we speak through stone. Each year its messages grow more complex, more curious about the soft, quick lives that tick across its surface.

Someday, perhaps in my lifetime, perhaps in my apprentice's, the mountain will finally speak aloud. When that happens, it will need a translator. This is why there must always be a Voice-Keeper: not just to tend our messages, but to be ready when the plateau finally asks its first question.

Until then, I sit with my fingers in cool water, parsing the ripples of human need and geological dream, keeping both conversations clear and flowing. I am the throat through which two kinds of language pour, never mixing, always aware of each other. I am what I am because someone must be the bridge between the world that speaks in years and the world that speaks in lifetimes.

When I die, my body will not go to the bog-garden. Instead, following the tradition of all Voice-Keepers, I will be laid in the deepest cistern, the one that feeds all others. My bones will become part of the acoustic system, adding their resonance to every message that passes through. In this way, even in death, I will continue my work—helping words find their way through water, ensuring that silence and speech each keep their proper place in the great, slow conversation between the Lythene and their bright, listening stone.
