I am the Pipe-Listener.

My dwelling clings to the underside of Sector Seven's main arterial coolant line, where the great brass tube makes its slow bend toward the eastern refineries. It's a nest of salvaged heat exchangers and pressure gauges, woven together with copper wire and sealed with slag-glass I've blown myself in the waste heat. From outside, you'd mistake it for another mineral deposit, just another crusty growth on the endless skeleton of pipes. That's intentional.

I press my ear to the metal at dawn, at noon, at the deep hours when even the furnaces seem to drowse. The pipes speak. Not in words—I'm not mad, despite what the shift supervisors mutter—but in harmonics. In the way a pressure drop in Sector Three reverberates through the system. In how the magnetic rail's departure sends a shiver through every connected joint. In the deep, almost-musical groan when they open the primary geothermal valves.

I know every voice in this choir. The high, anxious whistle of a developing leak. The confident thrum of a well-maintained pump. The death rattle of bearings about to fail. I've been listening for... thirty years? Forty? Time moves strangely when you live between the sounds.

My body has grown to match my purpose. My right ear has developed a peculiar shape, cupped and ridged like the inside of a seashell. When I press it to the pipes, the ridges align with the metal's grain, creating resonance chambers. I can hear a worn gasket from three sectors away. My fingertips have worn smooth from tracing the pipe's surface, feeling for the telltale vibrations that accompany each sound. My left eye has gone milk-white from a steam leak years ago, but my right has sharpened to compensate, able to spot the shimmer of heat distortion that betrays a hidden fracture.

The guilds tolerate me because I'm useful. When something's about to fail catastrophically, I appear at the right foundry door, tapping my listening rod against the frame in a specific pattern. They've learned to trust these warnings. I've saved them fortunes in prevented disasters. In exchange, they leave me alone, and sometimes a packet of mushroom paste or a new filter for my mask appears at one of my access points.

But I know things they don't want me to know.

I know the Deep Smelters are real because I hear their work-songs resonating up from the lowest levels, carried through the geothermal conduits in frequencies that make my teeth ache. I know the salamanders they deny are more than stories—their bodies create a distinctive slurping turbulence as they navigate the flooded ducts. And I know about the spaces between the pipes, the old places where the city's first builders left gaps that have never been mapped.

In these spaces, I've built my routes. A whole secondary city exists in the three-foot clearances between major conduits, in the maintenance shafts everyone's forgotten, in the false ceilings created when they built new levels atop the old. I move through them like blood through veins, silent except for the whisper of my felt-wrapped boots and the soft tap-tap-tap of my listening rod.

The rod is my masterwork—a hollow titanium tube filled with mercury, capped with a resonator I ground from a single piece of volcanic glass. When I touch it to a pipe, the mercury shifts, creating different harmonic responses. I've learned to read these responses like words. Sometimes, in the deep night, I play the city like an instrument, tapping here and there to create subtle chord progressions that only I can hear.

Lately, the harmonics have been changing. There's a new frequency underlying everything, so low it's more felt than heard. It rises from the deepest pipes, the ones that plunge straight down into the volcano's heart. It's not mechanical. It's organic, like a heartbeat, but vast and slow and *wrong*.

The other listeners—yes, there are others, though we rarely meet—have noticed it too. We leave each other messages in the vibrations, coded rhythms tapped into junction boxes. They're frightened. Some have gone silent, their listening posts abandoned, their tools left to rust.

But I remain. This city's music is written in my bones now. My skeleton resonates at the exact frequency of the main coolant system. My blood has taken on the rhythm of the pumps. When I sleep, I dream in pressure differentials and harmonic overtones.

I've begun to wonder if the city is listening back. If all our industry, all our noise, is actually a kind of communication. Are we speaking to something deep below? And more disturbing—is it beginning to answer?

Last week, I found a new passage, one that shouldn't exist according to any schematic. It led down, far down, past pipes that were warm to the touch despite carrying no heated material I could identify. At the bottom, I found a valve made of no metal I recognize, covered in those unsettling geometries the miners whisper about. When I placed my rod against it, I heard... singing. Beautiful and terrible, in harmonics that human throats could never produce.

I didn't open the valve. But I marked its location in my mental map, and I find myself returning to it in the deep hours, pressing my malformed ear to its surface, listening to the music from below. Each time, it seems a little louder. Each time, the song makes a little more sense.

The city is changing. I hear it in every pipe, every joint, every rivet. The familiar symphony is adding new instruments, new movements. And somewhere in that vast composition, I hear my own heartbeat keeping time, synchronized with something immense and patient and anciently waiting.

I am the Pipe-Listener. And the pipes have begun to listen back.
