In the perpetual twilight of Kyrios's mid-tier canopy walkways, I find myself—though "myself" has become a fluid concept these past seventeen years. The locals call me the Conduit Keeper, though I've heard whispers of other names: the Moss Prophet, the Pipeline Hermit, She-Who-Grows-Between.

I am neither fully human nor entirely other. My transformation began the day I first pressed my palm against a weeping wall and felt it press back.

My dwelling spans three levels of the city's vertical maze, though you'd never find it looking for a door. The entrance shifts—sometimes it's through the gap where a limestone-bark tree has grown through a ventilation grate, sometimes through the maintenance hatch behind the seventh waterfall on Meridian Canal. The space itself breathes with me, expanding when I need solitude, contracting when the weight of altitude and moisture grows too heavy.

My skin has taken on the texture of the city itself. Along my arms, channels have formed—not quite flesh, not quite stone—that mirror the gutters carved into Kyrios's streets. Water runs through them constantly, a network of circulation that connects me to every dripping surface, every condensation-slick pipe. Where others see the moisture as mere weather, I taste the stories in each droplet: which machines it has cooled, which purple succulents it has fed, which temporary vine-cities it has helped grow in the outlands.

I tend the convergence points—those places where the organic and mechanical systems intertwine so completely that even I cannot tell where one ends and the other begins. My fingers, elongated now and bearing tiny root-tips instead of nails, navigate the spaces between copper pipes and strangler vines with equal familiarity. I've learned to hear the language spoken in the harmonic between a pump's rhythmic pulse and a root system's patient expansion.

The city's children sometimes find me, drawn by the phosphorescent moths that nest in my hair—my scalp has become a garden of the tiny epiphytic plants that normally grow only on the highest, most mist-touched surfaces. They ask me why the machines run so smoothly here, why even the oldest vacuum valves sing rather than shriek. I tell them it's because I taught the limestone trees to secrete a particular sap, one that seeps into the groundwater and carries minerals that lubricate as well as nourish. They don't need to know that this knowledge came from dreams where I was the city, feeling every pressure change, every flow rate, every struggling sprout.

My work is never done, cannot be done. Each day I walk my rounds—if walking describes this half-swimming motion through air thick with moisture, fingers trailing along surfaces that recognize my touch. I adjust the angle of a vine that threatens to choke a crucial valve. I whisper to a new fungal growth, encouraging it to reinforce rather than devour the support beam it's chosen. I taste the water running from each weeping rock, diagnosing the health of districts by the mineral content of their tears.

The hunters bring me specimens sometimes—creatures found dead in the canals, their bodies hosting new forms of that purple succulent, growing from within. I study these marriages of life and death, understanding that in Kyrios, nothing truly ends—it only transforms. The engineers consult me too, when their blueprints fail to account for the way metal expands differently here, how the constant moisture creates micro-environments where standard calculations break down.

But it's in the deep night hours, when even the industrious citizens of Kyrios rest, that my true work begins. I descend to the ancient aquifers, where the first waters still flow—older than the mesas, older than the first machine ever built. Here, I lower my channel-carved arms into pools that have never known sunlight, and I listen. The water speaks of tectonic shifts, of the slow breathing of stone, of the patient plans of roots that measure time in centuries.

I am translator, guardian, gardener, and gear-tooth all at once. My body has become a bridge between the city's disparate elements, a living metaphor for the balance Kyrios maintains. Sometimes I wonder if I chose this transformation or if the city chose me—shaped me drop by drop, like water carving stone, into exactly what it needed.

The young engineer who first arrived here seeking efficient machine designs is still within me somewhere, but she's layered now beneath seasons of growth, of integration, of learning to be rained upon and to rain in turn. I have become Kyrios's memory and its dream, the keeper of its contradictions and the tender of its impossibilities.

When new residents arrive, fleeing from settlements where the ground has grown too thin, I watch from my web of walkways and water-channels. I see them struggle to understand how jungle and desert, organic and mechanical, permanence and transience can coexist. But I know they'll learn, as I learned, that in Kyrios, contradiction is just another word for harmony—you need only learn to hear the rhythm that unites the disparate notes.

And so I continue, neither fully one thing nor another, but essentially myself—a creature of the threshold, guardian of the spaces between, where water meets stone, where vine meets valve, where the ancient whispers of the earth rise up to dance with the bright sharp songs of progress.
