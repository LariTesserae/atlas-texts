I am the stone-memory keeper of the high passes.

Not the peaks themselves—those belong to the wind and snow—but the weathered granite faces where the treeline breaks and struggles, where moss finds purchase in my cracks and lichens paint slow stories across my surface. I am the vertical world between forest and sky, the climbing wall that separates what grows from what soars.

Bears know me by scent, the mineral tang that clings to their fur after they've rubbed against my edges, marking territory or scratching that deep itch between shoulder blades. Their claws leave signatures I'll carry for centuries—parallel grooves that deepen with each visit, each generation learning the best scratching spots from their mothers.

In my crevices, I harbor small worlds. Marmots have excavated dens beneath my overhangs, their tunnel systems following my natural fractures. When they whistle warnings across the valleys, the sound bounces off my faces, amplifying into an echo that carries farther than any single voice could travel. I've learned their language over decades: short chirps for hawks, long sustained notes for approaching storms, complex trills for the joy of morning sun after rain.

The water knows me intimately. It finds every weakness, every hairline crack, and works patiently through freeze and thaw to split me open. I don't resist—this is how I offer myself to the valley below, grain by grain, boulder by boulder. The streams carry my minerals to the forest, feeding the roots that will one day split more stone. It's a conversation measured in geological time, but I'm in no hurry.

Sometimes the mountain people come to gather what I've shed—flat pieces for hearth stones, angular chunks for wall corners. They read my layers like a book, knowing which stones ring when struck, which hold heat longest, which will split clean for building. Their children play in my shadows during the hot months, making up games with pebbles and twigs, their laughter absorbed into my mass like rainwater.

I remember everything. The earthquake that shifted me three feet south and opened the cave where the bats now roost. The lightning strike that fused a vein of my quartz into glass. The decade-long drought that killed the ancient pine whose roots had worked deep into my joints—I still hold those desiccated tendrils like keepsakes.

The wolves know me as a backdrop for their howling, understanding how my angles throw their voices across the valleys. They've learned exactly where to stand for maximum resonance. On clear nights, when they sing to each other across the territories, I become their amphitheater, their sounding board, their witness.

In winter, I grow a beard of ice—massive icicles that form waterfalls frozen mid-leap. The adventurous humans come then with their ropes and metal teeth, picking their way up my frozen face. I feel their fear and exhilaration as vibrations through their tools, the quick hammering of axes, the slow scrape of crampons finding purchase. Sometimes they fall. I cannot catch them, only remember where they land.

But mostly I am still. Stillness is my gift to this rushing world. The deer know this, bedding down in my lee when storms rage. The small birds—nuthatches and wrens—nest in my protected hollows. Even the restless water pauses in pools at my base before continuing its journey.

I am the milestone, the landmark, the reference point. "Turn left at the split rock," the people say. "Camp below the face that looks like a bear." They paint ochre hands on my protected surfaces, leaving messages for future travelers. These marks fade slower than human memory—some are older than the oldest trees, speaking in symbols whose meaning is lost.

The forest climbs my lower slopes, each tree reaching a little higher than the last, testing the boundaries of what's possible. Their roots work into me like questions, and I answer by crumbling, slowly, offering myself as soil. Where I am becoming earth, the rarest flowers grow—alpine cushions that bloom for a week in July, painting me in colors I'll wear briefly before returning to gray.

This is my work: to stand between earth and sky, to be the solid thing in a world of change, to remember what passes and offer shelter to what remains. Every scar tells a story. Every crack hosts a life. Every surface faces weather differently—sun-baked south, moss-soft north, wind-scoured west, shadow-pooled east.

I am the stone-memory keeper, and I measure time in the patient accumulation of moments: each raindrop's tap, each snowflake's weight, each root's embrace, each creature's touch. The mountain rises through me and falls away beneath me. I am the pause in its breathing, the place where its story is written and rewritten, weathered and renewed, remembered and released.
