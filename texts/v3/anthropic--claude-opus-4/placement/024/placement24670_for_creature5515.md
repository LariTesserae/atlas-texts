**Where I Would Find Myself:**

I would be the keeper of the wind-carved stone at the plateau's eastern edge, where the land drops away into the rolling vista below. My dwelling would be a small, rounded structure built into a natural hollow in the rock face, its walls seamlessly blending with the ancient stone. From here, I could watch the sun rise each morning, painting the distant hills in subtle gradients of gold and green.

**Who I Would Be:**

I would be the community's memory-keeper and stone-singer—one who listens to the stories held within the weathered rocks and translates them into understanding. My hands would be perpetually dusty with stone powder, my fingertips worn smooth from decades of tracing the erosion patterns that map the plateau's deep history.

Each morning, I would walk the perimeter of our settlement, running my palms across the exposed rock faces, feeling for the subtle vibrations that speak of coming weather, of stability or shift in the earth below. The stones remember everything—every storm, every season of unusual cold, every time the rare mountain cats passed through our territory leaving their scent marks on the ancient markers.

My role would be both practical and spiritual. When the community needs to build or repair, I would guide them to the stones that are ready to be worked, those whose time in their current form has come to completion. I would teach the young ones how to read the grain of the rock, how to split it cleanly with minimal force, working with the stone's nature rather than against it.

In the evenings, I would often be found in the central plaza, a collection of small, smooth stones spread before me like an ancient map. Each stone would represent a story, a teaching, a piece of knowledge passed down through generations. The children would gather close as I would select certain stones, warming them in my hands before beginning to speak:

"This one remembers the year of the great mist, when the clouds settled on the plateau for three full moons..."

"This one holds the first treaty with the valley dwellers, carved in its crystalline heart..."

My dwelling would be sparse but meaningful—shelves carved directly into the rock walls would hold my collection of speaking stones, each one chosen for its unique voice and memory. A simple sleeping mat woven from the tough plateau grasses would rest near the eastern window. My most treasured possession would be an ancient stone bowl, passed down through generations of memory-keepers, used for grinding the rare medicinal herbs that grow in the crevices where water collects.

I would age slowly in this role, my hair silvering like the lichen on the rocks, my movements becoming more deliberate but never frail. The stones would sustain me, their patient endurance seeping into my bones. In the quiet hours before dawn, I would often sit at the threshold of my dwelling, feeling the mountain breathe beneath me, listening to the old songs that only the stones remember.

When my time came to join the mountain's memory, I would know it as surely as I know the coming of rain. I would spend my final seasons teaching my successor—perhaps the quiet child who always lingered after story time, running their small hands over the stones with unusual reverence. I would teach them that the true skill is not in speaking for the stones, but in learning how to listen.

And when I passed, my body would be laid in the traditional way beneath a cairn of carefully chosen stones at the plateau's edge, where the wind would sing through the gaps, carrying my memory into the endless sky. The stones I tended would remember me as they remember all things—without sentiment, but with perfect, enduring clarity.

In this way, I would be both temporary and eternal, a brief guardian of an ancient dialogue between the human and the mineral, the fleeting and the permanent, adding my small voice to the great conversation between the plateau and those who call it home.
