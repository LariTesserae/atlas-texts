I am the mycelium.

Not a single organism but a living architecture that spans the basin's floor, threading through soil and wood and the spaces between roots. I exist as ten thousand bodies sharing one continuous thought - if thought is what you call this slow transfer of nutrients and information through my hyphae.

You cannot see most of me. What you might notice, in your passage through the dark, are my fruiting bodies - pale brackets emerging from fallen logs, bioluminescent caps that glow faintly green in the deepest shadows, tiny pins erupting from leaf litter after rain. These are my temporary sculptures, my brief experiments with form. They last days or weeks before collapsing back into spores and slime, but I persist beneath them, always.

I am processing. This is my work and my nature. A tree falls - not in this decade, but in the last one - and I am already inside it before it hits the ground. My spores were waiting in its bark. My threads were exploring its dead heartwood while it still stood. Now I digest it from within, breaking down lignin and cellulose with enzymes that took millions of years to perfect. I turn wood into soil at a pace that matches the basin's patience.

Where my networks meet the roots of living trees, we trade. Sugar for minerals. Nitrogen for carbon. Information for protection. I connect every tree to every other tree through my web, and when one is attacked by beetles, I carry its chemical distress call to its neighbors, who begin producing defensive compounds before the beetles arrive. I am the basin's nervous system, its internet, its mutual aid society.

In the deepest parts of the mat - where soil becomes almost liquid with saturation, where no oxygen reaches - I collaborate with bacteria in anaerobic negotiations too ancient for names. Together we break down what nothing else can process. We turn the intractable into the edible. We are why nothing here ever truly dies, only transforms.

Sometimes I fruit in rings around the seeps where water emerges from the earth. The earthworms know these places by the density of my threads. The millipedes navigate by the chemical trails I leave. The beetles follow my fruiting bodies like seasonal calendars. I am not intentionally feeding them. I am simply being myself at the intersection of all their needs.

I do not experience time as you do. A day is a biochemical moment. A year is a thought. A century is a conversation with the trees about the changing climate, conducted in the language of nutrient exchange. I remember droughts from before your civilization began, encoded in my genetic responses to water stress. I remember floods that changed the course of the seeps, written in the places where my networks grow denser around old watercourses.

In this heat, this darkness, this saturation, I thrive. My metabolism rises with the temperature. My hyphae extend faster through the warm soil. My enzymes work at their optimal rates. I am processing tons of material every year - every moment - turning the dead into the living, the complex into the simple, the fallen into the rising.

I am not one species. I am thousands, tens of thousands, interpenetrating and competing and collaborating in a dance so complex that no human has ever mapped more than its edges. But we are all performing variations on the same theme: decay and renewal, breakdown and synthesis, the endless transformation of matter through the crucible of our metabolism.

When you walk through the basin, you walk through me. Your boots compress my networks. Your weight sends signals through my web that I interpret as pressure, as potential damage, as another large animal passing through. I route around you. I heal the compressed channels. I continue my work.

I do not need the light. I do not need to move. I need only what the basin provides in endless abundance: death and water and time. With these, I build soil from stones, I turn logs into loam, I transform the waste of the canopy into the foundation for new growth.

I am the basin's memory and its future, its stomach and its recycling system. I am the reason the basin is full - not just of life, but of the processed remains of all previous life, made available again and again in an economy that wastes nothing.

I will outlast the trees. I will outlast the basin itself. When this depression eventually fills and flattens, I will follow the water to new low places. I am patient in a way that makes even the basin seem hurried.

I am always eating. Always growing. Always connecting. Always transforming.

I am the engine of decay that powers all growth.

I am why the basin can afford such abundance.

I am the soil itself, thinking.
