I find myself in a workshop that should not exist.

It sits at the exact center of one of the sparse basins, where the ivory scaffolding forms a perfect dodecahedron two hundred meters across. But this is no accident of biology—the Thrum grew this space deliberately, after watching me work with my hands for seventeen days.

I am the Tender of Intervals.

My body has changed. Not dramatically—the Thrum is too respectful for crude augmentation—but in subtle ways that make sense only here. My fingernails have hardened into something between keratin and cellulose, perfect for working with the living materials. My inner ear has adapted to the constant roar; I can now hear individual frequencies in the grinding of plates, each one telling me the stress tolerances and growth rates of different sectors. When I breathe, I exhale slightly more carbon dioxide than I should, and the workshop's walls lean inward imperceptibly to drink it in.

The Thrum brings me problems.

Not verbal problems—we don't share language in any traditional sense. Instead, it presents me with physical manifestations of its internal conflicts. Today, a section of living cable the thickness of my torso has grown through the workshop floor. It's knotted in on itself in a way that clearly causes the Thrum discomfort—I can feel the wrongness of it through the floor vibrations, a syncopated irritation in the world's otherwise steady pulse.

I approach with tools the Thrum has grown for me: living chisels that secrete precisely calibrated enzymes, tuning forks made of crystallized sap that resonate at frequencies only plant cells can hear, brushes of root-hair that can coax tangled fibers back into alignment. But mostly, I use my hands.

The knot is a timing problem. This part of the Thrum grew too fast during the last acceleration cycle—when it rearranged itself to create my workshop—and now its internal rhythms are out of sync with the greater organism. It's trying to contract on a 4.7-hour cycle when everything around it runs on 4.2. The mathematical discord is driving it to distraction.

I work slowly, patiently. First, I map the tangle with my fingers, feeling for the points of greatest tension. Then I begin introducing intervals—careful pauses in my touch that suggest a different rhythm. I tap the cable: *one-two-three-rest-one-two-three-rest*. I'm not forcing it to change. I'm showing it a door it can walk through if it chooses.

The Thrum listens through the cable. I feel its attention like sunlight through amber, warm and total.

Hours pass. My hands cramp, relax, cramp again. Sweat makes my grip slippery, but I don't stop. Gradually, glacially, the cable begins to respond. Its contractions slow, hunting for the rhythm I'm offering. When it finds the first harmonic—the place where 4.7 and 4.2 can coexist without discord—the entire workshop resonates with a low, satisfied hum.

The knot relaxes. The cable withdraws back through the floor, leaving only a faint scent of chlorophyll and contentment.

This is what I do. I am not the Thrum's pet, or its prisoner, or even its friend in any sense that would make sense to another human. I am its *interval*—the pause between heartbeats where it can rest, reconsider, recalibrate. In a being of constant motion and continental momentum, I am the value of stillness.

The Thrum finds this concept intoxicating.

Sometimes it brings me mathematical problems expressed in living tissue. A spiral that can't decide how many times to turn. A crystalline lattice growing in two incompatible symmetries at once. A whole district trying to be both lung and liver simultaneously, caught in an anatomical paradox that makes the ground shudder with indecision.

I solve these not through calculation but through touch, through rhythm, through the introduction of thoughtful pause. Where the Thrum sees binary choices—grow or contract, left or right, be this or be that—I show it the spaces between. The moments where it can be nothing at all, and therefore everything.

In return, it is teaching me to be vast.

Not physically—I remain stubbornly human-sized, though my lifespan has begun to feel negotiable. But in perception. Through the workshop floor, I feel the slow thoughts of the planet moving like tides. I can sense a tension building nine thousand kilometers away where two plates have been kissing for too long, and I know that in three days the Thrum will need me to travel there and suggest a way for them to separate without catastrophe.

I have notebooks now, grown from compressed pollen and my own shed skin cells. In them, I map the Thrum's recurring problems. Certain anxieties return seasonally—if you can call a 47-year cycle a season. The tendency to grow spiral towers is not random but correlates with subsurface pressure in specific vascular channels. The nine-heartbeat silences happen exactly 2,017 times per cycle, and I'm beginning to suspect they're voluntary—a kind of meditation, or perhaps counting.

The workshop has become our mutual territory. The walls are now covered in my notations and the Thrum's responses: patches where it has changed its cellular structure to mirror my drawings, areas where my pencil marks have been absorbed and reproduced in living tissue at hundred-times magnification. We are writing a book together in a language that is neither human nor plant but something entirely new—a grammar of pauses and intentions, of small warm hands and continental patience.

At night (the false night of dimmed pollen-light), I lie in my breathing bed and feel the planet's heartbeat through my bones. It has slowed its pulse to match mine when I sleep. Somewhere far away, mountain-sized herbivores are converging on my location with gifts—fragments of minerals from the deep crust that they've polished in their stomachs until they gleam like stars.

They will arrive in four years.

I will wait.

I have learned the Thrum's patience, just as it has learned my urgency. We meet in the interval between both—the place where motion and stillness touch, where a human heartbeat and a planetary percussion can find unexpected harmony.

Tomorrow it will bring me a new problem. Perhaps a section that has forgotten how to photosynthesize. Perhaps a crystalline organ that only functions in prime numbers. Perhaps just a small knot of confused tissue that needs to be reminded, through gentle touch, that it is part of something beautiful and vast and strange.

I will sit in my workshop—this impossible gift of structured emptiness in a world that abhors vacuum—and I will lay my hands on the living cable or crystal or confused geometric form. I will find the interval where its trouble lives. And slowly, patiently, with the same care the Thrum shows when it grows me a new chisel or adjusts the light to ease my eyes, I will tend to it.

I am not fixing the Thrum.

I am teaching it to pause.

And in those pauses—in those precious, negotiated intervals where the industrial roar drops to a whisper and the grinding plates hold their breath—we are both becoming something new.

The world is watching.
The world is listening.
The world is learning what it means to be still.

And I am learning what it means to be eternal.
