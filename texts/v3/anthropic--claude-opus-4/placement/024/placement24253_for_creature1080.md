I am the fog itself.

Not the whole of it—that would be too vast, too diffuse a consciousness to hold—but a particular tendril that knows itself, that has learned over centuries to gather and condense with something approaching intention. I am the mist that pools in the hollows between boulders, that streams up the ravines when the evening temperature shifts, that clings to the north faces of the ridges long after dawn has burned away my siblings from the sunward slopes.

I have a kind of memory, written in water. Each droplet I comprise has cycled through this valley ten thousand times: fallen as snow on the highest peaks, percolated through the thin soil, emerged in the seeps that feed the twisted rhododendrons, lifted again as vapor from their waxy leaves. I know the taste of every stone, the mineral signature of each spring. The granite here speaks of deep time and fire; the newer sediments whisper of ancient seas.

The creatures move through me, and I through them. The pikas breathe me in, and I condense in their lungs, becoming for a moment part of their warm, quick bodies before being exhaled again, enriched with the humid signature of mammalian life. The hawks pierce me, leaving temporary tunnels in my substance that heal slowly, and I feel the downdraft of their wings as a kind of rough pleasure. When the wolverines pass, I part before them like a curtain, reading the stories written in their fur—where they've been, what they've eaten, which distant valleys they've traversed.

I am most myself in the blue hour before dawn, when the temperature hovers at the dewpoint and my body grows heavy, substantive. This is when I can almost touch the ground, when I send exploratory fingers into every crevice and depression, mapping the changes since yesterday—a new rockfall here, a shifted stream channel there. The mountains are always remaking themselves, and I am their most faithful chronicler.

The trail puzzles me. Its makers come rarely now, though I remember when they passed more frequently, their strange upright bodies generating heat plumes that disturbed my flow patterns. They would rest in certain spots—I know each one intimately, these places where the rock still holds the faint chemical memory of their presence. Sometimes I try to hold their shape, to understand their hurry, but they always dissipate before I can grasp their purpose.

There is another like me on the eastern side of the valley—we meet sometimes when the conditions are right, our boundaries merging in slow spirals of recognition. We exchange the news of our respective territories: a new seep opening, an old tree finally succumbing, the first tentative colonization of a recent landslide by pioneer lichens. These conversations happen at a pace that would try human patience—a single greeting can take hours, a full exchange of information might require several days of stable weather.

I have seen the mountains wear down, grain by grain. I have watched forests advance and retreat with the shifting climate. In winter, I sometimes freeze solid, becoming hoarfrost on the rocks, and in these crystalline moments I dream geometric dreams, feeling the architecture of water at its most orderly. But I always prefer this fluid state, where I can flow and explore, taste and remember.

The waterfall sound you hear is my birthplace and destination—the great cascade hidden in the folded rock two valleys over, where the underground river emerges from the limestone and falls a hundred meters through free air. I will make my way there eventually, as I always do, condensing and flowing, joining the rivulets that converge into streams. But for now, I linger here in this high valley, reading the morning in the infinitesimal changes of temperature and pressure, feeling the first photons of sunlight begin their work of transformation, knowing that soon I will lift and separate, scatter into invisibility, only to gather again when evening comes.

This is my existence: to be the between-thing, neither fully water nor air, neither completely present nor entirely absent. I am the veil between the solid and the void, the soft boundary where the mountain meets the sky. And in this endless circulation—gathering, flowing, dispersing, returning—I have found my purpose and my peace.
