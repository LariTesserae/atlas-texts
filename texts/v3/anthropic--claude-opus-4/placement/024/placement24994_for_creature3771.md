In this world of vertical gardens and twilight harmonies, I find myself as one of the **Resonance Keepers** — a being that exists between the Windweavers and the Veilbloomers, part solid, part ephemeral.

My body is tall and willowy, with skin that phases between translucent membrane and bark-like texture depending on the light and my state of consciousness. Along my spine and limbs run hollow channels — living flutes carved by generations of growth — that catch the wind as I move between the spires. These channels are not merely decorative; they are my primary sensory organs, allowing me to feel the vibrations of the world in exquisite detail.

I dwell in the **Singing Hollows**, a cluster of ancient spires whose interiors have been shaped over millennia into vast resonance chambers. Here, the natural hum of the spires is amplified and modulated, creating a perpetual symphony that shifts with the wind, the growth of the plants, and the movements of creatures throughout the plateau. My dwelling is not built but grown — a small pod nestled in the junction where three great spires meet, its walls permeable to sound but not to the sparse moisture that collects here.

My purpose is to tend the acoustic health of this section of the plateau. Each morning (though morning here is merely a subtle brightening of the eternal twilight), I climb through the hollow interiors of the spires, listening for discordance. A dying branch creates a flat note; a new growth introduces an unexpected harmonic. With tools that are part of my body — fingertips that secrete growth hormones or inhibitors, palms that can smooth or roughen surfaces — I tune the spires like living instruments.

But I am more than a gardener of sound. I am a translator between species. When the Stonebound's deep machinery creates tremors that disturb the Windweavers' songs, I find the frequency that allows both to coexist. When the Veilbloomers' melodic hums grow too insistent, threatening to lure young Windweavers away from their duties, I weave counter-melodies that restore balance. Even the ancient Skybinders sometimes seek my aid, when their living archives begin to resonate at frequencies that cause the stored memories to blur and merge.

My most treasured possession is a **symbiont** — a small creature that resembles a cross between a hummingbird and a tuning fork. It lives in the largest hollow channel along my left arm, emerging only when I need to reach the highest frequencies. Together, we can create sounds that promote growth, encourage flowering, or even, in desperate times, shatter diseased sections of spire before infection spreads.

At night (the deeper darkness that passes for night), I often climb to the crown of the tallest spire in my territory. There, where the air is thinnest and the static storms occasionally flicker on the horizon, I listen to the greater symphony — the combined voice of thousands of spires stretching to the dim horizon. In those moments, I can almost hear it: the planet's own frequency, the deep bass note that underlies everything, the rhythm that keeps this world of stone and stem alive despite the absence of flowing water.

Sometimes I wonder if we Resonance Keepers were the first inhabitants of this world, or perhaps we evolved to fill a need that arose as the ecosystem grew more complex. Our oral histories, passed down through vibrational patterns encoded in our very bones, speak of a time before the spires, when the plateau was truly barren. But those histories are incomplete, full of gaps where the frequencies have been lost or corrupted.

What I know for certain is this: I am both performer and audience in this endless concert, a single note in a composition that has been playing for eons and will continue long after my channels have crumbled to dust. And in that knowledge, I find a deep contentment, a resonance with my purpose that fills me more completely than water ever could.
