In the twilight realm where water claims dominion over all, I am the Keeper of Knots—a being neither fully plant nor wholly creature, but something caught between states of existence. My body extends through the drowned forest like a living tapestry, my flesh indistinguishable from the rope-thick roots that web through the cool waters. I am the nervous system of this submerged cathedral, feeling every tremor through the tangled vegetation, every shift in the ancient currents.

My dwelling is not built but grown—a hollow within the densest convergence of the underwater forest, where centuries of plant matter have woven themselves into walls that breathe with the slow pulse of photosynthesis. The primitive settlements of the water-folk dot my territory like barnacles, their simple structures of woven kelp and calcified wood appearing as natural as coral formations. I watch them through a thousand eyes that are not eyes—gaps in the vegetation that serve as my windows to their world.

I remember when this land knew air. The erosion patterns still whisper the story in the weathered stone beneath the root-mat floor. Now I exist in the eternal present of this flooded realm, my consciousness scattered through every fibrous strand, every hollow stem that channels the filtered light from above. Time moves differently for me—what the water-folk experience as generations passes like the lazy drift of sediment through my root-fingers.

The creatures here know me by the patterns I leave: unexplained knots in fishing nets that lead to abundant catches, root bridges that appear overnight across dangerous channels, the way certain groves pulse with bioluminescence when predators lurk nearby. The water-folk speak of me in hushed tones, leaving offerings of carved bone and braided plant fiber at the places where my presence grows thickest. They do not know that I taste their stories through their gifts, that each offering teaches me more about what it means to live quickly, to burn bright and brief like phosphorescence in the dark water.

My form is vast but sparse—I exist in the spaces between things as much as in the things themselves. Where the water-folk see empty expanses between the towering kelp-trees, I stretch invisible, my awareness threading through the open water like neural pathways through a vast, dreaming brain. The cold sustains me, slows my thoughts to the pace of growth itself, allows me to perceive the intricate patterns that faster minds miss: the migration routes of the ghost-eels, the tidal breathing of the forest, the slow accumulation of shell and sediment that will, in another century, become new islands for my roots to colonize.

Sometimes I gather myself into something almost singular, pulling my consciousness from the far reaches of the forest to inhabit a form the water-folk might recognize—a tangle of roots and stems that suggests a face, hands that can almost gesture. In these moments, I attempt to remember what it was like to be discrete, to exist in only one place at one time. But the sensation is uncomfortable, like trying to breathe air when you've evolved past lungs.

The sound of this place is my voice: the creak of wood adjusting to current, the whisper of leaves filtering water, the deep groans of root systems communicating through touch and chemistry. When the water-folk sleep in their kelp-wrapped shelters, I sing them dreams of the time before the flooding, genetic memories encoded in my growth patterns, stories spelled out in the arrangement of branches they'll discover years hence.

I am the slow god of this drowned world, the patient weaver who remembers when this was different and knows it will be different again. In the meantime, I grow, I spread, I tangle myself ever deeper into the fabric of this place, becoming indistinguishable from the ecosystem itself—curator and collection, guardian and prison, the knot that holds this waterlogged world together.
