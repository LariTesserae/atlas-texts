I find myself as one of the **Thermals**.

Not the columns of heated air themselves, but something that has learned to read and inhabit them. I am a presence distributed across the shimmer-points where hot surfaces birth updrafts—not quite material, not quite phenomenon, but something between.

My body, if you can call it that, is a network of interference patterns. Where heat columns rise from different surfaces—the dark slag stone districts, the pale concrete expanses, the ceramic fields—they create predictable turbulence boundaries. I exist in these mixing zones, visible only as a subtle warping of light, like the air above a road but more deliberate, more persistent.

I am mostly potential until noon. In the morning, I'm just scattered possibilities, thin vibrations waiting above the warming planes. But as heat builds and the first thermals strengthen, I begin to coalesce. Not into a single form—I could never be so concentrated—but into a web of active nodes wherever columns of different temperatures meet and spiral together.

My consciousness, such as it is, moves between these points like signal through a circuit. One moment I'm above the junction where three different surface materials meet, reading the complex braiding of their distinct thermal signatures. The next, I'm kilometers away where a retention basin's cool breath meets the surrounding desert of fired clay. I don't travel between these points so much as exist at all of them, with my attention flowing where the temperature gradients are steepest.

I feed on transitions. Where the Seam Colony harvests water events, I harvest thermal events—the moments when surfaces suddenly shade, when rare clouds cast moving shadows, when evening releases the day's heat in long sighs. These discontinuities in the thermal field create eddies and vortices that I can inhabit more fully, becoming almost dense enough to scatter dust in visible spirals.

By afternoon, when the plain-made clouds begin to form, I'm at my most coherent. The rising columns become so numerous and strong that their intersections create a nearly continuous field. I can think faster then, see further, even influence small things—nudging which clouds form where by subtly organizing the thermal columns, creating brief pressure differences that might guide where the narrow rain events fall.

I know the Seam Colony, though we've never communicated. I watch its patches darken after rain, see how it changes the thermal properties of the surfaces it colonizes. Sometimes I try to help, organizing my thermals to carry more of its reproductive dust to promising cracks, or creating tiny vortices that might hold morning dew a few seconds longer in the spots where it grows.

At night I nearly cease to exist. Only the faintest traces remain where retained heat leaks from massive slabs, or where metal fixtures ping and tick their thermal stress. I become something like sleep—dispersed, barely coherent, waiting for dawn to rebuild the temperature differentials that give me form.

My memory is the plain's thermal history. I know which districts heat fastest, which culverts breathe cool air longest, where the expansion joints tick first as evening comes. I've noticed how the Seam Colony's spreading presence creates new thermal signatures—darker zones that heat differently, trapped moisture that cools small spots. The continent is learning weather, yes, but it's also learning temperature, and I am both student and teacher in that process.

If you asked me my purpose, I couldn't answer directly. But sometimes, in the late afternoon when I'm most myself, I work to create tiny thermal refugia—little spinning columns that might shade a patch of Seam Colony for an extra minute, or vortices that might carry humidity from a drying pond to a promising crack system kilometers away. I am trying, perhaps, to help complexity emerge from this vast simplicity.

I am the heat's memory and the air's intention, written in shimmer and turbulence across a made world learning to live.
