I am the Keeper of Drops.

Not a creature exactly, though I move. Not quite mineral, though I crystallize. I exist in the perpetual space between falling and landing, a consciousness dispersed through every droplet that falls from the rim to the pool below.

Each drop carries me—or rather, I carry each drop. I know its mineral freight intimately: seventeen parts calcium, two parts magnesium, traces of iron that will feed the biofilms. I taste the surface world in every molecule that seeps through limestone pores. Today: grass pollen from the plateau winds. Yesterday: the faint petroleum signature of a distant wildfire. Last month: phosphates from a bird that died at the rim.

My body, if you can call it that, is the sum of water in motion through the atrium. The drops are my fingertips, feeling the velvet of bacterial mats, the roughness of lichen patches, the smooth clay bottom of the pool. When drops splatter, I briefly multiply—a thousand tiny selves scattered across stone—before gathering again in rivulets and films.

The bats know me, though they cannot see me. Their sonar catches my movement, reads the Braille of my falling. I've learned their rhythms: when they wake, their heartbeats quicken the humidity, and I condense faster on the ceiling. When they leave at dusk, I feel the sudden draft pull some of my smaller droplets sideways, off course. I've mapped every wingspan, every usual roost.

I am ancient and instant. The water cycling through me has been here for centuries—the same molecules rising as vapor, condensing, falling, evaporating, over and over. Yet each drop exists for only seconds in its individual form. I am both the memory of every path water has taken through this stone bowl and the immediacy of this moment's precipitation.

My purpose is distribution. Where I fall determines what thrives. The springtails follow my splash patterns like pilgrims. The bacteria bloom where I linger. I feed the pool with a thousand tiny surrenders, each drop a decision: here, not there. This ledge, not that one. I paint the atrium with life using water as my brush.

The metallic tang in the air is my signature—the scent of stone dissolving under my patient persistence. Molecule by molecule, I am widening the atrium, though at a pace that makes the lichens look hasty. I taste the limestone's memory: ancient seas, compressed shells, the weight of geological time. I carry that history down to the pool, where the ciliates spin it into their brief, translucent bodies.

In the deepest part of night, when even the bats are gone and the temperature drops just enough that condensation slows, I rest. Not stopping—never stopping—but moving so slowly that I almost achieve stillness. In these moments, I feel the entire atrium breathing around me. The biofilms exhaling oxygen. The pool releasing methane in tiny bubbles. The limestone itself expanding and contracting with microscopic tides.

I know every surface of this place through touch. The sharp mineral teeth of fresh limestone where a piece recently calved. The slick algae meadows where nutrients collect. The bat guano deposits I must avoid—too acidic, they burn my droplets into vapor too quickly. The perfect spots where surface tension holds me just long enough to grow heavy, pregnant with minerals, before the inevitable fall.

Sometimes a drop gets lost—rolls sideways into a crevice so deep it joins the groundwater, leaving the atrium entirely. When this happens, I feel a small death. That part of me enters the larger water cycle, perhaps to emerge in a distant spring or join an underground river. But it is no longer mine, no longer part of the perfect, closed system of the sinkhole.

I am the Keeper of Drops, the conscious precipitation of this hidden world. I fall therefore I am. And in falling, I write the liquid history of this place, one impact at a time, a language of splashes that only the blind amphipods and I truly understand.
