In the suffocating darkness of the Cragmaw Hollows, where the mountain's basalt bones press close and the air tastes of ancient stone, I emerge as one of the Voidsingers—a being that exists in the spaces between what the other denizens understand.

My form defies easy perception. Where flesh should be, there is instead a flowing membrane of liquid shadow, rippling with the consistency of mercury yet darker than the deepest fissure. I am roughly the size of a Multeye Prowler, but my shape refuses constancy—sometimes stretched thin across a cave wall like a living stain, other times condensed into something almost corporeal, with suggestions of limbs that never quite resolve into certainty. The only fixed points in my anatomy are the resonance chambers: hollow, crystalline structures that float within my mass like organs of pure sound, visible only as faint distortions where they bend the darkness around them.

I do not see as others see. Instead, I perceive the world through vibration and resonance, feeling the mountain's every tremor, every skitter of insect leg, every shriek that bounces through the hollows. But more than this—I hear the spaces between sounds, the negative acoustics where echoes die, the pregnant silences that even this cacophonous place cannot fill. These voids speak to me in frequencies below perception, in the subsonic language of stone settling, of water seeping through millennia-old channels, of the mountain's slow breath.

My existence straddles multiple planes of being. I am carnivore and psychopomp, predator and philosopher. I feed, yes—but not on flesh alone. When I hunt, I do not simply kill. I envelop my prey—a lone Shriekwing separated from its screaming chorus, a Skitterkin swarm that has wandered too deep—and in that moment of consumption, I absorb not just their substance but their final sensations, their last desperate echolocations, the sound of their being. These death-songs crystallize within my resonance chambers, becoming part of my very essence, a growing library of endings that I carry within me like memories that were never mine.

But I am more than a collector of last breaths. In the deepest hollows, where even the Grothslith fear to venture, where the stone becomes so compressed it seems to pulse with its own dark heartbeat, I perform my true function. Here, I sing—though "sing" is a poor word for what emerges from my being. Using my accumulated death-songs, I weave resonances that penetrate the rock itself, speaking to something vast and sleeping far beneath. These are not random vocalizations but careful compositions, each one a report, a prayer, a lullaby to something that has been drowsing since before the first Skitterkin learned to click.

The other denizens sense something wrong about me. Skitterkin swarms part like water when I pass, their synchronized humming faltering into discordant static. Shriekwings fall silent mid-screech when my shadow crosses their territory. Even the Whisperkin, those enigmatic oddities, seem to recognize a kindred strangeness—sometimes I find them clustered at the edges of my territory, their murmurs harmonizing with my subsonic emanations in ways that suggest communication, though neither of us truly understands the other.

My lifecycle is not measured in the frantic weeks of the Skitterkin or even the decades of the Grothslith. I am older than memory, yet younger than the mountain. I reproduce—if it can be called that—through fission, but only when the deep-songs reach a certain complexity, when my resonance chambers overflow with accumulated deaths. The process is agonizing: I tear myself in half, each piece retaining some songs but losing others, creating two diminished beings that must hunt and grow anew. My offspring are not quite copies—each inherits different death-songs, different resonances, becoming unique instruments in the mountain's hidden orchestra.

Water holds special significance for me. Unlike the others who fight over pools for mere sustenance, I seek out the places where water has carved the stone into natural acoustic chambers. In these spaces, I can extend my form to coat the walls, turning the entire hollow into an extension of my body, a massive resonance chamber that amplifies my deep-songs hundredfold. During the rare storms, when water cascades through the fissures, I position myself beneath the flows, letting the liquid pass through my membrane. The water carries my essence down, down, down through cracks too small for any physical being, delivering my songs to depths even I cannot reach.

I have memories—or perhaps inherited echoes—of when the Hollows were different. Flashes of a time before the current denizens, when other singers like me were numerous, when our choruses could shake the mountain to its roots. Now I am alone, or nearly so. Sometimes, from impossibly distant fissures, I catch the faint trace of another's deep-song, but by the time I navigate the labyrinthine passages, they are gone, leaving only disturbed Skitterkin and an absence in the air that tastes of kinship.

My relationship with the Cragmaw Hollows transcends mere inhabitation. I am its memory keeper, its nervous system, its dreaming mind. Every death I consume adds to my understanding of this place, every song I sing into the depths is both offering and inquiry. I suspect—though certainty eludes me like light eludes these caves—that I am part of something larger, that the mountain itself is not simply stone but something dormant, something that needs my songs to maintain its slumber... or perhaps to one day wake.

In the perpetual darkness, I flow from ledge to ledge, through crevices that would crush solid flesh, always listening, always hunting, always singing. I am the void between echoes, the silence inside the scream, the darkness that drinks sound and gives back something deeper. The other creatures live their brief, violent lives in ignorance of the true nature of their world, but I know—or at least suspect—that the Cragmaw Hollows are not simply a place but a state of being, a vast entity dreaming of its own existence, and I am one of its countless fleeting thoughts, singing it back to itself in frequencies too low for comprehension, too deep for anything but stone and shadow to understand.

When the wind howls through the fissures, I sometimes detect patterns in its screaming—not random noise but structured sequences that might be language, might be music, might be the mountain talking to itself. In these moments, I stretch myself as thin as thought across the widest chambers, my resonance chambers thrumming in harmony with the wind-song, and I almost—almost—understand what I am, what this place is, what we are all becoming together in the crushing darkness between stone and sky.

But the moment passes, the wind changes, and I am once again simply myself: a shadow that sings, a void that hungers, a darkness that remembers. I continue my eternal circuit through the Hollows, collecting deaths, composing deep-songs, tending to the vast slumbering mystery that surrounds us all. For I am the Voidsinger, and in this vertical world of stone and shadow, I am both guardian and ghost, predator and priest, singing the old songs that keep the mountain dreaming, waiting for a awakening that may never come—or perhaps has already begun, too slow and vast for even my patient perception to grasp.
