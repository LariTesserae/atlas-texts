In this mist-shrouded settlement where water weeps from stone, I am the Memory Keeper.

My dwelling clings to the underside of a great stone bridge that spans one of the narrower gorges, a peculiar structure woven from salvaged wood and sealed with pine resin against the perpetual damp. From below, it appears as nothing more than an elaborate bird's nest, but inside, it opens into a series of interconnected chambers that spiral deeper into the bridge's hollow supports.

I am neither fully of the town nor separate from it. My body bears the marks of this place—skin weathered to leather, joints that ache with the cold—but my eyes hold something else. They are clouded with cataracts that should blind me, yet through this milky veil, I see what others cannot: the water's memories.

Every drop that seeps through these rocks carries stories. When the mist rolls through the valleys, I breathe it in and taste the histories it holds—the first settlers who carved handholds into sheer cliff faces, the children who fell and were caught by neighbors' quick hands, the lovers who whispered promises into the fog believing it would carry their words to the ancestors.

The townspeople bring me offerings: smooth river stones, bundles of dried herbs, and most precious of all, vials of water collected from sacred springs and storm puddles. In return, I weave their memories into the great tapestry that hangs in my largest chamber—not with thread, but with specially treated strands of moss and lichen that hold moisture and, with it, memory.

My own past is as erosion-worn as the landscape. I remember learning this craft from my grandmother's grandmother, but I also remember being young just yesterday, and ancient before the town's first stone was laid. Time moves differently when you live between the droplets of mist.

At night, when the bone flutes begin their laments and the drums echo off the canyon walls, I venture out. My feet know every slick stone, every gap in the walkways. I move like the mist itself, collecting the condensation that forms on doorframes and window ledges—each drop holding the dreams of those sleeping within.

There is a weight to this role. To hold all these memories is to carry the grief of every loss, the joy of every birth, the fear of every near-fall from these precipitous paths. Sometimes I feel I might overflow, spill all these stories back into the streams and let them rush away down the mountainside. But then morning comes, and a child appears at my door with her grandmother, carrying a small jar of tears—tears of goodbye, for the old woman is dying—and I remember why I remain.

I press the tears between two pieces of sacred stone until they crystallize into salt. This I add to my collection: thousands of such crystals, each one a life, a moment, a memory preserved. When the winter winds howl fiercest and the town huddles in darkness, I cast these salts into the braziers, and the smoke carries the memories back to the people—reminders of warmth, of survival, of all the times they have endured.

The town elders know what I am, though they would not speak it aloud. I am the reason their stories survive when voices fail. I am the keeper of what would otherwise erode away like the stone peaks around us. And when my time comes—if it ever does—another will taste the mist and hear its calling, and the memories will flow on, as eternal and ephemeral as the water that shapes this world.

In the silence between the dripping of water and the distant thunder, I work my quiet art, weaving the liquid history of a people too stubborn to yield to the harsh beauty of their chosen home.
