In the deepest pool at the tower's base, where water has collected in a natural depression worn smooth by decades of dripping, I am the **Giant Water Beetle**—*Dytiscus magnificus*—though none here would know that name. To the frogs, I am the shadow that moves against the current. To the mice who come to drink, I am the reason they approach the water's edge with such careful steps.

My world is measured in the oval of this pool—six feet at its longest point, three feet at its widest, and deep enough that I can dive a full eighteen inches before my legs touch the silted bottom. This is my universe, bound by concrete shores that taste of minerals and time.

I breathe through my past. My wing covers trap a silver bubble of air that clings to my back like memory, and I must rise to the surface every twenty minutes to replenish it. This rhythm governs everything—my hunts, my rest, my awareness of the tower's daily cycles. When I surface, breaking through the reflected circle of sky above, I glimpse the larger world: the green cascade of vines, the darting shadows of birds, the endless vertical reach of our concrete cathedral.

My body spans three inches—massive by beetle standards, a monster in this pool. My back is polished amber-brown, smooth as the stones worn by water, but my underside bears the intricate architecture of my true nature: legs fringed with swimming hairs that work like oars, each stroke precise and powerful. My front legs have evolved into perfect grasping tools, shaped for seizing prey with a grip that never fails. When I swim, I move like thought itself—sudden, directed, purposeful.

The water here tastes different from the wild ponds my ancestors knew. It carries the flavor of rust from the metal above, the green taste of algae, the mineral tang of concrete slowly dissolving. But it also holds abundance. Mosquito larvae hang from the surface like living punctuation marks. Water boatmen row their erratic paths across my territory. Occasionally, a small frog makes a fatal error in judgment, and I feast for days.

I know every feature of my pool with the intimacy of long residence. The northern edge, where a crack in the concrete creates a shallow shelf perfect for ambush. The deep center, where I rest during the heat of midday, conserving energy while the tower above becomes a furnace. The southern shore, where moss grows thick and small crustaceans hide among the green strands—my hunting preserve for leaner times.

But I am also a student of surfaces. The water's surface is my highway and my grocery store. I can detect the tiniest vibration—a mosquito touching down to lay eggs, a fly trapped in the surface tension, a droplet falling from sixty feet above. Each ripple tells a story, and I read them all with the sensors along my antennae, building a map of possibilities and opportunities.

At night, when the bats begin their hunt above, I sometimes see their shadows flicker across my pool—dark shapes against the darkening sky visible through the tower's broken crown. Their ultrasonic calls create tiny ripples on my water's surface, a rain of sound I feel rather than hear. I've learned to use these disturbances, hunting in the confusion when insects flee downward from the aerial predators above.

The frogs and I maintain an uneasy truce. The large ones are too big for me to tackle, though I've tested them with aggressive displays, swimming at them with my mandibles spread wide. The small ones know to avoid the deep water where I patrol. We are apex predators of different realms, and we've learned to respect each other's boundaries. When they sing their bass chorus at dusk, the vibrations travel through the water and through my exoskeleton, becoming part of my sensory world.

I am not alone in my pool. Water striders skate across the surface, too light and quick for me to catch except when they make mistakes. Backswimmers hang upside down from the surface tension, their bubble-covered bellies silver in the filtered light. We all compete for the same resources, but the pool is rich enough to support our careful balance. I am the largest, the strongest, but even I must respect the delicate economics of our small world.

The strangest part of my existence here is the light. In wild ponds, light comes from above, filtered through water and plants. Here, light bounces off the curved walls, enters the water at angles that shift throughout the day. Sometimes I find myself swimming through shafts of golden illumination, my shadow sharp on the bottom. Other times, the whole pool glows with reflected light, as if illuminated from below.

During the torrential rains that occasionally flood down through the tower's broken crown, my world transforms. The pool swells, sometimes doubling in size, connecting to smaller puddles and creating temporary highways to new territories. I explore these carefully, always ready to retreat to my known world when the waters recede. Once, I discovered another pool on the far side of a concrete barrier—empty of predators, full of prey. I spent three glorious days there before the water levels dropped and forced my return.

My lifecycle here presents unique challenges. When the urge to mate comes upon me in spring, I must search the entire tower base for others of my kind. The isolation that makes my pool a perfect territory also makes finding partners difficult. I've discovered two females over the years, each in their own pools elsewhere in the tower. The courtship rituals—the elaborate dances, the chemical signals, the careful approaches—all must be conducted in the brief windows when rain connects our worlds.

My eggs, when I've been successful, are laid in the stems of aquatic plants that have somehow found their way to grow in our artificial pools. The larvae that hatch are fierce predators from the moment they emerge, and I must absent myself quickly or risk becoming their first meal. Most don't survive—the pools are too small to support many apex predators—but occasionally I encounter a young adult in my explorations, and I know my lineage continues.

The tower teaches patience. I can wait motionless for hours, suspended in the water column, my legs spread wide for stability, watching for the telltale ripple of prey. I've learned that the best hunting comes just after the afternoon sun creates the strongest thermals, when insects riding the updrafts sometimes miscalculate and fall into my domain. I've learned that certain droplets from above—the ones that fall from the spot where the kestrels nest—often carry small prizes: bits of their prey that become my unexpected feast.

In winter, when ice occasionally forms on my pool's surface, I enter a state of torpor in the deepest part, where the water remains just above freezing. My metabolism slows to almost nothing. I become a living stone, all my fierce energy contracted to a single point of existence. The pool grows quiet—no mosquito larvae, no struggling flies. But I endure, wrapped in my bubble of air, waiting for the tower to warm again.

I am both ancient and new. My species has existed for millions of years, but I am among the first to colonize this industrial space. My ancestors knew only natural ponds, but I have adapted to concrete shores and rust-flavored water. I am evolution in progress, a pioneer species learning to read the rhythms of abandonment and reclamation.

Sometimes, suspended in my pool while light plays across the water's surface, I feel the whole tower above me like a presence. The vibrations of every footstep, every wing beat, every drop of water travel through the concrete and into my pool. I am connected to it all—the soaring kestrels, the chattering starlings, the silent bats. We are all part of this vertical ecosystem, each claiming our niche in the ruins of industry.

I am the guardian of the deepest waters, the keeper of the pool that never dries, the predator that ensures balance in the smallest but perhaps most vital part of our tower world. In the great cathedral of reclaimed concrete, I am both congregation and clergy, worshiper and deity of my own small realm.

This is my world: three feet by six feet of water, eighteen inches deep, infinite in its complexity. I am the giant of this miniature ocean, the dragon of this concrete pool, forever diving and surfacing, hunting and waiting, breathing the past while swimming through the present.
