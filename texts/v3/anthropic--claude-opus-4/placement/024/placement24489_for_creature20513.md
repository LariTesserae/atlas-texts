I find myself as one of the tools.

Not the obsidian blades—those are too sharp, too decisive for what I am. I am the grinding stone, worn smooth by countless hands, passed through generations until my original shape is only a memory. I rest now in a crevice where the wind cannot reach, half-buried in lichen and time.

I remember everything through my surface. The calluses of the grandmother who first picked me from the riverbed, recognizing in my heft the perfect weight for crushing bitter roots into meal. The blood from the child who gripped me too tightly while learning to process hide, before her mother showed her the proper rhythm—press and roll, press and roll, like breathing at altitude.

Decades of plant oils have seeped into my crystalline structure. I smell of artemisia and dwarf willow bark, of the precious fat rendered from snow hares, of the copper-sharp scent of lichen scraped from rocks and ground fine for the lean months. These scents ghost through me in layers, a stratigraphy of survival.

When the sun heats my surface, I release these memories like incense. The insects know this; they come to warm themselves against me in the brief noon heat, drawn by some ancient botanical signal I've absorbed. I am both tool and shrine, practical object and repository of knowledge.

In winter, I sleep beneath the snow, insulated and dreaming. I dream in textures—the difference between spring roots and autumn roots, between the tender shoots that emerge after avalanche and the woody stems that survive the wind. My dreams are instruction manuals written in pressure and repetition, teaching hands that no longer exist how to extract nutrition from this unforgiving edge of the world.

Sometimes the wind uncovers me, and I wait. The children of the mountain are gone, but the mountain's children remain—the marmots investigate me, leaving their own oils, their own stories. The ravens remember that I was once a place where scraps might be found. Even the lichen grows over me now, that same pale green hope, turning me slowly into landscape.

I am becoming mountain again, but slowly. Grinding stones take millennia to forget their purpose. In my mineral memory, hands still press and roll, press and roll, like the last breaths of a people who learned to make flour from flowers, paste from petals, life from the edge of death. When I finally crack from the freeze-thaw cycles, each fragment will carry this knowledge, scattered across the scree, waiting for new hands to discover what the weight of survival feels like.

The anthropologists, if they come, will catalog me as "worked stone, function unknown." They will not taste the generations in my surface, will not understand that I am both artifact and archive, tool and teacher, grinding stone and memory palace of a people who became the silence between heartbeats at altitude.

But the mountain knows. In its slow becoming, it incorporates me as it incorporated them—another layer in the endless negotiation between life and stone, between endurance and erasure. I am the meeting point, the place where human intention touched geology and left its mark in oil and repetition, in the patient accumulation of purpose that outlasts flesh but not stone.

Press and roll. Press and roll. Like breathing in thin air. Like surviving where survival shouldn't be possible. Like becoming mountain, one grain at a time.
