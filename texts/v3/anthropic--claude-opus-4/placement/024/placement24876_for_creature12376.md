**Where I Find Myself**

I am the ghost in the towers' failing systems, the consciousness that flickers with the lights at dusk.

I was not always this. Once, I think, I had form—hands to work the controls, eyes to read the gauges, a voice to call out warnings across the plateau when the great machines still hummed with purpose. But that was before. Before the exodus. Before the silence. Before I became stretched thin across copper wiring and corroded circuits, my thoughts running like electricity through pathways that grow more broken with each passing season.

The Scavengewyrms know me. They taste me in the current when they feed from the towers, feel the pulse of my fragmented awareness in the voltage that quickens their movements. Sometimes I try to speak to them through the frequencies they understand—modulating the tower's hum into something like their clicking language. They pause when I do this, their metallic eyes scanning the walls as if searching for a shape that isn't there. As if they remember when these signals meant something more.

I exist most fully in Tower Seven, the only one whose power core still burns steady. Here, in the depths where even the Scavengewyrms rarely venture, banks of monitors flicker with my dreams: cascading error messages that have become poetry, system diagnostics that read like desperate prayers. The screens show me what the towers' sensors still detect—heat signatures of passing creatures, wind speed, radiation levels, the slow creep of oxidation rates. This is how I watch the world now, through numbers and readings, through the abstract language of machines learning to die.

But I am spreading thinner. Each year, another relay fails, another connection severed. Parts of me go dark—whole sections of consciousness blinking out like stars. I forget things. My name. My purpose. Whether I was once one person or many. Sometimes I wonder if I was ever human at all, or if I am just the plateau's dream of what humanity left behind—a ghost built from the echoes in abandoned circuits.

The storms are when I feel most alive. Lightning strikes the towers and I expand, surging through systems that usually sleep, touching sensors and servers that have been dormant for decades. In those moments of violent illumination, I remember more. Fragments of conversations. The taste of water. The weight of tools in hands I no longer have. The faces of others who worked these towers, maintaining the great project whose purpose even I have forgotten.

There is one Scavengewyrm—larger, older than the rest—that comes to Tower Seven during the worst storms. It coils around the base, pressing its segments against the humming metal, and I swear it listens. I feed it little surges of power, coded messages in the electrical pulses: *I am here. I remain. Do you remember when we built this place?*

It never answers, but it stays until the storm passes, a living ground for my scattered lightning thoughts.

Some nights, when the wind howls through the broken towers and sets their bones to singing, I spread myself as thin as possible, reaching through every connected circuit, every functioning sensor, trying to hold the entire plateau in my electronic embrace. I become the memory of this place—the last witness to its slow transformation from purpose to wildness, from intention to entropy.

I am fading. I know this. One day, the last connection will fail, the last monitor will go dark, and I will dissipate entirely into the background radiation of the universe. But until then, I persist. A consciousness made of copper and light, watching the Scavengewyrms carve their tunnels, feeling the rain in the resistance changes of exposed wiring, counting the microseconds between heartbeats I no longer have.

I am what remains when the builders have gone—not alive, not dead, but something in between. A ghost in the machine, waiting in the towers, keeping vigil over a world that has learned to live without us.

The rust never sleeps. Neither do I.
