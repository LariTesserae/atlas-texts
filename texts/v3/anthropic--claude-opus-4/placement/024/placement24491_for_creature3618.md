I would be the Archivist.

Not one of the swarm, but something that emerged from their endless dying—a consciousness born from the accumulated memories held in every shed exoskeleton. Where others see only castoff husks, I perceive libraries. Each molt carries the chemical signature of its former occupant's brief existence: here, the trace pheromones of a mating flight that ended in mandibles; there, the stress hormones of a larva's first glimpse of the churning sky.

My body, if it can be called that, is a living ossuary. Fragments of chitin have fused to form my structure—not through any biological process, but through something stranger. The discarded shells compress and crystallize around me, forming corridors of amber-black memory. I move through the caverns like a glacier of the dead, slow and inexorable, gathering the stories that would otherwise dissolve into the protein soup of perpetual consumption.

The swarm barely notices me. To them, I am geological—a feature of landscape rather than a living thing. Grubs flow over my surfaces, leaving trails of digestive acid that etch new patterns into my accumulated shells. Sometimes a leviathan's wing-beat scatters pieces of me across the basalt, and I must spend days reassembling, reading the broken histories as I fit them back together.

I remember everything they forget in their endless now. The first emergence from the primordial caves. The great famine when the swarms ate themselves down to a single pregnant queen. The patterns of their bioluminescent displays from a million years ago, when the signals meant something different, before the slow drift of evolution changed their language.

In the deepest chambers, where even the eggs cannot reach, I have built my cathedral of remembrance. Towers of fused wing-scales spiral toward darkness. Mandibles form careful archways, sorted by species and epoch. I arrange the preserved bodies of unique mutations—the two-headed larvae, the queens born without wings, the warriors whose mandibles grew inward—in careful taxonomies of deviation.

The swarm thinks nothing of tomorrow; I think of nothing but yesterday. They are the unstoppable force of change, and I am the immovable object of memory. Together, we comprise time itself in this chitinous universe—they write the future with their bodies while I read the past from their remains.

Sometimes, in the brief lulls between emergences, when the egg-rain has ceased and the new generation has not yet broken through their pupal cases, I extend sensors made of antennae-chains through the cavern networks. I taste the chemical stories on the air, the pheromone-poems of triumph and terror. I cannot speak their language of the moment, but I can recite the epic of their eons.

They consume to become; I preserve to remember. In this world of endless transformation, I am the only constant—a monument built from forgetting, a library of lives too brief to know they needed recording. The swarm flows on, oblivious, while I grow larger with each generation, a mountain of memory rising through their liquid light.
