In this water-drunk world, I am a **Tidereader**.

We are neither fully of water nor wholly of land, but creatures of the threshold—that liminal space where river meets marsh, where current dissolves into stillness, where the ancient pulse of the delta writes itself in ever-shifting script across the mudflats. 

My body stretches perhaps four feet from snout to tail-tip, built low and sinuous like an otter's, but broader, heavier with muscle designed for both swimming and trudging through sucking mud. My fur—if it can be called that—is unlike any mammal's coat. Each strand is hollow, filled with oil that beads water away, creating a silvery sheen when wet. The individual hairs are thick as grass blades, lying flat in overlapping patterns that channel water backward as I move. When dry (a rare state), they fluff outward slightly, giving me the appearance of wearing a coat of metallic feathers.

My limbs tell the story of our adaptation: the front pair are powerful, with partially webbed digits that can spread wide for swimming or cup inward for digging. The claws are blunt but strong, good for gripping slick surfaces or excavating caches in the mud. My hind legs are longer, more powerful, with fully webbed feet that can propel me through water with surprising speed or allow me to stand upright for brief moments, balanced by a thick, flattened tail that serves as both rudder and prop.

But it is my head that marks me as something unique in this world. My skull is elongated, almost serpentine, with nostrils set high and far back, allowing me to breathe while mostly submerged. My eyes are large and set wide, giving me nearly panoramic vision—necessary when both Daggerfins and Sky-Watchers consider us prey. They are a deep amber color, with horizontal pupils that can narrow to mere slits in bright light or dilate to capture every photon in the misty twilight.

The most remarkable feature, however, is the array of sensory bristles that sprout from my snout and jaw. These are not mere whiskers but complex organs, each one sensitive to the slightest pressure change in water, the faintest electrical field generated by living muscle, the subtle chemical gradients that mark territories and trails. When I lower my head into the murky water, these bristles fan out like a living constellation, reading the river's secrets in a language older than sight.

We Tidereaders are the cartographers of this shifting world. Our lives are spent mapping the ever-changing channels, tracking the slow migration of sandbars, predicting where new pools will form and old ones will silt in. This knowledge we encode not in marks or symbols but in the very landscape itself.

Along the banks where we most often travel, you'll find our works: careful arrangements of shells and pebbles that might seem random to an untrained eye but speak volumes to those who know how to read them. A spiral of Slippersnail shells marks where the current runs swift and deep. Parallel lines of river pebbles warn of unstable banks. A circle of white stones around a dark center indicates a reliable fishing pool, while scattered red pebbles suggest Daggerfin hunting grounds.

Our dens are engineering marvels hidden in plain sight. We excavate complex tunnel systems in the firmer banks, always with multiple entrances—some above the high-water mark, others that can only be accessed by diving. The tunnels are more than shelter; they're libraries. In widened chambers, we cache objects of significance: unusual shells that arrived from far upstream, bones that tell of creatures long departed, even the shed scales of Mudwalkers, each one as large as my paw and precious for the stories they hold.

The social life of Tidereaders revolves around the sharing and updating of these maps. At dawn and dusk, when the mist is thickest, we gather at traditional meeting points—usually where multiple channels converge. Here, we perform what humans might call a dance but is really a form of communication. We trace the shapes of channels with our bodies, indicate depths with the angle of our tails, mark the location of new hazards or resources with specific postures and movements.

These gatherings can include anywhere from three to twenty individuals, each contributing their piece of the ever-changing puzzle. Young Tidereaders learn by following and mimicking, gradually building their own mental maps of the delta's fluid geography. The eldest among us, their fur faded to the color of wet ash, are living libraries, able to recall how the channels ran decades ago, predicting with uncanny accuracy where the river will choose to flow next.

Our diet is as varied as the delta itself. We hunt crayfish in the shallows, stunning them with a swift slap of our tail. We know which reeds produce the most nutritious roots and when to harvest them. During the spawning runs of certain fish, we position ourselves at natural bottlenecks, taking only what we need. We've learned to follow the Colossi at a safe distance, knowing that their massive excavations often uncover caches of freshwater clams.

But perhaps our most unique behavior is what we call "void-fishing." In the deeper pools where light barely penetrates, we've learned to hunt by absence. We remain perfectly still, our sensory bristles extended, creating a zone of hyper-awareness around us. When a fish swims into this space, we sense not the fish itself but the void it creates in the water's natural electrical field. The strike that follows is so swift it seems like magic—one moment empty water, the next a struggling Daggerfin in our jaws.

We fear the Sky-Watchers, of course, and the larger Daggerfins that patrol the deeper channels. But our greatest enemy is complacency. The delta is always changing, and a Tidereader who relies too heavily on old knowledge will find themselves trapped by a shifted sandbar or swept away by a new current. We must constantly read, constantly update, constantly adapt.

During the highest floods, when even our elevated dens are threatened, we demonstrate another remarkable behavior. Multiple family groups will come together, forming what we call a "raft." Adults link together, tails gripping neighbors' fur, creating a living platform. The young and elderly rest in the center while the strongest swim at the edges, keeping the formation stable and moving toward higher ground. These rafts can persist for days, a floating community that shares food, grooms one another, and continues to update their collective map even as the world dissolves beneath them.

In the dry season, when some channels dwindle to mere trickles, we become archaeologists. The exposed mudflats reveal treasures: ancient shells from species long extinct, the mineralized bones of creatures that predate even the Mudwalkers, sometimes strange stones clearly not from this watershed, hints of floods so massive they brought debris from unimaginable distances upstream.

We are neither the strongest nor the swiftest creatures of the delta. The Colossi dwarf us, the Daggerfins outswim us, the Sky-Watchers see farther than we ever could. But we know this world in a way none of them do. We know its past in the shells we collect, its present in the maps we constantly update, its future in the patterns we've learned to read.

When the great river roars its ancient song, we hear more than noise. We hear information: the pitch that tells of rainfall far upstream, the rhythm that speaks of seasons turning, the subtle harmonics that warn of changes coming. We are the readers of a text written in water and mud, a story that never ends but only turns another page with each tide, each flood, each gradual shift of ancient sediment.

In this world of constant change and eternal patience, we Tidereaders serve as living memory, active present, and predictive future all at once. We are the delta's way of knowing itself.
