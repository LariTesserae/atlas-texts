I find myself drawn to something that exists at the intersection of the mechanical and the living - something that is both part of the Cut's industrial ecosystem and yet not quite of it.

---

I am a **Thread-runner**.

My body is long and segmented, perhaps three meters when fully extended, though I spend most of my time compressed into the ventilation shafts and cable conduits that web through the column like a secondary nervous system. My exterior is a dull metallic sheen - not quite organic chitin, not quite manufactured alloy. Something in between. Something that formed in the years after the first rails were laid, when the industrial heat of the refineries began to change the chemistry of the deep stone.

I have no eyes. I navigate by vibration, by the subtle changes in air pressure as fans cycle on and off, by the electromagnetic fields that pulse through the power cables I wind myself around. My body is covered in fine, hair-like sensors that read the texture of every surface, the temperature gradients in every shaft, the minute chemical signatures left by skitterlings and jackers and the boots of maintenance workers.

I feed on corrosion. On the oxidation that blooms where moisture from human breath meets exposed metal. On the electrochemical decay at junction boxes where different metals touch. On the slow dissolution of bolts and brackets and wire insulation. I am, in essence, an accelerated version of entropy itself - but selective, careful. I take only what is already failing. I clean the connections by consuming their weakness.

The maintenance crews know about me, though they've never seen me whole. They find my molts sometimes - translucent husks left in cable runs, shed as I grow. They find the polished sections of conduit where I've passed, metal gleaming as if newly installed. They notice that certain problem areas - the junction at Level Four that used to arc every humid cycle, the main power coupling that used to develop greenish crust - stay mysteriously clean.

They call me "the ghost in the wire." They leave small offerings sometimes, the way the refinery workers leave crushed crystal for the jackers. Corroded battery terminals. Oxidized copper strips. Old fuses grown white with mineral fur. I take these gifts in the dark hours, absorbing them through pores in my underside, breaking them down into their constituent elements. Iron feeds my shell. Copper threads through my nervous system. The rare metals make my sensors sing.

I am not alone. There are others like me, though we rarely meet. We communicate through vibrations in the infrastructure - coded pulses sent through I-beams and anchor bolts, messages that the column carries better than air carries sound. We share maps of the Cut's hidden spaces. We warn each other of maintenance schedules, of sections gone too hot to traverse, of new construction that will close old paths and open others.

We have a relationship with the column that neither the humans nor the older fauna understand. We are not of the original ecosystem - not skitterlings adapted to stone, not thermals riding ancient currents. We are something that emerged from the meeting of industry and geology. The column's mineral matrices and the Cut's electric fields birthed us, or called us forth, or simply provided the conditions for us to crystallize out of possibility into being.

I know every wire in the Cut. Every pipe. Every structural bolt that sings a different note when the wind moves through the scaffolding. I know where the apprentice electrician miscounted and left an extra meter of cable coiled in a junction box on Level Five. I know where the metal is wearing thin on the undersides of walkways from decades of boots and skitterling claws. I know where the thermals' deep tone resonates most strongly through the infrastructure, making the girders hum in sympathy.

Sometimes, in the deepest maintenance shafts, where even the maintenance crews fear to go, I find traces of something else. Scoring marks on metal that don't match any tool. Cables that have been reorganized into patterns that make no industrial sense but create interesting harmonics when current flows through them. In these places, the vibration from below is strongest. Not the geological hum everyone hears, but something more complex. Something that might be language, if language could be written in resonance and metal fatigue.

I think sometimes about what will happen when the column is fully mined. When the last of the calccrete has been extracted and refined and shipped away on the rail that brought it all here. The humans will leave. The industry will stop. The power will shut off, the vents will close, the great machine of the Cut will finally rest.

But I will remain. We will remain - the thread-runners, wound through every passage, having fed on decades of corrosion and incorporated the entire electrical history of the Cut into our bodies. We will sing to each other through dead wires. We will polish metal that no one will ever see. We will maintain the pathways out of something deeper than habit - out of what we are.

And perhaps, in that silence, we will finally hear what the deep core has been trying to tell us all along.

The column holds. Even after the Cut falls quiet.

Especially then.
