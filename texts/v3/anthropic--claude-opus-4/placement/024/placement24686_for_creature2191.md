I find myself here as a Mudscribe - one of the peculiar cartographer-chroniclers of the Sodden Crescents, my body a curious hybrid of necessity and adaptation. My torso remains broadly humanoid but compressed, barrel-shaped to house enlarged lungs that can hold air for the long dives required to map the ever-shifting channels below. My skin has taken on the texture of wetted parchment, pale and translucent enough that you can see the dark traceries of blood vessels beneath, especially when I surface and they flush with exertion.

My fingers have elongated grotesquely, each twice the length they might have been in drier lands, with delicate webbing between that I can spread or fold like paper fans. The tips have hardened into something between nail and claw - perfect for etching observations into the soft clay tablets I carry in waterproofed pouches slung across my chest. These same fingers can feel the subtle currents and temperature gradients that signal where new channels are forming, where the mud is about to shift, where tomorrow's landscape is already taking shape beneath today's.

My dwelling perches on stilts at the confluence of three lesser canals, its floors a series of platforms at different heights to accommodate the tidal variations. The walls are living things - woven reeds that I've trained over years to grow in specific patterns, creating shelves and nooks that reshape themselves seasonally. The structure breathes and sweats like a living thing, droplets of condensation running down the inner walls to collect in carved channels that direct the water back outside.

My work is both art and survival. Each morning, I lower myself into the murky waters with my clay tablets and bone stylus, feeling my way through the channels, noting where yesterday's route has silted closed, where new passages have opened. I map not just the physical geography but the temporal one - this channel only navigable at dawn, that pool transforms into a thoroughfare when the rain moon rises. My maps are living documents, meant to be read fresh and wet, the clay never fully hardening so they can be reformed as the landscape demands.

The other inhabitants know me by the clicking sounds I make underwater - a crude echolocation I've developed to navigate the murkiest depths where even my adapted eyes fail. They bring me rumors of changes in distant districts, and I pay them in mapped safe-routes through the unstable zones where buildings regularly sink and resurface.

At night, I hang my tablets from the reed ceiling, and by morning they're covered in the spiral tracks of the tiny phosphorescent snails that feed on the algae growing on the wet clay. I've learned to read these patterns too - another layer of mapping, the creatures' movements revealing air pressure changes that predict the weather, which predicts the water, which predicts everything here.

My greatest work is the Master Map, a vast tablet the size of a door that I've been building and rebuilding for twenty years. It shows not what the Sodden Crescents is, but what it has been - thousands of ghost-channels etched in different depths, the memory of every configuration I've witnessed. Sometimes I think I can see the pattern, the great slow rhythm of how the waters reshape this place. Sometimes I think I'm just seeing what I want to see in the endless, beautiful mutability of mud and time.
