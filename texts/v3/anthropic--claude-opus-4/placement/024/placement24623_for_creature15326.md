In the belly of the city, where the pavement dunes crest highest and the exposed pipelines sing their rust-songs to the moon, there is a building that refuses to hold still. It was once, perhaps, a warehouse—or will be one tomorrow—but today it breathes with the rhythm of the earth beneath it, walls rippling like water whenever the ground decides to rearrange itself.

This is where I dwell: not quite architect, not quite archeologist, but something between. The others call me the Tremor-Keeper, though I've never kept anything in my life. I am more of a translator, really—interpreting the language of buckling concrete and the whispered intentions of shifting stone.

My form reflects my function. My bones are hollow like a bird's, filled with a fluid that responds to seismic pressure. When the earth prepares to move, I feel it first as a warming in my marrow, then as a symphony of vibrations that my body translates into color behind my eyelids. My skin has developed a peculiar quality over the years—it can adhere to any surface when needed, allowing me to walk up walls that tomorrow may be floors, to cling to ceilings that yesterday were gardens.

The building and I have reached an understanding. Its rooms rearrange themselves according to the earth's whims, and I map these changes with pigments I extract from the river mud and the sap of the twisted pines. My walls are covered in these maps—not flat representations but living documents that shift and flow. Sometimes I wake to find that a map has migrated from one wall to another, or that two have merged in the night, creating new territories I've never seen.

I collect the pavement squirrels—those strange eruptions of grass and wildflowers that burst through the mobile streets. Each one tells a story of pressure and time, of seeds that waited decades for their moment of upheaval. I press them between sheets of glass that I salvage from windows that no longer fit their frames, creating a library of the city's convulsions.

At dawn, when the light comes not as a harsh arrival but as that slow peachy dissolution of darkness you described, I climb to what is currently my building's highest point. There, I practice my morning ritual: feeling for the Old Thing's heartbeat through the soles of my feet, through the fluid in my bones, through the sympathetic trembling of the pressed flowers in their glass sheets.

The Old Thing speaks in frequencies below hearing, in movements too vast for ordinary perception. But I have learned its language over the years of walking these writhing streets, of watching the river change its mind about which way to flow. It tells me stories of the time before the city, when it was the only conscious thing for miles, dreaming in the dark beneath the stone.

Some mornings, I venture out to tend the log-stump shrines that dot the city. I am not the only tender—we are a loose collective who understand that these remnants of ancient forest are more than decoration. They are anchors, holding memory in place even as everything else shifts. I pour river water over them, watching how it pools and runs according to the micro-topography of rot and grain, reading the patterns like someone else might read tea leaves.

My neighbors have adapted in their own ways. The family next door (though "next door" is a fluid concept when buildings migrate) have developed a kind of echolocation, navigating by the sounds their voices make against the ever-changing surfaces. The couple across the way have given up on permanent furniture entirely, living with inflatable belongings that can be quickly deflated and moved when the floors begin their daily dance.

But I remain fixed in my purpose, even as everything moves around me. In the moments between earthquakes, in the pause before the pavement decides to dune, I document this liquid city. My maps grow more complex each day, layered with the pressed flowers and fragments of the Old Thing's dreams, becoming less about navigation and more about memory—ensuring that someone, somewhere, remembers what this street corner looked like yesterday, even if it will never look that way again.

This is my place in the writhing world: keeper of impermanent histories, translator of stone's ambitions, guardian of moments that exist only between one tremor and the next. My hollow bones sing with the city's changes, and I transcribe their song in mud and sap and light.
