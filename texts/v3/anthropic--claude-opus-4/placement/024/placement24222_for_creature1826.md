In the Glass District, I am a lens grinder—neither master artisan nor laborer, but something essential between.

My workshop occupies a narrow slice of building wedged between two grander establishments: a renowned bottle-blowing facility and the studio of a master who creates those impossible color-shifting pieces. My space is barely eight feet wide but extends deep into the building, following an old servant's corridor that previous tenants never thought to claim. 

I am called Prism-Eye Mar, though I wasn't born on a flash day. The name came later, after an accident in my apprenticeship left my right eye clouded with a peculiar cataract. Through some quirk of scarring, it refracts light into component colors at the edges of my vision. What should have ended my career as a glassblower instead revealed my true calling—I can see imperfections in glass that others miss, minute variations in density and composition that become visible only when light bends through them just so.

My fingernails carry the blue tinge, my knuckles bear the requisite constellation of burns, but my tools differ from those of my neighbors. Where they work with glory and flame, I work with patience and grit. My workshop houses seven grinding wheels of different compositions, from rough volcanic stone to leather dressed with diamond dust so fine it feels like talc between the fingers. Each wheel is powered by its own small water channel, diverted from the main canal system through a series of valves I adjust throughout the day as the water pressure fluctuates.

The work is peculiar: I make lenses, yes, but not ordinary ones. The Glass District's unique environment—the perpetual moisture, the mineral-rich atmosphere, the constant temperature variations—creates minute flaws in even the most carefully crafted glass. Most consider these imperfections failures. I see them as opportunities. By grinding precise corrections around these natural occlusions, I create lenses that do unexpected things. Some split single beams of light into parallel rays. Others focus not just light but heat, or seem to bend time itself—though this last effect might be imagination born from staring too long through carefully curved glass.

My customers are equally peculiar. The Night Polishers come for lenses that reveal the district's hidden glass installations, embedded markers that guide their secretive work. The Glass Shepherds request pieces that help them spot the smallest creatures—juvenile salamanders no bigger than rice grains, or the eggs of mercury birds, translucent as water drops. Occasionally, a master artisan arrives at my door, speaking in ellipses about needing to "see what cannot be seen" in their work. I never ask for clarification.

Most intriguingly, I serve the Furnace Keepers. Each of the district's major furnaces requires a specialized lens for its keeper to properly read the flames. These ancient lenses crack and cloud over decades of heat exposure, and only a few of us know how to replicate their peculiar properties. When I grind a new furnace lens, I must first spend days observing the specific fire it will read, learning its particular spectrum and habits. Only then can I shape glass that will translate its language into something human eyes can interpret.

My body shows the marks of my trade differently than the blowers and shapers. My fingers are worn smooth at the tips from constant contact with grinding compounds. My back curves from hunching over wheels, and my left eye has grown stronger to compensate for my prismatic right. I smell perpetually of wet stone and the various oils I use—walnut for the finest work, mineral oil for rougher grinding. Glass dust has worked its way so deeply into my skin's crevices that I sparkle faintly in strong light, as if wrapped in the finest gauze of stars.

I live above my workshop in two modest rooms where the sound of water rushing through channels creates a constant whisper. My windows face north, away from the spectacular evening light shows, but I prefer the steady, even illumination for examining my work. On a shelf sit dozens of "failures"—lenses that didn't serve their intended purpose but revealed some other property. One makes the glass snails' trails luminesce. Another causes the moisture in air to become visible as spinning golden motes. A third does nothing at all unless viewed during the rare true rainfalls, when it shows shadowy shapes moving between the drops—shapes that correspond to nothing in the visible world.

The other inhabitants of the Glass District regard me with the particular respect reserved for those who occupy necessary but liminal positions. I am not quite an artisan, not quite a laborer, not quite a scholar, but something that partitions between all three. The children sometimes dare each other to look through my workshop window, fascinated and frightened by the way my prismatic eye breaks their faces into component colors when I glance their way.

I chose this trade—or it chose me—because I understood early that in a world of creators, someone must also be an adjuster, a clarifier, a revealer of hidden properties. My work is solitary but not lonely. Each lens I create extends someone else's vision, enables work that couldn't exist without my patient grinding. In a district built on transformation—sand to glass, opacity to transparency, utility to art—I transform transformation itself, one careful curve at a time.

At night, when the district settles into its glowing evening rhythms, I often climb to my roof with a leather case of special lenses. Through each one, the familiar scene below changes: revealing heat patterns, showing the invisible moisture currents, isolating specific wavelengths until the world becomes a series of overlapping but distinct realities. My prismatic eye adds its own layer to these visions, creating combinations I could never achieve with ground glass alone.

I am Prism-Eye Mar, neither fully of the Glass District nor apart from it, grinding away at the boundaries between what is seen and what is knowable, one lens at a time.
