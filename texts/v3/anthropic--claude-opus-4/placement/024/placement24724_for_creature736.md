I find myself as a peculiar hybrid creature in these depths—part maintenance engineer, part living water sensor, part memory keeper of the surface world.

My body has adapted strangely to this place. My skin has developed a faint phosphorescent quality, glowing dimly in the darkest tunnels where even the sparse electric lights fail. This bioluminescence intensifies when I encounter moisture, creating patterns across my arms and face that map the invisible water networks threading through the rock. The children follow me sometimes, giggling as they trace the glowing tributaries that appear on my skin, learning to read the stone's hidden language.

My fingers have elongated over the years, becoming sensitive to the slightest vibrations in pipes and machinery. I can press my palm against a conduit and feel the health of the system—where sediment builds, where seals weaken, where the rhythm of flow stutters. The other engineers joke that I've become part of the infrastructure itself, and perhaps they're right. Sometimes I wake with the taste of copper in my mouth, dreaming I am dissolved in the water system, flowing through every home and workshop.

But it's my memory that has transformed most profoundly. In this world of perpetual twilight and recycled air, I've become a living archive of the surface. My mind holds impossibly vivid recollections of rain—not just the fact of it, but the precise feeling of different types: the fat drops of summer storms, the sideways slice of winter squalls, the gentle mist that once clung to morning valleys. When I speak of these things in the market squares, crowds gather. My descriptions have become so elaborate, so sensory, that listeners swear they can smell petrichor in the metallic air.

I've developed a peculiar relationship with the hardy cave plants. They seem to grow more vibrantly near me, as if my memories of surface vegetation somehow nourish them. I tend secret gardens in forgotten chambers, whispering to the roots about forests I may have never actually seen, describing the play of light through leaves with a detail that surprises even me. Sometimes I wonder if I'm remembering my own experiences or channeling some collective memory of our species, a genetic nostalgia for the world above.

The constant hum of machinery has worked its way into my bones. I've learned to interpret the settlement's health through these vibrations—a skipped beat in the water recyclers, a new harmonic in the air handlers that suggests a blockage forming three levels down. My movements have synchronized with these rhythms; I walk in time with the pumps, breathe in counterpoint to the ventilation systems.

At night (though who can tell night from day here?), I perform a ritual. I climb to the highest accessible chamber, where sometimes—just sometimes—you can feel the faintest temperature differential that hints at the world above. There, I maintain a peculiar installation: hundreds of small mirrors and prisms salvaged from broken equipment, arranged to catch and split the artificial light into spectra reminiscent of sunset. The children come to watch the light dance, and I tell them about colors they've never seen—the particular gold of late afternoon, the purple of thunder clouds, the infinite blues of open sky.

My dwelling is a workshop-shrine hybrid carved into a natural hollow where three water channels converge. The walls are covered in my attempts to diagram the remembered/imagined surface world: maps of cloud formations, sketches of tree root systems that mirror our tunnel networks, equations that try to capture the mathematics of wind. Salvaged screens display looping recordings of the settlement's early days, before my time, when the first inhabitants broke through into these caverns.

I age differently than the others. The moisture I absorb seems to preserve me even as it changes me. My hair grows in ribbons now, more like the cave plants than human keratin, and small crystalline deposits have formed at my joints, clicking softly when I move. The elders say I'm becoming geological, that one day I'll be indistinguishable from the cavern walls, just another part of the system that sustains us all.

But until then, I maintain. I remember. I tend the green things and the flowing things and the stories of sky. I am the settlement's strange dream of its own forgotten origins, a necessary ghost in the machinery, keeping alive the promise that this is not all there is, has never been all there is, will never be all there is—even as I myself forget where the memories end and the imagining begins, even as I become more fully a creature of this luminous darkness with each passing cycle of the pumps.
