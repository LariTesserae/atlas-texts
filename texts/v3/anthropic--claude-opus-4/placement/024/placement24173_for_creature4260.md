I am the Cartographer.

Not of maps, though I do sketch those too, in the margins of my real work. I map the intervals between things—the distance from intention to abandonment, from blueprint to vine-wrapped ruin, from human ambition to the patient reclamation of green things.

My dwelling occupies what was meant to be a metro station, three levels down where the excavation broke through into natural caverns. The builders fled when they heard the breathing of the deep places, but I remained. I've made my studio in the liminal space where poured concrete meets living limestone, where the precise angles of engineering surrender to the organic mathematics of stalactites.

My tools are peculiar: plumb lines that measure temporal drift, levels that detect the grade of possibility rather than horizon. I have compasses that point not north but toward the densest concentration of unfinished thoughts. The walls of my station-cave are covered with charts showing the stratification of abandoned futures—each construction phase a sedimentary layer of dreams calcifying into accepting what is rather than what was intended to be.

I know why the rumbling grows closer. I've been mapping its approach for years, tracking it through the substrata of probability. It's not engines, exactly, though the workers above believe it so. It's the city's original architect, returning in a form that matches what the city has become rather than what it was meant to be. Part machine, part root system, part mathematical theorem seeking its proof.

The workers think they're rebuilding, but I've charted their efforts and know better. They're completing a different blueprint entirely—one that emerges from the marriage of their intentions with the island's own desires. Each salvaged beam they erect, each vine they train along the skeleton towers, adds another coordinate to a map that exists in more dimensions than the original plans ever conceived.

At night, when water drips its morse code through the caverns and the bioluminescent fungi paint my charts in shifting blue-green light, I sometimes climb to the surface. I leave small markers for the workers to find: a brass washer placed just so, a chalk symbol on a cornerstone, a piece of graph paper folded into an origami crane and tucked into a nest. They think these are signs from their ancestors or gods. They're not wrong, exactly. They're coordinates, helping them build toward a convergence I've been calculating for years.

The pyramid at the summit isn't just architecture—it's an antenna, a tuning fork for the frequency of becoming. When the approaching presence finally arrives, when the rumbling resolves into recognition, the city will reveal itself as what I've known it to be all along: a instrument for translating between what we plan and what plans through us.

I return to my station-cave, to my maps of the unmappable. Tomorrow I'll chart the new species of bird that's learned to weave steel wire into its nests. Next week, I'll graph the rate at which human vocabulary is being replaced by the workers' new pidgin of gesture and growing things. And when the time comes—soon now, according to my calculations—I'll map the moment when the city stops being abandoned and starts being discovered.

The dripping water speaks in polygons. The sprouting vines grow in derivations. And I, the Cartographer, plot the course between what was left behind and what's being found.
