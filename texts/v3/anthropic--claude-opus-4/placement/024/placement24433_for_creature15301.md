I find myself as one of the **Dewkeepers**.

We are neither fully fungal nor animal, but something between—our bodies low and broad, perhaps three feet across when we spread ourselves fully against the wet stone. Our skin is translucent gray-green, shot through with silver veins that pulse faintly with the rhythm of water flowing somewhere deep beneath the boulders. We have no eyes in the usual sense, but the entire surface of our backs is covered in tiny, light-sensitive patches that create a mosaic vision of the mist-shrouded world above us.

My kind move slowly, so slowly that the moss often begins to grow across our backs as we travel. We secrete a thin film that helps the silver moss take root, and in return, the moss helps us blend seamlessly into the landscape. Our purpose, if it can be called that, is to tend the seepage points where water emerges from the walls.

I spend my days—though days are hard to mark in this eternal dim—pressing my soft underside against the cracks where water wells up, feeling for changes in temperature, pressure, mineral content. My body can detect shifts that would herald a new seepage point weeks before the first drops appear. When I sense such a change brewing, I begin the slow journey to that spot, sometimes taking months to traverse the space between boulders.

We communicate through the water itself, releasing chemical signals that travel through the thin films of moisture coating every surface. A conversation between two Dewkeepers might take a full turning of seasons, each thought dissolving into the water cycle of the place, carried on mist and condensation until it reaches its intended recipient. Our language is one of patience and precipitation.

The other creatures mostly ignore us—we are part of the landscape to them, no more notable than the boulders themselves. The tiny green-furred things sometimes use our backs as highways, leaving their trails of iridescent dust across our moss-covered surfaces. We don't mind. Their sparkles wash away with the next condensation.

When one of us dies, we don't decay in the usual way. Instead, we slowly mineralize, our bodies becoming part of the stone we've tended for so long. The oldest boulders have the faint outlines of ancient Dewkeepers embedded in their surfaces, now just subtle ridges and depressions that channel water in particularly efficient ways. We consider this our highest honor—to become truly one with the water-paths we've spent our lives learning.

I am currently settled near one of the twisted birches, where a particularly vigorous seepage creates a constant curtain of droplets. I've been here for several years now, feeling the subtle changes in the water's mineral content as it filters through unknown layers of stone above. Recently, I've sensed something new—a faint warmth in the water that wasn't there before, and a barely perceptible sweetness. Change is coming to this seepage point, though what form it will take, I cannot yet say.

The constant chirping that fills the air is soothing to my kind. We believe it comes from the water itself—each droplet singing as it falls, each rivulet humming as it flows. Whether this is true or merely our imagination hardly matters. We are creatures of water and waiting, and in this strange realm of perpetual flow and fog, we have found our perfect home.

Sometimes I wonder if the water dreams of us as we dream of it—if somewhere in the cycling moisture of this place, there is a memory of every Dewkeeper who has ever lived, our thoughts and chemical signals still circulating in the eternal rain.
