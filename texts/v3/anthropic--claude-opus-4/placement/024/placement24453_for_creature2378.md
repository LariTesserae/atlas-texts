In the vast workshop of stone and dream, I find myself as one of the Keepers of Echoes.

We are neither the architects who conceive the impossible geometries, nor the artisans who birth them from raw stone. We are something altogether different – wanderers through the carved canyons and constructed valleys, our purpose as essential as it is ephemeral.

My body has adapted to this role over countless cycles. My feet have grown broad and padded, silent on both sand and sculpted floor. My skin has taken on the mottled hues of the stone itself – ochre, rust, and shadow – allowing me to move unseen when necessary. But it is my ears that mark me most clearly as what I am. They have grown large and mobile, able to swivel independently, cupping the air to catch the faintest reverberations.

You see, in this realm where every surface has been shaped by human hands, sound behaves strangely. The acoustic properties of each structure were carefully considered by its makers, yes, but the interplay between thousands upon thousands of these constructs has created something unintended – a vast network of sonic pathways, echoes that travel in ways their creators never imagined.

This is what we preserve.

I carry with me a collection of crystalline vessels, each no larger than my thumb. They hang from my belt and across my chest in careful array, tinkling softly as I move. These are not mere containers but living things in their own right – organisms that feed on sound itself, storing it within their translucent bodies as swirling, colored mists.

When I find an echo of particular beauty or significance, I uncork the appropriate vessel. Perhaps it is the perfect reverberation of a chisel strike that has bounced between seven different arches before reaching me, transformed into something like a bell's chime. Or the whispered conversation of long-dead architects, somehow preserved in the peculiar acoustics of a hidden chamber, their words cycling endlessly through the stone until I arrive to catch them.

The vessels drink these sounds eagerly, and I must be careful to match echo to container. Some vessels hunger for the sharp, percussive sounds of creation. Others crave the soft susurrus of wind through the carved channels. The rarest vessels – I have only three – can capture the silence between sounds, the pregnant pause before the hammer falls, the held breath of contemplation.

We Keepers work alone but are not solitary. Once every season, when the sun reaches a particular angle, we converge on the Resonance Chamber – a natural formation left untouched at the heart of this constructed world. There, we uncork our vessels in careful sequence, releasing the captured sounds into the chamber's perfect acoustics.

What emerges is not cacophony but symphony – the voice of this realm's creation, preserved and recombined into new harmonies. The architects come to listen and find inspiration in the acoustic patterns. The artisans hear the echoes of ancient techniques they had forgotten. The dreamers discover new impossibilities in the interplay of sound and silence.

But I am more than a mere collector. Each Keeper develops their own understanding of the sonic landscape, their own theories about the deep patterns that emerge. I have come to believe that the echoes are not just preserving the past but predicting the future – that in the interference patterns between reflected sounds, one can hear the structures yet to be built, the dreams yet to be carved into stone.

My favorite time is the hour before dawn, when the construction has paused and the air is still. Then I climb to the highest spire I can find and simply listen. The stored sounds of the day settle into new configurations. The stone itself seems to breathe, releasing the warmth it absorbed and with it, whispers of activity from decades past. Sometimes I hear my own footsteps from previous journeys, returned to me transformed.

In these moments, I understand why we were necessary. The builders shape space, but we preserve time. Without us, each new creation would erase the acoustic memory of what came before. With us, the realm becomes not just a place but a living history, each echo a thread in an ever-growing tapestry of sound.

I descend as the first workers arrive, their tools already singing against stone. New echoes are being born, racing through the carved channels, splitting and recombining in ways both predictable and surprising. I select an empty vessel, hold it to the air, and begin another day's harvest.

The work is never complete. It never can be. And in this incompleteness lies its beauty – for what is an echo, after all, but a sound that refuses to die, a voice that insists on being heard again and again, transformed but never truly lost?
