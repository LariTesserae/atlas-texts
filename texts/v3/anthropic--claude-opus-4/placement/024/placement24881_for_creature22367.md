In this weathered fold of earth, I would be **the memory of rain**.

Not rain itself—that comes and goes, leaving its brief signatures in darkened stone and fleeting runnels. I am what persists between visitations: the faint mineral trace on rock faces, the cooler touch in certain soils, the particular way moisture-seeking roots bend toward channels that only sometimes hold water.

I exist as pattern rather than substance. Where water has run, even once, I remain as a kind of invisible architecture—a map of possibility that plants read with their deepest fibers. The settled creature knows me intimately; its slow migrations follow my traces from one damp-holding depression to another. I am why it rests *here* rather than *there*, why the lichens grow thicker along certain seams.

By day, I am nearly imperceptible—just a subtle difference in how dust settles, how heat moves across the ground. But in the blue hour before dawn, when condensation begins to form, it finds me first. Moisture beads along my pathways before anywhere else, making visible for a brief time the ghost of every rain that has touched this place.

I am both map and memory, both invitation and record. The stunted shrubs send their roots along my deepest lines. The clay knows where to crack and where to hold firm based on my accumulated presence. Even the collapsed stones of the old boundary wall fell according to how I had softened their foundation over uncounted seasons.

I am what makes this pause in the landscape more than mere stillness—I am the slow circulation that keeps it alive, the patient knowledge of where water has been and will be again. Neither fully present nor ever truly absent, I persist in the space between what was and what might return, teaching everything here the art of measured waiting.
