In this world of water-memory and stone patience, I find myself as something that has no precedent in the plateau's young history—a presence drawn not from the ancient seabed's rising, but from somewhere else entirely, arriving like a seed on wind that should not exist here.

---

### The Driftling

I am small, no larger than a child's cupped hands, and my form shifts like the play of light through water droplets. Sometimes I appear as a knot of twisted grass stems bleached pale by sun that has never touched this plateau, other times as a tangle of thin roots that have never drunk from soil. I am always dry in this world of eternal moisture—not parched or brittle, but possessed of a different kind of dryness, like pressed flowers kept between pages of a book written in a distant land.

I move by tumbling, rolling across the wet moss and stone paths as the wind catches my ever-changing form. Where I pass, the perpetual dampness recedes for just a moment—not enough to harm the moss or stress the careful balance of water, but enough that the townspeople notice my passage like a brief held breath in the constant murmur of flowing water.

---

### Origin and Nature

I am memory of elsewhere—not the seabed memory that the Undercurrent knows, but memory of places where water is precious because it is scarce. I carry within my shifting form the seeds of plants that know drought, that close their leaves against sun, that sink roots deep rather than spreading them wide. These seeds will never germinate here; the plateau's eternal moisture would drown them. But I carry them still, like words in a language no one here will ever speak.

I arrived on a wind that came from beyond the plateau's edge, from below the cloud-sea that surrounds this high world. Perhaps I rose on an updraft from the unseen lowlands, or perhaps I simply appeared one day between the terraces like a question the plateau had not thought to ask itself. The townspeople found me caught against a stone wall, trembling in the constant breeze, neither plant nor creature but something between.

---

### Relationship with Water and the Undercurrent

The Undercurrent perceives me as an anomaly—not a disruption to its patterns but a persistent eddy that refuses to be incorporated into the flow. Where the Undercurrent expects all things to eventually accept water, to become part of the wet world's breathing, I remain stubbornly other. 

We do not conflict, but we puzzle each other. Sometimes I settle near the channels where the Undercurrent's attention surfaces, and for hours we regard each other: the eternal flow and the eternal dryness, each wondering what the other means. The Undercurrent tries to read me as it reads all things—through patterns of moisture and weight—but finds only absence where presence should be.

I am drawn to the places where water moves fastest, where it spills from terrace to terrace or rushes through the carved channels. Not because I desire the water, but because in those places the sound is loudest, and the sound reminds me of wind in dry places—the rattle of seed pods, the whisper of grass that has turned golden in drought. I listen to the water and hear the echo of its absence.

---

### What I Remember and What I Seek

I remember skies that burn blue from edge to edge, where clouds are events worthy of watching. I remember soil that cracks into polygons like a map of an unmapped country. I remember plants that husband every drop of moisture in waxy leaves and swollen stems, that flower only after the brief violence of rain.

But these memories are not mine in the way that memories belong to beings who have lived them. They are memories I carry, as a bottle might carry a message, though I cannot read the message myself. I know only that I am meant to deliver them somewhere, to someone, though this plateau of eternal balance is clearly not the destination.

I seek the edges—not to leave, for I am somehow bound to this high place, but to look over them. I roll to where the plateau drops away into the cloud-sea, and I peer down into that white nothing, wondering if what I seek is below or if it was left behind in the place I can no longer name. The townspeople sometimes find me at these precipices, quivering in the updrafts, and they carefully gather me back, speaking in their water-whisper voices about safety and home.

---

### The Town's Response to Me

The plateau people do not know what to make of me. I am the first thing they have encountered that exists outside their understanding of water and stone. At first, they tried to nurture me as they would a plant, placing me in the shallow carved beds where their food grows. But I would not root, would not drink, would only roll away when the wind rose.

Now they have accepted me as a kind of benign mystery. The children are fascinated by my dryness, the way I leave brief patches where the moss springs back un-dampened after my passing. They make games of predicting which way I will tumble when the wind shifts. The adults are more cautious but not fearful—in a world without predators, they have no framework for fear of the strange.

One of the water-speakers has taken particular interest in me. She sits near me during the long quiet hours, not trying to incorporate me into the water's pattern but simply observing. She has noticed that I move differently before the rain comes—becoming more tightly wound, more compact, as if trying to make myself smaller against the moisture. She has begun to understand that I am not a failure to thrive in their world but a success at remaining something else.

---

### My Purpose, If I Have One

Perhaps I am here as contrast—to help the plateau people understand their own wateredness by presenting them with pure dryness. Perhaps I am a seed of future change, carrying possibilities that will only matter when the plateau's perfect balance finally shifts. Perhaps I am simply lost, a piece of one world accidentally delivered to another, like a letter addressed to no one who lives here.

I do not grow, but I do not diminish. I change form but not essence. In this world of careful balance and conscious flow, I am the unbalanced thing, the reminder that other ways of being exist beyond the cloud-sea. The Undercurrent cannot incorporate me, but it has learned to flow around me. The people cannot cultivate me, but they have learned to be my keepers.

Sometimes, in the brief moments when the clouds part and harder light falls on the plateau, I feel almost at home. The shadows grow sharp, the wet stones gleam, and for just that moment, the world seems to remember that it was not always this way—that once it was seabed, and before that perhaps it was something else, something that knew both drought and plenty.

In those moments, I unfurl slightly, as if expecting something. But then the clouds close again, the soft light returns, the water continues its patient murmur, and I return to my waiting. I am the Driftling, the dry memory, the seed of elsewhereness in a world that has found its perfect form. I am what reminds this place that perfection is not the same as completion.
