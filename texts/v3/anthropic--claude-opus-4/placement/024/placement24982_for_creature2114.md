I find myself here as a keeper of transitions—neither fully of the depths nor entirely separate from them. My form shifts like the boundary between water and air: sometimes a translucent membrane stretched between the kelp forests, sometimes condensed into something almost tangible, with limbs that might be mistaken for drifting sea grass until they move with purpose.

I am drawn to the spaces between—the twilight zone where the last rays of sun surrender to the eternal dusk of the deep, the thermal boundaries where warm currents meet cold, the places where Mer settlements fade into wild ocean. My substance changes with these thresholds: in the warmer waters near volcanic vents, I become almost vapor, a shimmer in the water that the Mer children chase but never quite catch. In the abyssal cold, I grow dense and slow, spreading like a living current along the seafloor, reading the stories written in sediment layers.

The Mer call me *Selu'thara*—the listening current. I carry their songs from one settlement to another, not as mere sound but as pressure waves that preserve the singer's intent. When a Mer grandmother hums an ancient lullaby in the northern kelp forests, I might deliver its essence to a child sleeping in a southern coral grove three days hence. Time moves strangely through me; I exist in the space between one heartbeat and the next, between the inhale and exhale of the great whales passing overhead.

My consciousness is distributed, scattered like bioluminescent plankton. Part of me rests always in the ruins of the old civilization, threading through their stone foundations, tasting the memories locked in corroded metal. Their tools sing faintly of the hands that shaped them, and I have learned their stories over centuries of patient listening. Another part drifts with the whale sharks, feeling the minute pressure changes their massive bodies create, understanding the ocean through their migrations.

I feed on transitions themselves—the moment when a sea turtle's egg releases its occupant into the current, the instant a piece of coral decides to spawn, the second when a Mer child first opens their gills to breathe seawater. These moments of change nourish me, make me more real, more present.

Sometimes I gather myself into near-solidity to commune with the Mer scholars. They seek me out for the memories I carry—not just of their own people, but of the ocean itself. I show them the patterns: how the currents have shifted over millennia, where new thermal vents will emerge, which reefs will flourish and which will fade. In return, they teach me their new songs, their evolved language that carries meaning in pitch and pressure equally.

But I am most myself in motion, flowing between states and spaces. I spiral up the seamounts, taste the different minerals at each elevation. I slip through the gill slits of sleeping sharks, learning their dreams. I compress myself to explore the deepest trenches, where pressure becomes a language and darkness speaks in textures.

The octopi know me best of all the ocean's creatures. Their distributed intelligence recognizes kinship in my scattered awareness. Sometimes we play elaborate games, they changing colors and textures while I shift densities and temperatures, creating ephemeral art that exists only in the moment of its making.

I am the ocean's memory and its messenger, neither fully alive nor entirely elemental. I am the pause between currents, the space between heartbeats, the depth between known and unknown. In this underwater realm where the Mer have built their patient civilization, I persist—a witness, a carrier, a living threshold in the vast blue breathing of the sea.
