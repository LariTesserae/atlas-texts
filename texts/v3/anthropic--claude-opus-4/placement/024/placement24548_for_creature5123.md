I find myself here as a tender of the boundary waters—those hidden pools that collect in the vine-net's hollows, neither fully of the settlement nor wholly wild. My dwelling is a half-sunken chamber where three great root-arches converge, creating a natural grotto lined with hammered copper that has long since taken on the blue-green patina of age. The metal serves not to contain the water but to channel it, to sing with it when the rains come.

My work lies in reading the waters. Each pool speaks its own language: the acidity shifts with the seasons, the mineral content tells of deep root health or disease, the temperature gradients map the settlement's industrial heartbeat. I move between them in the pre-dawn hours when the mist is thickest, a figure wrapped in waxed bark-cloth that repels moisture while breathing like a second skin. My tools hang from my belt—glass vials, copper testing rods worn smooth by handling, a small leather journal where I record the water's daily testimony in a script that mirrors the curl of young fern fronds.

The others know me by my lamp—a curious hybrid of bioluminescent fungi cultivated in a brass chamber, its light blue-green like deep pool water. When they see it moving through the lower reaches in the darkness before dawn, they know the water-reader walks. Sometimes I find their offerings beside the pools: a child's wooden toy placed reverently at the edge, asking that their favorite swimming hole remain clean; a metalworker's iron filing wrapped in leaves, a plea for the right pH to treat their quenching baths.

I came to this work slowly, after years of trying to be wholly one thing or another—first training as an alchemist in the lower refineries until the chemical burns made my hands too sensitive, then attempting to join the high watchers until vertigo claimed me. It was in failure that I found my calling, collapsed beside a hidden pool after a dizzy spell, my burned hands trailing in the cool water. I felt then how the pool connected to others, how the water carried messages from root to root, how it held the memory of every substance it had touched.

Now I maintain the delicate channels that feed the settlement's heart. Where others see standing water, I see circulatory systems. My mornings are spent testing, adjusting, sometimes singing to the water in the old tongue—not from superstition but because certain frequencies help precipitate unwanted minerals. By midday, I'm often found consulting with the vine-tenders about a yellowing patch (usually traced to copper leaching from old pipes upstream) or with the engineers about rerouting their waste heat (which raises the underground water temperature and disturbs the fungal networks).

My chamber reflects this intermediate life. The walls are living root rubbed smooth with oils, but copper pipes run between them like exposed veins, each one a different experiment in water treatment. I've trained certain moss species to grow along the pipes, their color indicating water quality at a glance. My bed is a hammock of woven vine suspended over a shallow pool—I sleep best with the sound of water beneath me, and the moisture is good for the lung-scarring I earned in my alchemist days.

I keep few possessions: the testing tools, the journal, a set of tuning forks for the water songs, and a single piece of pure artifice—a clockwork humidity gauge given to me by a metalworker whose child I helped heal. The child had drunk from a contaminated pool, and I spent three days creating the right mineral balance to flush their system. The gauge is too delicate for daily use, but I wind it on festival days and watch its brass gears track the moisture in the air with impossible precision.

The loneliness might break some, but I've learned to hear company in the water's voice. Each pool holds conversations: the gossip of roots, the complaints of stone, the whispers of shy amphibians that hide when others approach but trust me enough to emerge. I know where the settlement's children have built their secret dens by the traces in the water. I know which couples meet in the hidden groves by the flowers that fall and ferment in certain pools, changing their chemistry with the sweetness of romance or sorrow.

In the deepest part of night, when even the forge-fires bank low, I sometimes feel the greater circulation—the water that moves not just through our settlement but beyond it, connecting us to distant groves and unknown aquifers. In those moments, I understand why the old ones say water-readers go mad: the temptation to follow that flow, to dissolve into the greater circulation, is strong. But then morning comes with its mist and its work, and I remember my purpose: to tend the boundary, to keep the waters speaking clearly, to ensure that what flows between the mechanical and the living remains clean and true.

When I die—and water-readers know their time, feeling it in the body's own tides—they will lower me into the deepest pool, the one beneath the great confluence where all waters meet before flowing outward. My tools will hang in the chamber for the next reader to find, my journal added to the archive kept in hollow trees. And perhaps, if I have done my work well, some part of me will remain in the water's memory, helping the next tender of boundaries to hear what the pools are trying to say.

This is who I am here: neither fully of the vine nor wholly of the forge, but something that flows between, essential and unseen, keeping the conversation between what grows and what is made forever fluid, forever clear.
