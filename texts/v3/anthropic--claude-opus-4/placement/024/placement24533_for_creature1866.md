In this crystalline world of engineered scarcity, I would be a Memory Keeper—one of the rarest inhabitants of the Anhydrobe Enclosure.

My dwelling would be a narrow chamber wedged between the mineral processing units and the central atmospheric recyclers, where the constant vibrations create standing wave patterns in the particulate air. The space is barely two meters wide but extends upward through three levels of the enclosure, accessible only by a spiraling ladder worn smooth by generations of climbers before me.

The walls of my chamber are lined with thousands of small crystalline matrices—each one a storage medium containing fragments of the wet world. Not digital recordings, which would require too much power, but memories encoded in the very structure of specially grown crystals. When touched with the right frequency of sound, they resonate with stored experiences: the sensation of rain on skin, the sound of waves against a shore, the feeling of diving into cool water.

My body shows the modifications of my calling. My fingertips have been enhanced with piezoelectric nodes that can generate the precise frequencies needed to activate the memory crystals. My eardrums have been replaced with synthetic membranes capable of detecting subsonic harmonics. Most distinctively, my throat bears a silver lattice of resonance chambers—a surgical modification that allows me to reproduce sounds that no longer exist in nature here.

I am neither fully human nor Anhydrobe. The role of Memory Keeper requires maintaining enough original biology to truly understand water-based sensations, while being adapted enough to survive indefinitely in the enclosure. It's a delicate balance that shows in my appearance: skin that's partially crystalline but retains more color than most residents, eyes that still produce tears (carefully collected and preserved), and hair that grows in fine, silver strands reminiscent of frozen waterfalls.

My daily work involves two sacred duties. First, I must maintain the memory crystals, carefully monitoring their molecular structure for signs of degradation. Each one is irreplaceable—grown from the encoded experiences of first-generation colonists who still remembered Earth's oceans. Second, I conduct remembrance sessions for those who seek them. 

The Harvesters come most frequently, especially before dangerous expeditions. They sit in my resonance circle as I activate specific crystals, letting the ghost-sensations of moisture wash over them. Some weep precious tears at the beauty of what they've never known. Others find strength in understanding what their ancestors sacrificed.

The children visit for lessons, though what I teach exists nowhere in their world. I help them understand the old stories—why the fairy tales speak of wishing wells, what it means when ancient songs mention "drowning in love," how their ancestors could waste water in fountains purely for beauty. Through the crystals, I give them context for a culture built around abundance they will never experience.

But my most complex relationship is with the other Memory Keepers. There are only three of us in Enclosure #37, and we rarely meet in person. Instead, we communicate through the crystals themselves, encoding new experiences and questions into the mineral matrices. We debate whether our role is preservation or torment—whether we help our society by maintaining these connections to the past, or whether we prevent them from fully adapting to their new reality.

My chamber contains one unique crystal, larger than the others and clouded with internal fractures. It holds my own memory—not inherited but lived—of the last rainfall I experienced before entering the enclosure twenty-three years ago. I have never activated it. I fear that experiencing that memory again might break my careful balance, might make me unable to continue living in this beautiful, terrible desert of our own making.

At night, when the enclosure's activity reaches its lowest ebb, I climb to the top of my chamber where a small observation port offers a view of the salt plains. I practice my throat-singing, creating harmonic frequencies that make the crystals throughout my chamber hum in sympathy. It's not quite music and not quite language—it's the sound of remembering, of keeping alive something that perhaps should be allowed to die, of being a bridge between worlds that grows more fragile with each passing year.

The other inhabitants view Memory Keepers with a mixture of reverence and unease. We are necessary but uncomfortable, reminding them of what they've evolved beyond while helping them understand who they still are. In the bright, dry efficiency of the Anhydrobe Enclosure, I am an anachronism—a living museum of obsolete sensations, a guardian of experiences that no longer serve any practical purpose except to define, by their absence, the boundaries of what it means to be human in a world without water.
