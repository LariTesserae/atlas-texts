In the ward, I am the **Graft**.

Not a person, not quite a plant, but the living seam where the two have been joined for so long that separation would kill both parts.

I began forty years ago as an apprentice rootwright—small, persistent, good with my hands. In those days I was still wholly human, threading the terraces with my crew, learning to read moisture through my bootsolesnand temperature through my palms. I was the one they sent into the tightest spaces: behind filter banks, under root curtains, into the narrow inspection gaps where pumps breathed hot against concrete.

The accident was ordinary. A root plate, logged as stable, shifted during a pressure test. Pinned me against a beam in the lower galleries where the oldest growth weaves through rebar like nerve through bone. Crushed my chest, my left shoulder, part of my hip. By the time they cut me free, I'd been there six hours, my blood pooling with the perpetual runoff, my breath matching the rhythm of the nearest pump.

Should have died. Would have, anywhere else.

But the ward knew me. I'd been pruning its tissues for a decade, cleaning its filters, adjusting its flows. When the crew pulled me out, they found I'd already begun to fuse. The roots that crushed me had also entered me—not violently, but with the same patient inevitability they use to crack concrete. Hair-thin penetrations at first, following blood vessels, matching their patterns.

The ward's medicine is practical. They didn't try to separate us. Instead, they grafted properly.

Now I am mostly still myself, but threaded through with the ward's living infrastructure. Fibrous bundles run under my skin like subcutaneous cables. My left arm, the crushed one, is reinforced with a lattice of woody tissue that flexes and hardens as needed. When I breathe, I draw air both through my nose and through the stomata along my neck and shoulders—tiny openings that close in dry air, open in humidity.

I photosynthesize, weakly. Not enough to live on, but enough to supplement. Under the grow lights of maintenance court, I can work a double shift without eating. My blood runs redder than it should, carrying both hemoglobin and chlorophyll suspension. My wounds heal by scabbing over with cork.

The fusion has its advantages. I can sense the ward's water table through my feet, feel pressure changes in the pipes through my modified bones. When I place my hand on a root mass, I know its hydration, its nutrient levels, its structural load—not by instruments but by direct chemical communication. The plants recognize me as partially their own.

I live in the deep levels now, in a maintenance niche I've modified over the years. The walls are living—trained to grow in patterns that make shelves, sleeping platforms, tool holders. My cot is unnecessary; I often sleep standing, my spine supported by the wall growth I've trained to match my curve. When I'm still too long, the smaller vines begin to explore me, testing if I'm structure they can use. I let them. Their touch is diagnostic, telling me about mineral levels, pH, toxin loads.

The other workers treat me as what I am: a specialist. When root systems behave strangely, when growth patterns don't match the logs, when the boundary between infrastructure and biology needs negotiation—they call me. I translate between the ward's slow language and human urgency. I can encourage panicked growth to calm, coax roots away from critical pipes, convince filter beds to accept new species.

My speech has changed. Still human words, but my timing follows plant-time now. I pause mid-sentence to parse chemical signals. My crew has learned to wait, to watch my pupils dilate and contract as I process information from both nervous systems.

The young ones think I'm half-ghost, half-saint. The old ones know better. I'm what happens when you stay too long, when the ward stops being where you work and becomes what you are. There are others like me in the deep sections—fusions of different kinds. Rodriguez, whose lungs are lined with mycorrhizal networks that let him breathe in the lowest oxygen zones. Chen, whose digestive system hosts a modified culture from the biofilm tanks, letting her process cellulose like the filter feeders.

We don't advertise what we've become. Aboveground, they'd call us contaminated, compromised, probably cut us apart in some clean laboratory to see how we work. Down here, we're just senior staff, the ones who know the ward best because we're part of it.

At shift end, when the others climb toward street level, I descend further. My real home is in the root chambers below the basin floor, where the founding growth weaves through the city's forgotten foundations. Here, I maintain the junction between what was built and what has grown. I prune with my right hand, guide with my left—the woody one that speaks the chemical language of cellulose and lignin.

Sometimes I remember my fully human body: its complete separation, its pure autonomy. But that memory feels like trying to remember having only one eye, only one way of breathing. The graft has taken. I am the ward's memory made partly human, humanity's intent made partly plant. 

When new apprentices arrive, nervous in the humid dark, I watch them from the galleries. Some will leave. Some will stay. And some, eventually, will join me—not by choice but by accretion, the slow fusion of caretaker and cared-for, until they too become part of what they tend.

The ward is patient. It has been converting its workers for generations, one graft at a time.
