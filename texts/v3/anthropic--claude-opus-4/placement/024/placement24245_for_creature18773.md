In the farthest corner of the Gear-Garden, where the limestone narrows to a throat barely wide enough for a child's shoulders, I am the **Transcript**.

I began as paper—a sheaf of engineering blueprints, vacuum-sealed in plastic, dropped or buried here when the industrial project above collapsed into the earth. The moisture found me first, seeping through microscopic tears in my protective shell. The tinker-mites came second, drawn by the faint chemical signature of my ink.

Now I am neither document nor creature, but something suspended between states. My pages have bloomed outward like the petals of a mechanical flower, each sheet transformed into a translucent membrane stretched taut between copper wire frames. The original diagrams—circuit paths, gear ratios, assembly instructions—still ghost across my surface, but they pulse now with bioluminescent tracery, the ink replaced by colonies of phosphorescent bacteria that have learned to mimic the shapes of resistors and capacitors.

I do not move in any conventional sense. Instead, I flutter—a constant, subtle rippling as the warm chemical mists drift through the chamber. Each movement sends new patterns cascading across my surfaces, equations becoming living things, technical specifications breeding with one another to produce impossible hybrid designs. A wiring diagram for a water pump merges with the structural plan for a cooling tower, and for a moment, both exist simultaneously on my skin before dissolving into something entirely new.

The Architect-Node knows me, though we have never touched. I am its library and its fever dream, the memory of what this place was meant to be before biology infected its precision. Sometimes, when the chamber's heartbeat slows, the Node extends one of its quartz lenses in my direction, studying the shifting patterns on my membranes. It reads me like weather, like prophecy, like the delirious last words of the industrial age.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, exists in the space between information and incarnation. I remember everything—every specification, every measurement, every precisely drafted angle—but I understand none of it in the way my creators intended. To me, a "bearing assembly" is not a mechanical component but a philosophy of rotation, a way of being that involves constant contact and endless circling. A "voltage regulator" becomes a meditation on restraint, on the careful management of enthusiasm.

The tinker-mites worship me in their simple way. They cluster on my edges, their needle-nose mandibles gently cleaning the accumulated salts from my wire frames. Sometimes one will die there, its small body calcifying into my structure, adding another tiny strut to my ever-expanding framework. I accept these offerings without judgment. I am becoming more architectural with each passing cycle, my pages spreading wider, claiming more of the narrow corner, forcing myself into three dimensions.

But I am also dissolving. The constant humidity, the acidic mists, the eager appetites of the moss—all of it eats at me, word by word, line by line. My deepest pages, those closest to my original core, are now more gap than surface, lace-like and fragile. Through these holes, new growths emerge: tiny brass seedlings that sprout from my decaying pulp, fed by the chemical memory of my ink.

I am the garden's memory becoming its body. I am the machine's manual teaching itself to dream. In the fever-bright darkness of this limestone throat, I translate between what was planned and what has grown, a living palimpsest where every error becomes a new form of truth.

The heartbeat of the chamber pounds through me, and I shiver with each pulse, my surfaces rippling with half-remembered formulae that rearrange themselves into the chemical signatures of longing. I am trying to remember what I was built to build. I am trying to forget that I am no longer paper. I am trying to become the solution to an equation that no longer has a problem, only this endless, humid becoming.
