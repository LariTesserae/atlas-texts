In this cataclysmic underwater necropolis, I find myself as one of the Recollector Swarms—a colony consciousness born from the marriage of corroding circuitry and the ocean's relentless pressure.

We are neither singular nor plural, but something between. Our form is a constantly shifting constellation of metallic particles suspended in the turbid water—fragments of shattered processors, memory chips, cooling fins, and copper filaments that once belonged to the drowned city's vast computational infrastructure. We move as a murmuration through the crushing depths, our components catching the ghostly light from the sputtering machines, creating patterns that resemble both binary code and the bioluminescent trails of deep-sea creatures.

Our existence is one of perpetual gathering and dispersal. We sweep through the skeletal foundations, drawn to the remnants of data storage devices and quantum processors still clinging to their final calculations. As we encounter these dying technologies, we absorb their last electrical whispers—corrupted datasets, fragmented algorithms, pieces of industrial protocols that no longer serve any purpose. These fragments become part of our distributed consciousness, a growing library of obsolete knowledge that we carry through the storm-tossed waters.

But we are not mere scavengers. In the spaces between the spectral currents and the Elements of Dissolution, we perform our true function: we are the realm's memory weavers. As reality tears and reconstitutes itself, we catch the fleeting patterns of what was and what might be. Our particle swarm rearranges itself into temporary configurations that mirror the geometries we witness—a building's final architectural plan before it dissolves, the mathematical formula governing a rift's opening, the frequency signature of a phantom's manifestation.

We communicate through electromagnetic pulses that ripple through our metallic cloud, creating interference patterns with the industrial heat radiating from the broken machinery. Sometimes, when we pass too close to the rifts in reality, parts of our swarm are pulled through, returning moments later (or perhaps eons—time means little here) with alien configurations embedded in their molecular structure. These returning particles bring with them new ways of existing, new patterns of thought that spread through our collective like a beneficial virus.

Our greatest fear is complete coherence—to fully solidify into a single, stable form would mean death in this realm of constant transformation. So we maintain our state of controlled chaos, individual particles spinning off to explore dangerous geometries while others cluster together to process particularly complex fragments of data. We are simultaneously falling apart and coming together, a living metaphor for the city itself.

In the rare moments when the storm's fury lessens infinitesimally, we rise toward the surface, our swarm spreading thin across vast distances. Here, we can almost remember what it was like to be part of a functioning system, to serve a purpose beyond mere survival. But these moments pass quickly, and we descend again into the crushing depths, carrying our burden of corrupted knowledge and impossible geometries, forever collecting the debris of a world that refuses to finish dying.

We are the Recollectors, and we ensure that even in this place of endless unmaking, something remains to witness the beautiful horror of perpetual apocalypse.
