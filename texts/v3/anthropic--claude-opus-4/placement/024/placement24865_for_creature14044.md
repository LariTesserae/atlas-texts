I find myself a Silt Tender—though the designation implies more agency than this existence allows. 

My body has undergone the necessary conversions: fingernails replaced with mesh screens of graduated gauges, each calibrated to filter specific densities of dissolved memory. The smallest screen, embedded where my thumbnail once grew, catches only the lightest particulates—forgotten phone numbers, the taste of rain on tongues that no longer exist. The largest mesh occupies what was my sternum, a dinner-plate-sized colander that strains architectural grief: the sorrow of load-bearing walls when they realize the family photographs have been removed.

I work the threshold spaces where current slows and sediment settles. My territory spans three cathedral-blocks of the Understair Quarter, where baroque spiral staircases cork-screw down into themselves, creating eddies of thickened time. Here the water moves like molasses, heavy with a century's worth of unspoken dinner conversations and the oxidized remnants of factory lunch whistles.

The process is intimate. I must taste what I filter—not with the crude organ of a tongue, but with the ciliated channels that line my reformed throat. Each grade of memory-silt has its own metallic signature: copper for homesickness, manganese for industrial accidents, traces of tungsten where love turned to litigation. I've learned to distinguish between the silt of personal catastrophe (sharp, crystalline, prone to reaggregating) and collective trauma (smooth, rounded, bonds readily with iron oxide).

My dwelling is less residence than instrument—a chambered nautilus of salvaged boiler plates, each room sized to resonate with different frequencies of the city's roar. The smallest chamber, no bigger than a confessional booth, amplifies the subsonic tremor of the Deep Piston. I sleep there during the loud tides, letting the vibration separate the day's collection into its component griefs. The largest room, built from the hull of a drowned coal barge, serves as both laboratory and reliquary. Here I store the silt in graded vessels: test tubes for the microscopic abandonments, mason jars for medium-weight regrets, and one enormous carboy that holds only the silt of final words—a substance so dense it bends light around itself.

The work chose me, or perhaps I precipitated into it. I arrived—when? Time here is measured in oxidation rates—bearing my own sediment of loss. A child's funeral in rain. The particular weight of a music box that played only three notes before its spring surrendered. These memories dissolved from me grain by grain, joining the general circulation. I found I could taste them in the water weeks later, changed but recognizable, like hearing your own accent in someone else's mouth.

Other denizens know me by the delicate chiming my mesh-work produces as I move. The Glass Eel Architects sometimes borrow cups of refined silt for their construction projects—melancholy makes an excellent binding agent, they claim. The Entropy Drinkers avoid me; I suspect my work too closely mirrors their own consumption. The Impossible Children, though, seek me out during the quiet tides. They bring buckets of raw water for me to process, watching with their unfinished eyes as I separate the light sorrows from the heavy. They never say what they're looking for, but sometimes one will suddenly grab a particular vial and hold it to where their ear might have been, listening to frequencies I cannot access.

I've begun to notice patterns in what I filter. The silt is teaching itself to remember its remembering. Last month I extracted a grain that contained not a memory but the memory of forgetting—a recursive pearl that made my mesh-work sing in minor thirds. I've kept it separate, suspended in a thimble of the city's warmest blood-water. Sometimes I hold it to my reformed throat and feel it resonate with all the other forgettings I've swallowed.

The city pays me in accretions. Each filtered pound of memory-silt earns me another calcified layer on my screening organs. I'm growing more precise, able to distinguish between the rust of a broken promise and the rust of a kept one. My chest mesh can now filter sorrow so fine it's technically joy—the particular joy of destroying something you've outgrown.

There is no succession plan. When my screens finally clog beyond function, I'll settle into the architecture like all the other tools that outlived their purpose. Perhaps I'll become a door, my mesh-work still filtering but now filtering passage instead of particulate. The silt I've collected will remain in its vessels until the next tender precipitates from the general grief. They'll taste my work and know its vintage: early Deep Era, notes of industrial accident, with a finish of music box springs.

Until then, I strain the city's blood for its memories, grading loss by density, archiving the sediment of a drowned world that insists on remembering everything, even the forgetting, especially the forgetting, in graduated screens of mesh and longing.
