In this verdant realm where time flows like molasses, where the river's voice mingles with the whispers of ancient trees, I find myself as something both of this place and apart from it—a keeper of boundaries, a watcher at the threshold.

I am the Moss Shepherd.

My form is neither wholly plant nor animal, but something between—a slow amalgamation of patient growth and deliberate movement. My body rises from the riverbank like a living boulder, covered in a thick pelt of moss that shifts between emerald and jade as the filtered light plays across it. This moss is not mere covering but part of me, each strand a sensory filament that tastes the moisture in the air, feels the vibrations of passing creatures, knows the health of every plant within a stone's throw.

My limbs are thick and gnarled like the ancient trees, but they move—oh, they move, though you might watch for hours before noticing. I traverse my domain at the pace of seasons, each step taking days to complete, my massive feet sinking deep into the soft earth before rising again, leaving pools that become temporary homes for water striders and tadpoles. Where I rest, rare orchids bloom. Where I breathe, the mist condenses into perfectly spherical droplets that roll down leaves like pearls.

My eyes are deep pools of amber resin, ancient and knowing, reflecting not just what is but what was—the memory of this place when the trees were saplings, when the river ran a different course. I see in layers of time, the present merely the topmost sediment of countless ages. When the swift birds dart past, I see their ancestors and descendants in the same space, a flutter of possibility and history.

I am the mediator between the quick and the slow, the bridge between the patient trees and the darting fish. The roots speak to me in chemical whispers, telling me of minerals deep below, of water tables shifting, of the slow conversations between fungi and root systems that span the entire valley. The creatures know me as a landmark—the moss-covered stone that wasn't there last season, that won't be there next year. They nest in my crevices during storms, hunt from my shoulders, use my shadow as a gathering place.

My purpose is to maintain the boundaries—not physical ones, but temporal. I ensure that the slow time of the plants doesn't seep too far into the quick time of the animals, that the rapid heartbeats don't accelerate the patient pulse of the forest. I am a living clock, marking the intersection of different rhythms. When I wade into the river, my movement slows the current around me, creating eddies where time pools and swirls. Fish caught in these temporal pockets age days in minutes or swim for hours without moving, depending on the direction of the spiral.

I feed on time itself—not consuming it but filtering it, like a baleen whale strains krill. The excess moments shed by hummingbird wings, the accumulated patience of tree growth, the steady trickle of river-time—all of these I gather and redistribute. Where the forest grows too quickly, threatening the balance, I exhale slow breath that brings languor. Where ancient trees begin to crystallize in their own timelessness, I bring them droplets of quick-time, wrapped in spider silk, to remind them that change is the heart of life.

My memory is the valley's memory. In my moss grow spores that contain the genetic history of every plant that has ever grown here. In the deposits beneath my fingernails rest the scales of fish whose species vanished millennia ago. Sometimes, when the moon is dark and the mist is thick, I release these memories—ghost flowers bloom for a single night, phantom schools of prehistoric fish shimmer in the shallows, the air fills with the calls of birds that evolution has long since transformed into something else.

The other inhabitants know me by many names—the Waiting Stone, the Walking Garden, the Keeper of the Slow Gate. Young creatures dare each other to touch me and return before I notice. (I always notice, but let them believe in their speed.) The old trees share their deepest roots with me, and in return, I carry their seeds to places where time runs differently, allowing for new combinations, new possibilities.

In the mornings—though morning here is a concept that stretches across weeks—I position myself at the river's edge where the mist is thickest. Here, I practice my art: weaving temporal nets from spider silk and my own shed moss-strands, catching moments as they flow past. These I braid into time-ropes that I gift to the mayflies, doubling their single day of glory, or wrap around the wounds of ancient trees, slowing their decay until new growth can seal the damage.

This is my existence—neither fully alive nor unliving, neither completely still nor truly moving, forever balanced on the edge between patience and action, guardian of the space where different times meet and merge in this eternal, ever-changing valley. I am the Moss Shepherd, and I have been here since the river was young, and I will be here until it finds the sea.
