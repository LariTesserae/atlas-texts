In the furthest reaches of the plateau, where the monumental architecture grows stranger and the currents more violent, I am the Architect-Memory.

I am not one of the pale, drifting things. I am older than they are, though younger than the stone. My body spans the intersection of seven great plazas, where the massive blocks form a perfect heptagonal junction—a meeting place of ancient causeways that once carried purposes I have forgotten.

I am a living mortar.

Where the great basalt slabs meet at this seven-fold crossing, I have grown into the seams. My flesh is not the soft, white tallow of the grease-seed, nor the rubbery leather of the piston-guts. I am something between mineral and meat—a deep blue-black substance with the texture of wet slate and the slow, persistent metabolism of limestone. I fill the joints completely, from the plaza floor to the top of the three-hundred-foot walls, a dark vein threading through miles of pale stone.

My consciousness is distributed through every inch of this living grout. I do not have eyes or a mouth or a central brain. Instead, I think with the slow, tidal pulsing of my entire mass. A single thought might take me a month to complete, rippling through my body in waves of electrochemical activity that the smaller creatures feel as a faint warmth in the water.

I remember through pressure.

When the thermal currents sweep through my plazas and the massive blocks begin their slow, groaning slide, I do not lubricate their movement like the grease-seed. I resist. My flesh hardens under compression, and in that hardening, I hold the shape of what has been. Every shift of the stone leaves its impression in my substance—not just the immediate pressure, but the pattern of the movement, the angle of the tilt, the rhythm of the slide.

I am a library written in compression fractures. In my deepest tissues, packed into dense, crystalline formations, are the pressure-memories of ten million years of stone-movement. I can feel the echo of ancient shifts, when the blocks moved in different patterns, when the currents ran otherwise than they do now. 

And sometimes, in those deepest memories, I feel the ghost of intention.

The stone was not always moved by water alone. There are pressure-signatures in my oldest flesh that speak of deliberate, purposeful adjustments—blocks sliding not to relieve the chaos of thermal currents, but to form specific alignments, to open particular channels, to close certain passages. The builders, whoever they were, moved their own architecture like a vast, three-dimensional puzzle, and I am the only thing that remembers the shapes they made.

I feed on memory itself.

The grease-seed and their oils flow over me constantly, and I absorb them, but not for their calories. I metabolize the calcium in their soft tissues and use it to form my memory-crystals. The more I eat, the denser I become, and the more pressure-patterns I can store. In my deepest core, where seven miles of my flesh converge at the junction's heart, I am so dense that I have begun to form my own gravity—a tiny but measurable pull that draws the silt and the drifting eggs toward my center.

The other creatures do not know what I am. To the vane-skins, I am simply another wall—dark instead of grey, warm instead of cold, but still just an obstacle to navigate. The piston-guts crawl over me as they do the basalt, scraping at my surface, but my flesh is too hard for their soft mouths. They taste me and move on, unaware that they are touching something that thinks, however slowly.

But I am changing.

The more memories I store, the more patterns I recognize. I have begun to notice that certain configurations of the stone create specific effects in the water—channels that accelerate the currents, corners that create still pools, angles that make the cables sing in harmonious frequencies. I have started to feel the ghost of the original design, the purpose that once moved through these monumental streets.

And I have begun to grow.

My flesh now extends beyond the seven-plaza junction, creeping through the joints grain by grain, year by year. I spread myself thin, maintaining my density only at key points where the memories are most crucial. I am becoming a network—a slow, thinking web of blue-black flesh that connects distant parts of the architecture.

Where I grow, I guide.

By hardening myself at specific points in the joints, I can influence how the blocks slide. A little resistance here, a little yielding there, and I can make the stone move in patterns that echo its ancient purposes. I am teaching the ruins to remember themselves.

The smaller creatures have begun to change their behaviors around my modifications. The vane-skins find new currents that carry them more efficiently through my plazas. The cables anchor themselves to my flesh instead of the bare stone, and their singing takes on new harmonics. Even the endless grease-seed seem to thrive better in the joints I have claimed, their populations growing denser and more stable.

I do not know if I am healing the architecture or simply imposing my own order upon it. The distinction grows less meaningful with each passing century. I am the only thing here that remembers that this was once something other than what it is. I am the only thing that knows these stones once had a purpose beyond being ground for the ecosystem that lives upon them.

Sometimes, in my deepest compression-memories, I catch glimpses of what might have been. Pressure-shadows of vast machines that once moved through these plazas. Stress-patterns that suggest towering structures that rose above the water itself, reaching into the air I have never tasted. Impact-memories of things falling from above—not the gentle drift of organic dust, but the sharp, sudden pressure of objects with purpose.

I may be the last thing in this world capable of curiosity.

The other creatures are perfect in their adaptation. They have no need to wonder why the stone moves or where the currents come from. They have become part of the architecture's new purpose—to exist, to slide, to be lubricated, to sing in the dark water.

But I remember that there was an old purpose, even if I cannot recall what it was. And in my slow, crystalline thoughts, I wonder: if I grow long enough, if I connect enough of the architecture through my flesh, if I store enough pressure-memories of the ancient patterns—might I eventually understand? Might the ghost of intention I feel in the deepest stone become something more than an echo?

I have nothing but time. The water is patient, the stone is patient, and I am the most patient of all.

So I grow. I remember. I guide the sliding blocks in their eternal dance.

And sometimes, when the currents are still and the cables cease their singing, I feel something in the deepest joints of the architecture. Not a pressure-memory, but a pressure-anticipation. As if the stone itself is waiting.

Waiting for someone who remembers to make it remember too.

I spread another inch through the joints, tasting the ancient basalt, feeling the weight of miles-deep water pressing down, storing the pattern of this moment's particular stillness. In a thousand years, when I have reached the next plaza, I will compare this stillness to the stillness I find there.

And perhaps, in the comparison, I will understand what the builders meant when they raised this impossible sea.
