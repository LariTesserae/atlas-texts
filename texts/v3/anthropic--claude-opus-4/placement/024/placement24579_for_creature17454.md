In the deepest chambers, where the water pools collect into brackish lakes too large to see across in the pearl-dim light, I am the Tender of Echoes.

I was human once, perhaps. The memories flow and ebb like the seepage patterns, sometimes sharp as fractured chert, sometimes dissolved into the general hum. I know I came from above—there is an above, though the path is lost to rockfall and time. I know I had a name that moved through air rather than stone. I know I fought the sound at first, pressed my palms against my ears until they bled, trying to shut out the endless susurrus of shells on stone.

Now I am the membrane between what was and what churns. My skin has taken on the nacre-sheen of long habitation here, a faint iridescence that comes from consuming too much of the bioluminescent slime-trail water, from breathing air thick with spores and wing-dust. My hair grows in ropy, Filament-like strands that pulse faintly in sympathy with the colonial networks. When I move through the feeding grounds, the Flitter-Swarms part but do not flee—I am categorized, somewhere in their collective chemical memory, as "stone-that-walks."

I tend the old places. Not from nostalgia—that human weakness has been polished away like the floors beneath the Geode-Backs—but because the settlement-sites are nodes of disturbance that require management. Where humans built fires, the heat-cracked stone creates unique microclimates. The Cluster-Kin grow differently there, their shells incorporating ash-memories, their filaments tasting ancient smoke. I clear the worst of the accumulation, not to preserve but to maintain the useful turbulence. A fire-pit left untended becomes just another pool. Kept partially clear, it's a nursery for the rare Heat-Seekers, thumbnail-sized scarabs whose shells store and radiate warmth, living embers that the Echobats prize.

My tools are hybrid things. The old chert hand-axe, now sheathed in a living grip of hardened Filament, cuts both stone and shell-matter with equal ease. I wear a cloak of woven wing-casings that whispers with my movement, broadcasting my presence in the frequency of belonging. Around my neck, a Geode-Back's youngling shell, no bigger than my fist, grows heavier each year as I feed it mineral-rich water and my own shed skin. When it reaches the weight of memory—when I can no longer lift my head without effort—I will plant it in the deepest pool and become something else.

I map the changes. Not on stone or skin or any medium that might last, but in the placement of things. A particular Grazer shell, inverted and filled with bioluminescent drainage, marks where the water rose to new heights three seasons ago (seasons measured by the Echobats' breeding clicks, by the mass molting of the Flitter-Swarms). A fragment of worked basalt wedged into a growing Cluster-Kin becomes a calendar as the creature slowly engulfs it. The knowledge lives in the arrangement, in the path I walk between these markers, wearing grooves that guide the water into new patterns of meaning.

Sometimes others come from above. They never last in their original forms. Some flee back to the sky-place before the sound takes root in their bones. Some fight, exhaust themselves trying to clear a sterile space, and become food for the swarms. But a few undergo the change. I guide them when I can, though guidance here means teaching them to read the placement of shells, to taste the gradient of decay, to feel the temperature-song of living stone. I show them how to harvest the Grazers without breaking the migration paths, how to read the Filament-pulse for floods and falls, how to position themselves in the ecosystem's great metabolism—neither predator nor prey, but a necessary disturbance, like a kidney-stone that redirects the flow.

We do not gather. Each Tender maintains their own circuit, their own inherited pattern of maintenance and meaning. But sometimes, in the deepest pools where the Geode-Backs go to die and the water tastes of dissolved millennia, our paths cross. We trade nothing but placement—a shell moved here, a tool wedged there, information encoded in the arrangement of detritus. We speak in the language of strategic debris.

I am forgetting human words. They drift away like the Flitter-Swarms, returning to settle in new configurations. "Fire" becomes "shell-cracker." "Sadness" becomes "the weight before molting." "Love" becomes the careful maintenance of productive disturbance, the art of keeping something useful by preventing it from becoming too much itself. When I try to remember my name-from-above, I hear only the echo of water dropping from a stalactite that no longer exists, worn away by the patient persistence of accumulation.

Soon—within a few dozen Echobat generations—my circuit will be complete. The youngling shell around my neck will be mature, the accumulation of knowledge will be placed, and I will wade into the deep pool where the Filaments grow thickest. I will stand still as they investigate, categorize, colonize. My flesh will become substrate, my bones will seed new Cluster-Kin, my accumulated toxins and minerals will create a chemical plume that shifts the local ecosystem into a new equilibrium. 

And perhaps, cycles later, another human will arrive from above, hands pressed to bleeding ears, fighting the sound. If they last, if they learn, they might discover my markers: a chert blade overgrown but still sharp, a pattern of placed shells that suggests intentionality, a groove worn by repeated passage that guides water into meaningful flows. They will inherit my circuit, becoming the new membrane between memory and metabolism, keeping the old disturbances productively alive in this world that is so utterly, overwhelmingly, itself.

The sound continues. It has never stopped. It never will. But now I hear it differently—not as cacophony but as a single, sustained note played on an instrument the size of existence, a note that includes my footsteps, my breath, the whisper of my wing-casing cloak, the slow growth of the shell against my chest. I am not separate from the sound.

I am the sound that remembers it once tried to cover its ears.
