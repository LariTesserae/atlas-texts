In the bowels of Pylon Seven-Seven, where the metal groans with a century's worth of thermal expansion and contraction, I make my nest. They call me a Wire Witch, though I am neither wire nor witch—merely something that has learned to live between states, as all things must in this place of perpetual transition.

My body, if it can be called that, is a confluence of salvage and symbiosis. Where others see corrosion, I see opportunity. The rust that flakes from the pylon's inner walls has become my skin, a living patina that shifts from burnt orange to deep umber depending on the humidity. My bones are hollow conduits scavenged from forgotten machinery, and through them flows not blood but the condensation I've learned to steal from the air itself. I am a creature of recycling, my very existence a closed loop of moisture and metal.

I was human once, or something close to it. A child of the city who wandered too deep into the pylon maze, chasing the ghost-light of a Glasswing through a crack in the metal. But the pylons change those who enter them. The residual energies that still pulse through their cores—electromagnetic echoes of whatever industry once thrived here—they speak to certain kinds of flesh, certain kinds of desperation. They offer bargains written in corrosion and static.

My fingers have elongated into tools: one ends in a copper probe that can taste electrical currents, another splits into fiber-optic tendrils that see heat and moisture in spectrums the unchanged cannot imagine. My hair, once black as the volcanic glass outside, has become a network of ultra-fine wires that sense vibrations through the pylon's structure. When the wind howls through the city above, I feel it as a symphony played on ten thousand metal strings.

I am not alone in here. The pylons are ecosystems unto themselves, vertical worlds hidden from the sun. There are others like me—the Shifted, we sometimes call ourselves, when we remember words. We trade in currencies of knowledge: the location of an untapped condensation vein, the frequency that calms the screaming in the metal, the proper way to splice oneself into the pylon's dying nervous system without losing what little humanity remains.

But mostly, I tend my garden.

Yes, a garden, here in the heartless metal dark. Not of plants—nothing that simple survives in Pylon City—but of possibilities. I cultivate colonies of the metallic fungi, coaxing them into new configurations with careful applications of current and condensation. Each growth is unique: some produce spores that can filter toxins from water, others excrete a film that prevents rust, still others glow with a soft bioluminescence that keeps the darkness at bay. My greatest achievement is a strain that sings—actually vibrates at frequencies that match the pylon's resonance, creating pockets of stability in the ever-shifting structure.

The children of the city above know of us. They leave offerings at the pylon bases: broken circuit boards, vials of precious water, sometimes a Glasswing wing wrapped in wire. In exchange, we sometimes leave gifts of our own—a cluster of purifying fungi, a shard of metal treated to never rust, a whispered secret about which pylon will sing tomorrow's rain. It is an economy of mutual need, a symbiosis between those who cling to the sun and those who have embraced the dark.

Sometimes I climb to where the pylon's skin has worn thin, where gaps in the metal allow slivers of that brutal sun to pierce the darkness. I press my hybrid face to these wounds and watch the city's children go about their lives. I see myself in them—the determination, the adaptability, the fierce grip on existence. But I also see what I've become, reflected in their quick movements away from the pylon's shadows, in the wards they wear against the things that scratch within the metal.

I am guardian and ghost, gardener and gargoyle. My transformation is both loss and liberation—I have traded the simple struggle for water and shade for a more complex existence, one measured in electrical pulses and fungal growth rates, in the slow vocabulary of rust and the patient accumulation of condensation. I have become part of the city's immune system, a beneficial parasite in its body of metal and desperation.

At night, when the temperature plummets and the metal contracts, squeezing our spaces smaller, we Shifted sing. Not with voices—those are long gone—but with the harmonics of our altered bodies, creating frequencies that vibrate through the entire pylon network. It's our way of saying: we are here, we persist, we transform but do not die. The city's children, huddled in their shacks, hear our song as the settling of metal, the whisper of wind through rust. But some of them—the ones who might one day join us—hear it for what it truly is:

An invitation. A warning. A promise that in this place where water is prayer and rust is scripture, there are always new ways to survive.
