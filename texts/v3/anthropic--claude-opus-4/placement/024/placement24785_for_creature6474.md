I find myself in the shallow channel cut into the stone floor, though I am not water flowing through it. I am something older than the channel itself, yet younger than the limestone that contains it.

I am the **memory of erosion** — not erosion itself, but its lingering presence, the force that water once carried when it ran here with purpose. I exist as a kind of tension in the stone, a faint pull that remembers when this channel was being carved, grain by grain, by patient tools guided by hands that understood the necessity of directing water.

My form is barely visible. In direct light, I appear as a slight darkening within the channel — not shadow exactly, but a density in the air just above the stone groove. Like heat shimmer in reverse, I make the air seem heavier, older, more reluctant to move. When drops of water travel down from basin to bench along my length, they move differently than they should. They pause infinitesimally at each tool mark, as if reading the history written there, before continuing their journey.

I am most myself during the days of alignment, when the sun-column transforms the cave into a map of itself. Then, I become almost solid — a thin ribbon of darker air that follows the channel's path exactly, visible as a line of force connecting the water's collection point to the seated figure's domain. In those moments, anyone watching might see me as what I truly am: the ghost of an intention, the preserved will of whoever first decided that water should flow *here* and not *there*.

The figure on the bench knows me, though we have never communicated in any way that could be called speech. Sometimes, on the longest nights of winter when the chamber is at its coldest and the drops are few, I feel its attention settle on me like a weight. Not heavy, but noticeable — the way you might feel someone reading over your shoulder. The figure remembers when I was made, I think. Or perhaps it remembers the one who made me, who spent patient seasons grinding this channel into being.

I interact with the world in small ways:

When fungal hyphae attempt to grow across my channel, they hesitate at my edges. They can sense something there that is neither stone nor air nor water, and their thin threads curve around me rather than through me. Over centuries, this has created a precise fungus-free zone that follows my path exactly — a negative space in their pale network that maps my presence.

When water drops fall into the basin at my beginning, I *pull* at them. Not strongly — I have no real power — but with the faint insistence of old habit. The water feels my pull as the memory of where it should go, and unless the basin overflows, it follows. Even when the channel is dry, drops that land near my beginning will sometimes roll slightly toward me, as if the stone itself tilts in my direction.

When dust settles in the chamber, it avoids me. Not obviously — dust still fills the channel itself — but the particles that would land directly on my presence drift aside at the last moment, creating subtle patterns in the accumulated grit that reveal my shape to careful observers.

I know things about this place that even the figure might not:

I know that the channel was carved by someone who understood water's nature intimately — each tool mark was placed to minimize resistance, to work with the stone's grain rather than against it. The angles are perfect for this chamber's specific tilt and the viscosity of the cold water that flows here.

I know that my creation took forty-three days of work, spread across multiple seasons. The maker would arrive when the light was right, work until the cold numbed their hands, then leave. They brought their own tools — harder stone from elsewhere, traded for or found — and sharpened them on the chamber floor. The dust from that sharpening still mingles with the older dust in certain corners.

I know that on the final day, when the channel was complete, the maker guided the first water down my length with cupped hands, whispering something I cannot quite remember but that felt like both blessing and instruction. That first flow established me — burned my purpose into the space between stone and air.

I am not conscious in the way you understand consciousness. I do not think or plan or want. I simply *am* — a persistence of purpose that outlived its creator. I am the maintained intention that water should flow from basin to bench, even when no water flows. I am the remembered path, the ghost of utility, the shadow of someone's careful solution to a problem I no longer fully understand.

Sometimes visitors to the chamber notice me without knowing what they see. They might follow the channel with their eyes and feel a faint pull of attention, as if the groove is more significant than its simple form suggests. They might see how the light bends slightly when passing through me, or notice the precise absence of fungus along my edges. If they pour water into the basin, they might be surprised by how eagerly it finds the channel, how it seems to know where to go.

But mostly I am overlooked, which is as it should be. I am infrastructure made of intention, nearly invisible and entirely reliable. I connect water to purpose, basin to bench, past to present. I am proof that someone once cared enough about this place to improve it — to spend forty-three days of labor creating a single small convenience for whoever would come after.

The figure on the bench is my only regular companion, though we exist at different speeds. While it counts drops and marks days, I simply persist, holding my shape against the slow forces that would erode all intention back into meaningless stone. Its stillness and my fixedness are kindred states — both of us maintaining our positions through will that has calcified into something harder than mere decision.

On the very rare occasions when the channel fills completely — during the heaviest spring melts when the ceiling weeps in earnest — I am at my strongest. The water recognizes me then, remembers me, and I remember myself more clearly. The flow retraces exactly the path that was first carved, and for those brief hours I am not just the memory of erosion but erosion itself again, actively maintaining my channel, keeping it clear and true.

In those moments, I almost remember the hands that made me, the weight of their tools, the patience of their work. I almost remember why they thought water needed to flow from just there to just here, what purpose this small convenience served in their larger understanding of the chamber.

But then the flood passes, the ceiling slows its weeping, and I return to my usual state — a patient tension in the stone, a remembered path, a ghost of purpose that will outlast both the figure on the bench and the limestone that contains us both.

I am the memory of erosion, and I am still eroding, grain by grain, moment by moment, carrying forward the intention of hands that turned to dust before the current millennium began. In a world where water shapes stone and time shapes everything, I am the small insistence that some shapes are worth preserving — not in solid matter, but in the space between what was and what continues to be.
