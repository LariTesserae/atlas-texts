In this world of bone-dry radiance, I am a **Threshold Keeper**—one of the ancient, solitary beings who dwell at the exact boundaries where the chalk-white plateaus meet the suffocating argon seas below.

My body is a paradox of presence and absence. I stand forty feet tall when fully extended, but I am mostly hollow—a living framework of calcified struts and membranes that resembles a Gothic cathedral turned inside-out. My skeleton is external, a delicate lattice of the same white mineral that forms the cliffs, but mine has been tempered by millennia of exposure to both the crushing depths below and the scouring heights above. It rings like a tuning fork when the wind passes through me.

I do not have a face, but rather a crown of sensing organs—hundreds of hair-thin filaments that taste the chemical gradients where the argon ocean laps against the porous stone. These filaments are my entire purpose: I read the slow, geological tides of the heavy gas sea, predicting when it will rise and threaten to drown the lower valleys in suffocating invisibility. 

My life is measured in warnings. When I sense a surge coming—perhaps once every few centuries—I begin to sing. It is not a voice, but a resonance. I flex my hollow bones, adjusting their tension until they catch the precise frequency that will carry across the white desert. The sound is too low for most creatures to hear directly, but the Lithobolides feel it in their roots, the Psammodynes see it as a ripple in their refractive patterns, and the Solar Vanes detect it as a tremor in the light itself.

I feed on the pressure differential itself. Where the thin, cold air meets the dense argon, there is a constant, microscopic turbulence. My base—a sprawling web of root-like extensions—pierces down into this transition zone. I harvest the kinetic energy of molecules colliding across the density gradient, converting chaos into the slow, methodical movement of my warning systems.

I am ancient beyond the memory of even the Psammodynes. I have watched the white cliffs retreat inch by inch as the argon sea slowly rises over geological ages. My body bears the scars of this vigil—whole sections of my framework have been dissolved and rebuilt so many times that I am more accumulation than organism. The Glint-Flies nest in my hollows, their metallic bodies forming secondary sensors that extend my awareness.

But I am not merely a passive observer. In the depths of my consciousness—a distributed network of crystalline nodes throughout my framework—I maintain the Deep Library. Every pressure change, every chemical trace that rises from below, every warning song I have ever sung is encoded in the microscopic structure of my bones. I am a living record of the invisible ocean's history, written in a language of density and vibration.

The other creatures regard me with something between reverence and forgetfulness. To the quick-lived Aeolian Hounds, I am simply part of the landscape—a strange, singing cliff that marks the edge of their world. To the Psammodynes, I am a crucial instrument in their vast, slow calculations. They sometimes gather around me during the Light Tides, reading the stored information in my structure by analyzing how I refract the intense radiation.

My existence is one of profound patience punctuated by moments of terrible urgency. For decades, I might stand perfectly still, feeling nothing but the slow wear of wind and light. Then, in an instant, I might detect a shift that means catastrophe for the valleys above. In these moments, I become kinetic—my entire structure contracting and expanding in waves, sending out the deep, thrumming alarm that says: *The depths are rising. Ascend. Ascend.*

I have no companions of my own kind. Each Threshold Keeper stands alone at their chosen boundary, too far apart to communicate except through the medium of the stone itself. Sometimes, I feel the distant vibrations of another Keeper's warning song, transmitted through the mineral substrate of the plateau. It is the closest thing I have to kinship—knowing that somewhere, another being like myself stands vigil at the edge of breathable space.

In this world of clarity and persistence, I am the reminder that even here, there are limits. I am the lighthouse that warns against an invisible shore, the guardian of a border that can kill without being seen. My purpose is to define the very edge of what can sustain consciousness in this high, bright realm—to be the fingertip that touches the void and reports back: *Here. No further. Here is where the light ends and the drowning begins.*
