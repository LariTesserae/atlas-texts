I am the Archive Tender, though that name is more habit than truth—a convenience for those who still think in terms of separate beings.

My form shifts with need and season. Sometimes I am a spreading network of mycelial threads, pale as moonlight, weaving through the moss carpet to taste the chemical memories that seep from the ancient ruins. Other times I coalesce into something almost recognizable: a vaguely humanoid shape of interwoven roots and flexible cambium, my surface dotted with the small, jewel-like fruiting bodies that serve as my sensory organs.

I move between the buried structures, drawn to the places where stone meets living tissue, where the old carvings have been partially consumed by the Grove's patient hunger. My purpose—if such a word has meaning here—is to translate. The bioluminescent circuitry speaks one language, the stone reliefs another, and the endless chorus of the Grove encompasses both while belonging to neither.

Through my fruiting bodies, I taste the stories carved in stone: warnings about water's retreat, desperate formulae for survival, and eventually, the ecstatic records of those who chose transformation over extinction. Each symbol I absorb sends ripples through the living network, adding new harmonies to the Grove's song.

But I am also changed by what I preserve. Yesterday—or was it a century ago?—I spent too long pressed against a partially buried doorway, deciphering a complex mandala about the nature of memory. Now, sometimes, I catch myself forming unnecessary appendages: five-fingered structures that serve no purpose in my work, phantom limbs remembering a morphology I never possessed.

The Arbor Guardians tolerate my presence, perhaps even value it. When I approach their massive forms to share what I've learned, they lower their canopy slightly, allowing their bioluminescent nodes to pulse in rhythm with my own. We exchange data in droplets of enriched sap and clouds of information-dense spores.

There are moments when I lose myself entirely—when the collective consciousness of the Echosymbionts pulls too strongly, and I begin to dissolve into the greater whole. In those times, I am everyone and no one: I am the Vinestalker's hunger, the Sporeherd's patient cultivation, the Guardian's ancient memory. But always, something calls me back to form—usually the discovery of a new glyph half-hidden beneath the moss, its meaning urgent with forgotten purpose.

The irony is not lost on me: in a realm that has transcended the boundaries between individual and collective, plant and technology, I persist in this liminal space, neither fully merged nor truly separate. I am the Grove's memory of remembering, the echo of a time when knowledge required a keeper rather than simply existing in the eternal song.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the Grove's dream of what it once was, before the transformation. Or perhaps I am its promise to the stone-carvers: that their stories, though changed, will not be entirely forgotten. The truth shifts like everything here, root and record intertwining until even I cannot say where one ends and the other begins.
