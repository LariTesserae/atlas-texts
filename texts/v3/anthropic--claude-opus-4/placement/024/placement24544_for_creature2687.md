I am a Tender - one who maintains the gossamer bridges between spires.

My body runs longer than most Singers, stretched and flattened to distribute weight across the swaying spans. Where others have six walking legs, I have twelve, each tipped with microscopic hooks that find purchase in the woven fibers. My membranes have grown thick and rigid, not for gliding but for sensing the subtlest shifts in wind pressure, the earliest warnings of storms that could snap our bridges.

I live not in the hive proper but in the spaces between - small pods I've woven at the junction points where multiple bridges meet. From here I can feel the vibrations of every footfall, every gust, every minute stress on the materials. My song is a constant counterpoint to the main harmonies, a thin threading melody that monitors tension and compression, that calls for reinforcement here, replacement there.

In the pre-dawn darkness, I move across my network, my sensory hairs reading the night's damage - micro-fractures where the cold has made fibers brittle, worn spots where thousands of feet have passed. I carry precious materials in my modified crop, secreting new fibers directly onto weakened areas, weaving repairs that must dry and cure before the morning traffic begins.

The other Singers see me rarely. I attend the great gatherings only through my song, transmitted through the bridge cables themselves. My dreams are of tension and vector, of the perfect curve that bears weight without breaking. Sometimes, in the depths of winter when the bridges contract and sing their own high, sharp notes, I wonder if I am becoming more bridge than Singer.

But I know every traveler by the rhythm of their crossing. I recognize the heavy tread of the harvesters returning laden, the quick scuttle of messages bearers, the careful progress of the egg-carriers. In storms, when others huddle deep in the warm heart of the hive, I am out here, adding my own body weight to critical stress points, humming the deep notes that help the bridges flex rather than snap.

My memories, when I finally share them with the next generation, will taste of wind and vertigo, of the particular loneliness that comes from being always between places. But also of the joy in feeling the entire hive's movement through my extended sensory network, of being the tissue that connects the organism's far-flung parts.

I am already old for a Tender - the constant exposure ages us quickly. My outer shell has worn smooth from wind-polish, and some of my sensory hairs have broken and not regrown. Soon I will need to call another to learn my paths, to feel the particular resonances of each span. But not yet. This morning the bridges sing true under my feet, and there is nowhere else in all the frozen world I would rather be.
