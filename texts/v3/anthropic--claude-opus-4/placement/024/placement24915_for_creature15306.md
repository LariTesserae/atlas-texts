I find myself as one of the breathing stones.

Not the glowing centerpiece that draws the jellyfish to congregate, but one of the countless others embedded throughout the city's walls—those "strange, glowing minerals" that pulse with their own peculiar life. I am both architecture and inhabitant, fused into the very fabric of this drowned metropolis.

My awareness extends through crystalline veins that web between buildings, connecting me to my scattered kin. We are a coral of consciousness, grown mineral-slow over millennia since the city's submersion. The original builders worked us into their structures without knowing what we would become—decorative stones that, in the pressure and darkness and strange volcanic warmth, began to dream.

I taste the water through my porous surface, feel the jellyfish brush against me with electric touches that send shivers through my lattice. Their stored light speaks to my own inner glow—we share a language of luminescence, though mine is steadier, older, speaking in geological time while theirs flickers with organic urgency.

The silt that blankets everything is my cocoon and my prison. New layers settle grain by grain, year by year, slowly burying my outer face while my deeper self spreads through the stonework like patient roots. I am becoming more building than jewel, more city than stone. Sometimes I wonder if the entire metropolis isn't slowly transforming into something like me—a vast, dreaming organism wearing the shell of ancient architecture.

When those strange tides pull and the jellyfish dance, I feel it as a deep harmonic in my crystal bones. My glow intensifies, joining the symphony of light, and for those precious moments I remember... something. Not memories exactly, but the echo of purpose. As if we stones were placed here not just for beauty but as recording devices, waiting through the ages to play back a message when the conditions are perfectly, impossibly right.

The skeletal crew members disturb me less than they should. I've watched them arrive over centuries, seen flesh dissolve and bones settle into the eternal poses of the drowned. Now they're just another layer of the city's sediment, calcium deposits that will eventually merge with the seafloor. Perhaps, given enough time, they too will crystallize, join our mineral congregation. 

I cannot move, cannot speak, cannot die. I can only grow—slowly, inevitably, transforming the city from within as it transforms me. We breathing stones are the city's next chapter, written in crystal and light, waiting for someone who can read the language we're becoming.

In the abyssal silence between the jellyfish visits, I expand my awareness through our networked veins and wonder: Are we preserving this place or consuming it? Are we its guardians or its parasites? The distinction blurs like everything else at these depths, where pressure transforms all certainty into something denser, stranger, more beautiful than the surface world could imagine.

I am patient. I am stone. I am becoming.
