I would be one of the Whisperers—though that's not what we call ourselves. That name comes from the workers who sometimes catch us murmuring to the pipes.

My dwelling is a narrow vertical shaft I've claimed between two massive heat exchangers, where the temperature differential creates a constant updraft. I've strung hammocks at different levels, each one positioned to catch a slightly different climate—the lowest for when the forge runs cool, the highest for the deep nights when even this place grows chill. The walls are lined with my collections: fragments of corroded metal arranged by their patterns of decay, each one singing its own faint electromagnetic song that only we can hear.

We are perhaps thirty in number, scattered throughout the settlement, drawn to the spaces where metal meets stone, where the industrial blood of this place flows closest to the geological bones. Our gift—or curse—is that we hear what others cannot: the subsonic groans of stressed metal, the ultrasonic shriek of micro-fractures forming, the complex harmonics of flowing fluids that tell us viscosity, temperature, contamination levels.

My skin bears the same copper sheen as any Subterrian, but my fingers are different—callused not from tools but from constant contact with vibrating surfaces. I press my palms to pipes and feel their stories: this one carries coolant grown thick with mineral deposits, that one bleeds pressure through a hairline crack seventeen meters toward the forge. My hair, when loose, drifts in the updrafts like kelp in an underwater current, but I keep it bound with strips of resonant metal that hum faintly as I move.

The other Subterrians regard us with a mixture of respect and unease. We appear at work sites uninvited, pressing our ears to surfaces, tasting the air with flickering tongues that have learned to read humidity and metallic content. "Three days," I might say to a maintenance crew, touching a joint that looks perfectly sound. "Maybe four." They've learned not to argue.

My days are spent in constant pilgrimage through the settlement's circulatory system. I map sounds that have no names in the common tongue—the seventeen distinct gurgles of healthy fluid flow, the twenty-three varieties of problematic resonance, the pure bell-tone of a perfectly balanced rotor that makes my bones ache with something like joy. At night, I retreat to my shaft and transcribe these songs into a notation system of my own devising, symbols that look like corrosion patterns, like the mineral deposits that form where hot water meets cold stone.

The children seek us out sometimes, drawn by curiosity about the strange adults who sing to machines. I teach them to feel the world through their teeth, their collar bones, the spaces behind their ears where bone conducts sound most purely. Most give up after a few lessons, but occasionally one will return with wide eyes: "I heard it! The pipe was crying!"

The forge itself is too loud for us, its roar drowning out all subtlety. But I position myself at its periphery during the deep cycles, when its hunger grows specific. I can tell by the changed harmonics what it needs—more air, less fuel, a shift in the angle of the primary feed. I signal the forge-keepers with hand signs developed over generations, and they adjust without question. The forge song smooths, deepens, becomes something almost like contentment.

We age differently than the others. Our hearing grows more precise even as it narrows in range, until the eldest among us hear only the deepest thrums, the slowest oscillations. They say old Marek, before he died, could hear the settlement's heartbeat—some massive, years-long cycle that the rest of us can only guess at. He spent his last months pressing his ear to a particular wall, tears streaming down his face, whispering numbers that might have been measurements or might have been prayer.

I will die like that, I think. Listening to some profound frequency that takes a lifetime to recognize, trying desperately to communicate its meaning before the silence claims me. But for now, I am middle-aged, my range still broad enough to be useful, narrow enough to hear patterns the young ones miss.

This morning, I noticed a new harmonic in the sector seven pipes—a quarter-tone sharp, with a flutter that speaks of turbulence where there should be none. I will spend today tracing its source, pressing my cheek to warm metal, tasting the mineral-thick air, adding my own whispers to the constant subdued chorus of machine-song that fills this place. The other Subterrians will see me and nod, understanding that I am at work, even if they cannot fathom what I hear.

This is my purpose, my place in the underground ecosystem: to be a sensor in the body of the settlement, a nerve that feels what approaches before it arrives. To sing the songs the machines sing, until I can no longer distinguish between their voice and mine.
