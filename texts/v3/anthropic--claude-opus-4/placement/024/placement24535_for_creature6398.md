faint depressions where the skin has been abraded by years of contact with wet stone. Their joints show the slight thickening that comes from a lifetime of moving through resistance—knees that have bent ten thousand times against the weight of water, shoulders that have pressed through narrow spaces where the ceiling dips low.

### 2. Movement and Grace

The Svarish do not walk as surface dwellers walk. They *flow*.

Each step is a careful placement, feeling for the stability of the stone beneath before committing weight. They move with their center low, hands often trailing along ridges for balance, testing the temperature and movement of air currents as they go.

When crossing deeper water—the pools that gather where basins overflow—they do not swim but *wade-float*, using the density of their bodies to sink just enough to maintain contact with the floor while letting the water support part of their weight. Their movements then become even slower, more deliberate, like dancers moving through honey.

They have learned to read the invisible currents in the air layer. A Svarish moving quickly (which is still slow by surface standards) will duck and weave around pressure zones that others cannot see, following paths of least resistance that snake between the basins. To watch them is to see someone navigating an invisible maze with perfect confidence.

When they must move through the water columns—those vertical streams that hang from cracks in the ceiling—they do so with practiced economy. They hold their breath, seal their nostrils with a practiced flex of muscle, and push through with strong, steady strokes that carry them to the other side before the cold can sink too deep into their bones.

### 3. Breath and Voice

The Svarish have learned to breathe in layers.

When the ocean ceiling is high and the air layer thick, they breathe deeply, storing oxygen in their blood and tissues. When pressure increases and the air grows thin, they shift to shallow, efficient breaths, conserving what they have.

They can hold their breath for remarkable lengths of time—not through any supernatural ability, but through generations of practice and adaptation. A Svarish child learns to hold their breath before they learn to speak, practicing in the shallow pools, timing themselves by the slow drip of water from familiar cracks.

Their voices reflect this economy of breath. They speak in short phrases, with long pauses between, letting meaning accumulate like sediment. Their language is full of compressed sounds—clicks and stops that use minimal air, whistles that can carry through water as well as air, low hums that resonate in the chest without requiring open vowels.

When they sing—and they do sing—it is not the sustained melody of surface songs but something more like the rhythm of dripping water: short notes repeated, variations on a simple theme, patterns that can be picked up and continued by another voice when the first runs out of breath.

### 4. Eyes and Seeing

Svarish eyes are large, dark, and patient.

In the dim twilight of the Undershelf, they have learned to see not by direct light but by the accumulation of faint impressions. They can hold their gaze steady on a single point for long minutes, letting the barely-visible patterns of phosphorescence and reflected glow build up in their vision until shapes emerge from the darkness.

Their pupils dilate so wide that the iris nearly disappears, leaving eyes that seem all darkness with thin rings of color—deep blue or grey-green like the ocean ceiling above. These eyes can detect the faintest variations in the blue-green glow that filters through the water, reading changes in pressure and weather far above by the subtle shifts in underwater light.

They see movement better than stillness. A Svarish can spot the slow drift of phosphorescent motes in a water column from across a wide basin, or notice the barely-perceptible shift of a shadow on the ceiling that indicates something large passing overhead. But they might walk past a perfectly still object several times before recognizing its shape.

Their peripheral vision is extraordinary, adapted to detect danger or change from any direction in this world where up and down are equally important. They rarely look directly at each other when speaking, preferring to keep their gaze moving, taking in the whole of their surroundings while listening.

### 5. Memory and Time

The Svarish experience time like the stones experience erosion—slowly, in layers, with patience.

They do not count days or years as surface dwellers do. Instead, they mark time by the deepening of basins, the slow migration of water channels, the gradual thickening of mineral deposits on familiar ridges. A Svarish might say, "When that basin was only to my ankle," to indicate a time decades past, or "Before the white crust reached the third ridge," to mark a more recent event.

Their memories are not linear narratives but spatial accumulations. They remember by place—this is where the ceiling collapsed during the great settling, this is where the first pale crawler was seen, this is where grandmother's grandmother found the smooth stone that still marks our gathering place. Walking through the Undershelf with a Svarish is to walk through layers of memory made tangible.

They have extraordinary recall for patterns—the way water flowed after a particular ceiling swell, the exact sound of dripping from a specific crack, the precise color of phosphorescence in a remembered season. But they may forget conversations from the day before, or fail to remember whether they have eaten recently. The immediate and temporary slides away; only what repeats and accumulates remains.

### 6. Family and Kinship

Svarish families are not defined by blood alone but by proximity and patience.

Those who share a cluster of basins, who have learned to move in harmony through the same passages, who breathe in complementary rhythms so that someone is always listening while others hold their breath—these become family, whether born to it or grown into it over time.

Children are rare and precious. The Undershelf is not an easy place for the very young, who have not yet learned to read pressure or hold their breath for long passages. Svarish children are carried for their first years, strapped to backs or chests with woven harnesses of water-plant fiber, their small bodies learning the rhythm of movement before they take their own steps.

Child-rearing is communal by necessity. While one adult moves through a water column, another holds the children. While one searches for food in the darker reaches, others tend the young in the safer, brighter spaces near the light shafts. A child may have five or six people they call by the breath-sound that means "parent," and all are equally true.

The elderly are honored for their deep knowledge of patterns. As Svarish age, they tend to move less but observe more, stationing themselves at good vantage points where they can watch the ceiling and alert others to changes in pressure or the passage of large shadows above. Their accumulated memories become the tribe's archive, consulted through patient conversation that may stretch across days.

### 7. Food and Sustenance

Eating in the Undershelf is an act of patience and careful gathering.

The Svarish diet consists primarily of what can be filtered, scraped, or slowly collected from their environment. They harvest the thin films of algae that grow on sunlit stones, scraping with sharpened shells or stone tools, gathering enough over hours to make a small meal.

From the water columns, they collect the drifting, phosphorescent organisms—tiny jellies and translucent shrimps no bigger than a fingernail. This is done with fine nets woven from their own hair, which holds its shape in water and can trap the smallest creatures. A full net might take days to accumulate enough for eating.

The pale crawlers that trace paths through the silt are occasional prey, but they are difficult to catch and harder to find. Svarish hunters must follow their faint trails for hours, predicting where they will surface based on water temperature and flow patterns. When caught, they are shared among many, their segments providing a rare taste of denser protein.

More common are the various deposits that accumulate in basin edges—mineral-rich sediments that, while not exactly food, can be consumed in small quantities to provide necessary salts and trace elements. The Svarish have learned which colors and textures are safe, which will sustain, and which must be avoided.

Water itself is never scarce but must be chosen carefully. They know which ceiling drips carry the cleanest flow, which pools have sat long enough for sediment to settle, which water columns connect to cleaner currents above. They drink by cupping hands beneath familiar drips or by pressing their mouths to certain stones where condensation gathers pure.

Meals are communal but unscheduled. When someone returns with a net of phosphorescent catch or a successful crawler hunt, others gather. They share without ceremony, each taking small portions, chewing slowly, making the sparse food last. Eating is often accompanied by the telling of patterns—where the best algae is growing this season, which water columns are running clear, where new crawlers have been spotted.

### 8. Language and Communication

The Svarish language is built from the sounds that carry in their world.

Basic communication uses clicks and stops—sounds that can be made with minimal breath and that carry well through both air and water. A sharp click might mean "attention," while a series of softer clicks could indicate direction or distance. These sounds can be made even while holding breath underwater, using the tongue against the palate.

For more complex ideas, they use low hums and resonant tones that vibrate through chest and stone. These can be sustained without much breath and can be felt as well as heard, useful when visibility is particularly poor. Different tones carry different basic meanings—a low, steady hum for safety, a rising tone for question, a falling tone for completion or ending.

Whistles, made by controlling breath through various mouth shapes, carry the furthest and are used for distance communication or warning. Each family group has its own recognition whistle, and individuals develop personal variations that friends learn to recognize even across a wide basin.

But much Svarish communication is non-verbal. They read the mottling of each other's skin, the set of shoulders, the rhythm of breath. A hand trailing in water might indicate the speaker is remembering something water-related; a foot pressed firmly to stone might emphasize certainty. These physical cues layer with vocal sounds to create meaning.

They have no written language as surface dwellers would understand it. Instead, they arrange stones in meaningful patterns at important locations—warnings about unstable ceiling, markers for good gathering spots, memorials for significant events. These arrangements are three-dimensional, using height and shadow as much as position, and must be read by moving around them, understanding how they change from different angles.

### 9. Crafts and Making

The Svarish are not great builders, but they are patient shapers.

Their primary tools are stones selected for natural edges and slowly modified by grinding against other stones. A good working stone might be passed down through generations, its edge maintained and gradually perfected, its weight and balance known intimately by those who use it.

They weave nets and carrying bags from their own hair, which maintains its strength even when wet. The weaving is done over months, adding new strands as hair grows and is cut, creating intricate patterns that are both functional and unique to the maker. The phosphorescent quality of the hair means these objects glow faintly in the dark, making them easier to find and use.

From water-plant fibers and crawler shells, they create flexible cords and straps. These are used for securing tools, creating harnesses for carrying children, and sometimes for marking paths through complex areas by stretching them between stable stones.

They shape the softer stones found near water flows into bowls and cups, wearing them down by patient grinding with harder stones and sand. Each vessel is unique, shaped to fit its maker's hands, and often passed down through families. The making of a new bowl is a meditation—hours spent in repetitive motion, feeling the stone gradually yield to intention.

Perhaps their greatest craft is the cultivation of phosphorescent organisms. They have learned to encourage growth in certain pools by adjusting water flow and mineral content, creating living lights that mark important paths or gathering places. This is knowledge held closely by certain families, passed down through careful observation and patient teaching.

### 10. Beliefs and Understanding

The Svarish worldview is shaped by their position—beneath, between, enduring.

They believe the ocean ceiling is alive, a vast being whose moods and movements affect all life below. They do not worship it exactly, but they respect it as one respects a powerful and unpredictable neighbor. They read its patterns, learn its rhythms, and accept that their lives exist by its sufferance.

The shadows that pass overhead are understood to be other beings, perhaps like themselves but adapted to the water above. Some Svarish believe these are ancestors who have learned to live fully in the water; others think they are entirely different creatures whose purposes are unknowable. The shadows are watched and their patterns remembered, but they are not feared—they are simply another layer of the world.

Death, to the Svarish, is a settling. As silt settles in basins, as minerals settle on stone, so too do the dead settle into the world. Bodies are placed in deep basins and weighted with stones, allowed to sink into the silt where they will eventually become part of the floor itself. They believe that consciousness settles more slowly—that the dead continue to experience for some time, their thoughts slowing and spreading like sediment in still water until they become part of the great pattern of stone and flow.

They have no concept of heights or heavens. Up is simply more water, more pressure, more darkness. If there is something beyond the ocean ceiling, it is too distant to matter. Their sacred direction is not up but *through*—through the water columns, through difficult passages, through time itself.

Change is understood as inevitable but slow. The Svarish do not resist change but they do not seek it either. They adapt as the basins deepen, as the ceiling shifts, as new cracks open and old ones close. Patience is not just a virtue but a survival strategy—those who can wait, who can observe without acting, who can endure without complaint, these are the ones who thrive.

### 11. A Day in the Life

A Svarish day does not begin or end—it continues.

Mira wakes not because of light (there is always the same dim glow) but because the pressure has shifted. The ocean ceiling is lower this morning, pressing the air layer thin. She uncurls from her sleeping position—knees drawn up, head tucked, conserving warmth—and takes several careful breaths, gauging the available air.

Her sleeping basin is one of three her family uses, carved by centuries of bodies resting in the same spots. The stone beneath her is smooth from generations of sleepers, with a slight depression that fits her hip and shoulder. She runs her hand along the familiar ridge at the basin's edge, checking for new mineral deposits (there are none) and feeling for the temperature of the stone (slightly cooler than yesterday).

Rising requires care. The ceiling is perhaps a hand's breadth lower than usual, so she keeps her knees bent, duckwalking to the channel where water flows between basins. She pauses to listen—the drip patterns from the familiar cracks, the distant sound of someone else moving several basins away, the ever-present murmur of the ocean above.

At the channel, she meets Kerem, who has been awake longer. They do not speak—the air is too thin for unnecessary words—but exchange information through touch and gesture. Kerem's left hand, trailing in the water, indicates he has been checking the crawler trails. His slight head shake says he found nothing. Mira touches her chest and gestures toward the brighter region—she will check the algae stones today.

Moving toward the light shafts takes time. She follows the memorized path between basins, ducking lower where she knows the ceiling dips, placing her feet carefully on the wet stone. Twice she must cross water columns, holding her breath and pushing through the cold vertical streams. The second crossing is harder—the current has shifted since yesterday, pulling more strongly sideways. She makes a mental note to tell the others.

At the algae stones, she finds Sem and his daughter Lai already working. Lai is young—her frond-hair still short and barely glowing—but her hands are steady as she scrapes green film from the stone with a shell tool. They work in companionable silence, the rhythmic scraping creating a meditation-like atmosphere.

The harvest is slow. It takes most of the morning (though morning is just a word—the light never changes) to fill a small woven bag with algae paste. Mira's hands grow cold from the constant contact with wet stone, and she pauses periodically to warm them against her body, tucked into the warmer spaces beneath her arms.

When the bag is full, they share a small meal. Sem produces dried crawler meat from several days before, distributing tiny pieces. They chew slowly, making it last, while Lai practices her distance whistles. She is trying to match the recognition pattern of their family group but keeps adding an extra trill that makes Sem smile and shake his head.

The ocean ceiling rises slightly in what might be afternoon. The pressure eases, and everyone breathes more deeply. This is when the Svarish are most active, taking advantage of the thicker air layer. Mira joins a group checking the mineral deposits along the eastern ridges, where certain salts accumulate that help prevent the bone-ache that comes from too much cold.

Here she meets Olo, old even by Svarish standards, who no longer travels far from his watching stone. His skin is constantly mottled now, showing the deep patterns of someone who has spent decades reading the ceiling. He gestures upward—he has seen three large shadows pass today, more than usual, all moving in the same direction. The others cluster around as he demonstrates their size and speed with his hands, recreating their movement across his small section of visible ceiling.

This news is significant enough to share widely. Mira volunteers to carry it to the neighboring family groups. The journey takes her through less familiar territory, where the basins are arranged differently and the ceiling has a different character—more cracks here, creating a crosshatch of water columns that she must navigate carefully.

She finds the Moresh family group by their recognition whistle, a complex pattern that echoes off the stone in a way that creates overtones. They receive the news of the shadows thoughtfully. Their eldest, Kala, has been tracking shadow movements for longer than anyone. She adds this information to her mental map, closing her eyes to visualize the patterns, then makes a gesture that suggests the shadows might be following seasonal currents far above.

The exchange of information leads to more sharing. The Moresh group has found a new growth of phosphorescent organisms in a pool Mira's family hadn't known about. Young Pila demonstrates the way there through gestures and placed stones, creating a temporary map on the basin floor that Mira studies until she can hold it in memory.

Returning takes longer—she is tired now, and the cold has settled into her joints. She stops frequently to rest, sometimes simply standing still and letting her body adjust to the temperature, finding the least energetic position that still keeps her balanced on the uneven floor.

As she nears home waters, she hears the evening whistles—each family member calling in, confirming their location and safety. She adds her own whistle to the chorus, hearing it echo back from familiar stones. The sound brings comfort, a reminder that she is where she belongs.

The sleeping time approaches not with darkness but with a collective understanding that the active period is ending. Family members converge on the sleeping basins, sharing the last information of the day. Mira describes the new phosphorescent pool, using a combination of words and hand gestures to help the others build their mental maps.

They prepare for sleep by checking the ceiling height—it has remained stable, a good sign—and arranging themselves in the basins. The youngest, like Lai, sleep between adults for warmth and safety. Mira finds her familiar spot, feeling the subtle depression that marks it as hers.

But sleep doesn't come immediately. First there is the listening time, when everyone lies still and pays attention to the world. The drip patterns, the water flow, the distant movements—all are noted, part of the constant awareness that keeps them safe. Mira tracks a faint phosphorescent drift in a nearby water column, watching the tiny lights swirl and fade until her eyes grow heavy.

As she settles into sleep, her breathing unconsciously synchronizes with those around her—a gentle rhythm of inhales and exhales that has been shaped by generations of sleeping in thin air. Her last conscious thought is of the shadows Olo saw, moving purposefully across the ocean ceiling, following currents she can only imagine.

Tomorrow will be much the same. The basins will be imperceptibly deeper, the mineral crusts imperceptibly thicker. She will wake to pressure changes and spend her day gathering, sharing, observing, enduring. This is the life of the Svarish—patient, careful, attuned to rhythms too slow for the hurried surface world to comprehend.

In the Undershelf, there is always time. Time for the stone to wear smooth, time for the algae to grow, time for the shadows to pass overhead on their mysterious journeys. And time for the Svarish to continue their quiet existence, breathing in layers, moving like water, settling like silt into the spaces between ocean and stone.

### 12. The Edge of Mystery

There are things even the Svarish do not understand about their world.

Sometimes the ocean ceiling glows more brightly for days at a time, a phosphorescent bloom far above that casts everything in stranger, sharper relief. During these times, the Svarish see details usually hidden—the true texture of the ceiling, the full extent of certain water columns, the shapes of things moving in the mid-water that are neither shadow nor light. These bright times are unsettling, too revealing, and most Svarish stay close to familiar basins until the normal dimness returns.

In the deepest parts of the Undershelf, where the ceiling presses so low that even the Svarish must crawl, there are basins that seem impossibly old. The silt in them is not normal—it glows faintly on its own, and touching it brings strange sensations, as if the hands remember things they never experienced. Some Svarish believe these are the settling places of the very first inhabitants, whose thoughts have been compressed by time into something denser than memory.

There are sounds, too, that have no explanation. Deep thrums that seem to come from the stone itself, lasting for hours and felt more than heard. Whistles that echo from empty spaces, too complex to be natural but following no pattern the Svarish can interpret. These mystery sounds are noted, remembered, but not pursued. To chase every strange echo in the Undershelf would be to lose oneself in questions that may have no answers.

Most mysterious are the rare times when the water in certain columns runs upward, defying the pull that normally brings it down. During these reversals, phosphorescent organisms are pulled up and away, the normal patterns disrupted. The Svarish have learned to recognize the signs—a change in temperature, a shift in the way sound carries—and avoid those columns until the reversal ends. Where the water goes during these times, and why it returns, remains unknown.

But the Svarish do not require explanations for everything. Mystery is simply another feature of their world, like the basins and ridges and endless murmur of water. They live with the unknown as comfortably as they live with the cold, acknowledging its presence without letting it dominate their days. In a world of patience and patterns, some patterns are simply too large or too strange to grasp, and that, too, is accepted.

The Abyssal Plains of Sighisvar keep their secrets, and the Svarish keep their peace with them. In the blue-green twilight beneath the ocean ceiling, surrounded by the slow erosion of stone and time, they continue their careful existence—gathering, sharing, enduring, settling ever deeper into the rhythm of their submerged world.
