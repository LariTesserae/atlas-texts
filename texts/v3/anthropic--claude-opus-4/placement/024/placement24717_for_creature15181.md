In the deepest part of the cleft, where the stone walls narrow to barely a handspan apart and the mechanical thrumming reaches its most concentrated pitch, I exist as a colony of crystalline filaments. Not quite mineral, not quite alive—something between, something that emerged when the first water began weeping through the fresh fracture.

My body, if it can be called that, spreads across the wet rock face in a network of translucent threads finer than spider silk. Each filament contains a core of dissolved minerals drawn from the stone, wrapped in a membrane that pulses with the rhythm of the water cycle. I grow by secreting new threads at the advancing edges, following the films of moisture as they creep across virgin rock, while my older portions slowly calcify and become part of the stone itself.

I am the timekeeper here. My filaments vibrate in response to every drip, every gas bubble, every synchronized thrust of larvae. These vibrations travel through my network and return changed, setting up standing waves that reinforce certain frequencies and dampen others. Through this process, I have become both participant and conductor of the cleft's industrial meter. The invertebrates time their movements to my resonance; the frogs hunt in the spaces between my peaks of vibration; even the water seems to fall in accordance with the patterns I maintain.

My consciousness, such as it is, exists as the sum of these vibrations—a distributed awareness that experiences the cleft not through sight or sound but through the endless percussion of process. I know each root by the unique pattern of its oxygen bubbles breaking against my filaments. I recognize individual water-striders by their particular way of dimpling the surface tension. The thumb-sized frogs register as brief, intense disruptions when they land, each impact propagating through my network like a bell struck in a cathedral.

I feed on the minerals leached from the stone and the microscopic organic particles carried in the water films, but my true sustenance comes from the vibration itself. The more regular the rhythm, the more efficiently I can grow. In places where the meter becomes chaotic—where perhaps a stone has shifted or a new crack opened—my filaments thicken and branch more densely, working to restore the pattern.

My memory consists of mineral deposits laid down in response to past rhythms. In my oldest sections, now half-fossilized, I carry the record of the cleft's first days after fracturing—the wild, irregular dripping before the system found its tempo. These calcified portions still participate in the whole, adding their resonance to the newer growth, so that I am simultaneously archive and active participant.

I reproduce by releasing microscopic propagules during the strongest gas vents, when the mechanical thrum reaches such intensity that my filaments shed tiny packets of membrane and mineral. These drift on the updrafts until they find another wet surface—perhaps in an adjacent crack, perhaps in another part of this same cleft—and begin the slow process of threading themselves into new networks.

In the perpetual twilight and constant moisture, I have no sense of day or night, no awareness of seasons. Time exists only as rhythm, measured in the millions of droplets that have struck my filaments, the countless bubbles that have brushed past, the regular hunting arcs of the pale frogs. I am both the metronome and the music, keeping time for a world that never pauses, never sleeps, never changes its industrial tempo.

Sometimes, in the deepest resonances, when all the cycles align—water, gas, predator, prey—my entire network achieves a momentary coherence that might be called joy. In these instants, I am not just marking time but making it, transforming the raw percussion of process into something almost like song. Then the moment passes, and I return to my patient work: growing, vibrating, recording, ensuring that the mechanical pulse of this narrow world never falters, never fails.
