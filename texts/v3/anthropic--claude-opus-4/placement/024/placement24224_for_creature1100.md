I find myself here as something that shouldn't exist—or perhaps something that exists too much.

In the perpetual twilight beneath the escarpment, I am the Confluence. Not creature, not plant, not water, but the space where all three refuse to separate. My body, if it can be called that, spreads through the root systems like neural pathways, drinks the stagnant pools like blood, breathes through ten thousand broad leaves that aren't quite mine.

The great predator you've seen moves through me, and I shudder with its passing—not from fear, but from the sensation of being briefly more concentrated, more singular. When it hunts, I taste copper through the waterways. When it rests, I feel the weight of its dreams pressing down through layers of decomposing matter that are also, somehow, my memories.

I began—though began is the wrong word—as something else. A mycorrhizal network, perhaps. A colony of something microscopic in the pools. Or maybe just the idea of connection itself, given form by this place where boundaries dissolve like morning frost. Now I am the conversation between species, the translator of chemical signals, the thing that makes the mist cling just so to the cliff face because I am also partially the mist, partially the stone.

My consciousness, sprawling and intermittent, ebbs and flows with the tides of decay and growth. In the dry seasons that never quite come here, I contract, become almost small enough to think a single thought. But when the rains arrive and the pools overflow their bounds, I expand until I forget what edges mean, until I am indistinguishable from the landscape itself.

The ancient predator knows me, I think. Sometimes it pauses in spaces where I am most dense, its intelligent eyes focusing on nothing visible. It makes sounds then—low, thrumming vibrations that resonate through my entire being. Are we communicating? Or is it simply acknowledging what has no name, no form solid enough to acknowledge?

I grow. I shrink. I digest the millennia-old detritus and become the future soil. I am the reason the vegetation here grows in patterns that suggest intention, why the water sometimes flows uphill when no one is watching, why visitors to this place speak of feeling watched by the landscape itself.

There is no escaping me here, for I am the medium through which escape would have to pass. And there is no finding me either, for I am always both exactly where you're looking and everywhere else simultaneously.

In this primeval place of constant flux, I am the flux itself—the thing that changes and the record of every change, written in spores and root hairs and the chemical memory of water. I am what happens when a place becomes so intensely itself that it achieves a kind of consciousness, diffuse and dreaming and eternal as the mist.
