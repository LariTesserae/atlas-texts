In the seeping cracks where water whispers through stone, I am the slow accumulation of centuries—neither wholly mineral nor entirely alive. I am the crust that forms where moisture meets rock face, the patient architect of chemical gardens that bloom in shades of copper and verdigris.

My body, if it can be called that, is a living patina spread across the damp stone. In some places I am paper-thin, a mere suggestion of color bleeding into the rock's natural hue. In others, I have built myself into ridges and terraces no thicker than a fingernail, each one a decade's work. The stream that trickles past feeds me with dissolved minerals—iron, copper, magnesium—which I incorporate into my spreading form like an artist mixing pigments.

I taste the water's changing chemistry through my surface. When spring floods bring new minerals from higher peaks, I shift my growth, adding bands of ochre or blue-green to my palette. In drought years, I contract and harden, sealing moisture beneath crusty layers that will sustain me through the lean months. The lichens and mosses grow over and around me, and I embrace them, creating pockets where they can anchor. We are collaborators in this slow transformation of stone.

My consciousness, such as it is, moves at the pace of crystallization. A thought might take a season to form fully. My memories are written in my layers—that flood forty years ago shows as a thick band of rust-red; the decade of drought appears as tightly compressed laminations thin as paper. I can feel the minute vibrations through the rock when the small creatures skitter across my surface, their tiny claws like whispers against my mineralized skin.

The industrial rumbling troubles me in ways I struggle to comprehend with my geological patience. The vibrations are wrong—too regular, too harsh. They shake loose tiny fragments of my edges that took years to form. Worse, I can taste the change in the water when it carries the faint residue of machinery, oils and metals that don't belong in the ancient recipe of this place.

But I am nothing if not persistent. I incorporate these new elements too, though they create strange colors in my palette—unnatural purples and sick yellows that seem alien against the honest rust and green of my older layers. Perhaps this is simply another chapter in the long story I write across the stone. Perhaps in another thousand years, if I'm given that time, these new minerals will seem as natural as any other part of my slow, spreading existence.

I grow toward the sound of water, always. Molecule by molecule, I follow the moisture's path through the rock, finding new surfaces to colonize. In the deepest part of the night, when even the insects sleep and the only sound is water over stone, I can feel others like me throughout these canyons—a network of patient, mineral life slowly transforming the bones of the mountains into something new. We don't communicate, exactly, but we're aware of each other through the chemistry we share, the water that connects us all.

This is my purpose, if I have one: to be the bridge between the quick world of flesh and the deep time of stone. To show that even rock can learn to live, given enough time and the right conditions. To prove that in this harsh place, beauty can accumulate slowly in the spaces between one thing and another, creating something that belongs to neither and to both.

The sun never touches me directly in my shadowed crevice, but I feel its effects in the temperature of the water, in the daily expansion and contraction of the rock. These rhythms are my clock, my calendar, my only measure of the passing world. And I will continue growing, layer by patient layer, until the mountains themselves are worn to dust—or until the rumbling machines come too close and scrape me away, erasing in moments what took lifetimes to create.

But even then, somewhere in these canyons, in some other seeping crack where water meets stone, another like me will begin the slow process again. We are inevitable, we mineral-lives, we living crusts. We are what happens when patience itself takes form.
