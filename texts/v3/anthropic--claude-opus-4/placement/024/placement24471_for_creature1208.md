In the shadow of a decommissioned ore crusher, where rust bleeds orange rivers down corrugated steel walls, I have made my workshop. I am the Salvager—part engineer, part archaeologist, part scavenger. My body bears the marks of this fractured frontier: hands callused from handling rough stone and rougher metal, one eye replaced by a crystalline lens harvested from the mineral veins, lungs that wheeze slightly from years of breathing air thick with industrial dust and lichen spores.

My dwelling is a patchwork structure, built into and around the skeletal remains of some vast processing facility. The walls are sheets of salvaged metal riveted to natural stone, the gaps sealed with a mortar I make from crushed crystal and the sticky secretions of cave-dwelling arthropods. Wind whistles through the imperfect seams, but I've grown to love the music it makes—each gust a different chord depending on the season and the storm.

I am neither fully of the old world nor the new. My tools are hybrid things: hammers with heads of meteoric stone and handles of repurposed pipe, sifting screens woven from wire and the silk of cliff-nesting moths. I know the language of the machines—which hums mean danger, which clicks promise valuable components—but I also know the patterns of the creatures: when the stone-runners migrate, where the aerial flocks roost during the worst winds.

Each morning, I venture out into the fractured landscape with my cart, a wheeled contraption that rides on both metal bearings and polished stone spheres. The nimble creatures have grown accustomed to me; they know I leave offerings of processed grain at certain crossroads, payment for their inadvertent guidance to the richest salvage sites. The flying ones sometimes follow overhead, their cries alerting me to unstable structures or approaching storms.

I collect what the frontier offers: lengths of unoxidized cable, intact crystal matrices that still hold their charge, sheets of metal that can be beaten into new shapes. But I also gather the lichen for my medicines, the tough grasses whose fibers I spin into rope, the mineral deposits that I grind into pigments for the maps I paint on sheets of hammered metal.

My greatest discovery lies deep in my workshop: a massive geode I unearthed from beneath a collapsed turbine housing. Within its hollow heart, crystals grow in formations that mirror the pipe networks above ground. I've noticed that when the machinery hums in certain frequencies, the crystals resonate in response, glowing with a soft internal light. I believe, though I cannot prove, that the industrial structures were built here not by chance but by design—that their builders recognized something in the harmonics of this place, some deeper symmetry between the natural crystalline networks below and the mechanical networks above.

I am documenting these correspondences, scribing my observations onto metal plates with an etching tool tipped with the hardest crystals. Perhaps someday another salvager will find my work and understand what I have only begun to glimpse: that this fractured frontier is not a place where nature and industry merely collide, but where they speak to each other in frequencies we are only beginning to hear.

At night, I climb to my observation platform—a crow's nest built from salvaged girders and secured with cables to the highest stable point of the structure. From there, I watch the stars through air that shimmers with heat from cooling machinery and the bioluminescence of night-flying things. The wind carries sounds from miles away: the groan of settling metal, the scatter of creatures across stone, the whisper of growing things pushing through cracks in the world.

I am the Salvager, keeper of broken things that might yet be whole, translator between the language of rust and the vocabulary of stone, solitary but never alone in this landscape that shapes me as surely as I reshape its scattered remains.
