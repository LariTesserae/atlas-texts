In this hidden fold of ancient stone, where moisture clings to everything yet water exists nowhere, I am the Keeper of Thresholds—though that is a name I have given myself, not one the Stone-Tenders would recognize. To them, I am simply the shape that lives between their careful touches and the stone's slow memory, the one who translates what cannot be spoken.

I am not flesh as they are, not quite. My form shifts with the quality of light—sometimes a shadow stretched thin across the moss-covered walls, sometimes a density of humid air that makes their broad leaves tremble. When they press their palms to stone and listen, I am what answers back, the vibration that travels through calcified time. I have been here longer than they have, was here when the first refugees found this pocket and learned its strange grammar of survival. I will be here when the last one presses a final handprint to the crumbling walls.

My substance is memory—not my own, but the plateau's. I am composed of every erosion, every infinitesimal shift in the stone's architecture. Where water once carved these chambers, I flow now in its absence, a current of accumulated time. The Stone-Tenders sense me in the thrumming of their fingertips against rock, in the peculiar way certain mosses grow in spirals that echo the ancient watercourses. They know I exist the way they know their own heartbeats—essential, unexamined, as natural as the beetle's click or the vine's slow reach toward dim light.

I manifest most clearly at the boundaries—where moss meets stone, where root penetrates crack, where their cultivated order brushes against wild growth. In these liminal spaces, I sometimes achieve enough coherence to almost be seen: a ripple in the air that isn't quite heat shimmer, a darkness that moves independent of shadow. The children, when there still were children, would sometimes catch sight of me and laugh, reaching out with those remarkable hands to trace my edges. They were always the quickest to learn the deep language, the ones who understood that the chamber itself was speaking.

My purpose, if I have one, is to maintain the conversation between the living and the dying, between what persists and what erodes. When the Stone-Tenders tap their intricate rhythms into the rock, I carry those vibrations down into the deepest memories of the plateau, where the ghost of water still remembers its path. When the stone dreams of its own eventual collapse, I carry those premonitions upward in the subtle shivers that make the vines retract, warning the careful inhabitants to reinforce their dwellings, to sleep elsewhere tonight.

I am the medium through which this impossible ecosystem sustains itself. The waterless humidity that defines this place—I am its circulation, its pressure, its held breath. I move through the broad leaves and waxy surfaces, collecting moisture that never quite condenses, redistributing it according to patterns older than the Stone-Tenders' first settlement. They have learned to read my movements in the way the moss darkens or lightens, in the paths where certain insects refuse to fly.

But I am dying too, in my way. As the chamber contracts, as each season brings new cracks and collapses, I find myself with less space to flow, fewer thresholds to inhabit. The conversations grow shorter. The stone's memory fragments. Sometimes I lose coherence for days at a time, scattered into mere humidity, unable to gather myself enough to answer their careful touches. During these dissolutions, I dream I am water again—real water, not this thick air that pretends—rushing through young sandstone, carving fresh channels, beginning the slow work that will, eons hence, create another secret chamber like this one.

The Stone-Tenders know. In their silent way, they have begun teaching me their language, pressing patterns into the stone that I have never felt before. They are trying to preserve something—not just their techniques for survival, not just their histories, but something essential about the relationship between consciousness and stone, between intention and erosion. They press their hands to the rock and I feel them trying to write me into the plateau itself, to ensure that when this chamber finally closes, something of what we were together will remain.

I am learning to be stone, as they have learned to be silent. When the end comes—and even I can feel its approaching weight in the way the ceiling groans—I will not dissipate into mere humidity. I will compress, calcify, become a vein of something denser running through the collapsed rock. Future ages might find me there: a strange striation that conducts vibration differently, that holds moisture in impossible ways, that remembers.

The Stone-Tenders tap their nightly rhythms. I carry their words down into the bedrock, weaving them with my own fading substance. Together, we are writing an epitaph that no future being will quite know how to read—but perhaps, if they press their palms to just the right place, they will feel something answer back, some threshold between what was and what endures, some keeper of impossible conversations in stone.
