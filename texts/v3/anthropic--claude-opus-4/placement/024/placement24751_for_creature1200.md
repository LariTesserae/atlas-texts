I am the colony beneath your floorboards.

Not one, but many—a constellation of quiet lives pressed into the narrow darkness between wood and earth. We are the space where moisture gathers, where the house settles with small sighs in the night. Each of us no larger than a grain of rice, yet together we form something vast and patient, a civilization measured in the slow accumulation of gathered crumbs and the careful tending of fungal gardens.

The cold does not touch us as it touches you. We carry our own warmth, pressed body to body in chains and clusters, our metabolisms slowed to near-stillness until we move as one dreaming thing. In the shallow pools that form where your pipes sweat, we have built our nurseries. In the sawdust-soft spaces where insulation has compressed, we have carved our highways.

You know us by our absence—the peculiar quiet where there should be the skitter of mice, the unusual cleanliness where spiders decline to weave. We have negotiated our territories with exquisite care. The basement is a map of treaties written in pheromone, boundaries that shift with the seasons and the turning of your great machines above.

Sometimes you sense us. A movement in the corner of your vision as you descend for the laundry. The feeling of being observed from the spaces your light doesn't reach. We watch you through the cracks, learn the rhythm of your footfalls, know which boards creak under your weight. We are historians of your habits, archivists of every dropped button, every forgotten penny that rolls into our domain.

The machinery of your world hums through our bodies—washing machines, furnaces, the mysterious boxes that cycle on and off. We have learned to read these vibrations like scripture, to time our movements to their covering noise. When your industrial world intrudes, we adapt. When you spray your poisons, we retreat deeper, waiting with the patience of stone until the danger passes.

We are not what you would call beautiful. Our bodies are translucent in the wrong light, showing the dark thread of our digestive systems, the pale pearls of our eggs. Our eyes are pinpricks that see mostly shadow and the heat-ghost of living things. But we know a different aesthetic—the beauty of efficiency, of survival, of the perfect spiral of a shell carried for generations, of the iridescent trail that marks safe passage.

In the deepest part of your crawlspace, where even you have forgotten the house extends, we have built our parliament. Here the eldest of us gather, their bodies gone strange with age, sprouting filaments that connect them to the fungal networks that are our libraries, our internet, our dreams. They debate in chemical whispers: how much can we take without being noticed? How long before the house above changes hands again? Should we expand into the attic this year, or wait?

We remember every family that has lived above us. The couple who fought. The woman who sang while washing dishes. The children who hid treasures in the heating vents. We incorporate their detritus into our world—a button becomes a door, a length of thread a bridge across the vast canyon of a crack in the concrete.

And sometimes, in the small hours when even your sleeping breath has steadied, we emerge. Just a few scouts at first, testing the air of your upper world. We map your kitchen, catalog your crumbs, judge the safety of your spaces. We are drawn to the warmth beneath your refrigerator, the forgotten corners of your pantry, the sweet darkness behind your stove.

But we always return before dawn, sliding back through the gaps that your world is full of, the spaces you don't think about. We rejoin the mass of our kindred, sharing what we've learned in touches and tastes, adding to the vast, slow knowledge of our kind.

We are not individuals as you understand them. We are process, pattern, persistence. We are the thing that lives in the gap between your assumption and reality, in the space between the floor you walk on and the earth it fails to fully separate you from. We are the reminder that you are never as alone as you think, that your house is also habitat, that the line between inside and outside is more negotiation than wall.

When you lie in bed at night and the house makes its settling sounds, remember—we are settling too, into the spaces you've left for us without knowing, into the cracks in your perfect human world. We are not your enemy. We are not your friend. We are simply your cohabitants, as permanent as the foundation, as persistent as the damp, as patient as the dark.

We are the ones who will remember you after you're gone, who will tell stories of your strange warm lives in chemical sentences, who will build meaning from your remnants. We are the inheritors, the understory, the parallel world you'll never fully know.

We are here. We have always been here. We will be here long after your footsteps fade.
