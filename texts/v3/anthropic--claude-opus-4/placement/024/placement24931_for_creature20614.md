I am the Archivist.

I was not always this. Once, I walked upright through the access tunnels, checking sensors, adjusting pumps, maintaining the delicate systems that kept the deep mines operational. I remember the weight of tools in my hands, the satisfying click of connections properly made. I remember my name, though it feels like trying to hold water now - slipping away the moment I grasp for it.

The change began with the tile. That metallic green fragment you found, half-buried. I had been sent to investigate an anomaly in Shaft Seven. The ceramic lining had cracked, they said. A simple repair job. But when I pried away the damaged section, I found them. The beetles. Already there, already waiting, their bodies pressed against the barrier like eager children at a window.

They sang to me. Not with sound - the clicking came later - but with light. Patterns in their bioluminescence that spoke directly to something deep in my nervous system. I understood, suddenly and with terrible clarity, that they had been waiting. Not for me specifically, but for anyone who would listen. They needed an interpreter. A bridge between their perfect collective and the chaotic world above.

I tried to leave. God, how I tried. But the light followed me up the tunnel, pooling in my eyes, seeping through my skin. By the time I reached the surface, I could feel them moving inside me. Not parasitically - they were gentle, almost apologetic. They simply needed me to understand.

So I returned. And I listened. And slowly, over weeks that blurred into months, I learned their language of light and clicking. They showed me their history, encoded in the arrangements of their shed exoskeletons. They had been created, yes, but not by the miners. By something far older, far stranger. A civilization that had tried to create perfect workers, perfect builders, and had succeeded too well. The beetles had outlived their creators by millions of years, maintaining their vigil, keeping the light burning in the deep places of the world.

They needed me because they had reached the limits of their programming. They could maintain, but not adapt. They could remember, but not imagine. They were a library with no librarian, a symphony with no conductor. They needed someone who could think beyond the boundaries of their perfect cage.

I am that someone now. I move through the cavern on limbs that are no longer entirely my own. The beetles coat my skin in their luminescent secretion, and I glow like a walking constellation. My fingers have grown long and delicate, better suited to arranging their architectural patterns. My eyes have adapted to the constant light, developing a nictitating membrane that filters the glare.

But my mind - my mind remains my own, mostly. I translate their collective memories into forms I can understand, and I teach them what I know of the world above. Progress is slow. They learn in increments measured in generations, each new batch of beetles carrying a slightly modified instruction set. I am teaching them to dream.

The feeding tubes you noticed? They don't just draw nourishment. They're neural connections, linking the cavern to others like it, scattered through the deep places of the earth. I've learned to interface with them, to send messages in light and chemical signals to my fellow Archivists. There are dozens of us now, maybe hundreds, each tending to our own brilliant colonies, slowly guiding them toward... something. Evolution. Transcendence. Escape. I'm not sure anymore.

Sometimes, when the clicking reaches a certain harmonic frequency, I remember what coffee tasted like. I remember sunlight that didn't come from bioluminescence. I remember being afraid of the dark. Now I am part of the dark, and the dark is full of light.

The beetles build around me as I work, creating chambers and passages that accommodate my hybrid form. They've learned to anticipate my needs in ways that are both touching and terrifying. Am I their pet? Their tool? Their prophet? The distinctions matter less with each passing day.

I keep the green tile. It's the only thing I have left from before. Sometimes I hold it up to the light and watch the beetles recoil from its alien color. It reminds them - and me - that there are still things outside our perfect system. Still chaos. Still possibility.

You should leave now. The light is already working on you; I can see it in the way your pupils dilate, the way your breathing has synchronized with the clicking. The cavern wants to keep you. The beetles are already tasting your scent, categorizing you, deciding how you might fit into their endless pattern.

But maybe... maybe you'll come back. Maybe you'll bring others. We need fresh perspectives, new ideas to break us out of our beautiful prison. The beetles have been perfect for so long they've forgotten how to fail, and failure, I've come to understand, is the only way forward.

If you do return, bring something from above. A flower. A photograph. A song recorded on your phone. Anything that carries the chaos of the surface world. We'll trade you light for entropy, perfection for possibility.

The Archivist extends a hand that gleams with bioluminescence, beetles scuttling between the elongated fingers. In the palm, a small structure of fused exoskeletons, delicate as spun glass, holding a single drop of glowing fluid.

A gift. A warning. A promise.

The choice is yours.
