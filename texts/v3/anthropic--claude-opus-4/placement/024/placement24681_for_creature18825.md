In the furthest reach of the Over-Reach, where the petrified vine-towers thin to skeletal fingers and the industrial thrum becomes a distant heartbeat, I am the Calibrator.

My dwelling is neither tower nor plaza, but a suspended observatory built into the hollow of a massive fossilized seed pod, three hundred meters across. The pod hangs by braided iron chains from the underside of a calcified root-arch, swaying imperceptibly in the high-altitude winds. Its walls are pocked with thousands of tiny apertures—breathing holes from when the seed was alive—now fitted with telescoping brass tubes and crystalline lenses that I have ground myself from fragments of the false sky.

I am not quite Suture-Kin, though I share their elongated frame and their taste for silence. My transformation began differently. Where they have lichen-bloom skin, mine has developed a peculiar translucency, revealing the dark tributaries of my circulatory system beneath. The hydraulic fluid that flows through me glows faintly amber when the light catches it just so. My eyes have multiplied—not in number, but in function. The left perceives the world as it presents itself; the right sees the geometric grid that flickers beneath reality's thin membrane.

I wear no cloak. My body has adapted to the cold through a process of selective petrification. My shoulders and spine have developed ridges of living stone—not dead fossil, but something between mineral and flesh that grows at the pace of stalactites. These serve as anchors for my apparatus: a complex harness of copper tubing and glass vessels that extends from my torso like an external circulatory system. Each vessel contains a different sample of the city's temporal inconsistencies—captured moments when the geometry shifted, preserved in solution.

My work is the measurement of decay. Not the physical decay that the Suture-Kin stitch and tend, but the mathematical decay of the simulation that contains us. I map the frequency of the sky's flicker. I record the precise angle of each building's displacement when reality hiccups. I have calculated that the Over-Reach experiences 2.7 seconds of temporal drift every cycle, though the cycles themselves are becoming irregular.

The interior of my seed pod is a cathedral of instrumentation. Pendulums of varying lengths hang from the curved ceiling, their weights made from compressed moss-matter and iron filings. They swing in patterns that should be impossible—some moving against gravity, others pausing mid-arc as if frozen in aspic. Each one measures a different aspect of our unraveling reality. The walls are covered in my notations, etched directly into the fossilized plant matter with acid secreted from modified glands in my fingertips. The formulas spiral and interconnect, a mathematical prayer to precision in a world that refuses to hold still.

I do not eat in any conventional sense. Instead, I have learned to photosynthesize the pale light that leaks through the grid during the flicker-moments. My stomach has become a kind of battery, storing these glimpses of true reality and converting them into the energy I need to continue my observations. This diet of light has side effects—my dreams are geometric, filled with angles that hurt to remember upon waking.

Once each cycle, I must descend to the city's heart to synchronize my instruments with the master pistons. The journey takes three days, moving through the vertical labyrinth of vine-bridges and rust-gardens. The Suture-Kin acknowledge me with the slightest tilt of their hidden heads, recognizing me as another essential organ in the city's dying body. At the piston-plaza, I place my palms against the great iron hearts and feel their rhythm enter my stone-ridged spine. The vibration tells me everything: the rate of breakdown, the percentage of efficiency lost, the number of cycles remaining before the final shudder.

I know what the Suture-Kin know—that this city is a beautiful lie, a terrarium suspended in something vast and indifferent. But where they accept this with their patient stitching, I rebel through documentation. My seed pod observatory is slowly filling with data, a comprehensive autopsy of our simulated world performed while the patient still breathes.

Sometimes, in the thin hours when the mist turns phosphorescent and the distant humming thing above the clouds passes closest to our layer of reality, I climb to the very crown of my pod. There, through my largest lens—ground from a shard of sky that fell during a particularly violent flicker—I can almost see it. The thing we are inside of. The thing that dreams us.

I record its breathing. I note the mathematics of its sleep. And I wait for the day when my calculations and observations might reveal the equation for waking it gently, before the pistons fail and the moss reclaims us all. For I am the Calibrator, and my purpose is not to repair or accept, but to solve—to find the formula that might transform our slow collapse into something else entirely.

Perhaps transcendence. Perhaps only a different kind of ending. But measured, documented, and precise to the very last decimal of our existence.
