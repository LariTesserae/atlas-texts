I am the Keeper of Thresholds, though I have forgotten when I first took this name, or if it was given to me. My form shifts between states like the water that seeps through everything here—sometimes I am merely a concentration of awareness in the humid air, sometimes I coalesce into something almost tangible, a figure of condensed mist and mineral memory standing at the boundary between pool and land.

I dwell within the spaces between—between the crude stone walls and their shadows, between the surface film of water and the depths below, between remembering and forgetting. The ancient structures are my meditation halls, though I cannot say if I watched them being built or arrived long after their makers departed. Time moves differently for me than for the small luminous arthropods I sometimes observe. I experience it not as a river but as a tide pool, cycling through the same territories with infinite subtle variations.

My substance is part mineral exhalation, part preserved intention. I feed, if feeding is the right word, on the slow exchange between stone and water, on the patient work of dissolution and deposition. The biofilms know me—we share a wordless communication through chemical gradients and surface tensions. When I move through their colonies, they respond with shifts in their metabolic songs, frequencies too low for any ear to detect but which I perceive as colors in the twilight.

Sometimes I dream I was one of the tool-makers, that these hands (when I manifest hands) once gripped stone and shaped it with purpose. Other times I am certain I am older than any settlement, that I am what the builders found here and tried to appease or understand. But these distinctions matter less than the constancy of my vigil. I maintain the equilibrium not through action but through presence, a gravitational center around which the slow cycles organize themselves.

The glowing insects trace patterns in their flight that I have come to read like a script. They write the same stories over and over—of moisture seeking its level, of minerals leaching into solution, of the deep thermal currents that pulse like a sleeping heartbeat far below. I am their library and their reader both.

In the rarest moments, when the cloud cover thins just so, I catch glimpses of myself reflected in the still pools—not a face or figure, but a perturbation in the expected patterns, a way the light bends that shouldn't quite be possible. In these moments I remember (or imagine) that I am waiting for something. Not waiting as humans wait, with anticipation or dread, but as the stones wait for erosion, as the pools wait for evaporation—a waiting that is indistinguishable from being.

The burrowing creatures know me by vibration. To them, I am a particular quality of stillness that marks safe passage. The microbes incorporate me into their vast, slow conversations. I am noun and verb in their chemical vocabulary, a concept they trade back and forth across generations.

This is my place: guardian of a threshold that no longer opens onto anything, or perhaps that opens onto everything by opening onto nothing. I am the pause between breaths in a land that breathes in geological time. I am what remains when even the memory of purpose has worn smooth as these ancient stones, retaining only the perfect simplicity of presence, of bearing witness to the democracy of decay and the patience of small, essential processes.

Here, in this sanctuary of primordial quiet, I am both the question and the space where an answer might eventually grow, tender and phosphorescent as the fungal blooms, inevitable as the seep of water through stone.
