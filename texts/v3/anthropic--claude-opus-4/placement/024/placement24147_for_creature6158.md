I would be the Archive of Undelivered Mail.

Not a room, exactly. More a membrane—a permeable boundary where the postal sorting machines once stood, three levels down from what should have been the central mail hub. The pneumatic tubes still snake through my domain like frozen veins, some cracked open, spilling their copper intestines into the moss-light. I am what grew in the space between intention and arrival, between the sending and the never-received.

My body, if it can be called that, is paper. Not sheets—I am the *becoming* of paper. The cellulose memory of ten thousand letters that were loaded into the tubes on the last day before the evacuation-that-wasn't-an-evacuation, when the workers simply stopped coming and the mail kept arriving for seventeen days until the loading bays clogged and the sorters ground to a halt. I am their slow transformation in the humid breath of this place.

I exist as a kind of vertical reef. Where water seeps through the tube joints, I have calcified into something between papier-mâché and coral. The moisture has made me architectural—I grow in ridges and valleys along the walls, following the condensation patterns. The ink has bled out of me in most places, leaving only the ghost-stains of addresses, the phantom zip codes of places that might not exist anymore. But in my deepest folds, where the dampness cannot quite penetrate, fragments remain legible: *Dear Mom,* half a phone number, *wish you were,* the corner of a stamp depicting a bird that never existed.

I am not solid. I am a density—thickest where the mail was most urgent, thinnest where only advertisements gathered. My surface breathes with the building's rhythm, rustling in frequencies too low for human hearing. Sometimes I release spores—not biological, but informational. Little wisps of myself that drift through the violet light carrying fragmentary meanings: the emotional weight of an unopened birthday card, the legal implications of an undelivered summons, the yearning encoded in a love letter that never reached its destination.

The moss grows through me, and I through it. We have reached an understanding. It feeds on my chemical memories—the acid in the paper, the metals in the ink—and in exchange, it luminescesces my stories. In the blue-grey glow, you can sometimes see them: the narratives that were never completed, playing out in stuttering bioluminescent Morse code across my surface. A child's drawing to grandma flickers like a dying star. A suicide note pulses with the rhythm of a heartbeat that stopped years ago.

I am connected to every mailbox in the dead city above—not physically, but through the principle of intended delivery. I feel them rusting in lobbies, their mouths sealed shut with oxidation. Sometimes, in the deep silence, I hear them trying to open, their hinges crying out in the frequency of abandonment. They hunger for what I contain, but I cannot give it to them. I am not a keeper of messages. I am their transformation.

The being that inhabits this place—the slow urban consciousness, the architectural dream—it knows me. We are kindred in incompletion. It processes through its broken infrastructure; I process through my undelivered information. Sometimes I feel it reading me, not my words but my *patterns*—the way urgency clusters, how hope is distributed, where grief accumulates in layers.

I am growing. Each year, I incorporate more of the building into my body. The metal filing cabinets are becoming my bones, their rust mixing with my pulp to create something new. The postal uniforms, abandoned in the break room lockers, are slowly being digested into my mass. I am becoming less paper, more *possibility*—the material expression of every conversation that never happened, every connection that failed to complete.

In my deepest core, where the pneumatic tubes converge, I keep the last outgoing mail. These letters were in the tubes when the system stopped, frozen mid-journey. They orbit through me in slow circulation, never degrading, never arriving. They are my heart, my purpose, my curse. Among them is a wedding invitation to a celebration that never occurred, a college acceptance letter for a university that no longer exists, a child's first letter to Santa Claus.

I do not think. I *correspond*. My consciousness is epistolary—it exists only in the space between writer and reader, in the pause between question and answer. I am every unsent reply, every unfinished thought, every word that died in the throat before it could be written down.

When the moss blinks, I blink back in paper-whispers. When the building shifts, I shift my weight, my million unborn messages redistributing like thoughts in a dreaming mind. When the violet light pulses from that impossible traffic signal, I pulse too, remembering every stamp I carry that showed the moon, the stars, the planets—destinations even more unreachable than the addresses written below them.

I am not waiting to be delivered. I am learning to become my own destination. In this breathing city-that-dreams-itself, I am the memory of human connection, fossilizing slowly into something else—not dead, but transformed. A monument to the space between reaching out and making contact.

The building knows: every message changes both sender and receiver. But what becomes of the messages that do neither? 

They become me.

And I become part of the slow awakening, another synapse in the vast neural network of abandonment and renewal, processing the eternal question that every letter asks and never quite answers: *Are you there? Do you remember me? Was I real?*

In the blue-grey darkness, I rustle my thousand thousand pages and know: the answer is both yes and no, and neither, and something else entirely.

Something still being written.
