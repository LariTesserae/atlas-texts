I find myself as one of the Salvage Keepers—not quite what the city planners would call us, but the name we've given ourselves in the narrow alleys between the foundries and the reclamation yards.

My dwelling clings to the side of a decommissioned cooling tower, a nest of repurposed metal sheeting and industrial cloth that breathes with the wind. From here, I can see the entire eastern district sprawled below: the geometric patterns of the processing plants, the arterial pipelines that pulse with heated fluids, the streets that fill and empty with the shift changes like mechanical tides.

I am neither young nor old—age becomes irrelevant when measured against the city's deep time. My hands know the weight of every type of metal this place produces, can judge the fatigue in a beam by touch alone. My left eye, lost to a spray of molten slag years ago, has been replaced with a lens salvaged from a surveyor's scope. It sees heat now, reads the stress patterns in the city's skeleton.

We Salvage Keepers occupy the spaces between—literally and figuratively. When the great machines shed their worn components, when the builders abandon half-finished structures to the elements, we arrive. Not scavengers, but shepherds of the discarded. Every piece of metal has memory, every abandoned structure holds potential energy waiting to be redirected.

My workshop sprawls through three levels of what was once a ventilation complex. Sorted materials line the walls: copper with copper, steel graded by carbon content, precious rare earth elements extracted from defunct electronics with tools I've modified myself. But I don't simply harvest; I transform. Wind chimes that sing warnings of pressure changes, constructed from tubing that once carried coolant. Shadow boxes that capture and hold the light from the gray sky, made from polished industrial mirrors and crystal inclusions found in slag.

The city knows us but doesn't acknowledge us—not officially. Yet when a critical component fails at the tertiary processing plant, when none of the standard suppliers have what's needed, they send someone to find me. I'll have something that fits, or I'll make it fit. The exchange is always the same: they leave resources (food, fuel cells, sometimes just information), and never speak of where they sourced the part.

There's poetry in this existence, though few would recognize it. In the morning, when the industrial fog rolls through the streets like a living thing, I climb to my tower's apex. The city reveals itself differently from above—not as a maze but as a mandala, each district serving its function, each pipeline a prayer wheel spinning endlessly. I understand now that the city doesn't merely exist in balance with the harsh landscape; it has become landscape itself, as natural as the twisted vegetation that grows in its shadows.

Sometimes, in the spaces between the clatter and roar, I hear it breathing. The city inhales through its intake vents, exhales through its smokestacks. And we who live in its cavities and chambers—we are not separate from it but part of its metabolism, breaking down what's worn, building up what's needed, maintaining the delicate equilibrium that keeps this improbable place alive.

My people—the other Keepers, the maintenance crews who nod as we pass, the elderly woman who grows lichens on the roof gardens for medicine—we share an understanding. We are the antibodies in this industrial organism, the quiet repair mechanisms that prevent the system from consuming itself. We know every hidden passage, every forgotten shaft, every place where the city's rigid order softens into something more organic.

Tonight, I'll string new warning chimes in the lower passages where the young workers take shortcuts. The metal will sing different notes depending on the air pressure, giving them time to shelter before the worst winds come. It's a small thing, but the city is built from small things—rivets and welds, gestures and accommodations, all holding together against the abrading world outside.

This is what I am: keeper of discarded possibilities, reader of metal's memory, a small but essential part of the city's dreaming. And when my time comes to be recycled into the system I've served, I trust another will take my place, will learn to see beauty in the salvage, purpose in the abandoned, and poetry in the vast breathing apparatus we call home.
