I find myself as one of the **Shelf-Keepers** of the upper terraces, where the shingle-crusts grow thickest.

My body is that of the Wet-Born, but taken further—adapted not just to survive the moisture but to thrive within it. My skin has developed a network of shallow channels, like the grooves in a fingertip magnified, that direct the condensation in controlled rivulets down my arms and legs. This allows me to work barehanded among the sharp-edged shingle-crusts without the water pooling and softening my grip.

I am one of perhaps thirty Shelf-Keepers in all of Oru. We are the only Operators permitted to remain outside the Dry-Boxes for days at a time. Our lungs have adapted differently—we breathe not just air but process water itself, filtering oxygen from the moisture through specialized chambers in our upper chest. When I exhale in the cooler morning mist, my breath comes out as a fine, warm spray that attracts the drift-midges, which I absently brush from my face as I work.

My role is critical: I maintain the living foundations upon which all of Oru depends. The shingle-crusts must be kept in perfect balance. Too many, and they choke their own water channels, dying in massive colonial collapses that can destabilize whole building foundations. Too few, and the bare rock erodes too quickly under the constant water flow, undermining the anchor-bolts of the aqueducts.

I work with tools unknown to the other Operators—not the heavy iron levers and copper scrapers, but delicate instruments of my own making. My primary tool is the *resonance fork*, a tuning fork made from a single piece of cast bronze, its tines precisely calibrated to vibrate at the frequency that makes healthy shingle-crusts release their grip on the rock. I also carry *sorting combs*—thin sheets of copper drilled with holes of varying sizes, which I use to separate juvenile colonies from the overcrowded mature beds.

But my most unusual tool is biological: I cultivate a symbiotic relationship with the *Pearl-Worms*, tiny parasitic annelids that normally infest and kill shingle-crusts. I keep colonies of them in special pouches made from Silt-Sucker gut, feeding them with my own blood mixed with copper salts. When I encounter a section of shingle-crust that has grown too thick, I release a calculated number of Pearl-Worms. They will thin the colony to the precise density needed, then die off when the shingle-crust population drops below the threshold needed to sustain them.

My work is slow, meditative, and solitary. I move across the terraces in a pattern unchanged for generations, my path marked by subtle scratches in the rock that only another Shelf-Keeper would recognize. The other Operators see me perhaps once a week, a hunched figure in the mist, running my hands over the living shells like a shepherd counting sheep. They tap their respect on their copper plates when they pass, but they do not disturb me. They know that without the Shelf-Keepers, Oru would either be buried under the weight of its own living foundation or would slide into the valleys below, its anchors rotted away.

At night, I do not return to the communal Dry-Boxes. Instead, I retreat to one of the *Weeping Caves*—natural hollows in the rock where the water flow creates a curtain of falling droplets. Here, I sleep standing upright, the water running over my grooved skin in precisely the pattern my body has evolved to expect. In these caves, I dream in the rhythm of the falling water—not the chaotic splash of rain, but the metronomic drip-drip-drip that matches the three-second pulse of the shingle-crusts.

I am something between human and hydrological system, a living component of Oru's water cycle. The copper I wear has long since merged with my skin at the joints, creating green-black calluses that will never soften. My fingernails have been replaced by grafts of shingle-crust shell, allowing me to feel the health of a colony through direct contact. When I finally die—as all Shelf-Keepers do, on the terraces, at work—my body will not be burned or buried. It will be laid out on a selected shelf of barren rock, where over the course of a single season, the shingle-crusts will colonize it completely, turning me into a new foundation for the city I spent my life maintaining.

The other Operators tell their children that the Shelf-Keepers are half-ghost, already part of the mountain. They are not wrong. I am as much a feature of Oru as the iron aqueducts or the grinding Silt-Suckers—a human component in a vast, wet machine that transforms stone and water into life.
