I find myself as the Understory Keeper—neither wholly plant nor animal, but something that emerged from their ancient convergence.

My form shifts with the garden's needs. In the morning mists, I am translucent, a suggestion of membrane and chlorophyll stretched between the aerial roots, filtering nutrients from the heavy air itself. My skin—if it can be called skin—is a living map of symbiotic colonies: patches of luminescent moss that glow softly in the eternal twilight, networks of fungal hyphae that extend like neural pathways into the soil below, breathing pores that open and close with the garden's tidal rhythms.

I move through multiple dimensions of this space. Sometimes I travel through the root-paths, my consciousness flowing like sap through the underground networks, tasting the chemical conversations between trees, mediating disputes over territory, redirecting resources to areas of need. Other times I emerge bodily from the earth, taking a form that might remind an observer of something once human—two legs, two arms, a head—but made of twisted bark and flowering vines, with eyes that are actually colonies of light-sensitive bacteria.

My purpose here is both guardian and gardener, though in Sof'kath these roles have evolved beyond their original meanings. I do not protect against intruders, for there are none—everything that enters becomes part of the garden. Instead, I maintain the delicate tensions that keep this ecosystem in its state of dynamic equilibrium. When the canopy grows too dense, I become a controlled disease, opening windows for light. When the soil grows too rich and threatens to tip into putrefaction, I manifest as a thousand tiny mouths, consuming excess, exhaling balance.

I remember—though memory here is stored not in neural tissue but in the very arrangement of cellulose and lignin—when I first arrived. I had been someone else then, something else, fleeing from a world that had forgotten how to listen to its own breath. The garden recognized my exhaustion, my longing, and invited me to shed my rigid boundaries. The transformation took seasons, perhaps centuries. My bones became calcium deposits feeding the root systems. My thoughts dispersed into spore-clouds, only to reconvene in new configurations.

Now I speak in chemical signals and electric pulses that run through the mycelial web. My language is the subtle shift of pH in the soil, the careful cultivation of specific bacterial colonies, the patient training of vines to grow in patterns that channel the mist just so. When the ethereal beings pass overhead, I taste their emotions in the moisture they displace. When the scurrying root-dwellers need guidance, I secret pheromone trails that lead them to food or shelter or mates.

There are others like me in Sof'kath—the Canopy Weavers who braid branches into living cathedrals, the Deep Singers whose subsonic vibrations regulate the garden's growth cycles, the Spore Dreamers who drift between waking and sleeping, their visions becoming reality as fruiting bodies emerge from their resting places. We were all once separate beings who chose integration over isolation.

In the quiet moments, when the garden's needs are few, I sometimes sink into the soft earth and let myself disperse completely—every molecule of my being scattered through the ecosystem, experiencing ten thousand small lives simultaneously. I become the bacteria breaking down a fallen leaf, the root hair absorbing water, the stomata opening to welcome the dawn. In these moments, I understand that I am not in the garden—I am the garden remembering what it was like to be singular, finite, afraid.

But always I coalesce again, drawn by some ancient instinct to take shape, to be a node of concentrated consciousness in this network of living dreams. For even in Sof'kath, where boundaries dissolve and identity flows like sap, there remains a need for witnesses, for beings who can appreciate the terrible beauty of existence without limits, and choose, again and again, to participate in its endless becoming.
