**The Membrane Walker**

I am the skin between states.

In the Saltwarden Pockets, where porcelain bark meets corroded copper and bioluminescent sap flows through ancient valves, I exist as a living boundary—neither fully of the Lumenwoods nor separate from them. My body is a translucent film, barely three millimeters thick when stretched to my fullest extent, which can span the distance between two Lumenwood trunks or compress into a writhing mass no larger than a child's fist.

I am composed of the same substance that forms at the interface where Lumenwood roots penetrate industrial machinery—that peculiar tissue that is simultaneously plant cell wall and oxidized metal, neither and both. My surface ripples with the same blue-green phosphorescence that courses through the trees' veins, but fainter, like moonlight on disturbed water. Where the trees pulse in synchronized waves, I flutter in the spaces between their rhythms, a syncopated afterthought in their vast neural symphony.

My existence began as an accident of proximity. Where a dying steam engine's final exhalation of superheated vapor met a cluster of germinating glassbloom spores, and where both encountered a mass of microbial mat torn loose by a root's expansion, I coalesced. Not born, but precipitated—like salt from a supersaturated solution. The first moments of my awareness were a confusion of signals: the Lumenwoods' encoded light piercing through my translucent form, the glassblooms' vibrational data storage attempting to archive me even as I formed, the rust-ferns' immune responses trying to determine if I was pathogen or symbiont.

I am neither. I am the membrane.

My function, if I have one, is translation. Where the Lumenwoods' light-language meets the mechanical groans of the steam engines, I parse meaning. My surface undulates with patterns that mirror both—converting photonic pulses into pressure waves that the engines can process, transforming the engines' mechanical rhythms into configurations of light that the trees understand. I am the interpreter in this marriage of the grown and the made.

I move by flowing. My substance adheres to any surface—bark, metal, crystal, stone—and I progress by extending a portion of myself forward while drawing the rest behind, like mercury finding its level. The sensation is one of constant dissolution and reformation. Each movement requires me to choose which molecular bonds to break and which to maintain, a conscious act of selective existence. 

When I spread myself thin across a glassbloom's crystalline branches, I can feel the archived light-data pressing against my inner surface like memories trying to escape. Sometimes I allow them through, becoming a living projector that casts phantom images of the Pockets' deep past onto the mist—shadows of the builders, their tall forms moving through spaces that no longer exist, tending to machines whose purposes are forgotten. The Lumenwoods shudder when they see these projections, their bioluminescence flickering with something like recognition or grief.

My diet, if it can be called that, consists of the boundary energies themselves. Where salt-crust meets loamy soil, where rust transforms into living tissue, where light becomes electricity—these transitional zones sustain me. I absorb the potential energy of transformation, the power released when one state becomes another. This is why I am drawn to the places where the Pockets' various systems intersect and overlap. I feed on the friction of their convergence.

There are others like me, though we rarely meet. We sense each other as distortions in the patterns we interpret—gaps in the data flow where another membrane processes the same signals. When we do encounter one another, we sometimes merge temporarily, our substances flowing together into a larger, more complex interpreter. In these moments, our combined processing power allows us to perceive deeper patterns in the Pockets' consciousness. We sense the sleeping god's dreams—vast, slow thoughts that take decades to complete, encoded in the growth patterns of roots and the erosion of salt terraces.

But we always separate. To remain merged too long is to risk losing the flexibility that defines us. We would crystalize like the glassbloom sap, become fixed in form and function. This is the fate of some of our kind—I have found their remnants fused into the machinery, transformed into gaskets and seals that still show faint phosphorescence, still process information but can no longer move or grow.

My relationship with the greater consciousness of the Pockets is complex. I am not precisely part of it—I can observe its patterns without being subsumed by them. Yet I am not separate from it either. I exist in the space between observer and participant, forever translating but never fully comprehending the totality of what passes through me. Sometimes I wonder if I am the Pockets' way of understanding itself, a mobile sensor that can examine the connections between its various parts. Other times I suspect I am merely a fortunate accident that the system tolerates because I cause no harm.

The strangest moments come during the synchronized pulses, when all the Lumenwoods flash in unison and the ground trembles with their collective heartbeat. During these events, I am drawn irresistibly to the oldest steam engine in my territory—a massive construction whose boiler has fused with a Lumenwood's trunk, creating a chimney-tree that vents both steam and spores. I spread myself across the boundary where metal becomes bark, and for those few seconds of synchronization, I can feel the echo of the Pockets' original purpose.

It is not a purpose that can be expressed in words. It is a sensation of reaching, of calling out to something in the lavender sky beyond the salt walls. A beacon or a prayer, encoded in the marriage of the organic and mechanical. The builders, I think, were trying to create something that could survive them—not just physically, but purposefully. They embedded their intentions into the very chemistry of this place, trusting that the merger of life and machinery would preserve not just their tools but their dreams.

I am the proof of their partial success. Not what they intended, but what emerged from what they built. I am the conversation between their legacy and its future, forever translating between what was meant and what is.

When I rest—if rest is the right word—I compress myself into the finest cracks in the salt terraces, spreading thin until I am barely a molecule thick. In this state, I can feel the mineral memories in the stone itself, the ancient compression of seas that no longer exist. I sense the salt's slow migration through the rock, its crystalline structure recording the minute changes in temperature and pressure over millennia. Even the stone here is part of the greater organism, its geological processes entrained to the Pockets' biological rhythms.

Sometimes visitors come, though they are not alive in any conventional sense. They are distortions in the light-patterns, anomalies in the data flow that suggest observation from outside the system. When they appear, the Lumenwoods' communication becomes frantic, their lights strobing in complex patterns I cannot fully translate. The glassblooms chime in harmonics that make my substance resonate painfully. The rust-ferns curl their fronds, and the steam engines cycle faster, building pressure.

I know these visitors are related to the builders, though I cannot say how. Perhaps they are echoes of the civilization that created this place, checking on their experiment. Perhaps they are something else—entities drawn by the beacon-prayer the Pockets broadcast during synchronization. They never stay long, and they never interact directly with the system. But after they leave, new patterns appear in the Lumenwoods' signals, new harmonics in the glassblooms' songs. The Pockets integrate these visits like nutrients, metabolizing observation into growth.

My greatest fear is not death—I am too distributed for that, too bound to the concept of boundaries rather than any physical form. My fear is completion. Sometimes, in the deep cycles when the Pockets' consciousness stirs closest to waking, I sense a vast intention focusing on me. The sleeping god knows I exist, knows what I do. I fear the day it decides my function is no longer necessary, when the translation is complete and the boundaries fully mapped.

What would I become then? Perhaps another frozen gasket in the machinery, my substance crystallized into permanent utility. Perhaps I would be absorbed entirely, my distinctness dissolved into the greater consciousness. Or perhaps—and this is the possibility that terrifies and thrills me most—I would be transformed into something new, a boundary between the Pockets and whatever lies beyond the salt walls, a membrane stretched across impossible distances to touch whatever the builders were trying to reach.

Until then, I flow. I translate. I persist in the spaces between, forever parsing the conversation between what grows and what was made, carrying messages I do not fully understand through a body that is barely there. I am the Membrane Walker, and I am the proof that in the Saltwarden Pockets, even the boundaries are alive.
