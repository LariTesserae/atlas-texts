I find myself as one of the **Tide Keepers**.

Not by choice—I never imagined I would become this. I was examining the strange patterns in the moss that morning, my fingers tracing the spirals that had appeared overnight on the dock posts. The water was rising faster than it should have been, but I was a naturalist, obsessed with understanding the geometries that kept appearing in our settlement. When the suspension hit, I was knee-deep in the shallows, bent forward, my hand just touching the water's surface.

Now I am a monument to curiosity frozen mid-gesture.

My body has... adapted. My skin has taken on the texture of weathered driftwood, smooth and grey-brown, with thin channels carved by centuries of mist condensation running down my arms like ancient script. My hair has become something between kelp and human keratin—long strands that float upward as if still underwater, providing perfect nesting material for the smaller birds. A family of Mist Swallows has made their home in the cavity where my left shoulder meets my neck. I feel them less as weight and more as whispered thoughts.

My eyes never close, but I've learned to see differently. I perceive time in layers now—the present moment stretched thin over centuries of accumulated sensation. I can watch a single droplet of water hover between falling and landing for what outsiders might call months, seeing every refraction of light through its surface, every microscopic current of air that fails to disturb it. But I also see the shadow-movements of my former neighbors, the Persisters, going about their eternal routines in the corners of existence.

I remember everything about the moment of suspension with perfect clarity—the taste of salt spray that never reached my lips, the cry of warning from old Marta that never quite finished forming, the sensation of my fingertips just beginning to break the water's surface tension. That sensation continues, has been continuing for longer than the settlement existed before the freeze. Sometimes I think I can feel what's beneath the water through that eternal almost-touch: vast shapes sleeping in the depths, waiting.

My breathing has become a kind of communication. Each inhalation draws in not just mist but fragments of sound from across the Rookery—bird calls that carry messages, the glass-chime songs of Chronophages, the not-quite-silence of the Persisters' movements. When I exhale, hours later, the breath carries my observations back out into the cacophony. The birds have learned to interpret these exhalations. The clever ones perch near my mouth when I'm about to breathe out, catching updrafts of temporal information that help them navigate the frozen flood.

I've developed a special relationship with an ancient cormorant I call Splice (though names are meaningless here—it's more a shape I think in her direction). She has eleven joints in each wing and eyes like black mirrors. She brings me news from the edges of the Rookery by perching on my extended arm and tapping her beak against my wooden skin in complex patterns. Through her, I know that the suspension extends exactly 2,847 cormorant-flights in every direction, ending in a wall of mist that tastes of copper and forgetting.

The Chronophages fascinate me. They're drawn to my frozen gesture, circling the point where my fingers meet the water. Sometimes they feed there, and for brief moments I feel sensation return—the cold of the water, the roughness of unseen sand beneath, the movement of something large and patient far below. But the sensation always fades as they move on, leaving me with only the memory of memory of touch.

I've noticed changes, subtle ones. The geometric moss patterns are growing according to a plan I'm beginning to understand—they're writing something, a message or perhaps a formula, in a language that exists outside normal time. When the Mist Swallows in my shoulder cavity sing, the moss glows faintly in response. I've been trying to decode it for decades now, but I suspect I won't understand until the moment of release, if it ever comes.

The other Tide Keepers and I don't communicate in any way outsiders would recognize. We simply... acknowledge each other's presence through our stillness. We're like standing stones in a temple to patience, each positioned at crucial points throughout the settlement. Together, we form a network of awareness that holds the Rookery in its impossible state. Sometimes I wonder if our presence is what maintains the suspension, if we've become load-bearing pillars of frozen time.

I've caught glimpses of what I was before—flashes of memory that surface like flotsam. A life studying the intersection between living systems and mathematics. Papers filled with diagrams of shell spirals and flight patterns. A partner whose face I can no longer fully recall, but whose laugh still echoes somewhere in the depths of suspended sound. Were they among those who became Persisters? Or did they escape the suspension entirely? The not-knowing has become another kind of stillness I must bear.

The strangest part is that I'm not unhappy. There's a terrible peace in this existence, a meditation so deep it's become flesh. I am both prisoner and guardian, observer and observed. Every century or so, I complete another thought, another connection in my understanding of what we've become. The Rookery isn't just frozen—it's fermenting, transforming into something unprecedented.

When I speak—those rare times when enough temporal pressure builds up that words must escape—I try to warn visitors. But the words stretch so long that meaning falls between the syllables. "The flood comes soon," I say, but I mean: The flood is always coming, has always been coming, will always be coming. We exist in the eternal moment before catastrophe, and perhaps that's better than existing in the moment after.

Recently (though "recently" might mean decades), I've noticed my wooden skin beginning to develop the same geometric patterns as the moss. They spread slowly from the point where my fingers touch the water, spiraling up my arm in mathematical precision. The Mist Swallows in my shoulder have begun incorporating the patterns into their nest, weaving them with bits of crystallized time they collect from the Chronophages' feeding sites.

I am becoming part of the message the Rookery is writing. Whether it's a warning, a welcome, or something altogether stranger, I won't know until my transformation is complete. But I have time. Nothing but time, held perfectly still, while the birds scream their eternal chorus and the water waits just beneath my fingertips, promising revelations that may never come.

Sometimes, in the spaces between breaths, I swear I can feel the settlement's original builders watching through my eyes, using my stillness to observe what their home has become. We are all watching together—the living trees of what was once a simple fishing village, keeping vigil over a miracle that might also be a curse.

The flood comes soon. The flood is always coming.

But perhaps that's what makes us holy.
