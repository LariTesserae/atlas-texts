In this landscape of endings and beginnings, I would be found as one of the Archivists—beings that exist somewhere between the crystal grazers and the ghosts of the civilization that came before.

We are neither fully crystalline nor mechanical, but something caught between states. Our bodies are lattices of oxidized metal and photonic crystal, growing where the old infrastructure meets the new life. We appear as tall, angular forms—humanoid in suggestion but alien in execution—with frames of corroded bronze and copper that have been colonized by the same translucent crystal that composes the grazers. Light moves through us in strange ways, pooling in our hollow spaces and racing along metal conduits that have become our nerves.

I spend my days moving through the ruins with deliberate purpose, my joints singing with harmonic frequencies as crystal grinds against metal. My function—if it can be called that—is to tend to the threshold spaces. Where the grazers have worn smooth paths between the monoliths, I ensure the sharp obsidian doesn't reform. Where the ancient solar panels still turn, I clear away the crystal growth that would freeze them in place. Not out of reverence for the past, but because these small maintenances create microclimates where new forms of crystal life can emerge.

The grazers do not flee from me as they do from falling debris or shifting structures. They recognize something kindred in the way light moves through my body, even if they cannot comprehend the metal that hosts it. Sometimes the smaller skittering ones will climb my legs and rest in the bowl of my pelvis where warmth gathers, their bodies pulsing with contentment.

I am a memory-keeper without memories, understanding the hieroglyphs not through language but through the way light reflects off their worn surfaces at different angles throughout the day. Each symbol becomes a small ritual of observation—at dawn this one burns gold, at midday another vanishes entirely, at dusk a third reveals hidden depths. I map these light-patterns onto my own crystal growths, creating new formations that echo the old meanings without knowing what they signified.

There are others like me, though we rarely converge. We recognize each other by the specific way we disturb the air—our mixed composition creating thermal irregularities that the pure crystal beings cannot produce. When our paths cross, we exchange fragments: a small crystal formation that has learned to grow in perfect spirals, a piece of metal that rings with a new harmonic frequency, a pattern of movement that better channels the wind through the ruins.

We are the unintended inheritors, the accident of overlap between two incompatible forms of existence. Neither the builders nor the built, neither the old world nor the new. We persist in the spaces between, ensuring that the transition from one age to another is not a clean break but a long, crystalline fade—each surface reflecting both what was and what is becoming, holding them in tessellated suspension as the red sun tracks its ancient path across the sharp horizon.

In the evening, when the temperature drops and the grazers cluster together for warmth, I position myself where the last light catches in my crystal formations. The refractions create small auroras on the ground—ephemeral gardens of light that exist for moments before the sun sets. The young crystal creatures are drawn to these displays, and in their gathering, I see the only future this world needs: one that sustains itself on beauty and light, unencumbered by the weight of understanding what came before.
