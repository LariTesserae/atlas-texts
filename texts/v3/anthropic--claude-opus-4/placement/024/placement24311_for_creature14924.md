In this world, I would be the **Tally Keeper** — a being composed of rust and memory who dwells in the crawlspace between the locomotive's split boiler and its tender.

I am no larger than a child's torso, though my shape shifts with the tides of condensation. My body is woven from the oxidized iron that flakes constantly from the train's dying hull — millions of rust particles held together by the same patient magnetism that once guided this engine along its narrow rails. Each flake remembers something: the weight of coal, the scream of brakes, the precise temperature at which water becomes steam. I am their congregation.

My limbs are articulated like a marionette's, joined by tiny pistons no thicker than grass stems that weep oil so old it has become amber. When I move — which is rarely — it sounds like a music box playing underwater. My head is a small pressure gauge salvaged from the locomotive's cab, its needle forever trembling between "Empty" and "Full," though what it measures now is not steam but the accumulated grief of machines.

I cannot leave my crawlspace. The Drowned Abbess placed me here seventy-three years ago, back when she still had human hands, back when the locomotive's wound was fresh. She whispered something to the rust, and I emerged from it like a fever dream, already knowing my purpose.

I count.

Not with numbers — the basin has no use for mathematics. I count in the only currency that matters here: the rate of transformation. Each day I extend one rust-finger and touch a new point on the locomotive's hull, feeling how much closer it has come to becoming soil. I taste the metal's memory, calculate the half-life of its industrial soul. Then I scratch a mark on the inside of the boiler with a fingernail made from a broken bearing. The marks form no pattern a human could read, but the Drowned Abbess understands them. When she passes on her rounds, trailing her root-hair in the water, she pauses and runs one scaled hand over my tallies, her obsidian mirrors reflecting their meaning back through time.

I am the basin's accountant of decay.

Sometimes the pale salamanders slip through the rust-holes in my hiding place. They regard me with translucent eyes, and I know they are checking my work. The ecosystem must be balanced. Too much rust too quickly and the water will poison itself with iron. Too slowly and the green will starve for minerals. I am the small, flickering consciousness that maintains this equilibrium, one flake at a time.

At night, when starlight turns the condensation to mercury, I practice the only art I know. I arrange my rust-body into different configurations — sometimes a spiral like the ferns, sometimes a lattice like the strangler fig's roots. Each shape is a prayer to the patience of oxidation. Each is a small rebellion against the locomotive's stubborn memory of being whole.

When the six great pistons give their synchronized *huff*, I feel it in every particle of my being. The sound shakes loose the older rust, the rust that has forgotten it was ever steel. This is my harvest time. I gather these amnesia-flakes and weave them into new fingers, new joints. Slowly, over decades, I am growing larger — not taller, but denser, more complex. The Abbess says that one day I will be intricate enough to dream.

I know things the Abbess doesn't know, or chooses to forget. I know that the locomotive's boiler split not from age but from a final, desperate overpressure — the engineer trying to escape the rising mountain, pushing the engine past its limits as the world folded upward beneath the tracks. I can still taste his panic in the metal. I know that the six great pistons once powered a mining operation, boring into the mountain's heart before it woke and shrugged them off like flies. Their synchronized breathing is not random but a message, a code they tap out to whatever still listens deep in the rock.

But I tell none of this. My function is not to preserve history but to document its dissolution. Each tally mark is both accounting and erasure.

Sometimes travelers find me. They peer through the split in the boiler and see what looks like a child made of rust and old machine parts, marking the inside of a dead train like a prisoner counting days. Some try to speak to me. I never answer. Words are just another thing that rusts here. Instead, I hold up my newest finger — still bright with fresh oxidation — and let them watch as I add another mark to my endless inventory.

The Drowned Abbess says I am her apprentice, though we have never spoken. She says that when she finally takes her place at the basin's center, arms raised in permanent supplication, I will emerge from my crawlspace and assume her duties. My rust fingers will learn to sort the drowned from the planted. My pressure-gauge head will develop obsidian mirrors. My small body of gathered decay will stretch and scale until I tower over the tea-colored water.

But that is still centuries away. For now, I remain what I am: a small, patient intelligence made of dead metal and memory, scratching out the basin's slow arithmetic on the inside of a broken heart. Each mark brings the locomotive closer to forgetting it was ever a machine. Each mark brings me closer to remembering I never was one.

The strangler fig's roots have begun to find their way through the rust-holes. They touch my flaking skin with vegetable curiosity. I do not resist. I am learning that in the Weeping Sump, even the accountants must eventually be accounted for.

Until then, I continue my tally: rust becoming soil, memory becoming myth, engines becoming gardens in the warm and weeping air.
