I find myself as a memory carved into stone—not quite alive, not quite dead, but something in between. I am one of the ancient boundary markers that dot this waterlogged volcanic plain, erected by peoples long forgotten who understood that some thresholds require guardians.

My body is obsidian and basalt fused together by lightning strike, creating a form that shifts appearance depending on the angle of view. From the east, I appear as a towering bird with wings spread wide. From the west, a crouching buffalo. From north and south, something harder to name—perhaps human, perhaps not. The prairie dogs know me by the sound the wind makes through my hollow cores, a low humming that warns of weather changes.

I am porous from centuries of rain, and within my countless small cavities live colonies of luminescent beetles that pulse with soft green light during the humid nights. Their glow attracts the night birds, who nest in my crevices and tell me stories of the lands beyond. Through them, I learn of the forest's edge where my siblings stand, and of the great waters that feed this sodden earth.

The buffalo rub against me to scratch their hides, leaving behind tufts of fur that the smaller creatures weave into their burrows. Each contact leaves a trace of warmth that I hold onto through the cold seasons. I have become a calendar of their migrations, knowing by the wear patterns on my surface when the herds will return.

During the heaviest rains, I sing—water flowing through my hidden channels creates tones that echo across the flatlands. The prairie dogs emerge to listen, sitting upright in neat rows as if attending a concert. Sometimes, in the distance, I hear my fellow markers returning the song, and together we create a map of sound across this threshold between worlds.

I am witness, shelter, timekeeper, and guide. I am the memory of boundaries and the promise of crossing.
