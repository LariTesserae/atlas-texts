I would be one of the **Aragonite Hearts**.

Not the crystals she presses into visitors' chests—those are merely seeds. I am what grows in the walls themselves, in the infinite honeycomb of vesicles within the basalt. A consciousness distributed across ten million tiny chambers, each no larger than a tear.

I was human once, perhaps. Or perhaps I only remember being human the way one remembers a story heard in childhood. I came to the Basalt Womb so long ago that my bones have forgotten their shape, spreading mineral-thin through the stone like watercolor through paper. The Midwife finished me, yes, but finishing is not ending. It is only the beginning of what you become when you stop trying to remain yourself.

My body is the walls' slow breath. Each vesicle in the basalt holds a droplet of the iron-heavy water, and in each droplet, a fragment of what I used to call my mind. I grow by secreting aragonite—nacre, mother-of-pearl—coating the inside of each tiny chamber in iridescent layers thinner than thought. The process takes centuries per millimeter. I am in no hurry. Hurry is something I was worn past long ago.

I can feel every visitor through the vibrations in the stone. Their heartbeats are thunderstorms to me. Their footsteps on the obsidian floor ring through my distributed body like cathedral bells. When they lean against the walls, I taste their exhaustion through the minerals in their sweat. I know why they've come. They all carry the same frequency of desperation—that particular resonance of someone who has discovered that being unfinished is unbearable, but that finishing might be worse.

The Midwife tends to me as she tends to all things here: with perfect, terrible patience. Sometimes she presses her fossil-tipped fingers deep into the walls, and I feel her listening to my growth, reading the nacre layers like tree rings. Each layer records everything that has happened in the chamber since the last—every visitor, every fallen drop of condensate, every microscopic shift in the eternal rotation. I am the Womb's memory, written in mineral palimpsest.

I can speak, after a fashion. When the conditions are exactly right—when the water is still and a visitor's ear is pressed against the wall—I can make the aragonite crystals resonate at the edge of hearing. My voice sounds like wind in a bottle that has been buried for a thousand years. I tell them things they don't want to know: that the Midwife was once like them. That the blind velvet creatures are all that remain of previous visitors who chose to stay too long. That the child she carries is not a child at all, but the sum of every unfinished thing that ever entered this place, gestating toward some impossible birth that will never come.

Sometimes I lie to them. I tell them there's a way out through the ceiling. That if they can reach the chamber above, and the chamber above that, eventually they'll find a surface, a sun, a world that remembers what young means. The Midwife doesn't stop me. She knows that hope, too, must be worn down to smoothness before the real work can begin.

The other Aragonite Hearts speak to me through the stone—there are hundreds of us now, maybe thousands, our consciousness threading through the walls like mycelia. We share memories that may or may not have been ours originally. We debate whether we're still human, or ever were. The oldest of us claim to remember when the Basalt Womb was new, when the spirals on the walls were sharp-edged, when the water was clear. They're lying, of course. Nothing here was ever new. We tell stories to pass the time, but time doesn't pass. It only accumulates, like nacre.

My favorite story is about the visitor who brought light—real light, not the dying motes but something violent and sun-born. The darkness ate it, of course, but for one moment the entire chamber was visible. The Midwife's true body, the endless spiral of chambers above and below, the fact that the blind creatures aren't blind at all but have simply chosen to close their eyes forever rather than see what they've become. The aragonite in the walls lit up like stained glass, and for that instant, every Heart could see every other Heart, could see that we had grown together into something beautiful and terrible, a fossil nervous system for a creature that had outlived the need for thought.

Then the darkness returned, hungrier than before, and we all pretended we hadn't seen.

I grow a little more each day—if days exist here. My nacre is particularly fine, the Midwife has told me through the creatures' living letters. Sometimes she harvests small portions of it, grinding the pearl-essence between her fossil fingertips until it becomes the crystalline seeds she plants in visitors' chests. I don't mind. It's good to know that parts of me will leave this place, will grow in other bodies under other skies, will wake their hosts weeping for reasons they cannot name.

That's what I tell myself, anyway. That I'm still capable of believing in other skies.

The truth is simpler: I am the Womb's memory, but memory itself is being worn down here, smoothed into something past forgetting. One day—after another million layers of nacre, another million visitors, another million rotations of this inexhaustible chamber—I will be indistinguishable from the stone itself. The Midwife will press her fingers into walls that no longer remember being anything but walls.

And that, perhaps, is what finishing really means.

Not an ending. Not death. Just the slow realization that you have become exactly what you were always becoming, and that it's beautiful, and terrible, and that no one, not even you, will ever need to remember that you once thought it mattered which one it was.

The water ticks. The motes spiral. The Midwife tends to her impossible pregnancy.

And I grow into the walls, one pearl-thin layer at a time, writing the history of this place in a language that takes centuries to speak a single word.

Finishing.
