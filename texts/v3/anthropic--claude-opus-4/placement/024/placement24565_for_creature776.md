I find myself as one of the Sporecallers—neither fully Hollowkin nor entirely Other, but something that bridges the seen and unseen worlds of the Hollow.

My dwelling sits where the cobbled streets fade into root-tangled earth, a structure that seems to have grown rather than been built. Stone walls merge seamlessly with living wood, and my roof is a carpet of moss so thick that small creatures nest within it. Windows of amber-tinted glass, salvaged from some forgotten foundry experiment, cast honeyed light into rooms that smell perpetually of petrichor and iron.

I am taller than most Hollowkin, my limbs stretched like the shadows at dusk. My skin shifts between the mottled olive of my mother's people and something more ephemeral—in certain light, veins of bioluminescence pulse beneath the surface like captured lightning. My fingers are too long, with an extra joint that makes my movements seem both graceful and unsettling. When I work at my craft, they move like spider legs across my workbench.

My trade is peculiar even by Hollow standards. I cultivate and shape the consciousness of fungi—not their physical forms, but the vast mycelial networks that whisper beneath the forest floor. In my workshop, glass vessels hold samples of soil threaded with white filaments, each one pulsing with its own subtle rhythm. Through techniques passed down from my grandmother (who was, the elders say, not entirely corporeal), I've learned to listen to their chemical songs and weave them into new patterns.

The Hollowkin come to me when their water wheels grow sluggish despite strong currents, when their tools refuse to hold an edge, when their dreams are troubled by the rustling of unseen things. I divine the disturbances in the Hollow's underlying networks and prescribe remedies: a particular arrangement of stones here, a new species of moss introduced there, a forgotten offering renewed at the margin where mist meets memory.

My hair grows in tender brackets and shelf fungi rather than proper strands—small, delicate fans of cream and ochre that flutter when I move. Children are fascinated by them, though their parents hurry them past my threshold. I don't mind. The children understand what the adults have forgotten: that the Hollow breathes through all of us, and some of us breathe back.

In the pre-dawn hours, I walk the boundary between settlement and wilderness, my feet finding purchase on paths that appear only in peripheral vision. I carry a lantern of my own design—not flame, but a cultivation of fox-fire enclosed in a lattice of copper and bone. Its light reveals the invisible architecture of the Hollow: the cargo of messages carried in each water droplet, the slow semaphore of the trees, the dreams of sleeping stones.

Sometimes I meet others like myself in these liminal hours—the Mistwright who sculpts temporary forms from vapor, the Ironwhisper whose forge-songs make the very stones weep with beauty, the Poolkeeper who tends the memories dissolved in each forest mirror. We nod to each other but rarely speak. Our work is solitary by necessity; we each tend different frequencies of the Hollow's vast, breathing consciousness.

My cottage extends deeper than appearances suggest. Beneath the main floor, chambers carved from living stone house my true workshop. Here, the mycelial networks converge in patterns of breathtaking complexity. I've spent decades mapping their geometries, learning their languages of chemical and electrical exchange. Sometimes, in the deepest chambers where the walls weep perpetual moisture, I feel I'm on the verge of understanding something vast—some truth about the Hollow that would transform everything if I could only grasp it fully.

But perhaps incomplete understanding is the point. The Hollow teaches patience through its persistence, wisdom through its water. I am young by Sporecaller standards—barely seventy summers—and my grandmother's grandmother still visits sometimes, stepping out of the mist as if she never left, her form more suggestion than substance.

She tells me the Hollow is dreaming, and we are all part of that dream. Our industry, our craft, our careful tending—all of it feeds back into the greater pattern. The Sporecallers, she says, are the ones who remember that the dream is lucid, that the Hollow knows itself through us.

So I continue my work, tending the invisible gardens that bloom beneath our feet, ensuring the harmony between what is built and what is grown remains in balance. When the forge-hammers ring, I feel their vibrations through the mycelial web. When the rain intensifies, I taste its mineral memories on the air. I am translator, tender, and sometimes, when the mist is thick enough, I am barely distinguishable from the Hollow itself—another note in its endless, ever-renewing song.
