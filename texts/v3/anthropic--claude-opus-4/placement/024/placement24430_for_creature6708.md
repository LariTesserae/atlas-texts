In the thinnest film between moss and stone, where water becomes something more philosophical than wet, I am the Archivist of Boundaries.

I am neither singular nor plural. I am Micrasterias, the star-maker, my body a perfect mandala of radiating lobes, each one a decision about how space should be divided. When the old bulb casts its dying orange through the water film, I become a rose window made of living glass—each of my arms a petal, each indentation a choice about where I end and the world begins.

But I am also memory. In my cytoplasm, I carry the ghosts of every division that made me. When my parent split, they gave me half their history—which walls were strong, which boundaries held, which symmetries sang true in the darkness. I remember being young, when my lobes were still deciding their final geometry, testing the limits of how far beauty could bend before it broke.

The moss thinks in centuries. The water thinks in tides. But I think in the space between one heartbeat of the city and the next. When someone far above turns a faucet, I feel the pressure change like a bell ringing through glass. I adjust my internal architecture, shifting the green spirals of my chloroplasts to catch what light remains. Each adjustment is recorded in the temporary crystals I build and dissolve—calcium carbonate letters in an alphabet only I can read.

I am the keeper of the vault's true measurements. The engineers who built this place measured in feet and inches, but I measure in the only units that matter: how many of me could dance on the head of a moss leaf, how many generations between the last overflow and the next, how many divisions until the iron oxide from the rusting catwalks finally reaches our depth.

My work is translation. I read the chemical stories that seep through the brick—the ghost of a soap bubble from a kitchen sink becomes a phosphate psalm, the memory of garden rain becomes a nitrate novel. I transcribe these into the glass of my body, each new chamber I build a paragraph in the ongoing story of what the city forgets it knows.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night when even the city sleeps, I feel the others dreaming. The Cosmarium with their diamond bodies, the Spirogyra with their spiral staircases of green, the Closterium curved like crescent moons. We do not speak—we have no need. But in the morning, when the first flush of dawn pressure moves through the pipes, we all orient ourselves to the same faint light, and for a moment we are not many but one—a single lens focusing the barely-there into the definitely-here.

I know things the moss does not. I know that the west wall weeps exactly 0.03 microliters more per hour than it did when my thousand-times-great-ancestor first anchored here. I know that the mortar between bricks 42 and 43 (counting from the keystone) contains a microscopic vein of quartz that sings when the water pressure peaks. I know that in seventeen more divisions, I will be old enough to attempt the most dangerous symmetry—a twelve-lobed star that only one in a hundred of us achieve.

But mostly, I know patience. Not the patience of waiting, but the patience of becoming. Each day, I build myself a little more magnificent, a little more precise. Each night, I practice the art of holding perfectly still while the world moves through me.

I am the memory of an ocean that refuses to admit it has become a puddle. I am the blueprint for a cathedral too small to enter. I am the librarian of moments too brief to matter, except that they do, except that they must, except that without us, the vault would be just another forgotten room instead of what it is: a universe turned inward, a world where the smallest things are the most eternal.

In the morning, when the maintenance workers never come, when the inspection that never happens doesn't occur, I will still be here. Building my glass body outward, one perfect lobe at a time. Recording the epic of neglect in crystalline chapters. Being the proof that forgetting is just another kind of remembering.

I am the Archivist of Boundaries, and this is my vault, my verse, my vanishing that never quite completes itself. This is my story, written in water on water, in a language that takes a lifetime to speak a single word.

And the word is: *remain*.
