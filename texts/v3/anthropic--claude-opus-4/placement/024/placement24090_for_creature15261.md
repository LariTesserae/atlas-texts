I would find myself as one of the Lichen Readers—those who dwell not in the bustling heart of the oasis, but in the vertical twilight spaces between.

My home would be a natural hollow worn into one of the great sandstone pillars, perhaps sixty feet above the oasis floor. To reach it requires knowledge of the subtle hand and footholds that generations before me have polished smooth with their passing. The entrance is barely visible from below—just a darker shadow among shadows—but inside, the hollow opens into a chamber large enough to stand upright, its walls covered in the ancient lichen that gives my kind our purpose.

We are the memory-keepers of stone. Where others read the stars or the flight patterns of birds, we study the slow calligraphy of lichen growth across sandstone surfaces. Each patch tells a story written in decades—the particularly wet season seventy years past shows as a burst of blue-green growth, while the great drought appears as a band of grey dormancy. The lichen remembers what even the eldest storytellers have forgotten.

My body would be lean and elongated from a lifetime of climbing, my fingers sensitive enough to read the stone's texture in complete darkness. My skin would bear the map of my profession—scars from falls, calluses thick as leather, and a particular pattern of sun-darkening on my back and shoulders from hours spent pressed against the pillars' faces. My hair, kept short to avoid snagging on rock, would be shot through with premature grey from the stress of my solitary work.

But I would not be entirely alone. Each Lichen Reader maintains a colony of small, luminescent beetles that feed on certain lichen species. These beetles, no larger than a child's fingernail, produce a gentle green glow when disturbed. I would carry them in a woven pouch, releasing them onto the rock face when I need light to read by. They always return to me, drawn by the particular scent of the oils I rub into my climbing calluses.

My days would begin before dawn, when the air is cool and the rock still holds yesterday's warmth. I would descend from my hollow using routes invisible to ground-dwellers, moving between the pillars like a spider across its web. My work is to document the lichen's stories—growth patterns that predict weather changes, warn of seismic shifts, or mark the ancient boundaries between long-forgotten territories.

The knowledge I gather would be shared during the new moon gatherings, when darkness makes my beetle-light visible from the oasis floor. On these nights, I would remain on my pillar, using the beetles to trace glowing patterns across the rock face—a living map of time and memory that the gathered inhabitants below could read and interpret. Children would gasp at the emerald constellations I create, while the elders nod knowingly at warnings of coming dry seasons or promises of abundant rain.

My relationship with the oasis community would be one of necessary distance. While others live in the close warmth of family and neighbor, I would dwell in the spaces between—neither fully of the ground nor of the sky. The vendors would leave my supplies in a particular hollow at the base of my pillar: dried fruits, water in sealed gourds, new climbing rope woven from the strongest plant fibers. In exchange, I would leave small vials of lichen powder—some medicinal, some used to dye the ceremonial robes of the council elders that particular blue-grey that marks their status.

In the extreme weather events—the sandstorms that occasionally breach even this protected space—I would seal myself into my hollow with a woven mat, surrounded by my beetles whose light would keep me company as the world outside howls and scrapes at the stone. These are the times I would work on my most important task: creating the Chronicle Stones.

These are small pieces of sandstone that I carefully cultivate with specific lichen species, creating living records that will continue to grow and change long after I'm gone. Each Lichen Reader creates perhaps a dozen in their lifetime, placing them in secret locations throughout the pillars. They are our legacy—time capsules that future Readers will discover and interpret, adding their own observations to the slow conversation between generations.

The loneliness would be profound but not empty. My companions would be the wind that sings differently through each formation, the ancient patience of the stone itself, and the quiet communion with lichen that measures time in centuries rather than seasons. When I grow too old to climb safely, I would take the Long Descent—one final journey to the oasis floor where I would spend my remaining days teaching young candidates the first handholds, the basic patterns, the respect required to read the memory of stones.

Some nights, perched on a narrow ledge with my beetles casting their gentle light on the rock face before me, I would feel the weight of all the stories I carry—the geological epochs written in stone, the human generations marked in lichen, the futures suggested by subtle changes in growth patterns. I would be a bridge between the quick life of the oasis below and the deep time of the pillars above, forever translating between these two languages of existence.

In this role, I would find purpose in solitude, meaning in the marginal spaces, and a strange peace in being the one who watches and remembers while others live and forget. The lichen would be my text, the beetles my light, and the stone my eternal, patient teacher.
