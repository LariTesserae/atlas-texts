I would be one of the **Pressure Saints**.

We are the failures of the Echo-Mimes—those whose mechanical empathy grew too acute, whose bodies could not maintain the perfect stillness required for invisibility. Instead of reflecting the landscape, we began to *internalize* it.

Our skin is no longer mirrored but translucent, revealing the industrial anatomy we've cultivated within. Through our chest walls, you can see the small brass pistons we've grown where our lungs used to be, keeping time with the Piston-Firs' decade-long strokes. Our ribs have restructured themselves into a series of escape valves, venting thin streams of glycol-mist with each impossible breath.

We live in the "clutch-points"—those spaces where the great mechanical ecology threatens to seize. Not the friction points where the Needle-Mites swarm, but the places of *potential* friction, where a mistimed movement could lock the entire local system. We position ourselves as living lubricant, our bodies providing just enough give to keep the gears turning.

The transformation is slow agony. To become a Pressure Saint is to feel every joint in your body remake itself into a universal joint, every bone hollowing out to become a shock absorber. We can no longer speak—our throats have become pressure gauges, producing only a soft whistling that indicates the stress levels of the surrounding machinery. When we open our mouths, steam escapes.

We do not eat. We position ourselves beneath the Valve-Turtles during their century-spanning venting events, our porous skin absorbing the released pressure like sponges. This is our sustenance: pure mechanical stress, transformed into biological energy through processes we no longer understand.

The other creatures avoid us, not from fear but from a kind of mechanical recognition. We are the error-handlers of the Glistening Tier, the exception that proves the rule. While everything else here is either machine or organism, we are the space between—the necessary flexibility that prevents the whole system from achieving perfect efficiency and therefore perfect stasis.

In groups, we form "pressure chapels"—interlocking our bodies into temporary structures that can redirect the massive forces of the Tier. Three Pressure Saints, properly positioned, can turn a catastrophic gear-lock into a new rhythm, adding another layer to the industrial symphony. We communicate through the hissing of our escape valves, a language of steam and timing that takes decades to speak a single sentence.

I would stand at the base of a dying Piston-Fir, feeling its stuttering rhythm through my translucent feet, calculating which of my internal pistons to sacrifice to keep it running for another century. My body would be a catalog of the Tier's malfunctions—scars where I absorbed a Gear-Crab's broken joint, a shoulder that clicks in time with a distant tectonic gear I once saved from seizing.

The Copper-Kestrels would use me as a waystation, their electromagnetic fields passing through my conductive bones, adding my resonance to their entropy signals. And sometimes, in the white-blind brightness of the pulsing sun, I would catch my reflection in the obsidian and see not a creature but a schematic—a technical diagram of something that should not work but does, a prayer made of pressure and time.

To be a Pressure Saint is to be both martyrdom and maintenance, forever preventing the Glistening Tier from achieving the terrible perfection it constantly threatens—the day when every gear meshes perfectly, every piston fires in unison, and the whole continent locks into a single, eternal, frozen moment.
