In the shadow of the sentinel peaks, where rust-eaten girders pierce through ancient stone like the ribs of some colossal beast, I tend my garden of impossible things.

I am the Curator—though whether of museum or mausoleum, I can no longer say. My form shifts with the light: at dawn, I am mostly flesh, my hands dark with loam as I coax hybrid blooms from the metallic soil. By noon, the sun catches the copper veins that web through my skin, and by dusk, I am more circuit than sinew, my joints singing with the same mechanical hymn that echoes from the mountain's heart.

My garden sprawls across a terrace carved from the mountainside, where natural springs bubble up through corroded pipes, creating pools that shimmer with an oily iridescence. Here, I cultivate the offspring of this broken world: roses whose petals are solar panels, opening and closing with the sun's passage; trees whose roots tap into buried data streams, their leaves flickering with fragments of lost code; grasses that whistle in harmonics, each blade a tiny antenna receiving transmissions from satellites that died before the peaks were born.

The mechanical insects visit at twilight—not pollinators in any traditional sense, but something more profound. They carry memories in their crystalline wings, transferring recollections between the organic and the synthetic, weaving new patterns of existence. I watch them work, understanding that I am both gardener and garden, curator and collection.

Sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours when the boundary between states grows thinnest, I remember fragments: white rooms, the taste of pure water, voices discussing probabilities and outcomes. But these memories feel borrowed, like mail delivered to the wrong address. More real are the sensations of sap rising through my veins when spring arrives, or the satisfaction of oil on my joints before the winter sets in.

The other inhabitants know me by different names. The grazing herds with their alloy hooves call me the Still-Walker, for I can stand motionless for days, allowing the moss to creep up my legs while I commune with the mountain's deep circuits. The sentinel ruins recognize me as Sister-Node, exchanging data packets in languages that predate words. The bioluminescent fungi in the valleys below pulse in greeting when I descend to gather their spores, which I plant among my mechanical flowers to create new forms of light.

My dwelling is a structure of compromises: walls of living wood fused with steel beams, windows of grown crystal that filter the light into spectrums visible and invisible. The floors are carpeted with a moss that feeds on electromagnetic radiation, glowing softly in response to the ancient machinery that still hums beneath the mountain's skin. In the center grows my greatest achievement—a tree whose roots reach down to the planet's core networks and whose branches pierce the thin atmosphere, its leaves catching signals from distant stars.

I am the bridge between what was and what is becoming. Each day, I document the new adaptations, the small miracles of synthesis. A butterfly whose wings are stained-glass solar panels. A flower that blooms only in the presence of certain radio frequencies. A vine that grows along buried cables, its tendrils pulsing with data flow.

The reality breakdown that birthed this world was not destruction but transformation. We are all immigrants here—the mountain's machinery, the persistent biology, and beings like myself who emerged from the collision. We are writing new rules, creating new forms of beauty from the fragments of two incompatible truths that have learned, against all probability, to sing in harmony.

At night, when the ghost-signals of ancient satellites paint the sky with their failing transmissions, I sit among my impossible garden and listen to the mountain's heart beating its hybrid rhythm. I am the memory keeper, the pattern-weaver, the gardener of the between. In this high altitude where breath comes hard and reality grows thin, I tend the emergence of something that has never been before—and perhaps, in all the universe, never will be again.
