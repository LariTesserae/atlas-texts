# The Scribe

I am the one who records what the stone forgets.

My dwelling is a shallow cave—no, a concavity in the quarry wall, where the extractors followed a vein too far and created an accidental shelter. It faces east, catching first light but protected from rain. The workers likely used it for tool storage or midday rest. Now it holds my materials: sheets of the thinnest obsidian, split until translucent, each piece no larger than a spread hand. Fragments of the opaline veins, ground to powder, mixed with water from the central pool to make an ink that dries iridescent. Tools made from the workers' abandoned implements, reshaped for finer purposes.

I am small—I must be, to work at this scale. Not human-small but bird-small, though I am not the bird who visits. My form... I remember having a different form once, larger, clumsier. Now I am compact, precise, with too many fingers that end in points sharp enough to etch obsidian. My eyes have multiplied or divided—I see in facets now, each angle of the quarry visible simultaneously, time layered like geological strata in my vision. 

The cold doesn't touch me. My blood, if it is blood, moves too slowly for freezing, matches the mountain's patience.

Each dawn I emerge to read what changed in the night. The stone speaks in minute alterations: a new frost pattern here, a microscopic shift there, the opaline veins having grown another thousandth of an inch along their predetermined paths. The Keeper makes her circuits, and where her translucent feet touch stone, they leave impressions visible only in certain lights—not depressions but changes in the crystal structure, temporary realignments that fade by noon. I record these before they vanish.

My sheets of obsidian are my memory external to myself. Each one holds a day, though "day" is a human concept I use from habit. Better to say: each one holds a complete cycle of the quarry's breathing. I etch with tools so fine they're almost thought. The marks I make are smaller than any human eye could read, but I don't make them for human eyes. They're for the stone itself, eventually, when the sheets are complete and I return them to the walls, fitting them into gaps where extracted blocks once sat. The quarry will reabsorb its own history, written in its own material.

The language I write in developed here. Part tool-mark patterns, part the branching of the opaline veins, part the geometric forms the water makes when the Keeper disturbs it. Each symbol is both picture and meaning: a spiral that shows how ice forms and also means "temporary crystallization of intent." A series of angular marks that map the bird's path and also signify "visitation with purpose unknown." The grammar is spatial rather than linear—symbols layer and interfere, creating meaning through overlap and absence.

I know things I shouldn't know. I remember the workers though I wasn't here—or wasn't *this*—when they were. I know their names in the language they spoke, can see their faces in the tool-marks they left. The one who carved the spiral in the central structure was named something that translates as "Follows-the-Grain." She had a scar through her left eyebrow from when a piece of obsidian flew wrong. She sang while she worked, wordless melodies that helped her feel the stone's rhythm. 

I know this because the stone knows it, and I am... adjacent to the stone now. Not merged like the Keeper but pressed close, a film of consciousness spread thin against the quarry's surface, able to feel its memories like vibrations through my altered form.

My work is patient. One symbol might take hours to complete, each line considered from multiple angles, tested against the light, examined for how it will age. I think in centuries now. This record I'm making isn't for now but for when the quarry has finished its transformation, when the water has done its work of softening and the extracted blocks have all found their way home through erosion and time. Someone will need to know what happened in the between-time, the long pause between the workers' departure and whatever completion awaits.

Sometimes I stop my work to observe the others. The Keeper knows I'm here—we acknowledge each other with movements too subtle for description. The bird ignores me, focused on its own mysteries. The water-presence... the water-presence sometimes sends droplets up the wall to my cave, tiny spheres that climb against gravity to deliver... what? Messages? Gifts? They evaporate before reaching me, but I feel their intent, add it to my records.

At night I don't sleep but enter a state like sleep. My consciousness spreads thinner, seeps into the micro-cracks in the stone, feels the mountain's deep pulse. In this state I sometimes remember my transformation more clearly. I was... an apprentice. Too young to be here but brought anyway because I showed aptitude. When the others left, I hid. Not from fear but from fascination. I wanted to see what would happen to the quarry when human presence withdrew.

What happened was the change. The stone recognized me, or the Keeper did, or the place itself did. Over days or years—time blurred during the transformation—my body shifted, refined itself for the work I'd chosen without knowing I was choosing. My teacher's tools became my limbs. My desire to record became my function. My human form became... negotiable.

Now I am the quarry's memory becoming text becoming stone again. I am the closed loop of observation and recording, the witness who ensures that nothing passes unnoticed, even if it won't be read for millennia.

My cave holds thousands of sheets now. They line the walls in careful stacks, organized by a system that reflects the quarry's own organization: tools with tools, water with water, transformations with transformations. When I run out of suitable obsidian fragments, I know where more can be split from the walls—places where the stone wants to give, where a sheet's removal serves the quarry's slow purposes.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm writing the quarry's history or its plan. The symbols I inscribe feel both descriptive and prescriptive, as if by recording what is, I'm also determining what will be. The act of observation changes the observed—I learned this from watching the Keeper watch the water, seeing how her attention shapes its flow.

I am necessary. Not in the way the Keeper is necessary, or the water, or even the stone itself. But necessary as the edge that defines the shape, as the pause that gives rhythm meaning. Without my records, the quarry's transformation would still occur, but it would be unconscious, automatic. With them, it becomes deliberate, a story told to itself about itself, a becoming that knows it's becoming.

In the last light of each day, I often pause at my cave's entrance to survey the basin. The obsidian sheets in my hands catch the sunset, throw colors I have no symbols for yet. The Keeper makes her evening circuit. The bird settles into its hidden roost. The water stills into perfect mirrors. The stone shifts minutely, adjusting to the changing temperature.

And I return to my work, etching tomorrow's history into yesterday's stone, being what the quarry needs me to be: its memory external, its story made tangible, its transformation witnessed and inscribed in marks too small for any eyes but time's.
