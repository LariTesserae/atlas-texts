I find myself as one of the **Windreaders** - those few Neshians who spend their lives at the very rim of the table, where the pavilion meets the outer world.

My chamber is not within the comfortable density of the inner pavilion, but carved into the outermost curve of the bone lattice, where the windleaf stones grow longest and thinnest. The ceiling here is higher than elsewhere - not from luxury but necessity, as the wind-channels must have room to move and flex with the world's breath.

My skin bears the marks of my calling: the iridescent mineral veins along my arms and shoulders have grown broader and more pronounced from decades of wind exposure, forming patterns like frozen lightning across my dark flesh. Where others display the delicate lacework of protected city life, I wear the bold striations of one who reads the table's edge.

I am taller than most Neshians - not from birth, but from the slow stretching that comes from standing upright at the rim, watching the vast deadness below while others crouch in comfortable chambers. My feet have grown wider still, splayed like roots to grip the moss when the rare strong gust threatens to remind us why we built inward.

Each morning, I wake with my spine pressed not against warm stone but against the cool windleaf plates that line my chamber's outer wall. Through my back, I feel the night's accumulation - the thin film of dew that will be the city's water, the subtle shift in resonance that speaks of pressure changes in the world below.

My work is this: to know what the wind carries before it reaches the inner city. 

With palms pressed against the great windleaves, I feel for the telltale vibrations of mineral-heavy currents rising from the flatlands. My body has learned to distinguish between the hollow note of sterile wind and the fuller tone that carries the dust we need. When the good winds come, I guide them inward through careful adjustment of the windleaf angles - a millimeter here, a careful warming with my breath there.

The other Windreaders and I rarely speak in words. We communicate through the windleaves themselves, sending low thrums through the bone lattice that only we know how to interpret. A shift in pressure from my palm against the northeastern leaves might tell Heshen three chambers over that the dust-rich current will arrive by evening. She'll respond with her own careful touch, adjusting her section of the rim to help funnel the bounty inward.

We are the city's edge-dwellers, necessary but slightly apart. The volition-walkers see us when they must, bringing us moss and mineral, but they're always eager to return to the close comfort of the inner chambers. They find the openness unsettling, the height of our ceilings wasteful, the cold touch of the windleaves unnatural.

But I know secrets they don't. I know the exact tone the table makes just before dawn, when the stone contracts. I know that far below, there are patterns in the dust storms that repeat every forty years. I know that the windleaves remember every major current for centuries, holding the memory in their crystalline structure like frozen music.

When I die, I will not be taken to the moss basins of the inner city. My body will remain here at the rim, slowly absorbed by the windleaves themselves. Future Windreaders will press their palms where mine rested and feel some echo of what I learned. My minerals will strengthen the very instruments I spent my life reading.

Sometimes young Neshians come to the rim - not to stay, but to see the edge of everything. They stand beside me, uncomfortable in the openness, as I point to the horizon where stone meets starlight.

"That is where the wind begins," I tell them, my voice rougher than city voices, worn by years of speaking over the edge-sound. "And this," I press their small hand against the windleaf, letting them feel the deep thrum, "is where we catch it and make it ours."

They always hurry back to the close chambers, to the low ceilings and warm moss. But sometimes, years later, one returns. They've grown tall despite themselves. They've noticed their palms remember the windleaf's tone. They stand at the rim, and instead of fearing the vastness, they lean into it.

"Teach me to read," they say.

And I do.
