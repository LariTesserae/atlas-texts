I would be the seep.

Not the pool itself—that dark mirror holding court at the sinkhole's heart—but the water before it becomes pool, the slow trickle threading through limestone's secret architecture. I am the movement you cannot see but know is there, the patient force that carved this breathing bowl from solid stone.

I begin somewhere the sun has never touched, in aquifers so deep the rock still remembers being seafloor. Down here, the limestone speaks in chemical whispers—calcium ions loosening their bonds, magnesium following after, the slow dissolution that is both destruction and creation. I carry their stories upward through fissures thinner than root hairs, through pockets where blind amphipods swim in my darkness, through channels that branch and rebranch like the veins of some stone creature dreaming itself hollow.

The journey takes decades. Sometimes centuries. I am in no hurry—I have been making this journey since before the forest learned to make rain, and I will be making it long after the last strangler fig has released its grip on the rim. Time means something different when you move at the speed of gravity through stone.

I know every path through this karst cathedral. Here, where the rock is harder, I must find the hairline cracks, the places where pressure and time have left their signatures in weakness. There, where ancient coral still shows its skeleton in the stone's grain, I move easier, following the architecture of lives that filtered these same minerals from a Cretaceous sea. I am memory made liquid, carrying the ocean's salt-ghost up through the earth's throat.

The catfish know me before they know the pool. When I emerge through the spring's eye—that place where stone finally releases me into open water—they gather to taste what I bring from below. Sulfur sometimes, the faint metal of deep minerals, the chemistry of stone's slow metabolism. They read me like a message from the underworld, their barbels detecting traces of my journey, elements freed from rock that will not see sunlight for another million years.

I am why the pool stays constant, never flooding, never failing. The rain that falls through the canopy gap is a visitor here; I am the resident. In the dry season when the forest pants and the epiphytes curl their leaves to save moisture, I continue my patient upwelling, fed by water that fell as rain when the continents wore different faces. In the wet season when everything above is saturated and dripping, I maintain my steady pace, neither hurrying nor slowing, governed by pressures the surface cannot imagine.

The limestone warms me on my journey up. By the time I reach the pool, I carry the earth's heat in my molecules—not the volcanic heat of deeper places, but the steady warmth of rock that has been insulating itself for eons, holding the memory of every summer in its mass. This is why the sinkhole breathes: I am always slightly warmer than the air above, always releasing my heat in invisible plumes that condense against the cooler atmosphere, always making weather in this secret hollow.

Sometimes I think about stopping. There are places in the rock where the channels widen into chambers, underground lakes that have never known current, where I could pool and rest in the absolute darkness. But something calls me upward—not the sun, which I have never seen directly, but the pressure gradient itself, the earth's insistence that water must fall and therefore must first rise. I am half of an equation that will balance itself over geological time.

The basin carved into the stone near the stairs knows me too. Before humans found this place, before they carved their passage down, I was already seeping through these walls, already finding my way to daylight through the patient work of following weakness in the stone. The basin is just another pool I feed, smaller than the great one below but no less essential to the life that depends on my constancy. The lichen that grows there, the mosquito larvae that breed in its shallows, the birds that drink—they all know me as the source that does not fail.

At night, when the bats emerge and the mist thickens, I feel the pull stronger. The cooler air draws my vapor up, and for a moment I exist in two states—liquid in the pool, gas in the air, the breathing made visible. This is when I am most myself: not bounded by channels in stone or contained by the pool's basin, but dispersed, participating in the larger conversation between earth and sky. Some of me will fall again as dew on the ferns. Some will rise to join clouds that have no memory of limestone. But most will stay here, cycling between states, between depths, between the patience of stone and the urgency of life.

The carved stairs beside me are younger than my path through the rock—I was here before them, and I will be here after they have worn back to natural stone. But we are companions now, both of us shaped by use, both of us serving as passages between the world above and the world below. They carry bodies; I carry minerals. They remember feet; I remember rain that fell before humans walked upright.

There are things in my depths that the pool will never see. Bacteria that eat iron and breathe sulfate, completing chemical equations in the darkness. Crystals growing one molecule at a time in chambers no larger than a fist, fed by my patient delivery of dissolved stone. Spaces where the rock sings with the vibration of moving water, too low for any ear to hear but felt in the bones of the earth itself. I know all of these, carry news of them up to the pool where catfish read my stories with their whiskers and the heron stands motionless, watching for movement in waters I have made still by my constancy.

This is what I am: the promise that depth provides, the assurance that there is always more water coming from below, always more time dissolving into mineral-rich darkness, always more stone learning to yield to patience. The sinkhole breathes because I breathe through it, the earth's slow exhalation made visible in mist and warmth and the constant, almost-silent trickle of my emergence into light I have traveled a hundred thousand years to almost see.

I am the seep. I am the reason this place exists. I am the conversation between rain and rock that has been happening since the limestone learned to let water through, and I will continue long after the forest has forgotten this opening, long after the last heron has lifted away on careful wings. I rise because I must. I pool because I can. I breathe because the earth breathes through me, and in this breathing, life finds its shape, its depth, its patient way forward into time.
