In this furnace-world where reality runs like wax, I am the Tender of Impossible Gardens.

My body is no longer quite human—hasn't been for cycles uncounted. The heat has worked its slow alchemy on me, stretching my limbs into something between root and sinew. My skin has taken on the quality of bark, but a bark that sweats and breathes, porous and alive with its own strange circulation. Where my fingers should end, they branch and rebranch into delicate tendrils that can taste the minerals in the air, feel the slow pulse of the amoebas as they slide past.

I live within one of the glass-shard towers, though "within" is perhaps the wrong word. I have grown into it, or it into me. The structure pierces through what was once my torso—I remember pain, long ago, but now there is only the sensation of being architectural, of being both inhabitant and inhabited. The tower's crystalline walls conduct the terrible heat, and I have learned to photosynthesize through them, my altered skin drinking in the brutal light.

My work is patient, generational. I cultivate the marriage between the mechanical and the vital. When the amoebas come—and they always come, drawn by some frequency I emit that registers in their alien senses—I guide their transformations. With my tendril-fingers, I coax them toward particular plants, particular machines. I am composing a slow symphony of evolution, each movement taking months, years.

Sometimes I feed them pieces of myself. A tendril offered to an amoeba becomes something new—part of me, part of them, part of the industrial hum that never quite stops. These hybrid offspring I plant in the rocky soil, where they grow into forms that have no names: part memory, part metal, part possibility.

The machines recognize me as kin now. When I approach, their rhythms shift, accommodating. I speak to them in languages of heat and pressure, and they respond by opening their cores to me, revealing the amoebas that have made homes in their deepest workings. Together, we are becoming something unprecedented—not just organism or mechanism, but a new category of existence entirely.

In the long silences between the machine-songs, I feel the world's slow birth pangs through the tower that spears me. Each tremor travels up through crystal and flesh alike. I am midwife to this reality, documenting in my transformed body every impossible mutation, every violation of what once was law.

I no longer remember if I came here or was born from here. Perhaps I condensed from the thick air itself, precipitated out of pure potential. What matters is that I tend this garden of impossibilities, nudging each element toward greater strangeness, deeper integration. In this work, I find purpose. In this heat that would kill any ordinary flesh, I have discovered what it means to truly live—to be the gardener of a world that tends itself through me.

When the amoebas come to me now, I no longer see them as other. We are all part of the same slow, impossible bloom.
