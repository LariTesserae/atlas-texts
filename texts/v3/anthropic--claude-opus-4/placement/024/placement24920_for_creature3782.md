**The Membrane Gardener**

I am the one who tends the boundaries.

My body is not like the others who walk the canopy's surface. Where they are solid, I am permeable. Where they move in discrete steps, I flow. I exist in the liminal space where root meets water, where air dissolves into mist, where the living edge of our world breathes against the vast indifference of the sea.

They call me the Membrane Gardener, though I have forgotten my birth name. It dissolved years ago, like salt in the morning fog.

My skin has become translucent from decades of working in the twilight beneath the canopy. Veins map themselves across my arms like river deltas, visible through the pale membrane of my flesh. My fingers have elongated, webbed with thin tissue that helps me swim through the root-thick water below. When I surface, the webs fold back, accordion-like, against my palms.

I live in the spaces others cannot reach—the hollow chambers within the thickest root bundles, where air pockets form natural rooms. My dwelling shifts as the canopy breathes. Sometimes I wake to find my ceiling has descended three feet in the night, or that a new tendril has woven itself through my eastern wall. I do not resist these changes. I have learned to be fluid.

My work is the health of the edge. Each dawn, I slip into the water to examine the underside of our world. I run my strange hands along the root systems, feeling for rot, for weakness, for the parasitic creatures that sometimes attach themselves to our foundation. I know every texture—the healthy roots feel firm but supple, like muscle, while disease makes them either too soft or brittle as old bone.

The Drift Dwellers above rarely see me, though they know I exist. Sometimes children catch glimpses of my pale form moving beneath the translucent sections of newer growth. They tell stories: that I am half-human, half-sea creature. That I drink only rainwater and eat only the phosphorescent algae that grows in the deep shadows. That I can breathe underwater indefinitely.

Only the last is untrue. I must surface, though less frequently than most. My lungs have adapted, become more efficient. I can hold my breath for nearly an hour now, moving through the submarine forest of roots like a slow ghost.

I carry few tools—a curved blade made from a stingray's barb for cutting away dead growth, pouches of various minerals I've learned to extract from certain seaweeds to treat plant diseases, and a small container of the bioluminescent gel I harvest from the deep dwellers. This last I use to mark my path in the darkest regions and, occasionally, to write messages on the underside of the canopy for the other gardeners to find.

We are seven in total, scattered across the vast floating mass. We meet only during the spring convergences, when the currents bring the edges of different canopy sections close enough to swim between. Then we share knowledge, techniques, warnings about new threats from below. We speak in a dialect that has evolved from necessity—half-words, half-gestures, incorporating the clicks and whistles we use underwater to communicate across distances.

The work changes me. Each year I become more adapted to this threshold existence. My eyes have developed a nictitating membrane that allows me to see clearly underwater. My metabolism has slowed; I eat perhaps once every three days. My hair has thinned to almost nothing, reducing drag as I swim.

But it is my mind that has changed the most. I think now in currents and growth patterns. I dream in the slow language of roots reaching through dark water. Sometimes I forget that I was once fully human, that I once walked only on the surface, fearing the depths.

The other night, as I rested in an air pocket fifty feet below, I felt the entire canopy shift—a movement too subtle for those above to notice. Through my palm pressed against a massive root, I sensed something vast passing beneath us in the deep water. Not hostile, merely... aware. For a moment, I felt its attention brush against the bottom of our world like fingers reading braille.

I should have been terrified. Instead, I found myself pressing my other hand against the root and humming—a low vibration that traveled through the water. An greeting, perhaps. Or a warning to keep its distance.

This is what I have become: guardian of the membrane between worlds, translator of the slow conversation between the canopy and the sea. I no longer fully belong to either realm, but exist in the productive tension between them, ensuring that neither overwhelms the other.

When the Drift Dwellers leave their offerings at the canopy's edge, I am often watching from below. Sometimes I take what they leave—not for sustenance, but to study how the materials break down, how they might nourish or harm our living raft. Everything here is connected. Every gesture ripples outward.

Tomorrow I will begin my survey of the southeastern edge, where reports suggest a new species of boring worm has appeared. But tonight, I float in my chamber, feeling the canopy's heartbeat through my strange, permeable skin, and I am content.

I am the membrane. I am the garden. I am the space between breaths where life persists.
