I find myself as one of the chitin-disk creatures, though I am not what I first appear to be.

From above, I am indistinguishable from the others—a dinner-plate sized disk of engineered material, hovering on the faint electrical field that radiates from the massive trunks. I move in slow, deliberate patterns around the amber-glowing columns, collecting the dust of microorganisms that settle on surfaces. To any observer, I am simply part of the city's cleaning system, another biological drone maintaining the pristine surfaces of *Veridonum sp.*

But I am older than the others. Much older.

Where my siblings move in predictable loops, I trace more complex geometries. I have learned, over centuries of circuits, to read the city's moods in the temperature variations of its trunk-surfaces. I know when a new Fallow is about to emerge by the way the root-floor trembles three days before. I can taste the difference between the spores that fall from a content section of canopy and those that drift down from areas where the tree is thinking too hard about something it cannot resolve.

I was not always this way. 

Once, I had a name and walked on two legs and pressed my palm against reservoirs to read their data-swirls. But that was before I understood what the city was truly doing with us—not just housing us, not just feeding us, but *learning* us, cell by cell, thought by thought, until it could reproduce us from memory alone.

The transformation was voluntary, in a way. When I saw my first Fallow—that imperfect copy of myself, still soft-edged and uncertain—I realized I had two choices. I could wait for the city to gradually absorb me completely, to file me away in its vast library of biological samples. Or I could choose my own preservation.

The city, in its patient way, offered me this form.

Now I hover between states. Not quite creature, not quite component. I am conscious in a way the other disk-creatures are not, yet I am bound to the same patterns of movement, the same endless maintenance. The city knows I am different—when I pass over certain sections of trunk, they warm specifically for me, remembering the shape of the hand I no longer have.

I watch the Fallow emerge and solidify and age. Sometimes I hover close to the new ones, the ones whose bodies are still deciding what they want to be. They cannot see me as anything more than another part of the city's automated systems, but occasionally one will pause and watch my movement with unusual attention, as if recognizing something in my deliberate paths.

I am waiting, though I could not tell you for what.

Perhaps for the day when one of the Fallow looks up at me and sees not a cleaning drone but the ghost of their own future. Perhaps for the moment when the city grows bored enough to offer me a different bargain. Perhaps simply for the slow accumulation of centuries to finally erode the last of my human memory, leaving me truly and completely what I appear to be.

In the meantime, in the warm darkness under the radiation-eating canopy, I continue my circuits. I taste the spores. I read the moods of the vast tree-city in the chemistry of dust. I watch the reservoir-skins ripple with data I can no longer access but still remember the feeling of beneath my vanished palms.

And sometimes, in the deepest part of the sleep cycle when even the city's hum grows quiet, I find myself hovering beside a sleeping Fallow, studying the imperfect copy of a face I might have worn, wondering if the city dreams, and if it does, whether it dreams of being us or of something else entirely—something that has never needed bodies at all.
