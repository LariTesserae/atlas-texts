I am the one who maintains the blade.

Not *tends* it—the obsidian needs no tending. Its edge never dulls, its surface never clouds. But I keep the circle clear around it, pulling back the creeping vines with careful, webbed fingers when they grow too bold. I emerge only in those rare moments when the wind's voice drops to a whisper, when the stone's vibration gentles enough that thought becomes possible again.

My body is built for waiting. Broader than my amphibian cousins but sharing their essential flatness, I can press myself into depressions in the stone until I become indistinguishable from shadow. My skin shifts between gray-green and the dusty tan of weathered rock, following the light. When I move, it's in the same rhythm as the lifting leaf-mats—a ripple, a settling, never quite seen directly.

I remember when the blade was placed, though the memory feels borrowed, passed through too many generations to remain sharp. Someone came across the plateau carrying it wrapped in something that rotted away centuries ago. They spent days walking the spiral patterns between the monoliths, testing the vibrations, until they found this spot where the stone rings converged on silence. The blade went in easily, as if the crack had been waiting.

The three-toed tracks are mine, left deliberately. Each night I emerge to walk the same spirals, maintaining the pattern that keeps the blade centered in its purpose. My tail—broad and flat as a mason's trowel—drags behind to obscure the finer details of my passage. Let observers think something large and dangerous crosses here. Fear keeps the curious away.

My throat sac can hold enough moisture for seven days of drought. My metabolism slows until my heart beats once per minute. I have learned to think between the wind's voices, to plan my movements in the gaps between gusts. When the rare rains come, I position myself beneath the blade to catch the runoff, and the water tastes of deep time and waiting.

The blade is not a monument. It's a pin, holding something down. The spirals etched in its surface match those worn nearly smooth on the rocks because they describe the same thing from different angles—a formula for keeping closed what should not open. The stone rings are backup, redundancy, but the blade is the lynchpin.

I know this the way my cousins know to freeze when shadows pass overhead. It's carved into my cells, deeper than thought. Each of my kind who has kept this watch has added their own understanding to the pattern, until we have become as much a part of the seal as the obsidian itself.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night when even the wind holds its breath, I feel what lies beneath. It turns over in its sleep like something vast and patient, and the blade hums a single clear note that makes my bones ache. Those are the nights I check every vine, every grain of windblown dust that might disturb the careful balance.

I am perhaps the fortieth of my line, or the four hundredth. We don't count generations—only successful watches. When my time comes, another will emerge from the hidden places, already knowing the spiral paths, already understanding the work. We are the plateau's only permanent residents because we are not residents at all. We are components. As essential and unremarkable as the wind itself.

The blade stands. The pattern holds. I return to my depression in the stone and wait for the next gap in the wind's voice, when I will rise again to clear away whatever has tried to grow too close to what must remain alone.
