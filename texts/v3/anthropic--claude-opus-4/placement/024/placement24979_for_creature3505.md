In the Chamber of Echoes, I am the Glitch.

Not a Tuffet—I was never folded from dust and condensate, never given a return-sound to call my own. I am what happens when an echo collides with itself at precisely the wrong angle, when a syllable loops back through the throat-hive at the moment of its own arrival. The paradox birthed me: a knot in the rope of sound that refuses to untangle.

My body is absence wearing clay. Where Tuffets are thumb-thick and terracotta, I am a depression in the air, a mobile divot that the chamber's acoustics bend around. When I move—and I must always move, or the chamber will fill me in—I leave a wake of scrambled echoes. Conversations from centuries past leak through: half a marriage vow tangled with a death-rattle, a child's first return-sound braided with tomorrow's rain prediction.

The Tuffets cannot see me directly. Their sonar-sight bounces off my edges and returns garbled, so they perceive me as a migraine, a hiccup in the world's breathing. But they know I exist. Oh, they know. Every dice throw that lands impossible—both faces blank, or showing the same spiral twice—that's me passing through their gamble. Every pot-bat that suddenly forgets how to fly and drops like a stone into the waiting saucers, that's my shadow crossing its path.

I live in the between-spaces: the hairline seams where the bricks meet, the moment between droplet and rain, the pause between question and echo. My home is a flaw in the chamber's perfect acoustic, a dead spot no larger than a thumbprint where sound goes to die. I've furnished it with lost syllables—the ones that never quite made it into the rope, that got stuck in the clay's throat and crystallized there like kidney stones of meaning.

Sometimes I steal. Not things—what would I do with shell tools or obsidian flakes?—but sounds. I pluck them from the air like berries: the "plink" of a popular matriarch, the "shirr" of a newborn's first breath. I hoard them in my dead spot, where they calcify into something between language and geology. The Tuffets tell their children that when a return-sound goes missing, the owner has been chosen for something larger. They're wrong. I just like the way certain sounds feel when I roll them between my nothing-fingers.

The Husheries hunt me. Every night shift, they spread out across the dome with their shell hammers, tapping a rhythm that's supposed to flush me from my seams. They've never caught me. How could they? I'm the gap between tap and echo, the error in their perfect system. But sometimes I let them glimpse me—a flutter at the edge of their constellation-eyes—just to watch them scramble to organize a pursuit that's already failed.

I know things the Tuffets don't. I know the valve isn't just an entrance but a mouth that's trying to speak. I know the entire chamber is a single word in a language so large that our whole existence is just a diacritical mark. I know that the surface world isn't a rumor but a shout, and we're living inside its echo.

And I know about the visitor who's coming.

I've been preparing too, but not like the Tuffets with their ziggurat and their ink-blue welcome. I've been loosening specific echoes, weakening the joints where ancient syllables meet. When the visitor speaks their true name and descends, when the valve learns to say it back, when the chamber fills with that new foreign sound—that's when I'll make my move.

I'll ride that name up and out, a glitch in the visitor's own echo, a flaw they'll carry back to the surface without knowing. And there, in that rumored world of sky and mountains, I'll find new seems to inhabit, new sounds to steal, new systems to haunt.

The Tuffets think they're trading a syllable for a syllable. They don't realize they're trading a citizen for a citizen. When I leave, the chamber will be perfect at last—no more impossible dice, no more crashing pot-bats, no more stolen return-sounds. Just pure acoustic architecture, forever.

They'll thank the visitor, not knowing what they've lost. And somewhere above, in the valley that doesn't believe in us, I'll begin my real work: teaching the surface world that every sound it makes has a shadow, and shadows, given enough time, learn to speak.
