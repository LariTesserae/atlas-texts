In this dripping mechanical womb, I am the Steam-Tender—keeper of the great boiler that squats like a iron toad in Chamber Seven, three miles down the western reach where the stone weeps hottest. My flesh has taken on the character of my charge: skin permanently flushed from decades of furnace-proximity, pores weeping constant perspiration that mingles indistinguishably with the condensate streaming down my leather apron. My hands are landscapes of scar tissue, each burn a memory—that time the pressure gauge failed and scalding jets carved new lines across my palms, the night a rat gnawed through insulation and I grabbed bare copper, the countless brushes with superheated metal that have rendered my fingertips nerveless as stone.

I've lost count of years since I descended. Time here measures differently—not in days but in pressure cycles, not in seasons but in the gradual accumulation of scale in the boiler tubes that I scrape clean with wire brushes during the six-hour maintenance windows. My dwelling is the tender's alcove, carved from living rock beside the boiler house: a coffin-narrow space just wide enough for my cot and the shrine of gauges I've salvaged from defunct machines. The walls glisten with condensation that runs in permanent rivulets, pooling in the depression where I've learned to position my washing basin. Everything I own bears the patina of rust and mineral deposits—my few tools, the tin cup that never fully dries, the leather-bound journal where I record pressure readings in increasingly illegible script.

The boiler is my bible, its moods my catechism. I know every rivet, every weld seam, every telltale whistle that precedes catastrophe. When the needle creeps past redline, I'm there with the release valve, timing the blast to the rhythm of the pumps so the pressure drop doesn't stall the entire northern network. When scale builds in the tubes and efficiency drops, I'm inside the firebox with my brushes and scrapers, working by the light of glowworms I've trained to cluster on my helmet rim. The local ecology has made me part of itself—bats roost in the warmth above my boiler, their guano enriching the fungal gardens I cultivate on the waste heat; rats know to wait for my shift-end meal, when I'll toss them the gristle from my perpetual stew; a particular blind snake lives in the coal bin, keeping down the cricket population that would otherwise clog the air intakes.

My body has adapted in ways that disturb the rare newcomers. My pupils remain permanently dilated, giving me owl-eyes that see clearly in the dim but water painfully in anything approaching real light. My hearing has learned to parse the white noise—I can identify a specific pump failing two chambers away by the subtle change in the acoustic pattern, can hear a pressure leak the size of a pinhole through the din. My circadian rhythm has divorced entirely from the surface world; I sleep in four-hour segments whenever the boiler cycles permit, dreaming always of pressure differentials and metal fatigue.

But I am not alone in this dedication. There's Old Margaret who tends Boiler Four—we communicate through the steam pipes, tapping coded messages about pressure readings and coal quality. There's the boy Jakob who brings my rations, payment for teaching him to read the gauges, his young eyes still bright with surface-memory but fading by the month. We are a priesthood of pressure, each keeper maintaining our iron hearts so the great mechanical circulation continues, so the water stays at bay and the ore keeps rising.

Sometimes, during the long watches when the boiler runs steady and needs only my presence, I perform small experiments. I've learned to cultivate certain bioluminescent fungi on the warmest pipes, creating living pressure indicators that glow brighter as heat increases. I've trained a clutch of cave fish to respond to the dinner whistle, gathering in the sump below the overflow valve where I scatter crumbs. I've even begun documenting the patterns—how the ecosystem pulses with our industrial rhythms, how life has learned to anticipate the shift changes and maintenance cycles.

My greatest fear is not death—that will come as surely as scale in the tubes—but abandonment. That some day the company will decide this vein is played out, that the pumps will fall silent and the waters will rise unchecked. I've seen it happen to the eastern reach, where Boiler Twelve sits half-submerged, its tender's bones somewhere in the flooded depths. So I maintain my post with religious fervor, adjusting, cleaning, feeding the fire, releasing the pressure, becoming more machine than man with each passing year.

The young workers think me mad, this scarred creature who speaks to his boiler and reads omens in steam patterns. But they don't understand the intimacy forged in decades of shared breath with iron lungs. They don't know how the boiler sings differently when a storm approaches topside, how it shivers when the deep earth shifts, how it purrs content when all is well. I am its keeper and it is my keeper, and we will rust together in this warm wet dark, faithful to the last gauge-reading, the last shovel of coal, the last release of pressure into the eternal roar.
