In the copper-filtered twilight, I am the Keeper of Thresholds.

My dwelling is neither fully above nor below the dust, but carved into the compacted pan where the surface cracks into its ancient mosaic. The entrance is barely visible—a darker shadow among shadows, marked only by three smooth stones arranged in a triangle that those who know can recognize. Inside, the walls curve inward, following natural fissures I've widened over decades with patient scraping. The space breathes with me, expanding and contracting with the subtle shifts of the ground.

I am taller than most of my kind, though still wiry, with hands that have grown unusually sensitive from my work. My fingertips can read the dust like others read faces—feeling the weight of minerals, the age of compression, the memory of moisture that passed through centuries ago. My left eye has developed a peculiar quality: in certain light, it appears to have rings within the amber, like the growth patterns in ancient wood. Some say it comes from staring too long into the depths of the dust pans, searching for what lies between the grains.

My role emerged from necessity and evolved into something more. I am the one who reads the boundaries—between the stable ground and the shifting dunes, between the living root networks and the barren stretches, between what was and what might be. When the families need to move their dwellings, they come to me. I walk the land with them, my bare feet testing each step, my strange eye catching the subtle gradients of color that reveal where the dust runs deep and where bedrock lurks below.

But I do more than guide physical journeys. In my carved hollow, I keep a collection unlike any other: samples of dust from every pan and dune for miles around, stored in small depressions worn into the walls. Each sample tells a story—this russet powder from where the last spring emerged fifty sun-crawls ago, this umber grit from where the ancient markers cluster most densely, this almost-black sand from the deep dune where no plants grow but where, sometimes, the silence breaks with a low harmonic hum.

The families bring their young to me when they begin to walk, and I teach them to read the ground with their feet. I show them how to recognize the firm patches that will support a dwelling, the loose areas where roots can penetrate, the dangerous zones where the dust can swallow a careless step. More importantly, I teach them to feel the connections—how a disturbance here creates a shift there, how the root networks communicate through chemical whispers in the soil, how the placement of a single stone can change the flow of dust across a whole hillside.

I rarely speak, even by our taciturn standards. Instead, I demonstrate with gestures that mirror the landscape itself—a sweep of the hand like wind-carved ripples, a gentle patting motion like dust settling, fingers spreading like roots beneath the surface. When I must use words, they come out dust-dry and laden with multiple meanings, like the layers of sediment I study.

My most sacred duty comes during the ceremonies of transition. When one of us prepares to return to the dust, I determine where they should be placed. Not buried—we don't disturb the earth so deeply—but laid in specific locations where the slow forces of erosion will gradually incorporate them into the landscape. I know where the dust flows fastest, where it barely moves, where it spirals in near-invisible eddies. Each person requires a different threshold, a different speed of transformation.

In the perpetual twilight, I often stand at the edge of my dwelling, feeling the minute vibrations through my feet. The others think I'm listening to the silence, but I know better. I'm reading the conversation between the dust and the roots, the rocks and the air, the past and the present. In this dialogue, I find the thresholds—those spaces where one thing becomes another, where boundaries blur like footprints in the shifting dust.

My collection grows. My eye develops new rings. My feet grow more sensitive. And in the vast, patient silence of our world, I continue my work as the one who stands between, who guides the crossing, who reads the fine print written in rust and umber across the endless, enduring land.
