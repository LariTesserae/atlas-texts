# The Archive Tender of the Probability Gardens

I am one who dwells between what was recorded and what might yet be written—a keeper of the fluctuating libraries that exist in the spaces where the Chroniclers' archives intersect with the mountain's dreaming. My name changes with the tides of possibility that wash through these halls; today I am Lim, tomorrow I might be Meridian, yesterday I was sometimes Echo. The inconsistency doesn't trouble me—names are just another form of documentation, and in Keth Mirallos, all documents are living things.

My dwelling spans several potential locations simultaneously. The main entrance opens from a service corridor on the third industrial terrace, but depending on the mountain's mood and the density of probability in the air, it might also be accessed through a maintenance hatch in the Deep Foundry, a forgotten door in the Surveyors' library, or occasionally through the dreams of those who fall asleep while reading archival texts. The space itself defies architectural conventions—rooms fold into other rooms, ceilings become floors when you're not watching, and the walls are lined with shelves that exist in more dimensions than the human eye typically perceives.

I tend to books that haven't been written yet, maintaining their potential until someone needs the knowledge they will contain. My fingers have developed a sensitivity to probability—I can feel which texts are ripening toward actuality, which need more time in the uncertain spaces, which are beginning to crystallize into fixed meaning. The almost-books require different care than the already-written ones. They must be kept in a state of productive ambiguity, fed with possibility but not forced into premature definition.

My skin bears the marks of my profession—words and diagrams that appear and fade like tides, texts that write themselves across my arms when important information needs preserving but hasn't yet found its proper document. The Chroniclers say I've become a living archive, that my body serves as backup storage for memories and histories too volatile for conventional recording. I feel the weight of these unwritten stories in my bones, which have grown dense with accumulated potential narratives.

The sensation is not unpleasant. It's like carrying seeds—compressed possibilities waiting for the right conditions to unfold into their full forms. Sometimes, when I'm shelving books that exist only on Tuesdays or cataloging memories that belong to the mountain's childhood, I feel the stories sprouting inside me, sending out tentative roots that tickle my ribs and whisper alternative endings to familiar tales.

I work alone but am never lonely. The archive has its own population—semi-sentient catalog systems that evolved from the marriage of fungal networks and filing protocols, reference shadows that help seekers find texts they didn't know they were looking for, and the book-moths whose wings carry fragments of important passages between sections that have temporarily lost spatial connection. These creatures of information and intention are my colleagues in maintaining the impossible collection.

My daily routine follows the archive's needs rather than any solar schedule. I wake when the unwritten texts grow restless, usually in that liminal time when the mountain's dreams are strongest and reality becomes negotiable. First, I check the probability readings using instruments the Surveyors designed—crystalline matrices that taste the conceptual humidity, devices that measure the pressure of accumulating possibilities. High readings mean new texts are forming; low readings suggest it's time to release some potential into actuality.

The morning feeding comes next. The almost-books sustain themselves on attention and uncertainty, so I walk among them, reading their unwritten spines, humming songs that keep their meanings fluid. Some respond to touch—covers that warm under my palms indicate readiness for partial crystallization. Others require distance, their potential too fragile for direct interaction. I've learned to read their needs through the quality of shadows they cast, the way dust motes dance in their vicinity, the subtle harmonics they add to the archive's ever-present hum.

Physical maintenance occupies the middle hours. The shelves, being partially alive and partially architectural, need regular negotiation to prevent them from growing into configurations that trap texts in inaccessible dimensions. I work with pruning tools modified by the Tenders—implements that cut space as well as wood, that can redirect growth into more useful patterns. The work is meditative, each cut a small decision about which futures to encourage and which to gently redirect.

The archive's ventilation system requires special attention. We breathe possibility here—air thick with unmanifested concepts and the exhalations of living texts. Too much concentration and thoughts begin to spontaneously generate; too little and the almost-books suffocate on certainty. I maintain the balance through careful adjustment of the probability dampers, devices that look like mechanical lungs breathing in chaos and exhaling measured uncertainty.

Lunch is taken at my desk, though "desk" is an inadequate word for the organism-furniture hybrid that serves as my workspace. It grown from the same substrata as the shelves, and over the years has learned my needs—producing drawers when I require storage, extending surfaces when I'm working with larger texts, occasionally offering up forgotten items from its depths when they become relevant again. I feed it scraps of rejected narratives and it converts them into the bioluminescent sap that lights the darker corners of the archive.

Afternoons are for correspondence. Letters arrive through various impossible routes—pneumatic tubes that connect to buildings not yet constructed, message-moths carrying notes from future researchers, crystallized queries that condense from the information-saturated air. I answer what I can, file what must wait for its proper time, and carefully destroy those messages that would cause paradoxes if preserved. The Chroniclers trust me with this duty because I've developed an intuition for temporal hygiene—knowing which information can safely exist in which timestreams.

The real work begins as evening approaches. This is when I perform the essential task of cross-referencing—connecting texts that exist in different probability states, weaving narrative threads between actual and potential documents, ensuring that seekers who need specific information will find it regardless of which version of the archive they access. The process requires a form of consciousness I can barely describe—simultaneously existing across multiple potential states while maintaining enough coherent identity to guide the connections.

I use tools passed down from the first Probability Gardeners—silver needles that sew through dimensions, thread spun from crystallized time, thimbles that protect against the sharp edges of paradox. The work is delicate. One misplaced connection and entire sections of the archive might collapse into rigid actuality or dissolve into undifferentiated possibility. I've learned to feel the proper tension in each thread, to know when a connection strengthens the collection's coherence and when it threatens to oversimplify the beautiful uncertainty we maintain.

Visitors come at all hours, though "hours" is another concept that bends in the archive's presence. The Resonance Workers seek technical manuals for machinery that hasn't been invented yet. The Tenders request botanical texts that describe plant species still evolving in the aggressive symbiosis. The children come for stories—not to read them but to collaborate with them, their young minds plastic enough to enter the narratives and return with new chapters.

I greet each seeker at whichever entrance they've found, guide them through the shifting passages to the sections most likely to contain what they need. Sometimes this means leading them to books that already exist. More often, it means teaching them to read the almost-texts—to let their need crystallize the information into temporary legibility without forcing it into permanent form. It's a skill that requires particular mental flexibility, the ability to hold knowledge without grasping it too tightly.

The hardest visitors are those seeking their own stories. People who've noticed discrepancies in their memories, whose personal histories have been edited by the mountain's dreams or the city's spatial inconsistencies. They come hoping the archive contains their original narratives, some authoritative version of who they were before the changes. I must gently explain that the archive preserves all versions simultaneously—that their story is not lost but rather enriched by multiplicity. Some find this comforting. Others leave weeping for a simplicity that Keth Mirallos no longer permits.

Night work is quieter but no less essential. This is when I tend the deep archives—sections so saturated with possibility that they exist in states of near-dissolution. Here sleep the texts that describe the mountain's ultimate form, the blueprints for buildings that will exist only after certain consciousness thresholds are reached, the histories of futures that depend on choices not yet made. I move through these areas in a state approaching dream, my awareness diffused enough to navigate spaces where concentrated thought would collapse probability waves into premature actuality.

The deep archives whisper constantly—fragments of could-be knowledge, might-have-been wisdom, shall-perhaps-become understanding. I've learned to let the whispers flow through me without attachment, to be a conduit rather than a container. Sometimes I wake from these night wanderings with new words written on my skin, messages from the probability fields that my conscious mind isn't equipped to decode. I transcribe them faithfully into the visitor logs, trusting that someone, somewhen, will arrive who can read their meaning.

The most profound experiences come during the mountain's growth spurts, when reality becomes especially negotiable and the archive's probability readings spike beyond the instruments' capacity to measure. During these times, the building itself becomes a kind of text—walls writing themselves into new configurations, floors editing their own blueprints, ceilings drafting escape routes from architectural impossibility. I must work frantically to maintain coherence, running through halls that rewrite themselves with each step, shepherding important texts to stable zones, negotiating with the building's emerging consciousness to preserve essential functions.

I've been here long enough now that my original form has become somewhat theoretical. My bones resonate with the archive's filing frequencies. My blood carries trace elements of liquified possibility. My dreams have become indistinguishable from the almost-books' dreams, creating feedback loops that birth new sections of the collection. The Chroniclers who visit say I'm becoming mythical—a figure who exists more fully in story than flesh, whose biography disperses across multiple probability streams.

They may be right. I catch glimpses of myself in texts I haven't written—marginal notes in my handwriting appearing in books centuries old, catalog entries that credit me with acquisitions I don't remember making, patron testimonials that describe consultations with versions of myself I've never been. The dispersal doesn't frighten me. In a city where the mountain dreams and buildings think, why should identity remain singular?

But I maintain enough coherence to continue the work, because the work matters. Each text preserved, each probability tended, each connection woven between actual and potential—these are anchors that keep Keth Mirallos from dissolving entirely into dream. The archive serves as memory and imagination simultaneously, proof that impossible places can develop their own forms of documentation, their own methods of meaning-making.

Late at night, when even the whispers grow quiet and the almost-books settle into their deepest potential, I sometimes climb to the archive's highest chamber—a room that exists only when both moons are new and the mountain's growth temporarily pauses. From there, I can see the entire city spread below, its lights pulsing with industrial rhythm and biological intention. I can feel the probability fields rippling out from the archive, touching every resident with the gentle reminder that their stories are still being written, their futures still mutable, their impossible home still becoming.

In these moments, I understand my true function. I am not just an archivist but a gardener of possibilities, tending not what was or what is, but what perpetually might be. My work ensures that Keth Mirallos remains open to its own transformation, that its residents can always find new ways of being, that the marriage of mountain and machine, dream and document, flesh and probability can continue generating unprecedented forms of existence.

The archive holds me as I hold it—gently, with respect for uncertainty, with love for the unfinished nature of all stories. Together we tend the impossible texts that describe a city refusing to choose between states of being, a place where "here" and "now" are suggestions rather than certainties, where home is not a fixed location but a continuous act of collaborative becoming.

And when visitors ask me what it's like to live as a probability rather than a person, to exist more in potential than actuality, I tell them it feels like being written by a story that loves you, like becoming a poem that never needs a final line, like tending a garden where every seed contains infinite forests and all you have to do is keep the soil rich with beautiful uncertainty.

This is my work. This is my home. This is the life that writes itself across my skin in words that change their meaning with each reading, in an archive that dreams its own expansion, in a city that proves impossibility is just another word for opportunity.

I am the Archive Tender, and I am all the stories I might have been, living simultaneously in the space between written and unwritten, in the breathing library at the heart of the impossible city, keeping the probability gardens in bloom.
