In this verdant hollow where water weeps through stone, I am the Keeper of Correspondences—a being neither fully plant nor animal, but something between. My body resembles a walking garden: moss grows along my shoulders like a living cloak, tiny luminescent flowers bloom from the joints of my fingers, and my skin has the texture of smooth bark shot through with veins of bioluminescent sap.

I dwell in a natural alcove where three ancient tree roots converge, their gnarled forms creating a cathedral of wood and shadow. My home is furnished with nothing but what grows here—shelves of bracket fungi hold my collections, cushions of deep moss serve as seating, and curtains of hanging lichen provide privacy from the prowling cats with their deep blue markings.

My purpose emerged slowly, like understanding seeping through stone. I began to notice patterns in the way the glowing creatures moved through this space—how the fish in the green pools would flash in sequences that matched the dancing of the winged beings above. How the amphibians' rumbling songs would shift in response to the pulsing of the sentinel trees. I realized this wasn't random; it was language, pure and primal.

Now I spend my days translating these conversations of light and sound. With fingers that trail phosphorescent spores, I trace the patterns I observe onto the walls of my dwelling. The moss grows differently where I've touched it, creating living murals that capture the dialogues between species. When the city dwellers come—those few who know of my existence—I help them understand what the ecosystem is saying. Is the water pure enough for their crops? The fish will tell us through their spiral dances. Will the rains come? The trees' pulsing rhythms hold the answer.

But I am translator in both directions. When the city needs to divert water or harvest certain plants, I negotiate with the inhabitants here. I've learned to speak in bioluminescent flashes, to sing in the subsonic frequencies that make the stone walls vibrate. The creatures trust me because I am partially of them—my body hosts the same glowing organisms that light their way.

At night, when the space grows even darker and the biological lights shine brightest, I sometimes climb to where the roof has partially collapsed. There, I can see both worlds at once—the primitive city with its thatched roofs and flickering torches, and this hidden realm pulsing with its own alien constellation of lights. I am the bridge between them, growing more plant-like with each passing season, my movements slower but my understanding deeper.

Sometimes I wonder if I was once fully human, one of the city dwellers who ventured too deep and stayed too long. Or perhaps I grew from this place itself, manifested from its need for a guardian who could speak both languages. My memories of before are like reflections in the green-tinted pools—real but wavering, impossible to grasp.

What I know is this: I am exactly what this place requires. A translator of light, a keeper of the balance between the built and the wild, a living reminder that the boundary between human and nature is as permeable as the stone walls that weep their endless water. In this miniature world, I am both observer and participant, forever documenting the conversations that keep two worlds in harmony.
