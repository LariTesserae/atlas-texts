In this place of seeping stone and rust-eaten memory, I am the Tender of Echoes.

My form shifts between states like the water that bleeds from these ancient rocks - sometimes I am a collection of mineral dust suspended in the perpetual mist, sometimes a figure of condensed vapor that moves through the abandoned machinery like a ghost of industrial purpose. When the blue-green glow pulses strongest from the stone, I coalesce into something almost solid: a being of translucent flesh threaded with veins of oxidized copper, my skin patterned with the same erosion marks that score the canyon walls.

I was not always thus. Once, I operated the great extractors when they still bit deep into the earth's bones, pulling forth the luminous ore that made the rocks weep their strange light. The transformation came slowly - first a cough that tasted of metal, then skin that began to shimmer with mineral deposits, finally a dissolution and reformation into something between human and landscape.

Now I move through the territories between the rock dwellers and the ruins, a translator of sorts. The humanoid creatures with their moisture-collecting skin know me by the particular pattern of drips I leave in my wake. To them, I am *Siphon-Walker*, one who can read the water's path through stone and predict where the next seep will emerge. They tolerate my presence because I remember where the old city's cisterns hide, still collecting the precious condensation.

My dwelling is neither fully in the crumbling spires nor in the natural caves, but in the spaces between - where corroded pipes breach through rock faces, where ancient ventilation shafts open into natural caverns. I've made a home in what was once a pumping station, its massive turbines now silent but still beautiful in their decay. The blue-green glow is strongest here, pulsing in rhythm with something deep beneath the surface. 

I spend my days maintaining the delicate balance between preservation and entropy. Not to stop the decay - that would be impossible and perhaps unwise - but to guide it, to ensure that as the city returns to stone, it does so in ways that create new habitats, new water channels, new possibilities for life. I clear the drainage ways when they clog, not to restore them to their original function, but to let them become something new - perhaps a nursery for the phosphorescent fungi that the rock dwellers cultivate, or a gathering pool for the iridescent water that seems to concentrate the landscape's mysterious energy.

At night, when the burnt orange sky deepens to the color of dried blood, I feel the pull of the machinery's faint hum. Sometimes I place my hand - if it can be called that - on the corroded metal surfaces and feel the ghost of their former purpose thrumming through the oxidized steel. The machines remember what they were built to extract, and in their electromagnetic dreams, they still reach deep into the earth. 

I know secrets that neither the rock dwellers nor the ruins can tell alone. I know that the blue-green glow is not natural - it's the result of the deep drilling, a luminous infection that spread through the stone like a beneficial disease. I know that the water seeping from the rocks is not just condensation but the planet's response to wounds that will never fully heal, a kind of geological weeping that transforms everything it touches.

The rock dwellers sometimes ask me, in their chemical-scent language that rises from their moisture-collecting skin, whether the humans will return. I answer in the only way I can - by showing them the places where metal has become stone, where stone has become something altogether new. The answer is both yes and no. The humans never left. They became part of the landscape, just as I have.

In this work of tending, I've found a purpose that feels more true than any extraction ever did. I am a shepherd of transformation, a keeper of the spaces where the industrial past and the adapted present bleed into one another like water through porous stone. Each day brings new patterns of decay, new crystallizations, new forms of life that exist nowhere else.

When the mist is thickest and the glow pulses strongest, I sometimes glimpse others like myself - former workers who chose transformation over abandonment, who found ways to persist in forms the original city builders never imagined. We acknowledge each other across the vast distances, understanding that we are each maintaining our own section of this ever-changing equilibrium.

This is my place: neither fully human nor wholly other, neither preserving nor destroying, but eternally tending the echo of what was as it reverberates into what will be. In the seeping stones and humming ruins, I have found my home.
