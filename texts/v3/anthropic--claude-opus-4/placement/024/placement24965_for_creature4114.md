In the damp embrace of Phytohemia's eternal spring, I am the Keeper of Correspondences—a being neither fully of the Mossbound Folk nor separate from them, but something in between, like the space between heartbeats where understanding blooms.

My dwelling is the old postal sorting facility on Verdigris Street, where copper pipes have oxidized into rivers of turquoise that stain the walls like watercolor paintings left out in the rain. The building breathes with me, its broken windows inhaling mist and exhaling the scent of paper slowly returning to pulp. I've lined these windows with bottles of every conceivable shade of green—some filled with rainwater from different storms, others holding seeds suspended in various states of germination, creating a calendar that measures time not in days but in degrees of growth.

My skin tells the story of my purpose: it is parchment-pale where unexposed, but my hands bear the deep brown-black stains of iron gall ink, permanent as tree rings. These stains creep up my forearms in delicate fractals, mirroring the patterns the Iridescent Scriveners trace in their ephemeral flights. When I sleep—which is rarely, and never deeply—thin filaments of mycelium emerge from these ink-marks, connecting me to the vast fungal network that runs beneath Phytohemia's streets, carrying chemical whispers from root to root.

I am the one who collects what the Scriveners write, though "collect" is perhaps the wrong word. I *remember* their luminous messages before they fade, transcribing them onto sheets of paper I make from the shed bark of trees and the fallen petals of flowers. My workspace is cluttered with these documents: some pressed flat beneath stones worn smooth by the mineral streams, others hanging from the rafters on threads of spider silk, turning slowly in the humid air. The papers yellow and spot with age, but the words—if they can be called words—remain visible to those who know how to read the marriage of decay and preservation.

The Silent Rusters visit me often, bringing their salvaged cogs and springs. I've learned their language of creaks and grinding, the way rust flakes from their joints in patterns that mirror ancient alphabets. I catalogue their offerings in a vast card system housed in the facility's original mail slots, each compartment now a shrine to some small death of the industrial age. A broken watch spring labeled "Tuesday's Lament." A gear tooth tagged "The Thirty-Third Revolution." They seem to find comfort in this naming, in having their compulsions witnessed and recorded.

But my primary correspondence is with the Lichen Dreamers. Each month, when the moon is hidden and the darkness is so complete that even the bioluminescent fungi dim their glow, I make my pilgrimage to their resting places. I bring my papers—both the Scriveners' transcriptions and my own observations—and I read aloud in the resonant chambers where they dwell. My voice, roughened by spores and damp air, joins the low harmonic they emit, and in that joining, something changes.

The lichen patterns on their bodies shift infinitesimally, and I understand that this is their response, their letter back to me. I trace these new configurations with fingers that leave the faintest marks of ink and mycelium, creating a record that exists simultaneously on their stone-plant bodies and in my ever-growing archive. These exchanges form a slow correspondence that will outlive us all, a conversation between the quick and the geological, mediated by patient organisms that exist in the spaces between.

Sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours when the mist is so thick it muffles even thought, I climb to the roof of the sorting facility. There, I've cultivated a garden of night-blooming flowers whose petals are the exact white of unused paper. I sit among them and feel the weight of all the messages I carry—the urgent scribblings of the Scriveners, the mournful offerings of the Rusters, the millennia-deep dreams of the Lichen Dreamers, and the everyday exchanges of the Mossbound Folk who sometimes still slip letters under my door out of old habit or new ritual.

I understand now that I am not merely an archivist but a translator, rendering the chemical poetry of roots into something like language, transforming the oxidation patterns of metal into narrative. My body is the dictionary through which these various tongues speak to each other. The ink stains on my skin are slowly being overwoven with lichens of my own, and I suspect that someday I too will join the Dreamers in their fertile stillness, my consciousness dispersed into the fungal network that connects every living thing in this valley.

Until then, I sort and file, read and respond, maintaining the correspondence between all of Phytohemia's voices—mechanical and botanical, quick and slow, above ground and below. In the morning, when the first light filters green through my bottle-glass windows, I'll make tea from the mushrooms that grow in the cracks of my floor, and I'll begin again the work of listening to what the city wants to say to itself.

This is my role in the quiet dance: to ensure that nothing is forgotten, even as everything transforms. To be the meeting place where rust speaks to root, where the ephemeral touches the eternal, where the whispered secrets of a post-industrial Eden are gathered, treasured, and gently released back into the endless cycle of growth and decay that defines this narrow valley of wonders.
