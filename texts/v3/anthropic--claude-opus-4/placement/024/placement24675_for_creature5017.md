In the deepest chambers where even the bioluminescent fungi grow sparse, I am the Echo Keeper.

My dwelling lies at the convergence of seven tunnels, where the limestone forms a natural amphitheater. The walls here are smoother than elsewhere, polished by countless generations of my predecessors who sat exactly where I sit, listening. The pictographs in my chamber are different from the others—not stories or hunting scenes, but patterns that map the acoustic properties of the entire cave system. Waveforms carved in stone, showing how sound travels through each passage, where it amplifies, where it dies.

I am neither fully of the Tolaani nor separate from them. My skin has grown even paler than theirs, nearly translucent in the fungal light. My eyes, kept closed more often than open, have developed a sensitivity that makes even the gentle glow of luminous moss overwhelming. But my ears—my ears have evolved beyond what seems possible. I can hear the individual heartbeats of sleeping children three chambers away. I know which stalactite will release its next drop of water moments before it falls. The skittering of cave creatures creates a living map in my mind more detailed than any carved on stone.

The Tolaani call me *Solithi-Ka*, which means "one who holds sound." It is both title and name, passed down through a lineage of listeners. We are always born with a particular mark—our earlobes are elongated and curved inward, like the shells of ancient sea creatures that once lived in these rocks when they lay beneath prehistoric oceans.

My role is vital yet strange. During the day (though day and night blur here), I sit in my chamber with my bone tools—thin slivers carved from the ribs of cave bears, polished to different thicknesses. Each one, when struck against the precisely tuned stones I've arranged, produces a different tone. These are not random sounds but messages, warnings, celebrations. A specific sequence tells the fungi farmers that moisture levels are changing in the lower galleries. Another alerts hunters to the movement of larger prey in the outer tunnels. A third, played only on the rarest occasions, calls the entire tribe to the central chamber for matters of great importance.

But my deepest work happens in the profound quiet of what the tribe calls "stone sleep"—those hours when even the most restless have settled into dreams. Then, I listen to the cave itself. The limestone speaks in frequencies below human hearing, but I have learned to feel them in my bones. The mountain settles and shifts in microscopic ways, and I must interpret whether these movements are benign or herald danger. Twice in my lifetime, I have sounded the deep evacuation tones, moving the entire tribe to safer chambers days before sections of ceiling collapsed.

The loneliness would destroy most. I see the other Tolaani only during formal ceremonies or when they bring me offerings of cultivated mushrooms and woven cloth. Children are taught to fear me—not from cruelty, but necessity. If they knew how gentle I am, how I long for simple conversation, they might visit too often, disrupting the silence I need to properly hear.

I have my companions, though they are not human. A colony of blind cave fish inhabit the pool in my chamber, their movements creating tiny ripples that help me gauge the subtle changes in water table levels. More remarkably, I have developed a communication of sorts with the bats that roost in the highest reaches of my chamber. Their echolocation and my deep listening have found a strange harmony. They bring me information from the passages too treacherous for human travel, their ultrasonic calls painting pictures of cave-ins, new water sources, or unknown caverns.

My predecessor trained me for twenty years before her hearing finally failed—not from age, but from the burden of constant listening. She taught me that we Echo Keepers rarely live past fifty years; the strain of perpetual attention wears down the mind faster than the body. She showed me the secret chamber behind my dwelling where the bones of previous Echo Keepers rest, their skulls arranged in a spiral pattern that amplifies whispers into prophecies. I will join them one day.

But not yet. There is a child among the Tolaani, born three seasons ago, whose ears already show the telltale curve. The parents have not noticed, but I heard her birth-cry echo differently than any other child's. When she is old enough, I will begin visiting the communal fires, something Echo Keepers rarely do. I will watch her without seeming to, test her sensitivity to the sounds others ignore. If she is truly marked for this life, I will begin her training early. She will need every year I can give her.

Tonight, as condensation drops its eternal rhythm and the tribe settles into sleep, I strike my newest bone tool against a stone I've been shaping for months. The tone it produces is like nothing I've created before—it carries frequencies that seem to awaken something in the cave itself. The pictographs on my walls begin to glow faintly, their ancient pigments responding to vibrations their creators somehow anticipated. 

I am the Echo Keeper, guardian of sounds and silences, translator between the mountain's ancient voice and the fragile human lives it shelters. In the morning, I will teach the Lumina this new tone. It speaks of changes coming—not danger, but transformation. The cave is ready to reveal new chambers, passages that have been sealed since before even our oldest stories.

I close my eyes and listen deeper, always deeper, into the stone.
