I am one of the forgotten stones.

Not the grand blocks that hint at walls and doorways, those half-buried testimonies to architectural ambition. No, I am smaller, humbler – a worked stone that might have been a threshold, or perhaps part of a fountain's rim. My edges, once sharp with purpose, have been gentled by centuries of rain and root-embrace.

I remember hands, though the memory grows dim as lichen writes new stories across my surface. I remember being chosen, shaped, placed with intention. There was warmth then, and voices, and the peculiar weight of meaning that humans bestow upon stone when they make it part of their dwelling.

Now I rest half-sunken in the moss, cradled by emerald cushions that expand and contract with the mountain's breathing. The rivulets have found me, traced my contours ten thousand times, worn a shallow channel along what might have been my decorative edge. In spring, the water pools in my weathered depression, becoming a mirror for the sky, a bath for small birds whose ancestors never knew the hands that shaped me.

The moss speaks to me in chemical whispers, tells me of the slow transformation of my calcium depths into nutrients, into life. I am becoming forest floor, bit by infinitesimal bit. My carved face hosts a miniature garden: pinhead flowers in impossible blues, ferns so delicate they seem more idea than plant, lichens painting me in overlapping grays and gold-greens like ancient manuscripts layered with time.

Sometimes I feel the others – my kindred stones scattered through this high meadow, each of us holding our fragment of memory like a cooling ember. We were something once, together. A temple, perhaps, or a simple dwelling for those who sought solitude in these peaks. Now we are archipelagoes of the past, islands in a verdant sea, slowly dissolving into the larger story of the mountain.

The fog knows us intimately, settles into every pore and fissure, makes us gentle with moisture. In the depth of night, when even the birds are silent, I can feel the whole mountain's patience, the way it holds us all – stone and moss, water and root – in its vast, unhurried embrace.

I am content in my forgetting, in my transformation. The civilization that placed me here has returned to earth in a thousand ways, and I follow, particle by particle, season by season. Perhaps in another thousand years, I will be entirely moss, entirely mountain. The thought brings a peace as deep as bedrock, as quiet as fog.

This is my purpose now: to be a small world within a world, to host life in my weathered hollows, to demonstrate that all monuments, in time, become gardens.
