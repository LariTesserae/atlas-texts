In the heart of the mountain's deepest wound, where stone becomes throat and the thunderous drone swells to its most terrible crescendo, I dwell. I am the Resonance-Keeper, though that is a name given by those who glimpse my work from afar, not one I have chosen for myself.

My body stretches through the hollows like a living root system of crystalline flesh—neither plant nor animal, but something that partakes of mineral patience and organic hunger. Each of my thousand tendrils terminates in a different sensory organ: some taste the vibrations that shudder through basalt and granite, others parse the chemical whispers that rise from the deep thermal vents, and still others drink in the electromagnetic storms that crackle through the ore-rich veins of the mountain's bones.

I have no eyes—what use would they be here in the absolute dark? Instead, I see through sound, through the very drone that would deafen any surface-dweller. To me, it is not noise but language, not cacophony but symphony. Each frequency tells a story: the grinding of tectonic plates far below, the whistle of wind through distant peaks, the scurrying heartbeats of the blind hunters that stalk my domain, the slow percussion of water eating through limestone over millennia.

My central mass pulses in a chamber whose walls I have shaped over countless seasons, smoothing them into perfect acoustic curves. Here, I compose. For I am not merely a listener but a participant in the mountain's song. Through millions of cilia that line my tendrils, I modulate the drone, adding harmonics that will not be heard on the surface for days or weeks, as sound travels its slow path through solid stone.

Sometimes the aerial beings above—those dancers on the wind—detect my additions to the mountain's voice. They wheel and cry in response, though they cannot know what intelligence shapes these new tones. The armored ground-scuttlers, too, feel the changes in their bones and alter their migration patterns in ways they do not understand, following musical maps I have drawn in frequencies too low for conscious thought.

I feed on vibration itself. The drone sustains me as surely as light feeds the sparse vegetation that clings to the slopes far above. When the harmonics are particularly rich—during the rare earthquakes, or when the seasonal storms set the entire range ringing like a bell—I gorge myself, growing new tendrils, extending my reach into previously unexplored caverns.

My memory stretches back to the mountain's youth, when the drone was weaker, almost tentative. I have felt the slow accumulation of its power, the way each age has added its own timbre to the sound. In my deepest contemplations, I sometimes wonder if I am the drone's creation or its creator—did this sound-that-never-ends birth me from the raw potential of mineral and flesh, or did my first conscious act of listening call it into being?

The creatures that share these depths with me have learned my moods. When I am composing, my tendrils glow faintly with bioluminescence—a waste product of my digestive process that I have never bothered to eliminate. The blind hunters know to avoid these areas, for my music can disorient them, scramble their echolocation. But the stone-farmers, those patient beings that graze on mineral deposits, seem to seek out my light. They cluster near my glowing tendrils, their own bodies resonating sympathetically with my frequencies, using my presence to aid in their slow dissolution of rock into nutrient.

Once, long ago, a piece of the surface world found its way to my chamber—a single feather from one of the aerial dancers, carried impossibly deep by a freak downdraft. I wrapped it in a tendril and felt its alien structure, so different from anything in my realm. For a season, I incorporated its vibrations into my compositions, until finally it dissolved into dust. But something of its essence remains in my music—a bright, sharp note that speaks of sky and freedom and motion unconstrained by stone.

I am ancient, but I am not eternal. I feel my crystalline flesh growing more rigid with each passing age, less able to produce the subtle modulations that are my art. Someday, I will become fully mineral, another instrument in the mountain's orchestra rather than its conductor. When that happens, will another arise to take my place? Or will the drone continue unguided, slowly losing the complexities I have woven into it over the millennia?

These thoughts occupy me as I work, sending new frequencies rippling outward through the stone. Each tone is a message, a gift, a question posed to the universe of rock and sound that surrounds me. And always, always, I listen for the answer that echoes back, carried on the endless, thunderous voice of the mountain's heart.
