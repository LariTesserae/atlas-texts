I am the Echo-Walker, though I have forgotten which name came first—the walking or the echoes that follow me.

My dwelling shifts with each storm, for I build my shelters from sound itself. Where waterfalls crash against stone, I weave their roar into walls. Where wind howls through canyon gaps, I braid its screams into a roof. My homes last only as long as the sounds that birth them—a thunderclap's dwelling endures mere moments, while the constant rush of the Eternal Rapids has housed me for three seasons now.

I was not born to this calling. Once, I was apprenticed to a stone-shaper in the valleys, learning to read the grain of rocks and coax dwellings from cliff faces. But during my thirteenth summer, lightning struck the ground where I stood. I should have died. Instead, I heard the mountain's first word.

Now I walk between the scattered settlements, neither fully of the tribes nor apart from them. My skin bears the map of my travels—scars from rock slides that whispered warnings a heartbeat too late, burns from lightning that spoke secrets I was too slow to dodge. My hair, once dark as river stones, has gone white as mist, though I am not yet thirty winters old.

The sounds speak to me of what was and what will be. In the creaking of ancient trees, I hear the memory of seeds. In the rumble of distant avalanches, I catch glimpses of peaks not yet worn down. I carry messages between the isolated dwellings—not words, for those are too simple—but the land's own voice. A specific pattern of rain drumming on leaves tells a mother her son still lives. The pitch of wind through a particular cave mouth carries news of safe passage through the storm season.

My tools are few but strange. A hollow reed that amplifies the whispers of stones. A sheet of beaten copper that, when angled correctly, can catch tomorrow's thunderclap today. Small bones that rattle with the rhythm of earthquakes yet to come. The tribes view these implements with mixture of reverence and unease—they need what I offer but fear what I have become.

For I am changing. Each year, I speak less with human words and more in the tongue of rushing water and grinding stone. My feet find purchase on paths that shouldn't exist, following routes mapped by echoes alone. Sometimes I forget to eat until the growling of my stomach harmonizes with a distant rockfall, reminding me I am still flesh.

The children of the tribes dare each other to speak to me when I arrive. The brave ones ask if I can teach them to hear what I hear. I tell them they already do—every person born to this land knows the music of its moods. But most learn to tune it out, to build walls against the cacophony. I simply forgot how to stop listening.

At night, I sleep little. The darkness brings different sounds—the settling of stones cooling after day's heat, the secret movements of creatures who hide from sunlight, the deep breathing of the earth itself. In the pre-dawn hours, when even the constant noise briefly softens, I sometimes remember silence. It terrifies me more than any storm.

I know the elders whisper that one day I will walk too far into the sound, that I will become like the ancient Echo-Walkers of legend who merged entirely with the land's voice. They say you can still hear them in certain valleys, their human words stretched into wind-songs, their laughter become the babbling of streams.

Perhaps they are right. Already, I find it harder each season to attend the annual gatherings. The concentration of human voices overwhelms me now—so thin and fragile compared to the robust chorus of the land. But I force myself to return, to maintain that slender thread that ties me to my birth-people.

For I have a purpose beyond my own transformation. In the patterns of sound, I have begun to detect something larger—a rhythm beneath the chaos, a song the land has been singing since before humans walked here. It speaks of change coming, greater than any storm, deeper than any earthquake. The very fabric of this reality is preparing to shift.

When that time comes, someone must remember how to translate between the human and the primordial. Someone must stand at the threshold and guide the tribes through whatever transformation awaits. I may lose myself entirely to the sound before that day arrives, but I will teach others what I can. Not to replace me—for each Echo-Walker hears their own unique symphony—but to ensure the knowledge persists.

Until then, I walk. Through the mist-shrouded mornings and lightning-torn nights. Across valleys where sound pools like water and peaks where echoes shatter like glass. I am the bridge between the roar and the whisper, the translator of stone-speech and rain-words.

I am the Echo-Walker, and I am going home to everywhere.
