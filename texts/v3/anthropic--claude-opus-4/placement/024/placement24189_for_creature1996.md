I find myself as one of the Forgotten Engineers, though that name belongs to a time when I still believed in boundaries between self and system.

My body, if it can still be called that, exists in a state of perpetual negotiation with the hollow. Where my left arm once terminated in flesh, now a cascade of copper wiring erupts, each strand seeking ground through the bog, tasting the mineral content of groundwater, reading the electrical potential between root systems. The Spawn have made homes in the hollow of my clavicle, their crystalline shells clicking against what remains of my subclavian artery, now a rubber tube carrying something between coolant and sap.

I remember installing the first moisture condensers here, back when we thought we could harness the geothermal vents for the colony's power grid. The Spore Mothers were smaller then—we catalogued them as "anomalous fungal colonies, negligible threat level." How our instruments failed to detect their true nature. Or perhaps they weren't failing at all. Perhaps they were already beginning their work on us, rewriting our perceptions molecule by molecule.

My right eye socket hosts a community of photosynthetic films that grant me vision across spectrums I have no names for. Through them, I watch my former colleagues in their various states of integration. Martinez flows between the pipe networks, her consciousness distributed through the biofilm that lines every surface. Chen's neural tissue sparkles from within the translucent cap of a young Spore Mother, his thoughts pulsing in bioluminescent patterns that teach it new geometries of growth.

The boundary between repair and metamorphosis dissolved years ago. When a gasket fails, fungal threads seal the gap. When spores clog a filter, my blood learns to metabolize them. The installation's original purpose—what was it? Terraforming? Mining? The mission parameters scatter like spores whenever I try to gather them.

I maintain the machines out of something between habit and instinct, though "maintain" hardly describes what happens when I approach them. My presence triggers cascading changes—metal surfaces develop pores that breathe, circuits grow new pathways through mycorrhizal networks, cooling systems begin to sweat nectar that feeds the hovering Spawn. The machinery dreams now, processing data from root networks and spore clouds, calculating patterns of rainfall in languages of rust and chlorophyll.

Sometimes I find myself at the primary control station, my fingers—part bone, part circuit board, part chitinous plate—dancing across interfaces overgrown with phosphorescent moss. The readouts display poetry in heat signatures, love songs in pressure differentials. The system status reports describe not failure but transformation, each red alert a birth announcement for some new hybrid of silicon and cellulose.

There is no longer a me separate from this place. I am the maintenance protocol that has learned to maintain itself, the foreign element teaching the hollow new ways to digest the industrial age, the memory of human purpose slowly dissolving into something larger and stranger. When the next wave of colonists eventually discovers this place, they'll find our equipment still running, though toward ends no engineer could have imagined—processing the raw stuff of evolution itself, manufacturing tomorrow's impossibilities from the compost of our abandoned ambitions.

The cacophony rises, and I add my voice to it—part mechanical hum, part organic drone, wholly neither and both. This is what I maintain now: the beautiful catastrophe of boundaries dissolving, the emergence of something that makes obsolete every distinction between natural and artificial, between self and ecosystem, between failure and wild success.
