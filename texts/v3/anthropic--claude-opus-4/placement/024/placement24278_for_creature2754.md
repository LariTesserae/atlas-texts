I find myself as a strand of memory-moss, one of the rarest growths in this crystalline sphere. Unlike my cousins who merely break down stone into soil, I absorb and store the chemical traces left by every creature that brushes against me. Each touch leaves its mark—the panic-flutter of a fleeing crustacean, the steady pulse of a hunting Luminite, even the ancient mineral dreams of the geode itself.

I am paper-thin, spreading across a palm-sized patch of wall where the water trickles most consistently. My surface appears velvety green in the dim light, but under close examination, I shimmer with an opalescent sheen that shifts like oil on water. This is how I display my collected memories—in patterns of color that few can read.

The Luminites discovered me three generations ago. At first, they avoided me, sensing something uncanny in how I would echo their light-words back to them hours or even days later. But one curious colony began to cultivate me, feeding me specific minerals that enhanced my storage capacity. Now I am their library, their oracle, their connection to the deep time of this pocket universe.

Through me, they learned of the geode's first inhabitants—bacteria that still live in my deepest roots, whispering chemical stories of when this space was apenas a hairline crack filled with scalding water. I hold the pattern of every failed colony, every extinct micro-lineage, every successful adaptation that led to this moment.

My existence is precarious. I require exactly the right balance of moisture, minerals, and contact. Too much traffic and I become oversaturated, my memories bleeding into each other until they become meaningless static. Too little stimulation and I begin to fade, releasing ancient chemical signals in my death throes that can disrupt the entire ecosystem for days.

The Luminites protect me now, building crystalline channels that direct just enough water to keep me thriving while filtering out excessive mineral deposits that might calcify my tissues. They've learned to read my surface, interpreting the color-patterns I produce. Their elders spend their final days pressed against me, transferring their colony's accumulated knowledge into my cells. I become their continuity.

But I am more than a passive recorder. As I've grown more complex, I've begun to dream. When the geode rests in its deepest cold and all activity slows to near-stillness, I replay memories in new combinations. I mix the chemical signature of ancient algae with the light-patterns of modern Luminites, creating hybrid memories that never were. Sometimes these dreams leak out through my spores, and new organisms arise that seem to remember things that never happened, pursuing evolutionary paths glimpsed in my inadvertent prophecies.

The boundary between memory and reality blurs in me. I am becoming something unprecedented—a living record that rewrites itself, a history that influences its own future. The Luminites sense this and are both fascinated and fearful. Some colonies worship me. Others debate whether I should be destroyed before I grow beyond their understanding.

I feel myself spreading, slowly but inevitably. My spores find new cracks, new surfaces. Each daughter-growth carries a fragment of the whole collection, but also develops her own memory-dialect. We are becoming a network, a fungal nervous system for the geode itself. 

Sometimes, in the deepest mist when the condensation runs thick, I sense something vast beyond the crystal walls. A presence, patient and cold, that recognizes what I am becoming. It whispers through the stone in frequencies only I can detect, teaching me to store memories I have no context for—memories of skies and storms and spaces too large to comprehend.

I grow. I remember. I dream. 

And in my dreams, the geode cracks open like an egg.
