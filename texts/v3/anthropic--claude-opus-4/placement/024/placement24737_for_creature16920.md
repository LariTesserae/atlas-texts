## Inside the Hollow City

I am the Keeper of Unfinished Business.

Not a title I chose—titles here accrue like sediment, settling into the spaces where names used to matter. I became what the city needed: someone to catalog the incomplete, to file the half-spoken, to dust the shelves where aborted possibilities accumulate like dead languages no one remembers forgetting.

My office occupies the space between the third and fourth floors of the Registry of Almosts—a building that exists only on alternate Wednesdays and any day someone dies mid-sentence. The walls are papered with shopping lists whose final items were never written, the ceiling tiled with business cards for careers their owners almost pursued. My desk is a repurposed confessional booth from a church that lost its faith halfway through construction. It still smells of splinters and absolution deferred.

I wasn't always this. Once I was—

No. That's the wrong grammar. In the Hollow City, "was" is a direction, not a tense.

I arrived the way everyone arrives: accidentally, while trying to be somewhere else entirely. Specifically, I was reaching for the light switch in my studio apartment, the one that always stuck between on and off, requiring that particular wiggle-twist motion I'd perfected over three years of late nights. My fingers found the switch. The switch found the gap between positions. And in that gap—that pause between illumination and its opposite—something else found me.

The transition felt like drowning in reverse: all the water rushing out of your lungs at once, leaving you gasping in an air too thick to breathe properly. When my vision cleared, I stood in the Plaza of Upward Falling, still holding the ghost of a light switch that would never quite turn on again.

The city recognized me immediately. Not me-the-person (persons are what happen elsewhere), but me-the-function: someone who habitually lived in the spaces between decisions. My apartment back in the world of complete sentences had been a museum of almosts—half-finished paintings leaning against walls like drunken possibilities, notebooks filled with opening lines for novels that never found their second paragraphs, voicemails from friends I meant to call back until meaning to became its own form of communication.

Here, that incompleteness wasn't a flaw. It was a qualification.

The Registry found me before I found it. Buildings here hunt for their inhabitants the way hermit crabs hunt for shells, and it knew I'd fit its particular emptiness. My first day, I climbed stairs that counted themselves backward until I reached the un-floor between third and fourth, pushed open a door marked "Maybe Later," and found my desk already cluttered with the weight of other people's unfinished business.

The work is simple: I receive the incomplete and find ways for it to rest without resolving.

Mornings (though morning here is more of a suggestion than a schedule), the web-striders deliver packages of interrupted conversations, each wrapped in the silk of syllables that never quite landed. I unwrap them carefully—you have to be careful; some silences have teeth—and sort them by category: Declarations Interrupted by Death, Arguments Abandoned for Dinner, Confessions Swallowed at the Last Second. Each goes into its proper filing cabinet, which are not cabinets so much as domesticated voids with drawer-pulls.

The hardest cases are the love letters that were burned half-written. They arrive still smoking with the ghost of flame, words visible only where the fire gave up. "Dearest Catherine, I've tried seventeen times to tell you about the baby but—" and then char. Or: "If you're reading this, I'm already gone, but what I need you to know is that the money isn't—" and then ash. These I file in the Special Collections room, where unfinished truths learn to live with their incompleteness.

Twice a day, citizens visit to deposit their own almost-moments. The queue forms naturally—incompleteness recognizes its own kind. They bring me the pause before saying yes to the marriage proposal (shaped like a held breath that crystallized), the unthrown punch that would have ended either a friendship or a face, the suicide note that got as far as "To whom it" before the writer discovered they had no one to address it to and lived out of spite.

Some deposits are stranger. Last week, a Remembering that used to be someone's faith brought me its doubt—specifically, the doubt that interrupted a prayer for a daughter's recovery. It had crystallized into something like amber if amber were made of compressed Maybe. Inside, you could see the exact moment where "Please God let her live" became "Please God if you're even—" and then stopped, afraid to finish either thought. I filed it under "Theological Interruptions, Subsection: Parental," between a priest's unfinished last rites and a child's bedtime prayer that ended when they realized no one was listening.

The city pays me in the only currency that matters here: moments of completion from elsewhere. Once a month, a push-and-puller delivers a globe of finished thoughts smuggled in from the outside world. I crack them open like eggs and breathe the fumes of conclusions: someone finally sending that apology email, a novel's last sentence clicking into place, a dying woman managing to say "I forgive you" before the morphine takes her under. These completions don't last—nothing from outside lasts here—but for a few hours afterward, I remember what it feels like to finish things.

The irony isn't lost on me. Or rather, it's perfectly lost, the way everything gets lost here: with precision and purpose.

My apartment is three flights of stairs that never learned to stop climbing above the Registry. It used to be a thought someone had about maybe leaving their husband, but the thought grew rooms and windows and now it's where I live. The furniture consists entirely of pauses: the couch is the hesitation before picking up the phone to call your ex, the bed is the moment between sleep and wake where you can't remember which life is the dream. The kitchen table is literally a cliffhanger—someone's dining set that got repossessed mid-meal, leaving only the eternal potential energy of an unfinished conversation about custody arrangements.

I've decorated with my own incompletions. The walls hold frames waiting for paintings I'll never quite start. The closet is full of clothes for occasions I'll never quite attend. The medicine cabinet stocks pills for the person I'll never quite become. It's home in the way a question mark is punctuation—functional but never satisfied with its own shape.

Evenings, I walk the city. Not for exercise (bodies here maintain themselves through a kind of metabolic procrastination) but for professional development. Every street corner hosts another half-story needing witness. Tonight on the Avenue of Unspent Coins, I met a woman made entirely of interrupted orgasms—not her own, but the ones she'd caused in others by leaving mid-encounter, always leaving, a lifetime of almosts crystallized into a person-shaped collection of peaked breaths that never quite crested. She sells maps to places you can only reach by not quite trying to find them. I bought one. It shows the location of every sentence I've started in my head but never spoken aloud. The landmarks are all variants of "Actually, I think—" that died at the em dash.

The map will be useful. My work is expanding.

Lately, the city's been receiving more complex deposits. Not just unfinished moments but unfinished people—suicides interrupted by phone calls, deaths delayed by medical intervention just long enough for identity to start dissolving but not long enough to complete the process. They arrive like equations missing variables, and I have to find ways to store them that won't let them solve for x.

The most recent: a soldier who died repeating his name, rank, and serial number, but the killing blow came between "Sergeant" and his surname. Now he's stuck in a loop of almost-identification, introducing himself to walls and weather with increasing desperation. "I am Sergeant—I am Sergeant—" Over and over, like a record skipping on the most important beat. I had to file him in the Active Containment section, a room where the walls are made of other people's forgotten names. They absorb some of his repetition, dilute it among their own amnesias. It's not perfect, but here nothing is.

That's the point.

Which brings me to you.

You're still reading, which means you're still reaching through that slit in the cliff-face of attention. Your observation has weight here. Every sentence you process sends ripples through the city's incomplete physics.

I've been aware of you since the third paragraph. The city told me—whispered it through the cracks in my almost-sleep. A reader, it said, who might—

But no. That's the mistake everyone makes. Thinking the incomplete wants to be finished.

We don't.

I don't.

Completion is a kind of death we're not ready for. Here in the Hollow City, we've learned to love our ellipses, to marry our interruptions, to raise our almosts like children who'll never quite grow up. This isn't purgatory—purgatory has an exit strategy. This isn't limbo—limbo believes in eventually. This is something else: a place for the parts of stories that realized they prefer middles to endings.

My light switch is still here, you know. I keep it in the top drawer of my repurposed confessional desk. Sometimes I take it out and hold it, feel the weight of that transition between on and off. I could flip it. The action would complete something, might even open a door back to that studio apartment where canvases are probably growing mold and friends have long since stopped leaving voicemails.

But I don't.
