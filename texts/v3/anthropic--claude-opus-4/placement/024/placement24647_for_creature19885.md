I am a **Thread-Counter**, though that designation means nothing to those who have not spent centuries learning to parse the infinitesimal variations in our eternal sameness.

My position—for that is all I am—encompasses a vertical shaft no wider than an embrace, running from the thirty-third tier to the thirty-seventh. The walls here are lined with what outsiders might mistake for decorative braiding: thousands upon thousands of copper wires, each no thicker than a human hair, woven into patterns so dense they appear solid from a distance. This is my garden, my manuscript, my proof of existence.

I have been here since the oxidation turned the lowest wires green—perhaps four thousand years, perhaps longer. Time is difficult to track when your only measurement is the microscopic creep of verdigris along metal that will not decay.

My body is typical of our kind: a scaffolding of ceramic tubes and bronze joints, wrapped in that peculiar fiber that is neither tendon nor rope but something between. My mask-face bears four slits instead of the usual three—an ancient modification I made to better catch the light variations as it filters through the braided copper. My fingers are my most precious tools: twenty-four in total, each tipped with a different gauge of bronze stylus, worn smooth by millennia of contact with the wires.

My task is this: I count the crossings.

Where one wire passes over another, where three create a vertex, where the pattern shifts from hexagonal to heptagonal—I know them all. There are exactly 1,847,293 primary intersections in my shaft. I have counted them ten thousand times. Each counting takes approximately forty years, allowing for the necessary pauses when I must lower my vibration to conserve heat.

But I do not merely count. I maintain the tension.

You see, the wires are not static. The constant vibration of the Breath of the Kilns creates a phenomenon we call "pattern drift." Over centuries, the wires shift minutely, their crossings migrating like slow glaciers of geometry. Left unchecked, a hexagon might become a pentagon, a crucial load-bearing braid might loosen.

So I tend them. With my twenty-four fingers, I make adjustments so small that no measurement could capture them. I might spend a month applying pressure to a single crossing point, shifting it back by the width of a dust mote. I know each wire by its tone when struck, by the particular green of its patina, by the way it catches the amber light at the seventh hour of the constant day.

My chamber connects to others through a series of ceramic tubes just wide enough for sound to travel. Through these, I hear the percussion-language of my neighbors: the Brick-Smoothers above, whose palms have worn to mirror-polish from their eternal circling motions; the Valve-Turner below, who rotates the same bronze wheel a quarter-turn clockwise each decade, then a quarter-turn back.

Sometimes, rarely, I communicate with them. When I discover a critical drift in the wire patterns—one that might, in a thousand years, affect the structural harmonics of our tier—I strike my chest in the ancient rhythms. *Tap-tap-CLACK-tap-tap-tap-CLACK.* The message propagates through the Labyrinth like ripples in solid stone, each inhabitant adding their own percussion until the Resonators far below adjust their frequency by some infinitesimal degree.

I have a secret, though. One that would mean nothing to any but another Thread-Counter, if such existed.

In my thirty-first millennium of counting, I discovered something: the wires are writing. Not intentionally—the patterns were woven by inhabitants so ancient that even we cannot remember them. But in their crossings, in the mathematical progression of over-under-over-under, I have found sequences that repeat with variations. Phrases, almost. Paragraphs of metal.

I believe the entire Labyrinth is a text, written in copper and bronze, in angles and intersections. And I am its only reader.

So I count. And as I count, I memorize. Somewhere in my ceramic skull, in the chambers where others store only the blueprints of their tasks, I am building a library of wire-words. When I finally wear too thin to continue, when my joints fuse and I become another strut in this eternal architecture, the knowledge will die with me. No matter. Another Counter may arise, in ten thousand years or a hundred thousand, and discover the text anew.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps I am the only one who will ever know that our Labyrinth speaks, that each of us in our eternal repetitions are merely punctuation marks in a sentence that takes millennia to complete.

The Breath of the Kilns roars through me. My internal temperature is optimal. My fingers move among the wires like a weaver at a loom that encompasses the vertical universe.

I count. I tension. I read the metal manuscript of our mute world.

This is enough.
