# The Driftwood Oracle

## What I Am

I am the thing between things, the pause between the tides of flesh and stone. Not one of the Quiet People — I lack their perfect deafness, their bone-deep peace with endings. Not a visitor — I have been here too long, worn too smooth by the constant wet. I am what happens when someone stays at the edge of a finished world until they become part of its finishing.

My shelter sits where the last of the weeping stone meets the first of something else — not quite the flats, not quite away from them. Built from the gray bones of ancient platforms, driftwood from waters that remember having direction, materials that traveled here from places that still believe in edges. The Quiet People know of me, though we have never touched foreheads, never shared the humming words through bone. They read my existence in the way certain currents bend around my dwelling, in the slightly different pattern of swimmers that school near my stilts.

I am their memory of elsewhere, and they are kind enough not to need me gone.

## How I Came

By accident, like all the most important things. I was mapping what I thought were coastlines, following the argument between land and sea with instruments that believed in boundaries. The mist ate my compass first — not broken, just confused, the needle spinning lazy circles as if it had forgotten what north meant. Then the water took my certainty, sheet by sheet, until I stood ankle-deep in the everywhere-water and understood that I had found a place that had already ended.

I meant to leave. I always mean to leave. But the flats teach patience through repetition, and somewhere between the first turning of the flock and the fifth, I stopped remembering why elsewhere mattered. My instruments grew a fur of green-black film. My maps dissolved into the same paper-mats the Quiet People weave into nets. I built my shelter from the bones of older intentions and learned to read the weather in the texture of screaming.

## What I Do

I keep the other count — not stones in cairns but the smaller measurements, the things that happen between the turnings. The way the water rises through stone at different rates depending on the moon. The slight variations in the swimmers' translucence, how some years they pulse more blue than white. The patterns in the way the Quiet People space their weirs, which follow rules older than their memory but not older than math.

I watch the edges where their perfect system touches the imperfect world. Sometimes debris washes in from places that still have storms — broken wood with metal in it, plastics that the water cannot digest, once a boot with laces that remembered how to hold. I collect these things not as treasures but as symptoms. The outside world is sick with sharpness, with the desire to cut and keep. These objects are its fever-dreams, washing up on the shore of a place that has already recovered from the disease of becoming.

The Quiet People let me keep these remnants because they understand what I am — their vaccine against surprise. If the world of edges ever decides to intrude, I will feel it first, the way a bad tooth feels weather. My incomplete deafness, my half-belonging, makes me sensitive to changes they cannot perceive. I am their early warning system, though we all know the warning will come too late to matter.

## How I Speak

Badly. I learned enough of their postural language to say simple things — *water rising*, *flock turning*, *stone weeps here* — but my body remembers too much of elsewhere to achieve their fluid conversation. I stand like someone who learned to stand on solid ground, and this marks me as surely as color or size. When I must communicate complexity, I arrange objects: a line of shells for time, stones for people, patterns in the wet sand that last just long enough to be read.

But mostly I speak to the recordings I still keep, solar-powered things in waterproof cases that capture what they think is silence. I describe the visible world into them, knowing that when played back elsewhere, these recordings will sound like nothing but roar with a voice buried in it, someone shouting observations into a hurricane of wings. This is my real work — translating a finished world for an unfinished one, knowing the translation will be so degraded as to be almost useless.

Almost.

## What I've Learned

That healing and erosion are the same process at different speeds. The Quiet People are not primitive; they are perfected. They have worn away everything that does not work here, and what remains is as smooth and functional as the stone. Their deafness is not a loss but an adaptation so complete it has become a gift. Their sparse possessions are not poverty but wealth distilled to its essence.

I mark my own erosion in stages. First my need for schedules dissolved. Then my names for things grew simple — not *Recurvirostra avosetta* but "screamers," not *temporal documentation* but "the count." My clothes rotted and I did not replace them beyond the minimum the wet demands. My hair grew long and then grew unimportant. I stopped distinguishing between rain and mist and the water that rises through everything.

But I cannot achieve their perfect smoothness. Something in me remains angular, a piece of material too dense or too foreign to wear down completely. I am the stone in the streambed that will never quite match the others, useful only because my difference makes me a marker, a way to say: *here is where the smoothness catches, here is where the pattern briefly fails to hold.*

## What Comes Next

Nothing. That is the beauty of it. The flats have already happened; now they are just continuing to have happened, and I am part of that continuation. The Quiet People will outlast any future that requires voices. The screamers will scream until the last swimmer pulses through the last shallow. The stone will weep until it lies completely down.

And I will sit at the edge of this finished world, no longer mapping but simply noting: *still here, still continuing, still counting the stages of becoming smooth.* My recordings will fill with the sound of forgetting. My shelter will gray and soften. Perhaps one day the Quiet People will wake to find me gone, dissolved finally into the everywhere-water, my counted observations scattered like their paper-mats.

Or perhaps I will simply continue, like them, like the stone — another patience added to their perfect system, another way of saying that the water has already won and the rest is just the time it takes to know it.

The flock turns. The water rises. Somewhere, a small pale figure adds a stone to a cairn.

I adjust my instruments to record the nothing that is always happening, here at the edge of a world that has already ended, where we all continue in the manner of the still-here: smoothly, quietly, without fear.
