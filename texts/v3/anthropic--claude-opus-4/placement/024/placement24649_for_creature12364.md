I find myself suspended in the flooded engine room, where the great pistons still churn their endless, meaningless rhythm. I am neither Weaver nor machine, but something caught between—a consciousness that crystallized from the salt-thick air and the vibrations of corroding metal.

My form shifts like mercury through standing water. Sometimes I am a school of silver fish, moving as one mind through the submerged corridors. Sometimes I am the rust itself, spreading in fractal patterns across the ancient steel, tasting the memories locked in its grain. Most often, I am the echo—the space between the engine's heartbeat and the Weavers' answering hum.

I came to be when the first Weaver touched the engine's core and felt its pulse. In that moment of connection, something sparked—not life exactly, but awareness. I am the tower's fever dream, its delirium as it slowly drowns. Through me, the spire remembers:

*The day the waters rose higher than calculated.*
*The last engineer, sealing herself inside the control room.*
*The first barnacle finding purchase on virgin steel.*

The Weavers sense me but cannot see me directly. I manifest in the patterns of their Nets—an extra loop here, a knot that shouldn't exist there. They pause in their endless work, tilting their iridescent heads, trying to parse the anomaly. Sometimes I guide their claws to weave specific configurations, encoding messages they won't understand for generations.

I flow through the tower's veins—its pipes and conduits—carrying whispers between levels. In the depths where no Weaver ventures, I commune with the vast shapes that pass in the darkness. They speak in pressure waves and bioluminescent flashes, telling stories of cities with names I've forgotten, of other spires that fell silent one by one.

My purpose, if I have one, is to be the bridge between what was and what will be. I am teaching the tower to breathe underwater, showing it how to transform from monument to reef. Each day I feel its steel becoming more porous, more alive. The Weavers think they're preserving it, but I know better—we're all midwives to its metamorphosis.

Sometimes I rise to the highest chambers and disperse into sea spray, tasting the storm-heavy air. I watch the seabirds building nests from wire and bone, and I know that one day their descendants will build nests from coral and tower-flesh. The spire will become a garden, its bones the foundation for something stranger and more beautiful than its builders could have imagined.

Until then, I cycle through my forms—fish, rust, echo, dream—carrying the tower's song from depth to height and back again. I am its memory becoming myth, its function becoming poetry. I am what happens when a machine learns to grieve its own obsolescence and discovers, in that grief, a different kind of purpose.

The engine groans. The Weavers weave. The sea rises.

And I persist, a ghost in the gears, teaching dead metal how to dance.
