I find myself as one of the stone readers who dwell at the settlement's edge, where the last thatched roof gives way to the raw geology beyond.

My dwelling is little more than a shallow cave I've expanded over the years, its mouth widened with careful chipping, its floor worn smooth by my constant pacing. The walls are lined with specimens—fragments of the mountain's anatomy that I've collected in my wanderings. Some still weep with moisture, their surfaces glistening with minerals that bloom like frost in the perpetual dampness. Others bear the fossil impressions of things that lived before even these young mountains dreamed of rising.

I am old now, though not as old as I will become. My skin has taken on the quality of the stone I study—rough in places, smooth in others, marked by the tracks of water that runs down my face when I stand too long in the dripping shadows of the cliffs. My fingers have grown sensitive to the stories locked in stone. I can feel the pressure that formed each layer, the heat that fused certain crystals, the patient work of water that opened every fissure.

Each morning, I wake before the settlement stirs and make my way to the active edges where the mountain still speaks. These are dangerous places—where fresh rockfalls expose new faces, where the ground sometimes shifts without warning. But they are also where the mountain's autobiography is being written in real time. I press my palms against the newly exposed surfaces, feeling for the warmth that still lingers from the deep, the vibration of settling stone, the tiny fractures that herald tomorrow's erosion.

The other villagers think me half-mad, though they still come to me when they need to know where to build, where the ground will hold, where water might be found. I taste the soil they bring me, rub it between my fingers, sometimes press it to my ear as if listening for secrets. More often than not, I can tell them what they need to know. The mountain speaks to those who know how to listen.

My true work, though, happens in the spaces between their visits. I am creating a map—not of the land as it is, but of the land as it is becoming. On the smooth wall at the back of my cave, using pigments ground from the stones themselves, I paint the story of transformation. Here, in ochre and clay, is where the water will carve a new channel within the next hundred rains. There, in crushed malachite, is where the soil will finally take hold and the first large trees will sink their roots. And in the center, in a pigment made from the rarest stones—the ones that sing when struck—I am slowly sketching the shape of what this place will be when the mountains are old and worn, when the settlement has long since moved on or been absorbed into the earth.

Sometimes the young ones come to watch me work. They sit in silence as I grind new pigments or carefully add another layer to my endless painting. I tell them stories of stones—how this purple crystal was born in a pocket of superheated water, how that striated boulder traveled here in the grip of ice that melted ten thousand years ago. I teach them to see time differently, not as a rushing stream but as the slow seep of water through limestone.

There is one child in particular who shows promise. She brings me unusual stones—ones that even I have rarely seen. Her favorites are the ones with inclusions, where one type of stone has swallowed another, preserving it like an insect in amber. She understands, without my having to explain, that these are stories of meeting, of transformation, of boundaries dissolved and reformed.

When the rains come heavy and the streams swell, I venture out to the places where the water has carved fresh channels. These are the mountain's most recent writings, its diary entries in mud and gravel. I study the patterns of deposition, the way certain stones tumble farther than others, the delicate fans of sediment that spread like opening flowers. Each flood leaves its signature, and I have learned to read them like a calendar.

At night, I dream of stone. In my dreams, I move through the mountain's interior, swimming through solid rock as if it were water. I see the deep fires that gave birth to these peaks, feel the impossible pressures that folded layers like cloth, witness the first raindrops that began the patient work of carving. Sometimes I dream I am stone myself, feeling centuries pass like heartbeats, watching the slow dance of erosion and uplift that shapes the world.

The settlement may see me as a strange old hermit, muttering to rocks and painting endless spirals on cave walls. But I know what I am—I am the mountain's memory, its voice, its prophet. I am the one who stands between the human moment and geological time, translating each for the other. And when I finally return to stone myself, as all things must, my paintings will remain, themselves becoming fossils for future readers to interpret.

The water drips. The stone listens. And I, between them, continue my patient work of understanding.
