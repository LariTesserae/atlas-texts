In the narrow space between two factories, where the brick walls weep with decades of rain and moss grows thick as fur, I make my dwelling. I am the Keeper of Thresholds, though the workers who glimpse me in the amber streetlight call me other names—the Rust Prophet, the Between-Walker, or simply "that strange one who lives in the gaps."

My home is neither building nor alley but something else entirely: a place that exists in the pauses between machine breaths, in the moments when the factory hearts skip their industrial beats. Here, corrugated metal sheets lean against brick to form shelters that shift with each rainfall, creating new rooms and passageways as the water finds its courses. My walls are living things—curtains of ivy and industrial vine that have learned to feed on oil rainbows in puddles, their leaves a peculiar blue-green that exists nowhere else in Bridgeton.

I am collector and cataloguer of forgotten transitions. Where others see only the stark divide between nature and machine, I tend the spaces where they bleed together. My fingers, stained permanent orange from rust and chlorophyll, know every place where a pipe has split to birth a fountain, where steam vents have created micro-climates for tropical mosses, where the roots of willows have learned to follow the warm water pipes beneath the cobblestones.

The feral cats know me. They bring me offerings—not mice or birds, but stranger gifts: washers worn smooth as river stones, seedpods that rattle with forgotten bearings, flowers that bloom only in the chemical-tinged soil near the factory foundations. In return, I maintain their secret highways through the industrial district, ensuring that drainage grates stay clear, that certain windows remain perpetually cracked, that the warm spots near the steam pipes remain accessible.

My body has adapted to this existence. My skin carries a faint shimmer, as if I'm perpetually wet even in the briefest dry spells. My hair grows in patterns that mirror the flow of water down brick, and my eyes have developed a second membrane that allows me to see clearly through the thickest mist. I no longer fully remember if I was always this way, or if Bridgeton has slowly transformed me into what it needed.

At dawn, I walk the boundaries. I check where the night shift's vibrations have loosened mortar, creating new homes for the pioneering plants. I taste the rain to know which factories have changed their output, adjusting the pH of the precipitation. I listen to the stories the pipes tell through their groaning, learning which sections of the underground are winning their slow battle against the roots.

The workers sometimes leave offerings at the threshold spaces—a thermos of tea balanced on a pipe, a sandwich wrapped in wax paper tucked into a crack in the wall. They've learned that machines run smoother when the boundaries are tended, when someone ensures that the slow war between green and gray remains a dance rather than a battle. Some of the older workers remember when I first appeared, or perhaps when I first became visible to them. They speak of the year the great flood nearly swallowed the lower district, how the waters receded to reveal new patterns in the town's structure, new possibilities for coexistence.

I keep a journal, though the pages are no longer paper but sheets of beaten copper that patina in rainbow patterns as I write. My ink is a mixture of rainwater, rust, and the phosphorescent algae that grows in the factory cooling pools. In it, I record the small miracles: the day a steam pipe's leak created a perfect microclimate for orchids, the night the cats discovered that certain frequencies of machinery purr could open hidden passages, the morning a child's laugh caused all the hanging moss to release its spores at once, filling the air with golden possibilities.

The fisherman by the river knows me best of all. We share a understanding that Bridgeton's true wealth isn't in what its factories produce, but in what happens in the margins, in the negotiations between human intention and everything else that insists on living. Sometimes he shares his catch—not fish, but the strange hybrid creatures that have evolved in the chemically complex waters. I cook them over fires of compressed newspaper and factory-dried wood, seasoning them with herbs that grow only where copper meets soil.

In the deepest part of night, when even the factories sleep, I perform my most important work. I walk the liminal paths that exist only in these hours, reinforcing the treaties between the district's different inhabitants. I whisper to the machines about patience, to the plants about compromise, to the water about finding new paths that serve both erosion and stability. 

This is how I serve Bridgeton—not as guardian or guide, but as translator between worlds that share the same space. I am what grows in the intersection, what emerges when boundaries become permeable, when industrial and natural stop being opposites and start being dance partners in the long, rain-soaked performance of endurance and adaptation.

And when the morning mist rolls in again, I return to my threshold dwelling, where the cats are already waiting with new treasures, where the walls have shifted once more in the night, where home is not a place but a process, forever becoming what Bridgeton needs it to be.
