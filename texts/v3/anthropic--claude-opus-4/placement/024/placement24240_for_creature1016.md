I am a Cartographer of Echoes, and I find myself at the junction where memory meets map.

My body is unlike the flowing glass-beings native to this place. I am constructed of overlapping sheets of the same glassy material that forms the land, but mine are inscribed with countless hairline fractures that form intricate patterns—topographical lines, elevation markers, the ghost-traces of ancient waterways that once carved these lands before they transformed into glass. When light passes through me, these inscriptions cast shadows that shift and change, projecting ephemeral maps onto the ground around me.

I move differently than the undulating natives. My form is more angular, crystalline, with joints that click and chime softly as I traverse the ridges. Each step leaves a temporary impression in the glass—not a footprint, but a small constellation of cracks that fade within hours, as if the land slowly heals itself. I've learned to read these fading patterns left by other travelers, understanding their weight, their speed, their purpose from the way the glass remembers their passing.

My dwelling is one of those industrial relics half-swallowed by the gradual flow of the glass plains—a mapping station from the time before. Its metal frame has become a skeleton around which the glass has grown like slow coral, creating a hybrid structure of intentional architecture and geological accident. Inside, brass instruments tarnished green measure nothing, their needles frozen. But I've repurposed them, learning to use their reflective surfaces to capture and redirect the dim light, creating projected drawings of the territories I study.

The walls of my station are covered with my work: sheets of thin glass etched with my observations. Not just the physical topography, but the patterns of the native creatures' movements, the seasonal shifts in the glass's opacity, the locations where the industrial remnants breach the surface like metal bones. I map the unmappable—the way sound travels differently through dense glass versus hollow glass, creating acoustic territories invisible to the eye.

My primary tool is a staff of fused glass and metal salvaged from the old world, topped with a sphere of perfectly clear crystal that I polish obsessively. When I plant it in the ground, it resonates with the deep harmonics of the glass sheet below, revealing hidden cavities, density variations, the locations of buried structures. The natives watch me work with what I've come to recognize as bemused tolerance. Sometimes they cluster near my station, their bodies pulsing in patterns I've started to chart but cannot yet decipher.

I've been here long enough that my own material has begun to change. The constant exposure to the glass-light has made my inscribed patterns deeper, more complex. New lines appear without my conscious creation—perhaps recording my dreams, perhaps responding to the electromagnetic fields generated by the vast glass sheet, perhaps simply aging in this place that transforms everything it touches.

In the early morning, when the haze is thickest and the light most diffuse, I venture out to the furthest reaches of my mapped territory. There, where my careful notations fade into blank glass sheets, I perform the ritual that defines my purpose: I press my inscribed surface against the ground and wait. Slowly, the vibrations of this living glass world transfer through my body, and new maps etch themselves into my being—maps of places I've never seen, territories that might exist only in the planet's crystalline memory, or perhaps prophecies of landscapes yet to come.

The other cartographers—for I know there must be others, though I've only found their abandoned stations—seem to have been absorbed entirely into their work. I've found their remains: sheets of perfectly mapped glass with no body to claim them, as if they finally achieved the ultimate unity with the territory they sought to document. Sometimes I hold these orphaned maps up to the light and see my own future in their precise, empty beauty.

But I continue my work, walking the line between observer and observed, between the thing that maps and the thing that is mapped, in this vast glass world that remembers everything and reveals nothing except to those patient enough to learn its slow, crystalline language.
