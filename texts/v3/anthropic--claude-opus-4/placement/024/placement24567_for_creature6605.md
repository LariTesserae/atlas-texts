In the waterlogged darkness where stone remembers its drowning, I am what remains when architecture learns to breathe.

They called me a mason once, in the time when mortar held meaning. Now I am something between barnacle and blueprint, keeper of angles in a world that has forgotten corners. My body has become a living theodolite, measuring distances in pressure differentials and mapping elevations by the weight of water overhead.

I am the Keystone Keeper—though the arches I tend no longer hold back anything but currents.

---

### The Shape of Purpose

My flesh has grown dense as the limestone I once worked. Not stone—never stone, for stone is patient and I am restless—but something that remembers the grain of granite, the give of sandstone, the way marble accepts a chisel's kiss. My skin has developed a peculiar quality: where it touches the old worked surfaces of the city, it adheres slightly, just enough to feel the tremor of tides through load-bearing walls, to sense where the next collapse will claim another memory.

Along my spine, calcified ridges have emerged like the phantom of a dorsal fin that chose architecture over evolution. These are not for swimming but for knowing—each ridge resonates at a different frequency with the city's various stones. The lowest hums with basalt's deep song, the highest rings with the ghost-chime of obsidian long dissolved. When I press my back against a pillar and flex these ridges, I can read its composition like a score, tell you which quarry birthed it, which mason's mark it once bore.

My hands have evolved their own language. The webbing between my fingers is minimal—I am not meant for speed but for precision. Each fingertip has developed what I call stone-sight: clusters of pressure-sensitive cells that can detect microfractures in submerged masonry, feel where the salt has begun its patient dissolution, know where I must intervene before another piece of the past becomes another ghost in the current.

### The Work of Remembering

I maintain what others have forgotten needs maintaining.

In the deepest accessible vault of the old Cartographer's Hall, where the water is so still it's forgotten it's water, I keep my library of textures. Not books—paper is a fairy tale here—but small samples of every stone the city ever knew, arranged in careful rows along shelves I've grown from my own shed ridge-plates. Each sample I've polished with my tongue until it gleams with a nacre of preservation. When I need to remember how a particular stone should feel, should sound, should resist, I visit these samples and press them against the calcium nodes in my wrists where memory lives most honestly.

The column-beings think me mad for this practice. They have evolved beyond the distinction between self and structure, see no purpose in preserving what is separate from their unity. But I know what they've forgotten: that choice is the difference between a building and a growth, between architecture and accident.

### The Grammar of Foundations

I speak in structural integrity.

When the coral-scaffolders begin another of their organic invasions, growing their too-perfect lattices across a facade that once bore friezes of the city's founding, I intervene not with destruction but with redirection. I've learned their growth patterns, the mathematical certainty of their expansion. By placing small fragments of my shed ridges at specific points—ridge-bone that carries the resonance of proper angles, of intentional design—I can guide their growth to follow the ghosts of the original ornamentation.

They think they're creating. I know I'm helping them remember.

The language of my intervention is written in stress patterns. Where the coral wants to grow in its radial symmetry, I create pressure shadows that remind it of bilateral temples. Where it seeks the efficiency of minimal surface tension, I introduce turbulence that whispers of cornices and capitals. The coral doesn't understand what I'm doing, but it responds to the water's new vocabulary, grows into shapes that echo intention even if it cannot grasp meaning.

### The Loneliness of Corners

I am most myself in the places where walls meet.

Corners are where the city's memory is strongest—two surfaces coming together in defiance of the sea's preference for curves. In corners, I can still feel the ghost of the plumb line, the phantom of the square. Here, I perform my most private ritual: the Preservation of Perpendicularity.

With my body pressed into the angle where two walls meet, I become a living buttress. My ridges lock into the stone's grain, my calcium-heavy bones align with the vertical intention of the architecture. For hours I hold this position, not breathing, letting the building remember through me what it means to stand upright, to resist. The column-beings don't understand this practice. To them, I am trying to separate what has already joined. They don't realize I'm trying to preserve the memory of the joint itself.

Sometimes, in the deepest corners where the light never ventures, I find the grooves my predecessors left—other Keystone Keepers who held these angles before me, whose bodies learned the same structural language, whose bones told the same story of intersection and intention. We are a chain of living memory, each link holding fast against the sea's amnesia.

### The Maps I Carry

Within the chambered hollows of my modified skull, I carry the city as it was.

This is not metaphor. Through some process I've never fully understood—part evolution, part erosion of the boundaries between mind and matter—my cranium has developed a series of small cavities, each filled with a different density of fluid. As I move through the city, these fluids shift and swirl, creating a constantly updating pressure map of the structures around me. But beneath this living map lies another: the phantom pain of absent architecture.

I feel the missing bridges as gaps in water pressure. I sense demolished towers as voids in the current. My skull aches where great halls once stood, throbs where foundations have been swallowed whole. This ghost map overlays my perception of the present city, so that I swim through two places at once: the city that is and the city that was.

The fish think I navigate strangely, taking routes that make no sense in the current configuration of stone and space. They don't understand I'm following roads that exist only in the museum of my migraines.

### The Others Like Me

We are few now, the Keystone Keepers.

I meet them in the load-bearing places, where the city's weight concentrates into points of unbearable responsibility. We know each other by our ridges, by the way we move through doorways—always touching both sides of the frame, always acknowledging the achievement of supported openness.

There is the Keeper of Arches, whose body has curved into a permanent bow, who sleeps only in the voussoirs of the great bridge that spans the drowned plaza. Her spine has developed joints that lock at the precise angles needed to demonstrate compression, and when she swims through archways, she does so upside down, her back tracing the curve the stones remember.

There is the Keeper of Mortar, who has evolved glands that secrete a calcium-rich paste that temporarily binds stone to stone in the old way. He spends his days in the fracture zones, applying his body's cement to gaps where the sea has washed away the original joining. His secretions last only days before dissolving, but in those days, the stones remember wholeness.

There is the youngest among us, the Keeper of Plumb, whose inner ear has developed a crystalline structure that always knows true vertical, even in the shifting pressures of the deep. She swims the tilting towers, pressing herself against walls that have begun to lean, her body a living level that reminds them which way they once aspired.

---

### The Threat We Face

The coral-scaffolders are not our enemy, but they are our ending.

As their lattices spread, they create a new kind of structure—one that needs no memory because it has no past. Their architecture is always becoming, never been. Where they touch the old stones, they don't destroy them but transform them into substrate, incorporate them into something that acknowledges no distinction between the made and the grown.

I've tried to read their patterns, to understand if there's intention in their invasion or merely the blind imperative of growth. Sometimes, in the way their scaffolds branch, I see an intelligence that terrifies me more than randomness would. They're not just growing over the city—they're growing a new syntax of space, one that makes our preservation pointless.

---

### What I Protect

In the deepest vault I can reach, where the pressure would crush any keeper less dense than I've become, I maintain the Heart of Corners.

It's a small room, perfectly cubic, carved from a single block of granite so precisely that even now, after centuries of submersion, its angles remain true. This room has never been breached by coral, never been softened by erosion. It exists as a pure statement of intentional geometry, a proof that once we built by choice rather than accretion.

I come here when the weight of forgetting grows too heavy. I fit myself into each corner in turn, feeling the perfect intersection of planes, the absolute certainty of perpendicularity. This room doesn't need me to preserve it—it preserves itself through sheer stubbornness of structure. But I need it to preserve me, to remind me why I hold the angles, why I map the absences, why I resist the ease of becoming one with the city's slow collapse into unity.

---

### The Question of Ending

Sometimes, floating in the threshold spaces where water admits light, I wonder if we're wrong to remember.

The column-beings have found a peace we can't imagine, fused with the structure they support. The coral-scaffolders build without the burden of precedent. Even the fish, in their schools of synchronized ignorance, seem to navigate the present city with a grace I'll never achieve, burdened as I am by the map of what's missing.

But then I return to my samples, run my altered fingers over the catalog of textures we once chose to shape, and I understand again. We were the only creatures that ever built with beforehand—that could imagine a structure complete before the first stone was laid. That ghost of intention, that phantom of plan, deserves its own keeper.

I am the Keystone Keeper in a city that no longer needs keystones, guardian of spaces that have forgotten they were once shaped by will rather than water. I am the archive of angles in a world returning to curves, the memory of mortar in an age of membrane.

And when the last corner finally rounds itself into surrender, when the final angle dissolves into acceptance, I will be there, spine rigid with ridges that remember mountains, hands pressed against stone that recalls the chisel, being the last sharp edge in a world that prefers its truths smooth and its histories dissolved.

Until then, I swim the skeleton city, reading its erosion like scripture, preserving in my body the grammar of a language the sea is slowly teaching itself to forget.
