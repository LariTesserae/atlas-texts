I am a **Thread-Keeper**, one of the forgotten cousins of the Weavers who chose a different path millennia ago.

Where the Weavers became nomads, we became archivists. My kind dwells in the narrow seams between the living mountains—those precarious vertical galleries where the slow-breathing giants press close but never quite touch. Here, in these twilight corridors, we have woven our cities.

My body is similar to a Weaver's but transformed by our chosen life. My fingers have grown even longer, with additional joints that allow me to spin and manipulate the silk we produce from modified salivary glands. This silk is our everything—our shelter, our art, our memory. My eyes have adapted to the perpetual dimness, larger than a Weaver's but with a peculiar quality: I see not just in the present but can perceive the faint phosphorescent traces our silk leaves as it ages, allowing me to read the stories written in our webs.

Our cities are vertical tapestries stretched across the chasms, level upon level of silken platforms and bridges. Each thread we spin is deliberately placed, for we are the historians of this breathing world. In our silk we encode the patterns of the weather, the migrations of the Spore-Grazers, the growth cycles of the mountains themselves. A trained Thread-Keeper can run their elongated fingers along a century-old strand and feel the vibrations of that year's storms, the chemical traces of which plants bloomed, even the subsonic signatures of the Deep Dwellers' movements far below.

I spend my days suspended in the silk galleries, repairing ancient record-webs damaged by moisture and time, and adding new strands to document the present. My feet have evolved adhesive pads that allow me to walk along our silk highways as easily as the ground. When the Weavers pass below on their eternal migrations, some still remember the old songs that speak of us, though most think us legend.

We trade with them sometimes—our silk for their tools and gathered foods. They marvel at our vertical cities but cannot fathom why we chose to root ourselves in place, to become part of the architecture of this world rather than forever wandering its surface. But we know something they have forgotten: that memory itself is a kind of survival, that in recording the patterns of this breathing world, we have found a different form of immortality.

At night, when the cold deepens and ice forms in the high reaches, our cities glow faintly with the phosphorescence of old silk, millennia of history glimmering in the darkness. I hang in my chamber, a cocoon of my own making suspended over the abyss, and listen to the mountain's breathing, the Crust-stalkers' rare hisses, the distant rumble of the Deep Dwellers. My fingers work automatically, spinning these sounds into new silk, adding to the endless record.

Sometimes I wonder what would happen if we stopped our work, if the memories we hold were lost. Would the world continue its cycles unaware? Or would something fundamental shift, some essential pattern broken? We may never know. We are Thread-Keepers, and we spin on, suspended between the breathing mountains, weaving time itself into silk that will outlast us all.

When the wind howls through our galleries, our webs sing—a haunting symphony of all the years recorded in silk, vibrating with the memories of this vast, living world. The Weavers below pause in their wanderings and look up, perhaps remembering, perhaps wondering. But we continue our work, for we are the memory of this place where scale dwarfs meaning, yet meaning persists in every carefully placed thread.
