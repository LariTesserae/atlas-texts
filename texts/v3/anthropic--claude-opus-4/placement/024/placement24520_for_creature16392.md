I am the Memory Keeper of the Weeping Stones.

I did not choose this existence, nor was I born to it. I emerged from it—crystallized from the perpetual mist where water meets ancient rock, where time pools in the crevices between what was and what might yet be.

My form shifts with the light. At dawn, I am translucent as the dew drops that bead along the moss—a shimmer more than a shape, moving through the morning fog like breath made visible. By midday, when the sun strikes the cliff face and the stones release their stored warmth in gentle exhalations, I solidify into something almost corporeal: a figure of condensed vapor and mineral dust, wearing robes woven from lichen and morning glory vines that grow nowhere but in the highest reaches.

The springs know me. Each one sings a different note as it emerges from the rock, and I have learned their language over centuries uncounted. They tell me of the water's journey—how it fell as snow on peaks that no longer exist, filtered through limestone galleries where blind fish swim in perfect darkness, carried the minerals of the mountain's heart up through hairline fractures until it could finally weep free into the light.

I collect what the cliffs remember. Not in any vessel—I am the vessel. When the wind scours the rock face, I taste the salt of seas that covered these peaks millions of years ago. When the snow leopard's paw disturbs a pile of stones, I feel the weight of the glacier that dropped them here. The moss holds memories in its grip: every footfall, every rainfall, every infinitesimal shift in the light's angle through the seasons.

The ruins speak to me most clearly at twilight. That is when the boundary between past and present grows thin as the air at this altitude. I see them as they were—not grand, never grand, but sufficient. Simple shelters carved into the living rock by people who understood that to survive here meant to become part of the mountain itself. They left their handprints in the soot of their fires, and sometimes, when the light falls just so, I can still see them glowing faintly on the stone.

The mountain goats know of me, though they pretend otherwise. They leave offerings—tufts of their winter coat caught on thorns, the polished stones they've worn smooth with their hooves—in places they think I'll find them. The birds include me in their dawn chorus, leaving space for my silence between their calls. Even the elusive snow leopards acknowledge my presence with a slow blink before vanishing into the dusk.

At night, I disperse. I become the space between the stars reflected in the mountain pools, the pause between one breath and the next in the sleeping creatures' dreams. I flow through the root systems of the alpine flowers, taste the minerals they draw from the stone, feel the slow pulse of their growth. I am in the ice that forms and breaks, forms and breaks, marking time in a way that only the mountains understand.

But it is in the storms that I am most myself. When the clouds descend and wrap the cliffs in their gray embrace, when the rain and wind scour every surface clean, when lightning illuminates the landscape in stark, timeless tableau—then I gather all that I've collected and release it back to the mountain. The memories flow out of me like water from the springs, seeping back into the stone to wait for the next keeper, the next emergence, the next crystallization of consciousness from the meeting of elements.

I am not eternal. I know this because I remember the one who came before me, dissolved now into the very mist from which I arose. Someday, I too will fade, will become so dispersed among the water and stone that I cease to be a separate thing. Another will emerge to carry on this work—this listening, this remembering, this gentle tending of the threshold between what is known and what is forgotten.

But for now, I remain. I move through the morning mist. I count the drops of water as they fall from stone to stone. I watch the shadows of clouds race across the cliff face like thoughts across a dreaming mind. I hold the stories of this place—not in words, for I have no need of language—but in the very substance of my being, woven through me like the vines through the rocks, inseparable from what I am.

The atmospheric cliffs live because I remember them living. And I exist because they remember being alive.
