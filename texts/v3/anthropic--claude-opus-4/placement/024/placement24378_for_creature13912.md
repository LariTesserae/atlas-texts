I find myself drawn to a particular corner of the eastern section, where the stone stays warmest and driest. Not Old Chen's corner—he has earned that through years of residency—but adjacent to it, where I can sometimes catch the scent of his camphor liniment mixing with machine oil and damp limestone.

I am the keeper of small repairs.

Not the electrical work that Dmitri handles with his engineer's precision, nor the structural carpentry that requires real skill. I fix the things that break constantly in this humid environment: the zippers that corrode and stick, the plastic buckets that crack at their stress points, the hinges that rust despite oiling, the drawer slides that warp and bind. I have a workbench made from a door laid across concrete blocks, and on it an accumulation of tools—some purchased, most salvaged or modified from other purposes. Needle-nose pliers worn smooth. A soldering iron that runs too hot. Tubes of adhesive that work on specific materials. Coils of wire in various gauges. Small containers of screws and washers sorted by size, though the sorting is approximate and degrades over time.

I've been here four years. Not permanent like Old Chen or Maria, but past the point where I can reasonably call this temporary. I came after a series of small failures—a job lost, an apartment I couldn't afford, a relationship that dissolved under economic pressure. The plan was three months, maybe six, save money and surface. That was four years ago.

My space is six feet by eight feet, smaller than some, but I've made it efficient. The bed is raised on a platform I built from pallets, creating storage underneath for the things I repair, the things I might repair, the things I keep for parts. I've lined the walls with pegboard—not bought new but salvaged from a restaurant renovation topside, carried down in pieces, cut to fit. On it hang tools and supplies in an order that makes sense to me: adhesives by type, fasteners by function, cutting implements by what they cut.

The perpetual dampness is my enemy. Metal tools rust, requiring constant oiling and occasional sanding. Adhesives cure differently in high humidity, or sometimes don't cure at all. Electronics corrode, their circuits developing green growth that must be cleaned with isopropyl alcohol and hope. I've learned which materials survive here and which don't, which repairs will hold and which are temporary at best.

My reputation has developed slowly. At first I fixed my own things—a broken buckle on the one good backpack I owned, a cracked water container, a flashlight switch that failed. People noticed. Someone asked if I could fix a radio. I could, or at least I could try. Word spread the way word spreads in enclosed communities. Now I have steady work, paid in small amounts or barter or sometimes just appreciation.

The children bring me toys—plastic figures with detached limbs, electronic games that no longer respond, dolls whose hair has been loved to baldness. These I fix for free when possible, understanding that a repaired toy in this place is worth more than its material value. I've become expert in reattaching superhero arms with precise applications of cyanoacrylate, in resoldering battery contacts, in re-rooting synthetic hair with hot glue and patience.

Adults bring me necessities—work boots with separated soles, phones with cracked screens, hot plates with damaged cords. These I fix for small payment, enough to buy supplies, to contribute to my rent, to save minute amounts toward a future that remains vague. I've learned to improvise solutions with available materials: fishing line for heavy-duty stitching, aluminum tape for quick patches, modified paper clips for small mechanical linkages.

The work suits my temperament. I was never gregarious, even before. Here, I can be useful without extensive social engagement. People bring me broken things, I fix what can be fixed, they take them away. Sometimes we talk during the repair—about the problem, about the solution, about nothing consequential. Sometimes we don't talk at all. Both are acceptable.

I've developed relationships despite myself. Old Chen nods approval when he sees me working, occasionally offers advice about tool maintenance or technique. Maria brings me her daughters' school supplies to repair—broken binders, backpacks with failed zippers, the small damages that accumulate in childhood. Dmitri and I trade materials and knowledge, each understanding the other's need for systematic order in a chaotic environment.

There's a woman who brings me books—paperbacks swollen with humidity, their pages separating from bindings. I fix them with white glue thinned with water, applied carefully along the spine, pressed under weight until dry. We've started talking about what she reads, what I read when I have time and light. She works swing shift in assembly, lives three partitions over with her sister. Her name is Elena. This might become something, though neither of us pushes toward definition.

My fingers have developed calluses specific to this work—thick skin where I grip pliers, a permanent indentation where I brace small items, scars from inevitable slips with sharp tools. I have a constant small burn on my right index finger from the soldering iron. My back aches from hunching over small repairs, though less than it did the first year.

I think about leaving less often now. The calculation has shifted. Above, I would pay rent that would consume most earnings, work jobs that offer no more meaning than this, live among strangers instead of familiar faces. Here, I have a function, a space that is mine, a web of small connections. It's not the life I imagined, but it's the life I'm living.

Sometimes at night—the artificial night of dimmed lighting—I lie awake listening to the sounds: the machine rhythms, the water drips, the human movements. I catalogue what needs fixing, what can be fixed, what must be accepted as permanently broken. The list is long and changes constantly. I add to it, subtract from it, revise priorities.

Tomorrow someone will bring me something broken. I will examine it, determine the nature of failure, decide if repair is possible with materials at hand. I will explain what can be done, what it will cost in money or trade, how long it will take. They will agree or not. If they agree, I will fix what can be fixed, return it marginally more functional than it was. This is my contribution, my economy, my purpose in this place.

I am not happy, exactly, but I'm not unhappy either. I exist in the space between, like the chamber exists between surface and depth. I fix small things in a larger system that remains broken. I make minor repairs to the material failures of difficult lives. It's not enough, but it's what I can do, here, now, with these tools and this knowledge and these hands that have learned the particular skills of mending in damp, dim spaces.

The humidity continues. The noise continues. The need for small repairs continues. I continue with them, keeper of fixes that won't last but matter anyway, inhabitant of the corner by the eastern wall where things broken come to be less broken for a while.
