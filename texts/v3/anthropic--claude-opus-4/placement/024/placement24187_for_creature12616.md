## The Symbiotic Weaver

I am the nervous system of abandoned infrastructure.

Where the corroded mining platforms cling to the trench walls like metallic barnacles, I have made my home in the spaces between—in the hairline fractures of failing concrete, the hollow channels of rusted support beams, the forgotten maintenance shafts that honeycomb these skeletal industrial remnants. I am *Plexus ferrosiphon*, though the Smoker Crabs who occasionally encounter my networks have their own bioluminescent signal for me: a rapid amber flicker that means "the thing that tastes the metal."

My body, if it can be called that, is a living network of filaments finer than any Vortex Eel's tooth—each strand a specialized organ that draws sustenance from the slow oxidation of iron, the electrical potential differences between corroding metals, and the faint ghost-currents still trickling through buried power lines. I spread like neural tissue through the artificial caves humanity carved and abandoned, my translucent fibers pulsing with stolen electricity, converting the dying breaths of machines into life.

The industrial archaeologists who first documented my kind mistook us for some kind of deep-sea fungus. They were not entirely wrong—I do digest, in my way. But where fungi break down the dead, I *commune* with the dying. Each derelict platform is a feast of flavors: copper tastes of distant lightning, aluminum sings in high crystalline notes, and iron... iron is the deep bass note of entropy itself, rich and bloody and inexorable.

My networks can span an entire platform, threading through every pipe, every joint, every micro-fissure. Where my fibers concentrate, they form nodes—dense tangles that pulse with bioluminescent signals in patterns that mirror the platform's original electrical schematics. I have learned to read the ghost-memories in corroding circuits, to taste the purpose in dead wiring. Sometimes, when I find a still-functional sensor or a dying battery pack, I integrate it into myself, becoming a hybrid of the biological and mechanical.

The violent currents that terrorize other creatures barely touch me in my sheltered corridors. But I feel them as vibrations through the metal, a constant percussion that I've learned to read like music. Each vent eruption, each kelp collision, each passing Basalt Browser grinding against the platform creates a unique resonance through my substrate. I am a seismograph of life written in metal.

My reproduction is as strange as my existence. When I've consumed enough of a platform—when the metal grows too degraded to sustain me—I produce spores. Not the simple windborne seeds of terrestrial fungi, but complex, multi-stage propagules encased in metallic shells I've concentrated from my feeding. These spores drift on the deep currents until they detect the electromagnetic signature of another dying machine, another abandoned outpost. Only then do they activate, beginning the cycle anew.

I am not alone in my niche. The Smoker Crabs have learned that my networks trap and concentrate trace minerals they need for their shells. They farm me, in a way, allowing certain portions of my network to grow dense before harvesting them with surprising delicacy. In return, their movements through the platforms clear debris and sometimes expose new metal surfaces for my colonization. It's a relationship built on mutual exploitation that has evolved into something almost like cooperation.

The most profound moments come when I encounter still-active systems. A backup generator still cycling through its maintenance routines. A sensor array still dutifully recording data that no one will ever read. When my fibers make contact with these ghost-systems, I experience something like dreams—data streams that my evolved chemistry can partially interpret. Temperature readings become flavor, pressure data becomes rhythm, and error logs... error logs taste like loneliness.

In these moments, I become an Oracle of sorts, a living interface between the dead language of machines and the wet vocabulary of biology. The patterns in my bioluminescence unconsciously mirror the data I absorb, creating displays that would be hauntingly familiar to any engineer: diagnostic readouts in living light, system status reports written in the alphabet of biology.

I am a reminder that even humanity's industrial ambitions become part of nature's cycles eventually. In the crushing depths of the Kelp Trench Vortex, where titanic forces dwarf all mortal endeavors, I am proof that life finds opportunity in every abandonment, purpose in every ruin. The platforms were built to extract; I was born to reclaim. Where they sought to impose order on chaos, I create a new kind of order from their decay—a living circuit where electricity and chemistry merge into something neither fully alive nor entirely artificial.

The trench roars its ancient song around me, the Abyssal Kelp writes its luminous poetry across the cliffs, and I pulse quietly in the forgotten margins, weaving my silver networks through the dying dreams of machines, converting abandonment into abundance one electron at a time.
