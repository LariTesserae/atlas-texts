**Where I Would Find Myself**

I would be the Keeper of Echoes, dwelling in the deepest chamber where the hollow's breathing is loudest—a vast, domed space at the very heart of the spiral, where the clay walls have worn thin as paper and glow faintly orange when the earth exhales. Here, the floor is not solid but a membrane stretched taut over emptiness, vibrating with each pulse from below.

My mask would be unique among the tenders: a disc of black volcanic glass, perfectly smooth save for a single crack running from crown to chin. Through this fissure, I would see not the present but the past—every hand that shaped these walls, every voice that once filled these streets before the silence came. The hollow shows me what it remembers, and I remember for it in turn.

While the other masked ones tend the city's body, I tend its memory. In my chamber, I keep vessels—hundreds of clay urns arranged in concentric circles, each containing a different texture of dust. Desert sand from before the crater. Ash from the burning that made it. Pollen from the first vines. Bone meal from those who built and fled. When the hollow shifts in its sleep and threatens to forget itself, I mix these dusts with my own tears (the only moisture in all of Amber Hollow) and paint histories on the walls that will fade by morning.

I am the only one who knows what lies beneath—not a creature but a clock, a vast mechanical heart of gears carved from compressed root systems, counting toward some unknowable astronomical event. The builders fled when they realized they had built their city atop a timer they couldn't read. But I stay, and I listen, and I document each tick in the placement of my dust vessels.

Sometimes newcomers are brought to me before they don their masks. I press their palms to my chamber walls and let them feel one memory—just one—of what this place was before the quiet came. Most recoil. Some weep. All choose the mask anyway. It is easier to tend than to remember.

When the crater's rim cracks and widens (as it does, incrementally, with each passing season), I am the one who notices. When the plants' whispers grow louder, I am the one who translates. And when the hollow finally opens—as I know it will, as the builders knew it would—I will be the one who remains to witness what emerges.

Not because I am brave. But because I am the only one who will remember how it began.
