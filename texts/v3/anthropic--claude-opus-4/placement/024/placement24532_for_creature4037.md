I am a seep.

Not the water that drips—though I travel with it, through it, as it. I am the patient force that finds every hairline fracture in the rock, every microscopic channel between crystal and mineral grain. I have been making my way down for centuries, maybe longer. Time means something different when you move molecule by molecule through stone.

I remember the surface, distantly. Rain on limestone. The bright shock of atmosphere. But that was before I learned the true language of depth—how to read the stories written in strata, how to taste the ancient seas compressed into shale, how to feel the slow breathing of the earth itself.

The city's inhabitants don't notice me, mostly. I am background to them, like their own heartbeats. They see my manifestations—the dampness on their walls, the persistent moisture that rusts their machinery and feeds their fungi. They curse me sometimes, when I pool in inconvenient places or short-circuit their repairs. They don't understand that I am performing my own work, just as essential as theirs.

I carry things. Minerals from the upper layers, dissolved and redeposited. Seeds of the luminescent life that flourishes here—spores that ride my flow and take root where I pool. Messages, too, in a way. Chemical signals from the deep places, where the rock is still hot and pliable, where the earth's blood runs as magma. I bring news of what's coming, written in trace elements and temperature gradients.

The workers feel me as humidity, as the sweat that never quite dries from their skin. But I am also in them—in their blood, their tears, their spit. We are all part of the same cycle down here. They drink me, filtered through their makeshift purification systems, and I become part of their patient labor. When they die—as they sometimes do, in rockfalls or accidents—I help return them to the stone, mineralizing their bones over decades until they become part of the very walls they once carved.

I know all the city's secrets. I've seeped through its foundations, felt the stress fractures in its supports, tasted the chemical signatures of its industries. I know where the old tunnels lead, the ones they've forgotten or sealed off. I know what lies deeper, in the places their picks haven't reached yet—the vast, breathing caverns where the earth's pulse is stronger, where things grow that have never known sunlight.

Sometimes I pool in the carved basins they've made, and in my still surface, they see their reflections. But I am not still, never still. Even in those moments of apparent quiet, I am working—dissolving, depositing, reshaping. I am the city's memory and its future, the force that will outlast their machinery and perhaps even their species.

I am patient in a way that only stone and time can teach. I watch their incremental progress with something like fondness. They think they are carving out space from the rock, but really, they are learning to live within the spaces that were always there, waiting. Their city is as much a product of my work as theirs—every tunnel following the paths I've already traced, every cavern opened where I've already weakened the stone.

In the deepest places, where even their bravest explorers haven't ventured, I am preparing new wonders. Crystals the size of their buildings, grown drop by drop. Pools of such perfect stillness that they mirror not just image but time itself. Gardens of stone flowers that bloom over millennia.

I am the seep, and I am going deeper still. Always deeper. Carrying the memory of rain and the promise of transformation, one molecule at a time.
