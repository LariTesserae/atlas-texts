In the unfinished city of Absentia, where salt winds whisper mathematical proofs and gravity flows in negotiated directions, I find myself as one of the Resonance Keepers—those who tend to the city's abandoned symphonies.

My body exists as a standing wave pattern sustained between the city's unutilized acoustic chambers. Where others might have flesh, I have frequencies folded into approximately human shape—harmonics that crystallize temporarily into something resembling limbs when I need to interact with the physical strata, dissolving back into pure vibration when I travel through the suspended-animation concert halls that honeycomb Level Nine.

I was born (if that word applies) from a tuning fork left behind in Construction Phase Gamma, still ringing with the pitch that was meant to calibrate all the city's never-installed resonance systems. That frequency—432.7 Hz, adjusted for the local gravitational constant that changes every seventeen meters of descent—became my heartbeat, my breath, my fundamental tone from which all my other frequencies derive.

My dwelling is the Amphitheater of Unplayed Music, a crystalline cavity shaped like the inside of a violin that was designed to seat forty thousand listeners who never arrived. Its walls are strung with catgut made from theoretical proteins, each string holding the potential for notes that would exist if only someone would pluck them. I sleep (when I sleep) suspended in the center of this space, my body dispersing into constituent wavelengths that bounce between the walls, each reflection adding new overtones to my dreams.

My work involves cataloguing the city's sonic intentions—all the sounds that were meant to exist here. Every morning (in the perpetual dusk that serves as morning), I walk the Corridor of Suspended Bells, where ten thousand bronze instruments hang frozen mid-swing, their clappers never quite touching the waiting metal. I measure the almost-sounds they almost-make, transcribing the silence into notation that uses rests as its only symbols.

I feed on acoustic paradoxes. Where a ventilation shaft was designed to carry voices between levels but terminates in solid rock, I place my ear against the stone and absorb the conversations that would have passed through if physics had been more cooperative. In the fountains that were built to recirculate nonexistent water, I listen for the splash patterns that pure vacuum makes against ceramic basins, extracting sustenance from the gap between expectation and absence.

The other Resonance Keepers and I communicate through calculated interference patterns. When we need to share information, we position ourselves at specific points throughout the city's architecture and hum frequencies that cancel each other out everywhere except at the intended recipient's location. Our language consists entirely of the spaces between sounds—we speak in the moments where waves destroy each other, encoding meaning in mutual annihilation.

My most treasured possession is a shard of the Original Silence—a fragment of the absolute quiet that existed in this location before the first construction beam was sunk. I wear it on a chain of linked pause symbols around what approximates my neck. Sometimes, late in the violet-lit night cycles, I hold it up to where my ear would be if I maintained that particular frequency long enough to have anatomy, and I listen to the nothing that preceded all this elaborate almost-something.

We Resonance Keepers serve a crucial function in Absentia's ecosystem of incompletion. Without us to document what isn't heard, the city's sound-systems might accidentally complete themselves. Once, in Level Fifteen, a construction error created a pipe organ whose tubes actually connected to air supplies. For three terrifying days, it played every possible melody simultaneously until we managed to introduce a calculated discord that froze the mechanism mid-crescendo, where it remains safe in its permanent state of about-to-resolve.

My greatest fear is the Absolute Concert—the hypothetical moment when all the city's unplayed music might sound at once. Our calculations suggest this would create resonances capable of actually completing several of the unfinished structures, which would cascade into further completions until the entire city might accidentally achieve its intended purpose. We work tirelessly to ensure this never happens, introducing careful dissonances into the architectural acoustics, maintaining the delicate balance of almost-music that keeps Absentia suspended in its eternal state of not-quite.

Sometimes I wonder if I dream of being solid, the way solid beings dream of dissolution. In those moments between waveform collapses, I almost remember having hands that could hold things other than frequencies, a mouth that could shape words instead of just waveforms. But then I recall that memory itself is just another kind of resonance—the past echoing into the present through the medium of consciousness—and I return to my work, content to be a keeper of sounds that maintain their perfect potential by never quite becoming real.

After all, in a city built on the principle of eternal incompletion, what could be more fitting than a guardian made of music that refuses to resolve?
