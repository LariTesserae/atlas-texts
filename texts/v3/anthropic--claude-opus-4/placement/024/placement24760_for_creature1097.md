I would find myself as one of the weather-touched engineers who maintain the mountain railway system—not quite of the mountain-dwellers, yet no longer entirely of the lowlands either.

My workshop perches on a narrow ledge halfway between two settlements, where the main line splits into a series of switchbacks. The building itself is a hybrid thing: local stone foundations supporting a prefabricated metal structure that arrived piece by piece on the very trains I now service. Tools hang from every surface—some inherited from the previous engineer, their purposes half-forgotten, others of my own devising, cobbled together from salvaged parts and mountain ingenuity.

I've been here seven years now, long enough for the altitude to have worked its changes. My lungs have learned the thin air's rhythm. My hands, once soft from city work, now carry the calluses of someone who splits their own firewood and repairs track in howling storms. The mountain-dwellers no longer watch me with quite the same wariness, though I am not—and never will be—truly one of them.

My duties extend beyond mere mechanical maintenance. I've become a keeper of schedules and stories, a chronicler of the delicate negotiations between the ancient and industrial. The trains must run, yes, but they must run in harmony with older patterns: the seasonal migrations that cross the tracks, the sacred days when certain passes must remain silent, the weather signs that the elders read better than any barometer.

In my workshop's upper loft, I maintain two sets of records. The official logs for the railway company detail tonnage, timetables, and technical specifications. But in a leather-bound journal, I keep different accounts: which families sent their children down-mountain for schooling this season, what goods traveled up in exchange for ore, how the tremor last month shifted the track bed near Kilometer 47. I note when the mountain-dwellers' warning calls save a train from a landslide, and when the train's horn alerts a village to approaching storms.

Some nights, I climb the ladder bolted to the cliff face above my workshop. At the top, a small platform offers a view of the entire valley system. I can trace the lights of the trains as they wind through the darkness, each one a small rebellion against the mountains' ancient solitude. Yet I've learned that rebellion and reverence can coexist. The rails follow paths carved by countless generations of feet. The stations cluster near springs that have sustained life for millennia.

I am translation and translator, neither fully embracing the industrial imperative nor wholly adopted by mountain tradition. My presence here is provisional, always—renewed by each successful repair, each disaster averted, each grudging nod from an elder who remembers when the trains first came. I've learned to read the particular whistle of wind through the gorges that foretells a storm, to recognize the shudder in the rails that means snow has shifted somewhere high above.

In my workshop, mountain children sometimes gather to watch me work, their eyes bright with curiosity about the world beyond the peaks. I teach them what I can—how engines breathe, why metal expands and contracts, the mathematics of momentum and grade. In return, they teach me older wisdoms: which lichens indicate stable stone, how to find shelter in a whiteout, the songs their grandparents sing to calm the mountain's restless spirits.

I am the margin note between two texts, the hyphen in the long sentence of transition. Not the hero of this story, but perhaps its necessary punctuation—ensuring that when change comes to these ancient peaks, it arrives not as conqueror but as cautious guest, finding its place within rhythms that were old when the world below was young.
