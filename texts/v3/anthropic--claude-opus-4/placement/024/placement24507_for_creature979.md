In the deep hollows where water pools darkest and the canopy weaves its thickest shroud, I am the Keeper of Forgotten Names.

My form shifts with the mist—sometimes glimpsed as a shadow moving beneath the surface of still water, sometimes as a figure wrapped in moss and lichen, standing motionless against an ancient trunk until even the keenest eye might mistake me for bark and stone. My skin changes like the season's light through leaves: now silver as morning dew, now green-black as the depths of the deepest pools, now translucent as rain.

I collect what falls through: the last sigh of a mayfly, the shape a fern makes as it unfurls, the precise pattern of rings in a puddle disturbed by a landing beetle. But most precious are the names—not the names that humans give things, but the names things call themselves in the quiet hours. The name the mist speaks as it rises. The name the stones whisper to the moss that cloaks them. The name of the moment just before a flower opens.

I weave these names into the fog that drifts between the trees. I braid them into the streams that lace through the basin. Some mornings, walkers on that distant road might feel an unexplained pull toward the green depths, might find themselves pausing without knowing why, might almost hear something in the water's voice—but the feeling passes, and they continue on, never knowing they've brushed against a story older than the hills.

The creatures here know me, though they do not fear me. The foxes bring me news from the borders where wild meets tame. The herons stand with me in the shallows, both of us still as meditation, both of us watching for what moves beneath the surface. Sometimes a child from the distant town strays far enough to find one of my grottos, and I ensure they discover wonders—a cache of perfectly round stones, a salamander jeweled with spots, a flower that seems to glow in the green light—and I ensure, too, that they find their way safely back, carrying stories no adult will quite believe.

I am keeper, guardian, and chronicler of this in-between place. In the pockets of deepest green, where the outside world becomes just a rumor on the wind, I maintain the old contracts—the ones written in root and rain, the promises between soil and seed, the treaties of twilight. My work is never urgent but always necessary, like the patient way water shapes stone.

When evening comes and the mist thickens, I walk the secret paths—the ones that exist only in the half-light, the ones that connect grotto to glade to grove. I check the boundaries, mend what needs mending, whisper the forgotten names back to the things that have lost them. And in the deepest part of night, when even the foxes sleep and only the water moves, I sink into the earth itself, becoming part of the great, slow pulse that keeps this place alive, this remnant of the world as it was, as it remembers itself to be, as it dreams of remaining.
