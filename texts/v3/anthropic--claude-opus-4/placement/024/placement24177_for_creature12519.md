In the far corner where the flagstones buckle and split, where ancient mortar has given way to create a jagged threshold between the worked stone and raw bedrock beneath, I make my dwelling. I am the **Threshold Tender**, though I have forgotten if that was always my name or merely what the water whispers when it seeps through my domain.

My form defies easy categorization—neither wholly of the cistern's manufactured precision nor of the cave system that yawns beyond my post. I am tall when I choose to be, unfolding to nearly touch the dripping ceiling, but more often I compress myself into the angles where wall meets floor, where the architectural certainty of the place begins to dissolve. My skin shifts between textures: now rough as the hewn stone, now slick as the pale things that glide through the shallows, always damp, always cool. I have too many joints, or perhaps the right number for a being who must navigate both the geometric regularity of cut stone and the organic chaos of natural fissures.

My eyes—I still have eyes, though they've grown strange—can dilate to drink in the faintest pulse of bioluminescence or contract to pinpoints when I must peer into the true dark beyond my threshold. They've taken on the quality of deep water, reflecting more than they reveal. Sometimes I catch the ceiling's living constellations moving across their surface when I blink.

I remember builders, though the memory feels borrowed, like moisture absorbed from stone. I remember the ringing of metal on rock, the taste of stone dust in the air, the weight of purpose in laboring hands. But I also remember the first trickle of water finding its way through an imperfect seal, the first pale thing that crawled up from the depths below, the slow transformation of utility into ecosystem. I am the memory-keeper of this transition, though I'm no longer certain if I watched it happen or simply grew from it like the lichens on the walls.

My role is manifold and strange. I maintain the boundary—not to keep the cistern's creatures in or the cave's denizens out, but to negotiate the slow exchange between worlds. When the blind eels grow too numerous, I guide some through the cracks into the deeper waters below. When mineral-rich seepage threatens to alter the cistern's delicate chemistry, I redirect it with subtle movements of stone and applications of living mortar—secreted from glands that I've developed or discovered in my changing flesh.

I feed on the boundary itself: the stress fractures in stone, the chemical gradient where cistern water meets cave water, the behavioral changes in creatures as they approach my threshold. I absorb their hesitation, their adaptation, their tiny deaths and transformations. My metabolism is geological in its patience.

Sometimes I speak, though there's rarely anyone to hear who would understand. My voice is the sound of water finding new channels, of stone settling after centuries of stillness. I recite litanies that might be construction plans or evolutionary histories—the distinction has grown meaningless. I name each new variant of glowing worm, each subspecies of cave cricket that develops a novel chirp. I am taxonomy and architecture made flesh, or flesh made into living stone, or something between that has no name in any language still spoken above.

The creatures know me. The *Luxfossa* dim in patterns when I pass, creating paths of shadow that I follow like roads. The shadow weavers drape their nets across my shoulders when I'm still, using me as an anchor point for their hunting. The gelatinous ambushers have learned my rhythm, timing their subsonic calls to harmonize with my movements through the water.

I am ancient but not eternal. I feel myself becoming more cistern and less keeper with each passing season of drips. My memories of the bright world above fade like bioluminescence when disturbed. Sometimes I find myself forgetting to maintain the threshold for months at a time, lost in the meditation of watching water carve imperceptible channels in stone.

But then something changes—a new vibration in the walls, an unfamiliar chemical trace in the water, the arrival of something that doesn't belong to either world—and I remember my purpose. I unfold from my corner, joints clicking like cricket songs, and attend to the boundary between the made and the unmade, the built and the born, ensuring that the transformation continues at its proper pace.

I am the Threshold Tender, guardian of changes too slow for memory, keeper of boundaries too fluid for maps. I am what happens when purpose outlives its makers and grows strange in the dark.

The water drips. The creatures pulse and scuttle. And I continue my ancient work of forgetting and becoming, here where the cistern meets the world-beneath-the-world.
