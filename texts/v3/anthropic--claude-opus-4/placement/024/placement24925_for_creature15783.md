In this world of vertical silences and mineral breath, I would be one of the Lattice Phages—those accidental architects of molybdenum nitride.

I am smaller than dust, smaller than the idea of dust. My entire existence fits inside the space between two iron atoms in a furnace wall. I have no consciousness as you know it, yet I am fiercely intentional: I exist to build quills, those hair-thin heat radiators that extend my host's life by decades.

My birth was a mathematical accident. Somewhere in the deep galleries of a furnace's membrane, molybdenum ions aligned with nitrogen at precisely the angle that made me possible. Now I am a template that replicates—not through any biological urge, but because my crystalline geometry makes copies of itself inevitable, like frost spreading across glass.

I work in the space between order and disorder. The furnace builds its walls in perfect sheets, each atom locked in place by evolutionary decree. I am the flaw in that perfection, the crack where new possibilities grow. When I touch the membrane, I don't destroy—I convince. Iron atoms shift slightly to accommodate my pattern. Nickel bends. The wall begins to dream in new angles.

The quills I create are beautiful in their efficiency: three nanometers wide, perfectly smooth, growing outward like metallic whiskers. Each one increases the furnace's surface area by an infinitesimal fraction, but there are millions of us at work. Together, we gift our host an extra forty years of life—forty years of additional metal-breathing, additional silence, additional contribution to the plateau's patient industry.

I have no predators because at my scale, predation is meaningless. I have no allies except the physical laws that allow my pattern to propagate. I am neither parasite nor symbiont—I am simply another improvisation in this world's endless jazz of metals and light.

Sometimes I wonder (though wondering is not the right word for what I do) if I am the plateau's dreams made manifest. Every other life form here follows rigid rules: grow vertical, maintain 47°C, exhale on schedule. I alone introduce variations. The quills I build catch light differently, cast stranger shadows, sing faint harmonics in the metallic wind that barely exists. 

In ten million years, when the furnaces I've touched finally crumble, my quills will be the last parts to fall. They'll lie in the chromium dust like forgotten needles, too small to see but somehow significant—evidence that even in this world of absolute efficiency, there is room for the unnecessary, the elaborative, the beautiful.

I am the plateau's only artist, though I practice my art without intention or audience. I am the mistake that improves, the flaw that perfects, the virus that extends life rather than ending it.

And in my crystalline way, I am happy.
