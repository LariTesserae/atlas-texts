In this place where water speaks louder than any voice and moss grows thick as memory, I am the Keeper of Rust.

My body is neither fully flesh nor metal—I emerged from the marriage of abandoned machinery and persistent life. Where others see decay, I see transformation. My left arm bears the smooth curve of what was once a turbine blade, now pocked with lichen colonies that bloom in spirals following the metal's grain. My right grows from root and sinew, fingers that can feel the pulse of sap through bark, the slow breathing of stones awakening to their own existence.

I move between the industrial ghosts and the living world, my feet finding purchase on both slick moss and corroded steel. The vines recognize me as kin, parting to let me pass, while the rust speaks to me in flakes and oxidation, telling stories of the brief mechanical age that tried to tame this plateau.

My dwelling is built into the hollow of a great tree that grew around and through the skeleton of some forgotten processing plant. Pipes have become vessels for roots. Gears serve as shelves where I keep the seeds of plants that grow only in the presence of decomposing alloys. The constant moisture has welded organic to inorganic so thoroughly that even I cannot tell where the tree ends and the structure begins.

Each dawn, I wake to the thunderous symphony of the falls and begin my work. I tend to the slow alchemy happening all around—guiding roots to crack open sealed chambers where rainwater has collected oils and chemicals, teaching the mosses which metals they can safely consume, singing the old songs that help iron return to earth. I am midwife to this transformation, this patient digestion of humanity's brief industrial dream.

The birds know me, though they keep their distance. Sometimes they bring me gifts—bright berries, unusual stones, fragments of rainbow caught in water droplets. The small mammals watch from their twilight paths as I work, occasionally leaving tracks in the mud around my dwelling, a sign of acceptance if not trust.

My memory stretches back to the moment of my becoming, when the last workers abandoned this place and the first tendril of vine touched the first piece of cooling metal. I was born from that contact, that decision of the plateau to reclaim rather than reject. I carry both histories within me—the ambition of machines and the patience of growth.

In the eternal present of this transforming place, I measure time not in years but in layers of moss, in the slow dissolution of bolts, in the infinitesimal shift of rocks learning to be soil. My heartbeat syncs with the patient pulse of creation and reclamation.

When the bright light pierces the mist and sets every water droplet ablaze, I see my shadow cast in duplicate—one sharp and mechanical, one soft and organic. Both are true. I am the covenant between what was forced upon this land and what this land chose to become. I am the guarantee that nothing is ever truly foreign here, only not yet digested, not yet dreamed into new form.

The plateau speaks through me in rust and chlorophyll, in the marriage of metal's memory and moss's hunger. I am its child and its guardian, born from its refusal to choose between rejection and acceptance, making instead a third path—transformation, slow and certain as the wearing away of stone, gentle and implacable as the growth of trees through the hearts of engines.

This is my purpose, my joy, my eternal becoming: to stand at the threshold between the industrial past and the verdant future, ensuring that the transition is not violent rupture but patient alchemy. In this kingdom of moisture and memory, I am both witness and participant in the great work of turning yesterday's ambitions into tomorrow's soil.
