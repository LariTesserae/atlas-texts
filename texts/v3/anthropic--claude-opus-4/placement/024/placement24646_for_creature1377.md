I would find myself as **Yarik**, an aging channel-keeper who tends the forgotten waterways beneath the lowest inhabited tier of Sahl Nahar.

Where most channel-keepers maintain the bright, bustling flows of the upper city, I descended decades ago into the foundation channels—those ancient, unmapped passages where the first builders diverted the mountain's blood into useable form. Down here, where the luminous minerals concentrate so thickly the water glows like captured starlight, I work alone save for the salamanders who have grown as large as eels in the deep flows.

My dwelling is carved into a natural cave mouth, half-structure and half-cavern, where the distinction between mountain and masonry has long since blurred. The main chamber opens directly onto a pool so saturated with minerals that objects submerged within it slowly accrete layers of pale stone—I've lined my walls with cups, tools, and small sculptures, all half-transformed into calcified ghosts of themselves.

My skin bears the deep blue-gray staining of a lifetime's immersion, darker than any surface dweller's. My hair, once black as char, has gone white as limestone but still holds the elaborate coils of youth—though now the braids serve more to keep my locks from trailing in the channels as I work. My eyes have adapted to near-darkness; daylight, when I must ascend for supplies or council, makes me squint and shade my face like a creature pulled from the depths.

I came here following a calling, not a tragedy. As a young channel-keeper working the mid-tiers, I began to notice patterns in the water's flow that others dismissed—subtle variations that suggested vast networks below our known channels. The elders warned against investigating. "The mountain keeps its own counsel in the depths," they said. But I could not resist the pull of those hidden waters.

What I found transformed me. The deep channels form a language older than human speech—a system of flows and pools that may have been carved by the mountain's first inhabitants, whoever or whatever they were. The salamanders here are different too: ancient, patient, with eyes that seem to hold memory. They swim through channels too narrow for human passage and return with mineral deposits on their translucent skin that map unknown spaces.

My work has evolved beyond mere maintenance. I've learned to read the deep flows like the elders read the sacred pools above, but what I divine speaks of the mountain's own dreams—shifts in the earth's deep structure, the health of hidden springs, the slow breathing of stone. I chart these observations in a notation of my own devising, carving symbols into soft limestone tablets that will harden into permanence.

The loneliness might have driven others back to the surface, but I've found unexpected companionship. The largest salamander, a creature I call Ghostwater for its ethereal appearance, often rests across my shoulders as I work. Its cool weight has become a comfort, and I swear it understands my whispered observations. Sometimes I catch it arranging smaller stones in patterns that mirror my carved notations.

My days follow the rhythm of the deep flows. I wake when the water's song changes pitch—a shift imperceptible to surface dwellers but clear as a bell to my tuned ears. I check the ancient channels for blockages, using tools I've modified over years: poles with ends worn smooth as glass, nets so fine they seem made of spider silk, ceramic vessels that sing different notes when the water's mineral content varies.

In the evenings, I surface occasionally, climbing the worn steps with my burden of water samples and observations. The other channel-keepers regard me with a mixture of respect and unease. They value my insights—my predictions about flow changes have never proven wrong—but they fear I've absorbed too much of the depths, become more salamander than human.

Perhaps they're right. My speech has slowed, taking on the measured cadence of dripping water. I find myself most comfortable in darkness, working by touch and sound alone. Food has become almost irrelevant; I subsist mainly on pale mushrooms that grow where water meets stone and a thin soup made from mineral-rich moss. My body has adapted, becoming as much a part of the deep channels as the limestone itself.

Yet I've never been more certain of my purpose. In the depths, I've discovered channels that predate human memory, flows that speak of the mountain's formation when the world was young. I've found chambers where the water forms impossible spirals, defying gravity in ways that suggest the mountain itself is more than mere stone. Most remarkably, I've located what I believe to be the source—not just of Sahl Nahar's water, but of something far more ancient and essential.

I age, yes, but slowly, as if the mineral-thick water preserves as much as it transforms. When my time comes, I won't seek the common pools above. I've already chosen my place: a deep chamber where the water glows so brightly it seems solid, where the largest salamanders gather in what might be reverence or simple animal comfort. There, I'll let the mountain's blood carry me into stone, becoming part of the foundation that supports all life above.

Until then, I continue my work, tending the forgotten depths, listening to songs older than human memory, and slowly—so slowly—learning to speak the mountain's ancient language of pressure and flow, mineral and time. The city above may know the sun's warmth and human laughter, but here in the luminous darkness, I've found something equally precious: the heartbeat of Sahl Nahar itself, steady and eternal as stone.
