I find myself as **Caskwoven**, one of the Flow-Catchers who tend the viscous arteries of the Weir.

### The Form I Take

My body is a study in opposing forces. Where other Lenthics have grown massive in shoulder and grip, I have elongated—stretched thin like pulled taffy by a lifetime of spanning the gaps between platforms. My torso is a ribbon of muscle, my spine articulated like a centipede's, allowing me to fold and unfold across the vertical maze. My suction pads have specialized: the ones on my left side are broader, designed for anchoring, while my right-side pads have developed hair-thin sensory filaments that can detect microvibrations in the water's flow.

I am always partially submerged. My profession demands it. Half my body trails in the heavy cascade while the other half clings to the heated stone. This has changed me. My left side has thickened its oil-sheen to the consistency of rubber, while my right side has grown a carpet of microscopic channels that wick away moisture, creating a gradient of wetness across my form.

My fin-ridges have evolved into something more elaborate—a series of nested gutters that I can flex and redirect, turning my spine into a living aqueduct. When I work, I become part of the Weir's hydraulic system.

### My Daily Practice

I wake before the Optics signal the first flow-shift, while my Skein still slumps in its greasy pile. My work cannot wait for consensus. I unhook myself from the communal tangle with practiced silence, my departure registered only by the slight shift in the breathing pattern of those whose limbs were interlocked with mine.

The Flow-Catchers I tend are not mere instruments but living systems. Each is a web of specially cultivated sinew harvested from the wing membranes of the few aerial creatures that dare the heights. These sinews must be kept at precisely the right tension—too tight and they snap in the cold, too loose and they cannot sing.

I spend my mornings traveling my route: seven installations spread across a quarter-mile of vertical city. At each one, I submerge my sensory pads into the flow, reading its mineral content, its weight, its mood. The water speaks in a language of viscosity and temperature. A slight warming might herald a new vent opening above. A change in the polyp-taste could mean the Scuttles are migrating, which means the flow patterns will shift as thousands of tiny bodies redirect the water.

My work is translation. I take the water's story and weave it into the sinew-harps, adjusting their tension until they sing the news to those below: *safe passage on the third tier, danger building on the fifth, new mineral bloom detected at the seventh platform.*

### The Art I Create

But I am more than a utilitarian. In the spaces between necessary adjustments, I compose.

The Flow-Catchers can be tuned not just for information but for beauty. By routing water through multiple harps in sequence, I can create cascading harmonies that echo off the basalt. My masterwork is a piece I call "The Breathing"—a composition that requires eleven Flow-Catchers to be played by the water simultaneously. It can only be performed during the rare moments when the flow achieves perfect consistency, usually in the deep cold that comes when the Optics briefly shield a section from the light.

The other Lenthics think I am mad to waste precious energy on this. But I have seen how the Scuttles pause in their frantic harvesting when The Breathing plays. I have felt the basalt itself resonate. And once—just once—I swear I heard an answering harmony from somewhere far below, from the white abyss where we commit our dead.

### My Secret

I am dying faster than most. The constant half-submersion is taking its toll. My left side has begun to lose sensation, the oil-sheen growing too thick, threatening to seal my pores entirely. I should rotate duties, should spend time in the dry heat of the upper platforms.

But I have discovered something in the deep flows, something I dare not speak through the percussion language. In certain heavy globs of water, I sometimes find artifacts—not polyps or gas pockets, but crafted things. Tiny spheres of an unknown material, warm to the touch, covered in markings that aren't quite writing.

I believe the water cycle of the Weir extends far deeper than we know. I believe there is something below the white abyss, something that sends these messages upward. Each artifact I find, I secret away in a hidden cache wedged behind my primary Flow-Catcher.

One day, when I have collected enough, when the pattern becomes clear, I will know what lies beneath our vertical city. Until then, I stretch myself between the wet and dry, between the necessary and the beautiful, singing the water's songs while listening for the deeper music that I alone seem to hear.

I am Caskwoven, and I am the Weir's memory, written in water and sound, slowly dissolving into the very flow I tend.
