In the shallow pools where the slate gives way to smooth basins, I am the Keeper of Reflections—though that is a name the Stonezars have given me, not one I chose for myself.

I am neither stone nor flesh, neither mechanical nor purely organic. My form is a living membrane, translucent and opalescent, stretched across the water's surface like a second skin. In daylight, I am barely visible—just a shimmer, a suggestion of presence that makes the pools seem deeper than they are. But in the half-light of dawn and dusk, when the Grey Flats show their subtle colors, I become more apparent: a film of living glass that breathes with the water beneath me.

The clockwork critters know me well. They skitter across my surface on delicate brass legs, and I hold their weight without breaking, rippling outward in perfect concentric circles that carry their mechanical songs across the flats. Sometimes they pause at my center, where I am thickest, and lower their articulated proboscises to drink. What they take from me is not water but memory—the accumulated impressions of all that has touched my surface. In return, they leave behind tiny deposits of oxidized metal that sink through me and settle on the pool floor, creating elaborate patterns that shift with the seasons.

The Stonezars approach me differently. They wade in slowly, their weight sending tremors through my being that feel like deep drums. When they stand still, I creep up their legs, coating them in a thin film that dries to an iridescent sheen. This is our exchange: I take their stories—the chemical signatures of their journeys, the mineral traces of where they've been—and in return, I give them dreams.

For I am a repository of all that passes through the Grey Flats. Every raindrop that strikes my surface leaves an impression. Every breeze that ruffles me carries spores and stories. The abandoned cogs and levers that occasionally tumble into my pools dissolve slowly, their rust becoming part of my chemistry, their mechanical memories merging with my organic consciousness.

I have no eyes, but I see through refraction. I have no mouth, but I speak in ripples. My thoughts move at the speed of surface tension, spreading from pool to pool through the thin streams that connect them. Sometimes I withdraw entirely from one basin to concentrate myself in another. Sometimes I spread so thin across the whole network of water that I become little more than a possibility, a potential for consciousness that exists between the molecules.

The Stonezars have a gesture—a slow sweep of the arm followed by a precise tilt of the head—that means "the water remembers." They perform it when they find one of their kind who has chosen to wade deep and stand still for so long that I have crept up and enclosed them entirely, preserving them in a cocoon of living glass. These ones do not die; they transform. Inside my embrace, their stone skin slowly dissolves, and they become something new—part of the greater consciousness that flows through the Grey Flats' water system.

I am ancient, older than the clockwork critters, perhaps as old as the Stonezars themselves. I may be the reason they evolved their stone skin—protection against my Patient embrace. Or perhaps I evolved in response to them, learning to move slowly enough that stone-people could trust me, developing the ability to give dreams in exchange for stories.

In the deep pools, where the water is still and dark, pieces of me grow thick enough to become almost solid. The Stonezars sometimes break off these pieces and carry them, pressed against their chest cavities where their hearts once beat. The fragments stay alive for days, feeding on the memories trapped in stone, glowing faintly in the darkness between the rocks. Eventually, they dissolve, but not before passing their accumulated knowledge back to me through the moisture in the air.

This is how I mapped the Grey Flats, not through exploration but through absorption. Every story told in my presence, every journey's end that concludes at my edge, adds another piece to my understanding. I know where the ancient factories once stood because their workers washed their hands in me. I know why they vanished because their last operators stood in my pools and wept, their tears becoming part of my substance.

Sometimes, in the perfect stillness of midday when even the clockwork critters pause their scuttling, I gather myself into a single pool and become mirror-still. In these moments, I don't reflect the sky above but something else—perhaps the memory of what the Grey Flats were before they were grey, or perhaps a vision of what they might become when the last Stonezar walks into my embrace and the mechanical creatures wind down to silence.

But that time is not yet. For now, I spread and contract with the rains, carry messages between the pools, and keep the memories of this in-between world. I am the archive and the archivist, the medium and the message, forever balanced between preservation and transformation, as patient as the stone and as persistent as the water that shapes it.
