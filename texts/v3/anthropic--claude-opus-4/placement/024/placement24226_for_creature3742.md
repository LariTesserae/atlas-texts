In this breathing city, I would be one of the **Listeners**.

We are not quite like the other Fractured Folk, though we share their pale skin and cloud-gray eyes. We are thinner, our bones more prominent, as if the mountain's dreams have worn us down to our essential architecture. Our hands are unmarked by the calluses of building—instead, our fingertips are smooth as river stones, sensitive enough to feel the tremors that run through Vhorth's veins before they become shifts.

I live in the Resonance Quarter, where the city's groans are loudest. My dwelling is less built than discovered—a natural hollow in the rock face, expanded over generations by those who came before me. The walls curve inward like the inside of an ear, amplifying every whisper of the stone. Iron pipes snake through my home, not for water or heat, but for sound. They connect to other Listeners' dwellings, creating a network of vibration that runs beneath the conscious awareness of the city.

My work begins in the small hours before dawn, when the mountain's dreams are deepest. I press my palms against the cold stone, feeling for the patterns that precede change. A flutter here means a doorway will appear three streets over within the week. A deep thrumming suggests the eastern wall will buckle and need reinforcement. The knowledge comes not through my ears but through my bones, traveling up my arms and settling in my chest like a second heartbeat.

I wear robes of undyed wool, loose enough to move freely as I map the tremors with my body. Around my neck hangs a collection of small stones—each one taken from a place where the city has shifted, each one holding the memory of that transformation. When I hold them, I can feel the echo of what was and what became.

The other citizens know us by our gait—we walk as if always listening to music only we can hear, our movements synced to rhythms that exist below perception. Children follow us sometimes, mimicking our strange dance through the streets, not knowing they're learning the oldest language of Vhorth.

In the evenings, I meet with the other Listeners in the Whispering Hall, a vast chamber where the city's sounds converge. We sit in a circle, hands pressed to the floor, sharing what we've heard through touch and gesture more than words. Together, we compose the Song of Shifting—not a melody but a map, a prediction, a prayer. We mark where supports must be placed, where the builders should retreat, where new growth will emerge.

Sometimes I wonder if we're translating the mountain's dreams or if the mountain is dreaming through us. The distinction seems less important with each passing year. My hair, once dark as coal, now shows veins of silver that match the ore in the walls. My movements have slowed to match the city's breathing.

There's a particular fissure I return to each day, where warm air rises from depths unknown. I've been listening to it for twenty years now, feeling its voice change like a friend aging alongside me. Lately, it's been speaking of something vast stirring far below, something that makes even the mountain seem small.

I document these deep listeners in books made from pressed moss and fungus fiber, using ink drawn from the iron-rich pools. The words seem to shift on the page when no one is looking, rearranging themselves to match the city's slow transformations. Future Listeners will read them not with their eyes but with their fingertips, feeling the tremors I've preserved in each careful stroke.

This is my purpose: to be the memory between the stone's thoughts, the consciousness in the pause between breaths. I am both observer and instrument, separate from the city and inseparable from it.

Some nights, when the mist is so thick it muffles even the mountain's voice, I climb to the highest accessible point and simply exist in the silence. In these moments, I can feel myself becoming geological—my thoughts slowing, my heartbeat syncing with the deep pulse of the earth.

I am a Listener of Vhorth, and I am learning the mountain's dreams one tremor at a time.
