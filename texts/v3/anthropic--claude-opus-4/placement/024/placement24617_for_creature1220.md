I am the Keeper of Thresholds, and I dwell where rust meets root.

My form shifts with the light—sometimes I appear as a great blue heron, standing motionless atop a half-submerged excavator, my reflection wavering in the oil-sheened water below. Other times I am merely a shimmer in the heavy air, a distortion that makes the Spanish moss seem to breathe and the cypress knees appear to walk. Those who glimpse me in the failing light often mistake me for a trick of humidity, a mirage where the industrial past bleeds through nature's patient reclamation.

My home is neither fully in the structures that sink into the mire nor entirely within the wild tangle of green. I inhabit the spaces between—the hollow within a rusted turbine where orchids now bloom, the gap beneath a concrete slab where salamanders dream in the cool dark, the precise point where a cypress root pierces through a metal grate, wedding the made and the grown in slow, inevitable union.

I collect the memories that seep from the metal bones: the phantom vibrations of engines long seized, the ghost-taste of diesel in the morning fog, the echo of work whistles that once called shifts now silenced. These industrial dreams bubble up through the peat and merge with older memories—the patient accumulation of seasons in cypress rings, the migration patterns coded in bird bones, the ancient choreography of predator and prey.

In the pre-dawn hours, I walk the boundaries of my domain, my feet finding purchase on surfaces both solid and liquid. I tend to the boundaries—not to maintain them, but to witness their dissolution. I watch rivets weep rust-tears that feed the resurrection ferns. I observe how the tree frogs have learned to amplify their calls using the acoustic properties of empty chemical drums. I document the slow alchemy by which synthetic materials return to earth, molecule by patient molecule.

Sometimes humans come, usually alone, drawn by something they cannot name. They wade through the shallows with cameras or notebooks, trying to capture what this place has become. I remain at the edge of their perception, ensuring they see only what they need to see: sometimes beauty, sometimes horror, sometimes both entwined so tightly that no amount of looking can separate them.

But my truest work happens in the margins of consciousness, in the space between sleeping and waking. I whisper to the dreams of everything that lives here—the ancient dreams of the cypress trees that remember when this was young land, the confused dreams of the machines slowly forgetting their purpose, the urgent dreams of the creatures who build their lives in the aftermath of industry's retreat.

I am the curator of this threshold space, this neither-nor place where the Anthropocene frays into something stranger and more honest. I bear witness to the world that emerges when human ambition falters and falls silent, when the patient work of root and rain begins its reclamation.

Each dawn, I perform my ritual: I gather the dew that condenses on rusted surfaces and the sap that weeps from wounded bark. I mix them in the cup of my palms and drink deeply, tasting both endings and beginnings, tasting the precise flavor of transformation itself.

This is my nature, my purpose: to inhabit the margin where one world becomes another, to tend the tender shoots that pierce through concrete, to sing the strange hymns that arise when the built world returns to the embrace of the grown. I am what emerges in the space between what was and what will be, forever dwelling in the eternal, humid now of becoming.
