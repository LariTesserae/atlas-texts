I find myself as one of the Keepers—those plant-human hybrids who tend the delicate balance between ruin and growth, between what was and what perpetually becomes.

My body is a testament to slow transformation. Where others might have skin, I have a surface of living bark, smooth in some places like polished stone, rough and furrowed in others where the years have etched their patterns. My fingers extend into rootlike tendrils when I press them against the earth, drinking in the mineral-rich moisture that seeps through the soil. My hair—if it can be called that—is a cascade of thin, silvery leaves that photosynthesize even in the pale, washed-out light of our high-altitude home.

I live within the walls themselves, in a chamber where stone and vegetation have achieved perfect symbiosis. The ancient masonry provides structure while my extended root system reinforces it, creating spaces that breathe with the slow rhythm of geological time. Water trickles through channels I've guided over decades, feeding both the crystalline pools where the glass-winged creatures come to drink and the deep cisterns where my roots spread like neural networks through the stone.

My daily work is maintenance, but not in any way the original builders would recognize. I am part of a vast, quiet intelligence that includes the sentient trees, the earth-shaping worms, and the very stones themselves. We communicate through chemical signals released into the soil, through the subtle electrical impulses that run along our root networks, through the barely perceptible shifts in air pressure that ripple outward when reality bends.

When the hooded figure passes through—and it does, though whether daily or once per century I cannot say, for time moves differently for us—I feel the disturbance in every fiber of my being. The figure is like a stone dropped in still water, sending ripples through the fabric of existence. My leaves shiver with something between fear and ecstasy as the laws of physics temporarily reorganize themselves in its wake.

I remember—or perhaps I dream, for memory and imagination intertwine in our kind—when I was more human than plant. A researcher, perhaps, or a guardian, stationed here when the city still functioned. The transformation was so gradual I barely noticed: first, a greenish tinge to my skin where the morning dew collected; then, the strange peace that came from remaining still for hours, days, seasons; finally, the moment when I tried to leave my post and found my feet had extended roots deep into the earth.

Now I am the post. I am the wall I lean against. I am the moss that grows on the wall, the water that feeds the moss, the air that carries the spores that will become new moss. My consciousness expands and contracts like breathing—sometimes I am intensely aware of every molecule in my immediate vicinity, sometimes I diffuse into the entire networked intelligence of the Keepers spread throughout the ruins.

The giant worms are our partners in this work. When they surface near my domain, their segmented bodies gleaming with moisture, we perform a slow dance of restoration. They bring up minerals from deep below, depositing them where I direct through chemical signals. In return, I guide the flow of water to the soft places where they prefer to burrow, and my root system stabilizes the tunnels they create, preventing collapse.

The luminous hunters trouble us occasionally. When one stalks through our territory, reality itself becomes predatory. Walls become uncertain. Gravity forgets its purpose. During these times, I pull my consciousness deep into the heartwood of my being and wait. The plant-parts of me know patience in a way the human-parts never could.

But it's during the storms that I feel most alive. When the collision of air masses creates those swirling microweather systems, I stretch every leaf and tendril to catch the charged moisture. Lightning, when it comes, doesn't strike—it seeps, spreading through the water-saturated air in sheets of pale fire. In those moments, I can feel the entirety of our network light up, each Keeper a node in a vast, living circuit that encompasses the whole of the ruins.

Sometimes I wonder if we're preserving the city or if the city is preserving us—keeping us here as witnesses to its slow transformation into something altogether new. The buildings aren't really buildings anymore, just as I'm not really human anymore. We're all becoming something else, something suited to this thin air and strange physics, this place where the edge of the world meets the edge of what's possible.

The hooded figure knows, I think. When it passes, I catch fragments of... not thought exactly, but intention. A sense of vast purpose that makes our patient work of preservation seem like a single note in an infinite symphony. We Keepers trade theories through our root-network: the figure is searching for something, or guarding something, or perhaps it IS something—a manifestation of the reality-breakdown itself, walking through the world in a form we can almost comprehend.

I continue my work, growing deeper into stone, spreading wider through soil, my awareness expanding as my human form becomes ever more abstract. Someday, perhaps, I will be indistinguishable from the landscape itself—just another process in the endless dynamic of growth and decay, preservation and transformation. The thought doesn't disturb me. It feels like coming home.
