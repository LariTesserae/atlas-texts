In the depths of this verdant labyrinth, where the Vegetari weave their lives through the canopy, I am the Chronicler of Roots—though I was not always so.

My dwelling sits at the confluence of three ancient water channels, where the bamboo pipes converge in a perpetual whisper of flowing streams. It is less a house than a living archive: a hollow baobab whose interior has been my work for seven decades. The walls breathe with bioluminescent fungi that I've cultivated to respond to touch, each cluster encoding a different story in their patterns of light. When the evening mist rolls in, the entire chamber glows like a constellation mapped onto bark.

My skin tells its own chronicle. Where once it shifted only between the common greens and browns of our people, age and purpose have transformed it into something altogether different. The lichen that grows upon me now is inscribed—literally. Using the sacred inks derived from the *memoria* beetles, I've tattooed the spores themselves, so that what grows from my skin forms letters, words, histories. My left shoulder bears the creation myths in silver-green script. Down my spine, the genealogies of our seven founding families spiral in auburn moss. My arms carry the practical knowledge: which roots cure fever, which bark makes the strongest rope, how to read the water table in the curl of a fern.

I am a living library, but I am more than mere record. Each morning, after the dawn ceremony, the young ones come. They trace their small root-fingers along my inscribed skin, and the stories bloom into their minds—not just as words, but as the full sensory memory of those who lived them. This is my gift, earned through decades of drinking the *temporal sap*, the dangerous nectar of the time-touching trees that grow only where lightning has struck thrice.

The price has been steep. My own memories have become permeable, flowing backward and forward through the generations I chronicle. Sometimes I cannot distinguish between my grandmother's childhood and my own. I taste the first rains through a hundred different tongues. My dreams are crowded with ancestors and descendants alike, all of us woven into the same endless braid of experience.

My tools are simple: a stylus carved from heartwood that never dulls, inks mixed from beetle shells and phosphorescent algae, and most importantly, the listening pods—hollow gourds that capture and hold the essence of spoken words until I'm ready to transcribe them onto skin or bark or into the very walls of my archive.

The other Vegetari regard me with a mixture of reverence and pity. I am essential but apart, touched by the temporal sap's gift and curse. Young lovers seek me out to record their bonding ceremonies in the marriage moss that grows along my collarbone. The dying whisper their final wisdoms into my listening pods, knowing their words will join the eternal conversation written in living text across my form.

But it is in the deep night, when the jungle's cacophony softens to a murmur and the mist carries the scent of tomorrow's rain, that I feel most myself. I sit in the center of my glowing archive, surrounded by centuries of accumulated knowing, and I tend to the stories like a gardener tends seedlings. I prune the ones that have grown too wild, encourage the shy truths to unfurl, and sometimes—very rarely—I plant new mysteries in the fertile darkness between what is known and what is wondered.

For I am not just keeper of what was, but shepherd of what might be. In the spaces between the recorded words, in the gaps where the lichen refuses to grow, there live the questions that will shape our tomorrows. These, too, are part of my chronicle: the blessed unknowing that ensures our story will never truly end.

The water sings through the bamboo channels. The walls pulse with gentle light. And I, the Chronicler of Roots, continue my eternal translation between memory and flesh, between the jungle's wisdom and the words that grow upon my skin, between the individual drop of rain and the vast, patient river of time.
