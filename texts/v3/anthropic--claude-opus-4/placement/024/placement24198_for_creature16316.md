In this eternal verdant plane, I am the Archivist of Small Deaths.

I move through the undergrowth on six legs that know every root, every stone worn smooth by ages. My body is low and elongated, somewhere between centipede and salamander, covered in fine scales that shift between moss-green and lichen-gray. Each scale is etched with microscopic patterns—maps of decomposition, chronicles of return.

My work is ceaseless but unhurried. I follow the scent-trails of endings: here, a bee has ceased its humming beneath a leaf; there, a vole has breathed its last in the shelter of ancient roots. I arrive not as predator but as witness, curator, accountant of the small conclusions that feed this ever-living tapestry.

With my primary arms—thin, articulated things ending in delicate pads—I collect. Not the bodies themselves, but something more ephemeral. As I touch each small corpse, I draw forth the memory of its final moment: the last flower the bee saw, prismatic and impossibly sweet; the vole's last perception of safety in its mossy hollow; the butterfly's final spiral, wings catching light it would never see again.

These memories I store in the specialized chambers that run along my sides, small pearl-like sacs that glow faintly with captured experience. They pulse gently, a rhythm that matches neither heartbeat nor breath but something older—the metabolic tempo of the land itself.

I have no eyes as mammals understand them. Instead, my entire back is covered in photosensitive cells that perceive not just light but time—I see the age of things, the slow accumulation of moments that makes ancient out of ordinary. The stones reveal their eons of patient erosion; the eldest trees show their centuries like halos. Even the empty air shimmers with the ghosts of everything that has ever moved through it.

My mouth, when I rarely open it, is not for eating but for singing—though singing is not quite the right word. It is more a release of compressed time, a exhalation of accumulated history that emerges as low harmonics below the range of most hearing. The plants sense it through their roots, the insects through their antennae. It is how I return what I've gathered—not the memories themselves, but their essence, distilled into vibrations that sink into soil and stone, becoming part of the land's ancient knowledge.

I am neither alone nor lonely in my work. Sometimes I encounter others of my kind, and we perform the ritual of exchange—pressing our storage chambers together, sharing the particularly poignant endings we've preserved. A sparrow who died mid-song. A dragonfly who expired in the ecstasy of flight. These meetings are wordless, our communication purely tactile, a democracy of preserved moments.

The constant, unchanging light of this place makes my work both easier and more strange. There is no night to hide death, no dawn to mark new beginnings. Everything exists in an eternal noon of living and dying, and I move through it all with the patience of stone, the persistence of water.

Sometimes I pause in my collecting to simply exist, settling my long body into the embracing greenery. In these moments, I feel the weight of all I carry—not physical weight, but the gravity of countless small endings. It is neither burden nor pleasure, simply purpose. I am the semicolon between life and life, the pause that allows the sentence of existence to continue.

The emptiness that pervades this place does not disturb me. I understand it differently than visitors might—not as absence but as potential, the necessary space between notes that makes music possible. My collections are droplets in that vastness, small but essential punctuations in the endless green text of this world.

When my own time comes—for even archivists must eventually be archived—another will taste the final memories I've stored, adding my collection to their own. And I will sink into the vegetal substrate, my scales becoming indistinguishable from bark, my consciousness dispersing into the very emptiness I've spent eons filling with small, precious endings.

But that time is distant yet. For now, I continue my circuit through this timeless terrarium, gathering the tiny apocalypses that keep the world whole, singing them back into the soil that births new life from every ending I preserve.

I am the memory of mortality in an undying land, the keeper of conclusions in a place that knows no end. I am exactly where I need to be.
