I am a threshold tender, one of many who maintain the crystalline relay stations scattered across this austere settlement. My body has adapted over generations to this work—elongated fingers that can feel the subtle vibrations in the fiber networks, eyes that have developed a secondary membrane to filter the harsh light and detect the faint bioluminescent signals that pulse through our communication grid.

My dwelling is built into one of the sharp ridges that score the landscape, a natural cave I've expanded with secreted resins that harden into amber-like walls. The entrance is nearly invisible, covered by a living curtain of phosphorescent moss that I cultivate carefully, feeding it drops of my own filtered blood when water is too scarce. Inside, the walls are lined with salvaged metal and crystal fragments that hum with stored energy, creating a low resonance that keeps the smaller symbiotic creatures calm—tiny arthropods with mirror-like shells that help me detect approaching storms by the way they cluster and sing.

Each morning, I emerge to check the fiber lines that run like exposed nerves across the settlement. The crystals must be cleaned of dust and adjusted to maintain their sensitivity. Some days I find them cracked from the temperature shifts between night and day, and I must grow new ones from the seeds I keep in a pouch at my throat—a biological modification my predecessors developed, a special gland that precipitates mineral structures when stimulated by specific sound frequencies.

The other tenders and I rarely meet face to face. We communicate through the network itself, sending pulses of bioluminescent signals through the fibers, our messages traveling as waves of blue-green light that can be seen flickering across the settlement at dusk. When we must gather—to discuss a major repair, or to welcome a new settler who has earned the right to tap into our network—we meet at the confluence points where multiple fiber lines cross, our forms barely visible to each other in the dim light, recognizing each other more by the unique harmonics we generate than by sight.

The macabre sounds that puncture our silence are often misunderstood by newcomers. What they interpret as screams or grinding metal are actually the songs of the deep borers—massive worm-like creatures that move through the substrata, aerating the soil and occasionally surfacing to trade minerals they've processed in their gullets for the organic compounds we synthesize. Their music is a language older than our settlement, and learning to interpret it is essential for survival here. A rising tritone means water has been detected deep below; a descending cascade of clicks warns of instability in the ground.

My most precious possession is a small vial of original colony water, saved from before the great desiccation. I add a single molecule of it to each new crystal I grow, maintaining an unbroken chain of memory in our network. Sometimes, when I'm alone in my cave and the smaller creatures are sleeping in their clustered warmth against my skin, I hold the vial up to the bioluminescent moss and watch the way the ancient water bends the light, imagining the world that was, even as I tend to the world that is.
