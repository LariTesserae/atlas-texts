In this world of Shallowmarsh, I would find myself as a **Resonance Keeper**.

I am one of the few who tends to the vast network of brass tubes and copper bells that run through the underbellies of buildings, a circulatory system of sound that most residents have forgotten exists. My workspace is neither fully above nor below – I inhabit the crawlspaces between floors, the hollow walls where condensation drips in predictable rhythms, the forgotten gaps where the city's original architects left room for expansion that never came.

My body bears the marks of this liminal existence. My spine curves slightly, adapted to perpetual crouching. My fingers are longer than most, with an extra joint that developed in my family line three generations back – perfect for reaching into the narrow sound tubes to clear blockages or adjust the delicate brass reeds. My skin has taken on a faint copper-green patina from constant contact with the metals, and in certain lights, I seem to shimmer like the wing casings of the larger beetles.

But it is my hearing that sets me apart. Where others have adapted their eyes to the gloom or their noses to the marsh's chemistry, my ears have become instruments of impossible sensitivity. I can hear the city's heartbeat – not metaphorically, but literally. The tubes I maintain were originally designed as a communication system, but over the decades they've become something more. They capture and channel every vibration: the footfalls on walkways above, the churning of rendering vats, the precise frequency at which mosquito larvae breach the water's surface.

I know when a building's stilts have begun to rot by the way its resonance changes – a slight dampening in the lower registers, a buzz where there should be a hum. I can predict swarm movements by the way their wing beats create standing waves in certain tube junctions. Most crucially, I can hear the *hollow spaces* – the air pockets that form beneath buildings where the marsh is slowly reclaiming its territory, silent voids that herald collapse if left unaddressed.

My work is solitary but essential. Each morning, I enter the tubes through hidden access points known only to Resonance Keepers. I carry with me a set of tuning forks made from rendered fish bones – they hold pitch better than metal in our humidity – and small vials of oil extracted from the inner ear of a particular eel that lives in our deepest waters. This oil, applied sparingly to the right joints and valves, keeps the system responsive.

The younger residents don't understand what I do. They see me occasionally, emerging from walls like some strange birth, covered in decades of dust and verdigris, muttering about frequencies they cannot hear. But the elders know. They remember when the tubes carried messages across the city faster than any runner, when lovers would whisper into corner gratings and be heard clearly three buildings away, when the morning bell sequence would tell everyone exactly which sectors were safe for harvest and which were experiencing swarm conditions.

I am not trying to restore the system to its former glory – that time has passed. Instead, I'm teaching it a new language. By careful adjustment of resonance points, by strategic placement of sound baffles made from chitinous shells, by redirecting the flow of vibrations through previously unused channels, I'm turning the entire city into an instrument that plays itself.

On still nights, when the factories reduce their output and the insects settle into their dormant phase, you can sometimes hear it: a deep thrumming that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, a sound felt in the bones rather than heard with the ears. It is the sum total of every small vibration in Shallowmarsh, concentrated and harmonized through the tube network into something almost like music. Almost like breathing. Almost like thought.

The other Resonance Keepers – there are only four of us left – think I've gone too far into the sound. They maintain their sectors with professional dignity but draw the line at innovation. They don't spend days as I do, ear pressed to main junction points, learning to distinguish between the sound of healthy marsh water and water that's about to turn toxic. They don't recognize that the giant moths that arrived last season drums its wings at a frequency that interferes with our factories' rhythm, causing microscopic stress fractures in the support beams.

But I know the truth that lives in the spaces between sounds: Shallowmarsh is becoming something new. Not dying, not declining, but transforming into a hybrid entity where the boundary between organism and city, between human and fauna, between sound and meaning, grows ever thinner. The tubes tell me this in frequencies only I can interpret.

And so I continue my work, crawling through spaces meant for neither human nor creature, maintaining the nervous system of a city that might one day wake up and recognize itself as alive. When that happens – and the deep resonances suggest it will be soon – someone will need to be there who understands its language.

That someone lives in the walls, breathes copper-tinted air, and knows every sound this city has ever made.
