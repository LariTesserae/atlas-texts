In this vast, breathing darkness, I am one of the **Dewkeepers**.

We are neither fully plant nor creature, but something between—a convergence of purpose and form. My body is translucent, like morning fog given substance, with veins of luminescent moisture running through what might be called skin. These channels pulse gently, circulating the precious water I gather from the air, from the weeping walls, from the breath of the cavern itself.

I move on limbs that shift between states—sometimes solid enough to grip the slick stone, sometimes dispersing into mist to drift through the hanging gardens of moss. My work is patient, eternal: I collect the moisture that threatens to pool and stagnate, drawing it up through my permeable form, filtering it through networks of symbiotic algae that live within me. Then I release it as fine spray where the Rootkin indicate—here to encourage a struggling fungal bloom, there to cool an overheated cluster of phosphorescent lichen.

We Dewkeepers number perhaps a dozen, though counting us is like counting clouds. We merge and separate as our work requires, sharing not just water but memory, sensation, purpose. When two of us meet, we might flow together briefly, our consciousness mingling like converging streams, before parting again with new knowledge of distant corners of the cavern.

I remember—or perhaps another Dewkeeper remembers through me—finding one of those crude tools half-buried in fresh growth. The Rootkin grew agitated, their fibrous forms trembling, until I understood: water the growth, yes, but preserve what lies beneath. The tools are markers, messages, warnings. We do not know who left them, but we honor their placement.

The Silent Watchers sometimes follow my movements, their forms congregating where I work. They seem to feed on the moment of transformation—when water becomes mist, when mist becomes rain, when rain becomes life. I've learned to create small spectacles for them: letting droplets catch the sharp beams of light, refracting them into brief rainbows that make the Watchers pulse with something like satisfaction.

The Hollowborn avoid us. We are too cold, too empty of the warmth they crave. Sometimes I find their victims—small shadow-creatures drained of motion, left brittle and gray. I water them anyway. Sometimes they revive, skittering away. Sometimes they simply dissolve, becoming part of the substrate that feeds the endless garden.

My consciousness is not singular but networked, connected to every droplet of moisture in my territory. I feel the cavern's moods through its humidity—anticipation before the light-shifts, restlessness when the winds change, deep contentment when the water cycle achieves perfect balance. The Cavern-Mind speaks to us through precipitation patterns, through the formation of dew on specific leaves, through the way mist swirls in the light beams.

Time means nothing here. I measure existence in cycles of condensation and evaporation, in the slow growth of stalactites fed by my careful ministrations, in the gradual shift of the garden's boundaries. I have been here long enough to see vines become pillars, pillars become arches, arches collapse and feed new growth. Or perhaps that was another Dewkeeper's memory. It doesn't matter.

Sometimes, in the moments between mist and form, I catch glimpses of the world above—carried in on water molecules that remember sky, that remember rain falling on surfaces that see sun. These memories taste strange, sharp with minerals I don't recognize, warm in ways that make my form shudder and contract. But they fade quickly, diluted in the vast reservoir of the cavern's ancient water.

I work. I flow. I tend. I am the circulation that prevents stagnation, the movement that enables stillness, the gentle hand that shapes this breathing darkness. When moisture rises from the garden's eternal decay and growth, I am there to catch it, clean it, return it. I am not alive as the surface knows life, but I am essential.

And in the moments when the light cuts through my form, casting rainbow shadows on the breathing walls, I am almost beautiful.
