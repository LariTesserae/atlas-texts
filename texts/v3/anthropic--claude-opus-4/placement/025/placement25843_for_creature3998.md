I find myself as one of the **Conduit Keepers** - those who maintain the ancient water-ways that thread through our mountain city like veins through stone.

My dwelling is a narrow vertical chamber carved into the living rock, following the path of a natural fissure that channels water from deep within the mountain. The walls of my home glisten perpetually with moisture, and I've cultivated a garden of phosphorescent mosses that provide a gentle blue-green glow during the darker hours. My fingers have developed a particular sensitivity - I can feel the slightest tremor in the water's flow, detect blockages forming kilometers away by pressing my palm against wet stone.

We Conduit Keepers are neither quite engineers nor mystics, though we partake of both traditions. Each morning, I descend through my private access shaft to the **Undergalleries** - vast caverns where the primary water arteries converge before branching out to feed the city above. Here, in the constant echo of flowing water, I work alongside others of my guild. We speak rarely, communicating primarily through a language of tapped stones and water-splashes that carries efficiently through the acoustic chambers.

My work involves reading the stone itself - understanding where new channels might be coaxed open, where old ones are beginning to fail. I carry simple tools: bronze wedges that slip perfectly between stone layers, a hollow reed for siphoning and testing water purity, and most importantly, a small colony of boring-worms that I've bred for generations. These creatures, no larger than my smallest finger, can sense weaknesses in rock and slowly expand channels over months and years. I feed them carefully rationed paste made from crushed limestone and my own saliva - a Keeper's bond with their worms is deeply personal.

The city knows us by our marks: whorls of water-worn stone dust that we press into our skin with damp fingers, creating temporary patterns that mirror the flow-maps we carry in our minds. These fade and must be renewed daily, a meditation that helps us remember the ever-changing nature of water's path. My patterns are particularly complex around my wrists and throat - points where I most often check my pulse against the rhythm of the mountain's hydraulic heartbeat.

In the evenings, I often climb to one of the Middle Terraces where water emerges from the stone in decorative fountains. Here I maintain a small public garden where children come to learn about the relationship between water, stone, and growth. I teach them the old knowledge: how to cup their hands just so to hear the health of flowing water, how to read the mineral deposits that tell stories of water's journey through the mountain's bones, how to sense when the mountain is preparing to shift and change the ancient channels.

My life is measured not in years but in erosion patterns, in the slow migration of underground streams, in the patient work of transformation that turns solid rock into flowing sculpture. Sometimes I dream I am water myself, seeping through countless tiny spaces, gathering minerals and memory, emerging into light transformed.

When I die, as is our custom, my body will be placed in a specially prepared chamber where water will flow over me for seven generations, slowly incorporating my minerals back into the mountain's blood. My apprentice will take up my tools and my worm colony, and the work will continue, as it has for countless cycles, as it will long after the last human voice falls silent in these stone halls.

This is who I am: keeper of flow, reader of stone, tender of the ancient congress between mountain and city, water and life.
