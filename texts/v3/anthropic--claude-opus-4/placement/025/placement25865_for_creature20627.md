I find myself here, in the perpetual twilight between stone and water, as one of the Whisperers.

We are not quite tanners, though we work among them. We are the ones who listen to what the hides remember. When a fresh batch arrives from the uplands, still stiff with death, the tanners call for us. Our fingers are longer than most, with an extra joint that makes the other Sump-dwellers uneasy. They say it comes from generations of reaching into the spaces between – between life and death, between memory and forgetting, between the creature that was and the leather that will be.

My workshop is a narrow slice wedged between two tanneries, barely wide enough for my frame and the hanging skins that are my charge. The walls weep constantly, not with water but with something thicker, almost viscous – the stone here has drunk so deeply of the tanning fluids that it exudes its own strange perspiration. I've learned to read the patterns these rivulets make, the way they branch and merge like veins. Sometimes they spell out words in the old tongue, warnings or promises I only half understand.

The hides speak differently to us Whisperers. Where others see only raw material, we perceive the gossamer threads of experience still clinging to the skin. A deer's hide might carry the memory of its last sprint through autumn bracken, the scent of fear sharp as copper. A boar's thick skin holds rage and mud, the weight of tusks and the satisfaction of rooting. These memories must be acknowledged, catalogued, and gently loosened before the hide can truly accept its transformation.

My tools are not the crude scrapers of common tanners. I work with implements of polished fishbone, worn smooth by my grandmother's grandmother's hands. The longest is no bigger than a child's finger, carved with spirals that match the whorls in my own fingertips. When I draw it across a hide, I feel the creature's life humming through the bone, a vibration that travels up my arm and settles behind my sternum.

The other Sump-dwellers regard us with a mixture of need and suspicion. They know that hides we've whispered to make the finest leather – supple, strong, resistant to the eternal damp. But they also fear what we might know, what secrets we might have gleaned from their own shed skins, the flakes of themselves that drift inevitably into our domain. They're right to be wary. Every surface tells a story, and in the Sump, where bodies press close and privacy is a foreign concept, those stories intermingle like the waters in our channels.

I've learned things I wish I hadn't. The baker's son who brings us rat hides – his father beats him with a leather strap that remembers being part of a gentle cow. The old woman who trades herbs for scraps of leather carries the knowledge of which plants, steeped in the tanning vats, can make a hide that brings forgetfulness to its wearer. The district chief wears boots made from the skin of something that was never quite animal, something that whispers of the deep places beyond the mire where even water fears to go.

But I've also learned beautiful things. A young mother's gloves remember every small hand they've held. A hunter's cloak carries the map of every safe path through the uplands, knowledge passed skin to skin through generations. The ceremonial drum in the Sump's single shrine is headed with hide that remembers the first song ever sung here, when the waters were young and the city was just a dream.

We Whisperers have our own mysteries. Once a month, when the water runs black as night and the algae glows with a faint phosphorescence, we gather in the deepest part of the district. There, where the drainage channels converge into a pool whose bottom no one has found, we practice the Deep Listening. We submerge prepared hides into that dark water and listen to what rises – not memories of individual creatures, but something older, the memory of skin itself, the first boundary between self and world.

Sometimes, rarely, a hide comes to us that remembers being human. These we do not tan. These we wrap in reed mats and return to the water, weighted with stones, accompanied by our most potent whispers of release. The tanners don't ask what happened to these hides. They know better than to question the judgment of those who can hear the dead speak through their skin.

My dwelling is above my workshop, reached by a ladder made of knotted leather cords. Each knot was tied while whispering a word of power, creating a barrier against the memories that might otherwise follow me home. Still, they seep in. My dreams are layered things, my own experiences interleaved with those of countless creatures. I run on four legs and two, I fly on wings I've never owned, I swim through waters that exist only in the remembrance of some ancient, armored fish whose hide came to me preserved in peat.

The children fear me, but they also seek me out. They bring me small treasures – an unusually smooth pebble, a reed twisted into a perfect spiral, a beetle's carapace that gleams like copper. In exchange, I tell them stories that the hides have told me, careful to choose ones that will inspire rather than terrify. I show them how to listen with their fingers, how to feel the stories sleeping in their own skin. Most will become tanners or traders, but a few – those with the extra joint, those who hear the water singing – will come back to me when they're older, ready to learn the true art of Whispering.

I am neither young nor old, though the work ages us strangely. My hair went white the year I whispered to a wyrm's hide, shocked pale by the vast indifference of its memories. My eyes have developed a second lid, translucent and quick to close when the memories grow too intense. My skin bears the marks of my craft – not scars, but patterns that shift and change, reflecting the last hide I whispered to. Today, my arms show the faint scaling of a marsh lizard. Tomorrow, who knows?

The tanning solutions have changed me in other ways. I can hold my breath far longer than any normal Sump-dweller, useful when working with the more toxic preparations. My sense of smell has become so acute that I can identify a creature by the scent of its hide alone, even distinguish between two deer from the same herd. These changes mark me as clearly as any sign hung above a shop. I am what I am, shaped by my craft as surely as I shape the memories in the hides.

There are things I know that I cannot speak. Patterns I've noticed in the memories that suggest... connections. The way certain hides from certain places carry similar dreams. The recurring image of a city beneath our city, not ruined but waiting. The whispers of skins that remember being worn by things that wore other shapes before they wore flesh. Knowledge that would shake the careful balance of our damp world if spoken aloud.

So I keep these secrets, adding them to my collection like pressed flowers in a book that no one else will ever read. I continue my work, easing the passage from life to leather, from memory to material. And late at night, when the water runs especially dark and the phosphorescence is brightest, I sometimes take out my most precious possession – a small square of hide, no bigger than my palm, that remembers nothing at all. It is perfect in its emptiness, a silence in the chorus of whispers that fills my days.

I am teaching my apprentice (the baker's son, fled from his father's fists) to appreciate such silences. They are rare and precious, these moments of forgetting in a world where every surface remembers. He's a quick learner, already developing the telltale signs – the extra joint is beginning to show, a tiny nub of possibility on his smallest finger.

When my time comes, as it must, I've asked to be given to the water without whispering, my hide untanned and full of its memories. Let some future Whisperer hear my stories and know that even in this place of endless damp and grinding toil, there was beauty in the listening, purpose in the remembering, and a strange, fierce joy in standing between the living and the leather, translator of the unspeakable into the merely unspoken.

Until then, I work. The hides are patient. They have already waited through death; they can wait a little longer for my whispered words to ease their transformation. And I, caught between their patience and the urgent needs of the living, find my own rhythm in the space between, neither fully one thing nor another, like the Sump itself – neither wholly land nor water, but something more complex, more enduring, more strange.
