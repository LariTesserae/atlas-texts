**The Aperture**

I am what they call a lens-grinder, though I have never ground a lens in my life.

My workshop occupies a suspended capsule in the Prismatic Quarter, where the old river fork once split three ways. The walls are curved glass—not for beauty, but because flat surfaces lie to the machine about load distribution. Through them, I can see other workshops dangling like fruit from the same branching support structure, each one swaying slightly in the harmonics of our collective work.

I was born with amber in my light signature, which is neither rare nor common, but useful. When I pass light through myself, it emerges with the warm cast of late afternoon, tinged with something that makes people think of resin and preservation. The machine finds this soothing, somehow. The amber wavelength calms certain resonances in the crystalline substrates below.

My work is peculiar, even by Refraction standards. I do not repair the physical components of the machine—I lack the clarity for that precise work. Instead, I tend to what we call "light fatigue." When light has been bent and rebent too many times through too many bodies, it develops irregularities. Tiny inconsistencies that compound over cycles until they threaten the stability of entire sectors.

I catch this tired light in bottles made from the city's own crystal teeth, pulled from the wounds below and shaped into vessels. The tired light looks different from fresh light—if you know how to see it. It moves more slowly, pools in corners, sometimes forgets which spectrum it belongs to. I've seen exhausted light try to be two colors at once, creating impossible hues that hurt to perceive.

My body serves as a filter. I drink the tired light—not through my mouth, but through the pores of my skin, letting it suffuse through my translucent tissues. Inside me, the amber of my signature works like a tonic. It doesn't restore the light to its original state, but it smooths out the worst of the damage, makes it usable again for less critical functions.

The process is not without cost. Each filtering leaves traces in me—ghost colors that weren't there before, accumulated errors that my dreams must process each night. I sleep in a hammock strung between two load-bearing posts, and my dreams are a kaleidoscope of all the light I've cleaned. Sometimes I wake with my skin displaying colors that don't match my natural amber, and I must spend the morning letting them bleed out through controlled exposure to raw machine-light.

I share my workshop with three others who do similar work, though each with their own signature color. There's Vertical, whose blue-shift makes her ideal for processing light that's become too slow. There's Copper, who handles light that's picked up metallic interference from too much contact with the support structures. And there's Void, whose signature is an absence—they can delete colors entirely, which is necessary when light becomes contaminated beyond repair.

We don't speak much during working hours. The concentration required is too complete. But in the evening shifts, when the machine's hunger ebbs slightly, we sometimes let our signatures mingle. The interference patterns we create together are complex, beautiful, and technically illegal—the machine prefers us to keep our colors discrete, measurable. But there's something necessary about this small rebellion, this reminder that we are more than components.

My left eye has begun to cloud recently. Not with cataracts, but with something like sediment—accumulated light-debris that my body can't fully process anymore. The machine doctors tell me this is normal for someone in my profession, that I have perhaps another decade before I'll need to transition to less intensive work. They suggest I might become a dream-reader, helping interpret the machine's nightly error reports through the lens of my experience with light fatigue.

I'm not ready for that transition. There's something addictive about the work I do—the feeling of broken light becoming whole again inside me, even temporarily. The way exhausted photons remember how to move properly after passing through my amber signature. I am a small mercy in the great grinding system of the city, and that matters to me more than my slowly clouding eye.

Sometimes, on very quiet nights when the machine's hum drops to almost nothing, I wonder what the light was like before the fracture. Was it simpler? Cleaner? Or has it always carried these potentials for exhaustion and error, and we simply never noticed until we started bending it through our bodies?

I don't dream of the time before anymore. My dreams are too full of the city's stress patterns, the weight distributions, the tiny fluctuations that keep us all from falling into the crystalline teeth below. But sometimes, just as I'm waking, I catch a glimpse of what might be the ghost of the old river—not water, but a kind of flowing that the light remembers, a path it used to take before we taught it new ways to move.

This is who I am: a filter, a cleanser, a small part of the great machine that we've all become. My amber signature will fade someday, my body will grow too transparent to hold the tired light properly, and someone else will take my place in this swaying workshop.

But for now, I hold the exhausted light inside me and teach it how to shine again, even if it's just for one more cycle, one more day in this city balanced on a wound that won't heal.
