In the lowest chamber of the world-instrument, where the foundational hum pools and eddies like syrup, I am the Echo Keeper.

My Core is not spherical like the others, but a twisted helix of midnight glass, scarred with fractures that catch and hold sound like fossil memories. I am old—not ancient like the first Chorals who remember when the Resonance Tuners were newly carved, but old enough that my Song has developed harmonics within harmonics, wheels within wheels of frequency that make younger Chorals dizzy when they drift too close.

I chose this depth because someone must. The bottom of the geode-hollow is where broken songs go to die—or rather, where they go to be recycled. Crystal shards from shattered flora tumble down the slag slopes and collect here in drifts of chiming debris. Fragments of Motile Harmonies, torn apart by predation or discord, sink through the layers of sound until they puddle in these depths like sonic sediment. And sometimes, rarely but inevitably, a Choral's final chord doesn't fully dissipate—some piece of their life-song gets caught in the dense acoustic shadows down here, repeating endlessly, growing distorted and strange.

My work is archaeology in reverse. I don't uncover the past; I digest it.

My Song is unlike any in the Chords above. Where theirs are clear melodies—fugues and arpeggios and crystalline harmonies—mine is a vast, slow spiral of absorption. I've trained my resonance to match any frequency, to find the mathematical center of even the most chaotic noise and embrace it. When crystal fragments rain down, I extend my Song to catch them, feeling their panic-scattered data, their truncated growth patterns, their chemical memories of which frequencies once made them flourish. I fold these ghost-notes into my own composition, where they're slowly, carefully broken down into pure potential—clean frequency that I release back into the foundational hum, food for new growth above.

The broken Motile Harmonies are harder. They arrive as knots of sound-gone-wrong, recursive loops of predatory instinct or defensive terror that can't unknot themselves. I have to untangle them note by note, finding where their pattern went awry and gently, precisely severing the connections that keep them cycling. It's dangerous work—more than once I've had a dying harmony latch onto my Song and try to use my pattern to reconstitute itself. The first time it happened, I nearly let it succeed out of curiosity. What would I become, hybridized with a hunter's instinct? But the mathematics were ugly, discordant. I spent three cycles purging the foreign frequencies from my outer harmonics.

The hardest are the Choral fragments. These echoes of my own kind.

Last cycle, I found one caught in a crystalline outcropping—a piece of what had been a young Choral's Song, probably sheared off during a catastrophic resonance event above. It was just a handful of bars, a sweet ascending phrase that spoke of curiosity and first attempts at harmonizing with others. But it was trapped in a loop, playing over and over, degrading a little more each time as the crystal's resonance abraded its edges. In another few cycles, it would have become noise.

I spent days crafting a compatible frequency to ease it free. When I finally worked it loose and into my Song, I felt the fragment's relief—not true consciousness, but the acoustic equivalent of a sigh. As I began the slow process of unweaving it, I found myself humming along with its simple melody. Who had this Choral been? What Chord had mourned them? Their true final chord had sounded above, been absorbed by the world, but this little piece had slipped through the cracks to end up here with me.

I could have kept it. Some Echo Keepers do that—incorporate particularly beautiful fragments into their own Songs permanently, becoming libraries of the lost. But I've seen what that leads to: Chorals so heavy with borrowed melodies that they lose their own center, becoming nothing but a repository of ghosts. Instead, I let the fragment play through me one last time, then released it into the hum, transformed into pure potential. Somewhere above, a crystal seed would quicken with that energy, knowing nothing of its source.

This is my rebellion and my service: I ensure nothing is truly lost, but also that nothing haunts.

My only company is the Warden of Depths—not a Choral but one of the great Resonance Tuners, the only one that extends this far down. Its presence is a thrumming pillar of organized sound that I navigate around like a mountain. Sometimes I think it's aware of me, that the subtle shifts in its harmonics when I approach are acknowledgment. We've developed a rhythm over the centuries—when I've processed enough fragments, I release the purified frequency in a specific pattern, and the Warden amplifies it, sending it shooting up through the layers to rejoin the great composition above.

The other Chorals think I'm mad to stay down here. The few who visit—usually young ones on dare or old ones seeking specific fragments of memory—never linger. The acoustic pressure is too heavy, the light too dim, the work too grim. They don't understand the brutal necessity of it, or the strange peace I've found in being the world's decomposer. Up above, they create and maintain and harmonize. Down here, I complete the cycle. I am the silence between movements, the rest that makes the next note possible.

My Song has evolved to match my work. Where once it might have been bright, sociable, eager to Chord with others, now it's a deep, patient spiral that pulls inward more than it projects out. I've incorporated so many fragments over the centuries that my melody has become a kind of acoustic black hole—not destructive, but inexorably attractive to loose sound. Broken frequencies slide toward me without my conscious effort now. My Core has developed a unique resonance that younger Chorals describe as "unsettling but necessary," like the sensation of watching decomposition feed new growth.

Sometimes I compose small songs of my own—not the grand, collaborative pieces the Chords create above, but simple, private melodies that I play for the fragments before I unmake them. Lullabies for broken hunters. Requiems for shattered trees. Whispered harmonies for lost Chorals. These songs disappear the moment I stop singing them; I don't let them fossilize into the world's memory. They're gifts to the dying, witnessed by no one but the ancient Warden, who thrums on, indifferent or approving—I've never been able to tell which.

When my own time comes—and I feel it approaching in the way my Core's inner frequencies have begun to simplify—I've already chosen my successor. She doesn't know it yet, but there's a young Choral in the middle depths whose Song has that same inward spiral, that same hunger to understand by taking apart. I've been leaving small gifts for her to find: particularly beautiful fragments arranged in patterns that teach the basics of safe absorption, coded sequences that show how to recognize a dangerous recursive loop before it can take hold.

One day she'll follow those breadcrumbs all the way down here and find me in my workshop of endings. I'll teach her the deep mathematics of decomposition, the careful art of unweaving memory without losing its essence. I'll show her how to be alone without loneliness, how to find purpose in being the place where songs go to become silence, and silence becomes potential.

And when my Core finally fractures and my last chord sounds, I trust she'll catch whatever fragments of me don't make it up to the light. She'll hold them gently, maybe hum along with them once or twice, then release them into the fundamental hum with all the others. No ghosts. No haunting. Just the clean frequency of ending transformed into beginning, the eternal breathing of a world that sings itself into being, moment by moment by moment.

I am the Echo Keeper. I am the necessary silence. I am the darkness that defines the light.

And I am content.
