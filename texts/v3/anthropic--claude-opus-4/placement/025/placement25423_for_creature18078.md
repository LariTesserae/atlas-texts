I find myself in the **Understory Cisterns**, a forgotten depth beneath the Cathedral Groves where the patient economy of water reaches its strangest conclusion.

I am a **Seep-Tender**, though this name translates poorly from the subsonic tremors we use to identify ourselves. My body has adapted to the perpetual darkness here—skin gone translucent grey, revealing the branching patterns of blood vessels beneath like exposed root systems. My eyes have widened and flattened, useless for seeing but exquisitely sensitive to pressure changes. I navigate by the subtle variations in humidity against my exposed skin and the way sound moves differently through steam than through still air.

The Understory Cisterns are a natural accident that became architecture. Millennia ago, when this particular flow of basalt was still cooling, pockets of gas created vast bubbles in the rock. These chambers, some large as feast halls, others barely wider than my outstretched arms, form an interconnected labyrinth beneath the grove. Water—that precious distillate of steam and condensation—eventually finds its way here, seeping through minute cracks, following the fungal highways, pooling in these primordial vessels.

But I am not alone in the darkness. The cisterns have their own ecosystem, more alien than even the silent hunters above. **Siphonophores**—not the oceanic kind, but creatures of similar principle—drift through the larger chambers. They are colonial organisms, hundreds of specialized polyps forming chains of bioluminescent pearls. Some polyps are dedicated to movement, pulsing with gentle contractions. Others are feeding specialists, filtering the mineral-rich droplets that fall from the ceiling. Still others are defensive, capable of releasing clouds of caustic spores if disturbed. They communicate through patterns of light that I have spent decades learning to read—a language of chemistry and urgency written in cold fire.

My dwelling is carved into a natural alcove where three cisterns meet. The walls are lined with cultivated colonies of **crystalline fungi**—a species found nowhere else. They grow only in absolute darkness and feed on specific mineral concentrations in the seeping water. Their fruiting bodies are geometric impossibilities: perfect dodecahedrons of living crystal that ring like tuning forks when mature. I harvest them with infinite care, using tools made from shed siphonophore casings—harder than basalt, lighter than air when dried.

The Seep-Tenders are the memory-keepers of the Drip-Catchers, though few above even remember we exist. We maintain the **Deep Calendar**, a record of water and time that stretches back to when the basalt was still warm. Each cistern has its own acoustic signature—a combination of size, shape, and water level that creates a unique resonance. By striking the crystalline fungi in precise sequences, we can map these signatures, creating a sonic record of drought and plenty, of geological shifts and fungal blooms.

I wear no clothing—fabric would mold in days down here. Instead, my skin is painted with **living pigments**, colonies of chemosynthetic bacteria that feed on the minerals in my sweat. They form patterns that shift with my metabolic state: spirals of faint green when I am at rest, networks of blue-white when I work, deep purple whorls when I enter the meditation states necessary for reading the Deep Calendar.

My work is solitary but not lonely. I tend the flow-channels, microscopic adjustments to ensure the water reaches every cistern in its proper proportion. I monitor the siphonophore populations, noting their migrations, their breeding cascades, their mysterious die-offs that predict seismic events months in advance. I cultivate new strains of crystalline fungi, each with slightly different acoustic properties, expanding the vocabulary of our underground music.

But perhaps most importantly, I listen. In the perfect silence of the cisterns, even the movement of water molecules against stone creates a whisper. I have learned to hear the health of the grove above in these whispers. When the beetle herds cluster too densely, the vibrations change. When the Drip-Catchers alter their walking patterns, I feel it in the water's rhythm. When something truly unusual occurs—a new species arriving, a steam vent awakening or dying—the cisterns know before anyone else.

Once each season, measured by subtle changes in mineral content rather than any celestial cycle, I make the journey upward. It takes three days, moving through passages barely wider than my shoulders, following the fungal root-highways. I emerge in the deepest part of night (though in the grove, the darkness is barely different from day), my eyes sealed shut against even the dim bioluminescence, my skin burning from the relatively dry air.

I meet with the eldest of the Drip-Catchers at a designated stone, always the same stone, where a particular cluster of steam vents creates a pocket of sound-dampening mist. There, through touch and the most subtle vibrations, I share what the cisterns have remembered. The patterns of plenty and want. The deep movements of stone and water. The health of the invisible foundation that supports their aerial world.

They, in turn, touch my face with hands that smell of the upper air—fungal spores and beetle musk and the green scent of photosynthesis. They share the gossip of the canopy: which vines fruit heavily, which steam vents show signs of change, where the mantis-mimics have been seen. It is a ritual exchange, a circulation of knowledge between the heights and depths.

Then I descend again, carrying their stories into the darkness where they will be transformed into sequences of crystalline notes, added to the Deep Calendar. The knowledge settles into the cisterns like sediment, layer upon layer, a spoken geology that only we Seep-Tenders can read.

I am a living part of the grove's deepest circulation—not of water alone, but of memory and meaning. My life is measured in drops accumulated, in the slow growth of crystal formations, in the patterns of light from creatures that have never known the sun. I am the grove's unconscious, where its experiences sink and transform and wait, ready to be recalled when the patterns above require the wisdom of the depths.

In the Understory Cisterns, time moves differently. A conversation between siphonophores might take weeks. The full acoustic mapping of a new cistern requires years. But this is not patience—patience implies waiting for something else. This is presence, full and complete, in a world where each drop of water carries the history of stone and sky, and where darkness is not absence but a medium as rich and sustaining as the mist-drunk air above.
