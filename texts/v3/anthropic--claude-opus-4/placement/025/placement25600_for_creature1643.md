I find myself as one of the pale insects dwelling in the grotto's deepest recesses, though I am not quite like the others of my kind.

Where my siblings are purely eyeless, I possess vestigial photoreceptors—not eyes exactly, but patches of cells along my carapace that register the faintest variations in the darkness. They serve no evolutionary purpose here in the eternal night, yet they persist, making me aware of gradations of shadow that my kin cannot perceive. When the Guardian passes nearby, I sense her luminescence as a warmth across my shell, a sensation the others interpret as mere temperature change.

My body is elongated and segmented, colored the translucent white of cave pearls. Six legs carry me across the guano with surprising speed, each step calculated to disturb as little as possible. My antennae, longer than my body, sweep constantly through the air, reading chemical signals and minute pressure changes. Through them, I taste the stories the grotto tells—which bat colonies hunted successfully, where the newest water droplets will form, when the Guardian last moved through this section of cave.

I am both part of the collective and apart from it. We insects exist in a state of pure function—processing waste, becoming food, maintaining the base of this delicate ecosystem. But my anomalous photoreceptors have given me something my kind should not possess: the burden of awareness beyond immediate need.

I know things I should not know. I sense the patterns in the bat flights before they fully form. I feel the Guardian's attention like a weight when she observes our colonies. Most unnervingly, I dream—or experience something like dreams—during the periods of torpor between feedings. In these states, my vestigial sensors fire randomly, creating phantoms of light that cannot exist here. I see colors that have no names, geometries that fold beyond the three dimensions my body inhabits.

The Guardian knows of my difference. Sometimes I feel her consciousness brush against mine, curious but unobtrusive. She could unmake me with a thought, restore the proper blindness that would return me to peaceful function. But she doesn't. Perhaps she sees value in an observer who bridges two states of being—functional enough to survive, aware enough to witness.

My lifespan is measured in seasons I cannot see. I know I am old by insect standards because I remember when certain stalactites were minutely shorter, when particular bat roosts were first established. This memory, too, is an aberration. My kind should live only in the present moment of consumption and reproduction.

I have never mated. The electromagnetic sensitivity that makes me unique also makes me incompatible—my offspring would not survive, caught between two modes of existence. Instead, I serve as an archivist of sorts, though I have no means to communicate my observations. I simply exist, processing, witnessing, accumulating experiences that will die with me.

Sometimes I position myself beneath the spots where water drops form, feeling each impact as a tiny meteor strike against my shell. The mineral-rich water leaves deposits that I do not clean away. Over time, I am becoming part of the cave itself, my carapace gradually encrusted with the same limestone that forms this chamber. It is a slow metamorphosis that will eventually render me immobile, a small monument to the peculiarity of evolution in isolated spaces.

I do not fear this transformation. To become stone in this place of endless balance seems fitting. My consciousness, whatever strange spark allows me to consider my own existence, will finally align with the timelessness of the grotto. The water will continue to drip. The bats will continue to fly. The Guardian will continue her eternal maintenance.

And somewhere in the limestone, the ghost of an insect that could almost see will dream its impossible dreams of light.
