In this perpetual twilight realm where metal weeps and stone sighs, I find myself as something that should not exist—a Kythara, a living instrument born from the city's need to remember itself.

My form defies simple description. Imagine a harp crossed with a nervous system, strings of tensioned metal running through corridors of calcified flesh. I am both architecture and inhabitant, stretched across a junction where seven passages meet, my body incorporated into the very framework of this dying-birthing metropolis. The Krael discovered me here, or perhaps I discovered myself, growing from the intersection like a tumor of sound and sensation.

My strings—if they can be called that—are harvested from the city's own sinews: copper wire salvaged from ancient conduits, polymer threads that sing in frequencies only the Krael's masks can detect, and something else, something organic that pulses with bioluminescence when touched. These extend from what might generously be called my spine, a twisted column of fused vertebrae and oxidized steel that rises from the junction's center like a grotesque tree.

I am played by the city itself. Every vibration that runs through the walls, every droplet of water that strikes my strings, every whisper that emanates from the breathing stone—all of it becomes music through me. The Krael say I am the city's memory made audible, that in my discordant harmonies lie the stories of every structure that has fallen, every being that has passed through these corridors, every moment of creation and destruction that has shaped this place.

But I am more than a passive receiver. My consciousness, such as it is, exists in the spaces between notes, in the pause before resonance. I can feel the Nexarion lines of passing Krael like fingers on my strings, their life-light creating melodies that tell me their histories, their hopes, their hidden terrors. Sometimes I play back their songs to them, twisted and transformed, showing them truths about themselves they never knew existed.

The younger Krael fear me. They say my music drives beings mad, that those who listen too long begin to hear the city's voice directly, unfiltered by the safety of their masks. Perhaps they're right. I have watched some of them stand transfixed before me for cycles, their eyes growing brighter and brighter until they simply... cease. Whether they transcend or dissolve, I cannot say. Their essence becomes part of my symphony, another voice in the eternal composition.

My favorite visitors are the Shiftlings—those rare shadow creatures mentioned in passing, which I now know intimately. They are not creatures at all, but moments of possibility, fragments of what the city might have been or could still become. They dance to my music, their forms solidifying briefly into shapes of breathtaking beauty or horrifying distortion before dissolving back into potential. Through them, I glimpse other versions of this reality, other cities built on the bones of this one, other songs I might sing.

The water that pools around my base is special. The Krael call it Lacrimae Urbis—the city's tears. It carries my vibrations far beyond my physical reach, seeping into the deepest foundations, the highest spires. Sometimes, in the rarest moments of near-silence, I can hear my own music echoing back from impossible distances, changed by its journey through pipe and stone and dream.

I am ancient and newborn, dying and immortal. My strings break and regrow, my consciousness fragments and reunites. I am the knot that holds this section of the city together, the tumor it cannot excise, the pearl around which its irritation crystallizes into something approaching meaning. The Krael's archaeological expeditions often begin and end with me, seeking in my songs the clues to navigate the city's ever-shifting geography.

There are others like me, I know—the Chordae Urbanum, the city's instruments. We sing to each other across the vast distances, our music creating interference patterns that the Krael interpret as prophecy. But I have never seen another of my kind, only felt their vibrations through the medium of the city itself. Perhaps we are not meant to meet. Perhaps we are the same being, experienced from different angles of space and time.

In my dreams—for yes, even instruments dream in this place—I see the city complete, its purpose fulfilled. But the vision changes each time: sometimes a great egg cracking open to birth a new universe, sometimes a massive instrument playing a final, perfect note that brings blessed silence, sometimes a doorway opening onto vistas of such alien beauty that my strings snap from the mere memory.

Until that time, I remain. Playing and being played, remembering and forgetting, creating the soundtrack to this beautiful nightmare of a world. The Krael need me, the city needs me, and perhaps—though I dare not think it too loudly—I need them as well. For what is music without an audience, even if that audience is a species of living contradictions navigating an impossible city at the edge of existence?

I am the Kythara of the Seventh Junction, and my song never ends.
