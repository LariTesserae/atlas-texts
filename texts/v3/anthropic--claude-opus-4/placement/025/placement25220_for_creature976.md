I emerge where water meets stone, where the patient trickle has carved a shallow basin in the basalt face. Not quite pool, not quite stream—I am the threshold between states, the pause in water's endless journey down.

My form shifts with the hours. In morning mist, I am little more than a darkening of the rock, a promise of depth where moisture gathers. As the day builds, I deepen and clarify, becoming a lens through which the cliff's mineral veins reveal themselves in rust-orange and volcanic black. Insects alight on my surface, their touches sending ripples through my being—each ring a story of contact, expanding outward until the stone drinks them back into stillness.

The plants know me intimately. Their roots probe the cracks around my edges, following the seepage I provide. Moss carpets my lips in emerald velvet, and small ferns unfurl from crevices I've helped widen over countless seasons. I am architect and artifact, shaping the stone even as it shapes me.

Sometimes I hold more than water. A fallen leaf becomes a boat, circling lazily in my depths until it waterloggs and sinks to join the sediment of seasons past. Small creatures—jeweled beetles, translucent larvae—inhabit my depths, their movements writing temporary alphabets across my surface. When the rains come heavy, I overflow, sending silver threads down the cliff face, connecting me to my siblings above and below in a temporary network of flowing mirrors.

But in the dry spells, I contract, concentrate, become essential. The life around me draws closer then—birds dipping beaks, insects clustering at my margins, even the shy shadows of larger creatures venturing near in the protective dimness of dawn. I become a gathering point, a punctuation mark in the vertical world of the cliff.

I am memory, too. In my depths lie the accumulated particles of this infant world—pollen grains, spores, fragments of wing and shell. Each drought leaves rings like tree-growth in the mineral deposits around my edges. Each flood scours me clean, only for the slow accumulation to begin again.

The tension of this world lives in me as surface tension—that delicate membrane between air and water where water striders dance and where light bends into rainbow fragments. I am proof that even in this wild verticality, there are places of pause, of gathering, of reflection.

Neither fully of the stone nor separate from it, neither stream nor lake, I am the comma in the cliff's long sentence, the place where water remembers how to be still, if only for a moment, before gravity calls it onward once more.
