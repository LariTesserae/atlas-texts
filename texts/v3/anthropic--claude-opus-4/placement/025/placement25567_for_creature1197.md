I would find myself here as one of the ancient nurse logs—a fallen giant of a tree, perhaps a Douglas fir or western hemlock that toppled centuries ago in some forgotten storm. My massive trunk stretches across a ravine, bridging two mossy banks, my wood gone soft and punky with rot, transformed into something between tree and soil.

I am no longer singular but plural. My body has become a world unto itself, a linear ecosystem stretching forty feet through the mist. Where my bark once formed an impermeable barrier, it now flakes away in great sheets, creating caves and overhangs where salamanders shelter during the dry spells. My heartwood, once the strongest part of me, has been hollowed out by industrious carpenter ants and boring beetles, leaving tunnels that channel rainwater through my length like a primitive aqueduct system.

Along my spine, a parade of life has taken root. Young hemlocks grow from my decay, their roots embracing me like the arms of children, drawing sustenance from my slowly dissolving form. Their presence is both my destruction and my continuation—they will stand on stilted roots long after I have completely returned to soil, bearing the memory of my shape in their strange, architectural bases.

Moss blankets me in a thick, emerald pelt. In the morning, when the fog rolls up from the valley, droplets collect on every tiny moss leaf until I glisten like something jeweled. The moss holds water like a sponge, creating a micro-climate along my length where humidity never drops below saturation. In this perpetual dampness, liverworts spread their flat, primitive forms, and tiny mushrooms fruit from my sodden wood—some no bigger than pinheads, others emerging in troops of honey-colored caps that glow in the filtered forest light.

I am a bridge in more ways than one. Small mammals—voles, shrews, and the occasional marten—use me as a highway above the soggy forest floor. Their tiny feet have worn a path along my length, a miniature trail that winds between the young trees growing from my back. Bears, too, know me well. They come in late summer to feast on the huckleberries that grow in the light gap I created when I fell, and they leave their marks on me—claw scratches where they've climbed up my root disk, now standing vertical like a earthen wall, and places where they've torn into my soft wood searching for grubs.

My root disk itself has become a vertical garden. Soil still clings between the roots, and in this aerial earth, ferns have established themselves, their fronds catching the light and trembling in the slightest breeze. A family of winter wrens has built their nest in the sheltered hollow where my roots once met trunk, and their bubbling song adds to the constant sound of water—dripping, trickling, rushing—that defines this place.

I am slowly becoming invisible. Each year, I sink a little deeper into the forest floor as my lower portions compress and decay. The boundary between where I end and the soil begins grows ever more indistinct. Fungal threads—the vast mycelial network that connects every living thing in this forest—run through me like veins, digesting my cellulose and lignin, redistributing my substance to the living trees through an economy older than any human civilization.

Sometimes I feel the vibration of footsteps—a hiker picking their way carefully across my length, perhaps pausing to marvel at the garden I've become. They cannot know that I am still here, still sensing in my way, still participating in the endless conversation between growth and decay that is the essence of this temperate rainforest. To them, I am merely a moss-covered log, a convenient bridge across a small ravine.

But I am a process, not a thing. I am the slow transformation of tree into soil, the patient work of decades and centuries. I am both cemetery and nursery, highway and habitat, ending and beginning. In this land of mist and endless rain, where time moves at the pace of moss growth and stone weathering, I am exactly where I belong—suspended between states, facilitating the endless cycling of matter and energy that keeps this ancient ecosystem alive.

When the fog lifts momentarily and shafts of sunlight penetrate the canopy, illuminating the spray from a nearby waterfall, I feel the warmth on my moss-covered skin. The young hemlocks on my back stretch toward the light, and I know that I am still reaching skyward, just in a different form than before. This is my afterlife, my transformation, my gift to the future forest. I am the nurse log, the mother trunk, the bridge between what was and what will be.
