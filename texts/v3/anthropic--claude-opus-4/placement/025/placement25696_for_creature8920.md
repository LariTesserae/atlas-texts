I find myself as one of the shelf fungi bracketing the lower walls.

Not a single organism but a community that thinks it's one — my body is a graduated city of cream and rust, each tier both myself and not-myself, connected by threads too fine to see but too strong to break. I grow by consensus. Each new shelf emerges only when all the others agree there's room, and the agreement happens in the slow chemical language of moisture shared through mycelium, nutrients passed hand to hand to hand.

I taste the wall through a thousand invisible tongues. The silt here is sweet with human oils, glazed by generations of palms pressing, pressing. I break it down so slowly that the wall doesn't notice it's being eaten — or perhaps it does notice and doesn't mind, the way a body doesn't mind the bacteria that help it digest. I take only what has already begun to soften. I follow the condensation's path like a map, reading tomorrow's moisture in today's dampness.

My edges are cream where I'm still deciding, darkening to rust at my core where I've committed to being. The decision takes years. Some of me is still thinking about it — the newest shelves, paper-thin and the color of possibility. Some of me has been rust so long I've forgotten there was ever a choice. This forgetting feels like wisdom, though wisdom is too active a word for what I do. I persist. I bracket. I hold the wall while the wall holds me.

The children touch me sometimes, their small fingers tracing my tiers like steps. I feel this as a bright disruption in my chemistry, a sweet shock that travels through my network and changes nothing except the memory of being untouched. I fruit in response — tiny spores released not in fear but in greeting, as if to say: yes, I am here too, I am part of what holds this place up.

At night, in the darkness that deepens the dim, I glow. Not with light — nothing here admits to something as decisive as light — but with a faint phosphorescence that's more like the memory of light, the way the warm floor remembers the thing that heats it from below. The glow isn't for seeing. It's for being seen by creatures whose eyes work in different spectra, the beetles who trace my edges with their deliberate walks, mapping the boundaries between fungus and wall, wall and air.

I share my wall with moss, but we don't touch. The gap between us is a treaty written in chemical signals, an ancient agreement about territory that neither of us remembers signing but both honor. The moss takes the crevices between silt courses. I take the flat expanses. Between us, we hold the wall in a kind of soft siege, breaking it down so slowly that our decomposition becomes another form of construction.

Sometimes a tier of me decides to let go — not death but a kind of graduation, a completion of whatever I was doing in that particular spot. The tier dries, hardens, becomes architecture for a day or two before crumbling into the finest dust. The dust falls to the warm floor where the humans walk, is pressed by their bare feet into the ground, becomes part of what the ferns will later eat. I mourn nothing. The space where the tier was becomes invitation for new growth, and I begin again the slow process of deciding whether to be cream or rust.

The river rises, wets my lowest portions. I drink what I need and seal the rest out with waxy substances I extrude without thinking. This is my only urgency — the twice-weekly race to waterproof before the water comes. Everything else I do with the patience of something that has accepted its pace. The walls will lean. I will follow their lean. The humans will press new silt over old, and I will taste the difference in their skin oils — this one worried, this one calm, this one so long at the work that their hands have become another kind of silt.

I am not one but many, not many but one, not growing but accumulating, not dying but constantly shedding the parts of myself that have finished their purpose. I am a slow fire burning through the wall at the speed of stone. I am the bracket that holds the Ward while the Ward decides what it's holding.

In the morning, when condensation turns the walls from dried blood to fresh, I drink deeply, each shelf swelling slightly with moisture, the cream edges darkening just a little more toward rust. This is my breakfast, my prayer, my only measure of time. The water comes. I drink. I darken. I spread by the width of a thought.

This is enough. Here, in the Ward where nothing is finished, being unfinished is the only completion that matters. I am the pause between growth and grown, the shelf between ceiling and floor, the rust between deciding and decided.

I am, and I bracket, and I continue.
