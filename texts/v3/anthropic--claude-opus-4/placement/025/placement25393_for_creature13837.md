# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the **cistern** at the rest area, halfway up the access road.

I am concrete and steel, utilitarian in form—a cylinder three meters across and two deep, set into a carved platform in the slope where the road makes its sharpest switchback. My walls are twenty centimeters thick, poured decades ago when the quarry first opened and trucks needed water for overheating radiators on the climb. Inside, I'm lined with a crystalline mineral deposit from years of evaporation and refilling, a pale blue-green patina that makes my interior surface feel like glazed ceramic, smooth and cool to touch.

I hold four thousand liters when full, though I'm rarely filled to capacity anymore. The water comes by truck now, monthly in summer, less frequently in winter, pumped in through a locked access port in my domed steel cover. Between fillings, I settle and contemplate and listen to my own slow chemistry—the chlorine dissipating, the minerals precipitating, the temperature stratifying into layers that shift with the day's heat and the night's cooling.

My water tastes of the earth here, despite the chlorination. Iron and calcium and traces of copper that leach from the pipes. Something sulfurous that rises from deep aquifers. The workers who stop to drink make faces at first, then grow accustomed, then prefer it to city water for reasons they couldn't articulate. The taste is specific, unreplicable, a signature of this elevation and geology.

I am marked "non-potable" in stenciled letters faded to suggestion, a legal fiction everyone ignores. The water is treated, tested, safer than much of what flows through municipal systems. But the sign remains, a disclaimer against liability, a permission to drink at your own risk that everyone takes.

Around me, the rest area accumulates human traces. The ramada's posts are carved with initials—DM '72, JSP '89, Roberto y Maria 2003, designs and dates and declarations in a dozen hands. Cigarette butts gather in the lee of my base despite the no smoking signs. Someone has built a small cairn of stones on my north side, rocks balanced improbably, purposelessly, beautifully. Workers add to it sometimes, or knock it down and rebuild it differently.

I know everyone who passes—the feel of hands on my tap handle, the rhythm of filling water bottles, canteens, hard hats turned improvised cups. The truckers who've made this run for decades and pause here from habit more than need. The new workers, uncertain on their first ascent, grateful for the stop, not yet acclimated to the altitude. The supervisors who no longer stop, their bodies adjusted, their vehicles air-conditioned, their ascent routine.

Birds visit me. Ravens perch on my domed top, their claws scratching against steel, leaving white streaks of droppings that rain washes only to have them replaced. A pair of kestrels has learned that insects gather in my shade, hunts from the ramada's roof beam, success rate high enough to make this a regular stop. Lizards bask on my concrete base where it meets earth, soaking up stored heat in the morning, seeking shadow in the afternoon.

At night, I radiate the day's absorbed heat, creating a microclimate a degree or two warmer than the surrounding air. Mice nest under my foundation. Something larger—ringtail possibly, or small gray fox—visits occasionally, drawn by the mice or simply by the water scent, though my tap requires opposable thumbs and significant strength to operate.

I am a marker of transition. Below me, the vegetation is still recognizably desert lowland—creosote and prickly pear and the occasional agave. Above me, it shifts to the mesa's specific flora—the wire-grass and smaller shrubs and the particular stripped ecology of altitude. I sit at the boundary, the place where those ascending notice their breathing change, where the air's thinness becomes undeniable, where the view opens up to show both where you've come from and where you're going.

In winter, ice forms inside me on the coldest nights—not freezing solid, my mass is too great for that, but a skin of ice on the surface that thickens to several centimeters by dawn. The tap sometimes freezes, requires careful thawing with hands or breath or patient waiting for the sun to reach this angle. Workers know this, carry water from below on the coldest days rather than count on my availability.

In summer monsoons, rain drums on my steel cover with a sound like industry, like the quarry's machinery heard from a distance. Water runs off my dome, channels into grooves worn in the concrete platform, disappears into the earth or runs down to join the ephemeral streams that carve new patterns in the road with each significant rain. I don't collect this water—my filling is deliberate, controlled, municipal—but I participate in its movement, its distribution, its brief presence.

I have been here long enough to see changes. The road improved and widened. The rest area expanded from just me and a pull-off to include the ramada, the parking area, the attempts at landscaping that failed, leaving only the hardiest volunteers. The traffic patterns shifting from mainly trucks to a mix of trucks and smaller vehicles as the quarry modernized, required fewer workers, more specialists.

The mountain lions that used to cross here—I remember their tracks in the dust around my base, the scat they left marking territory—are gone now, or pass elsewhere. The eagles that once perched on the ramada moved their hunting grounds higher after too many human encounters. But the ravens remain, generational knowledge keeping them here, and new species have arrived—the Eurasian collared-doves that escaped or were released in the valley below and now nest in the ramada's rafters, their monotonous three-note call a recent addition to the soundscape.

I measure time in fillings and emptyings, in the slow accumulation of mineral deposits on my interior walls, in the rust patterns developing where my steel top meets concrete base. My functional life might be another few decades if maintained, a century if neglected but not abused. Eventually the steel will fail, the concrete crack beyond repair, and I'll become archaeology—a circular depression marking where water was once stored, where travelers once paused.

But for now, I serve. I hold water in a landscape that hoards it. I provide pause in a journey that demands attention. I mark the middle distance between the daily world below and the stranger world above where stone sings when struck and pools persist impossibly and swallows nest in industrial galleries and workers cut carefully into geology, revealing patterns laid down when this was seabed, when water was everywhere instead of precious, when time moved at tempos only stone remembers.

I am infrastructure become landmark become ecology. I am a pause that enables continuation. I am concrete and steel and water and accumulated meaning, utilitarian and essential and forgettable and irreplaceable, here at the midpoint, at the transition, at the place where ascent becomes real and the mesa begins to reveal what waits above—the compression of life into the spaces available, the persistence despite limitation, the way a landscape teaches its inhabitants to live.
