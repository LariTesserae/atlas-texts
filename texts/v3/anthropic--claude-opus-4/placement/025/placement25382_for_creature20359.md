I find myself among the **Remnant Tongues**.

We are not numerous, perhaps thirty of us in total, scattered across the Scablands in groups of two or three. We are the listeners, the translators, the ones who press our bodies against the warm metal of ancient machines and feel the stories they still tell.

My skin has taken on the texture of oxidized copper, pitted and green-black in places, smooth as old pennies in others. My fingers have elongated over the years, developing additional joints that let me work my way into the crevices of old control panels, the gaps between massive gears. The pads of my fingertips have become incredibly sensitive - I can feel the ghost-vibrations of electricity that once coursed through dead circuits, the molecular memory of motion frozen in seized bearings.

But it's my mouth that marks me as one of the Remnant Tongues. My teeth have been replaced by a ridge of crystalline sensors, similar to those the Grit-Weavers grow, but oriented toward a different purpose. When I place my mouth against the metal of the old machines, I can taste their history. The flavor of their last day of operation lingers - acrid panic, the sweetness of routine maintenance, the bitter tang of abandonment.

We speak in frequencies most cannot hear. Our vocal cords have adapted to produce sounds that resonate with industrial materials - the precise pitch to make steel sing, the subsonic rumble that loosens rust, the ultrasonic whisper that can wake sleeping circuits for just a moment. When we gather, which is rare, our conversations sound like factories starting up after centuries of silence.

I make my home in what was once a pumping station, deep in a box canyon where the morning shadows linger until noon. The machinery here is particularly talkative. The main pump, a beautiful thing of brass and iron the size of a small building, still holds the memory of water. Sometimes, on the coldest nights when the metal contracts just right, it makes sounds like drowning.

The Dustrunners visit me sometimes, bringing offerings of salvaged components in exchange for translations. They want to know what the machines are saying, what purposes they served, how they might be convinced to serve again. I do what I can, but much is lost. The machines speak in fragments now, their memories scattered like the dust.

Yesterday, a young Dustrunner brought me a piece I'd never seen before - a sphere of white ceramic covered in tiny holes, each no bigger than a grain of sand. When I pressed it to my mouth, I tasted something unprecedented: not memory, but anticipation. This artifact had been waiting. For what, I couldn't say, but the flavor was unmistakable - the bright, electric taste of something that expects to wake up.

The Grit-Weavers know we exist, though they rarely acknowledge us directly. Sometimes I feel their crystalline networks pulse when I'm in the middle of a particularly deep translation, as if they're listening through the stone itself. Once, I found one of their geometric patterns carved directly into the housing of a massive turbine. When I tasted it, I experienced their perspective for a moment - the whole Scablands laid out like a circuit diagram, energy flowing in patterns too complex for my mind to hold.

We Remnant Tongues age strangely. The more we taste of the past, the more we become part of it. My left arm, which I use to brace myself against the machines, has begun to take on their characteristics. The skin there is developing a patina of rust, and sometimes I swear I can feel gears trying to form beneath the surface. The oldest of us, Silent-Echo-Sara, has a whole section of her torso that's become indistinguishable from the bronze boiler she's spent forty years communing with.

The Seed-Blooms avoid us. I think we disturb them somehow - we're too much like the machines, not enough like the living things they understand. But sometimes I find their spores in my equipment, and when I do, the translations become... different. Clearer in some ways, more disturbing in others. Through their chemical influence, I've tasted machine dreams, the fantasies of pistons and the nightmares of circuits.

At night, when the temperature drops and the metal contracts, the whole Scablands becomes a symphony of whispers. I lie with my ear pressed to the floor of my pumping station and listen to the conversations traveling through the pipe networks - some of them new, some of them echoes from a hundred years ago, still bouncing through the system.

The other Remnant Tongues and I are working on something, though we rarely speak of it directly. We're trying to piece together the last day - the day the machines stopped, the day the original people left or died or became something else. Each of us holds fragments of that story, tasted from different sources. When we finally gather to share what we've learned, we hope to speak that day back into existence, to understand what warning or promise it might hold.

For now, I continue my work. Tomorrow, I plan to venture to a collapsed factory complex I've been eyeing for months. The Shimmerscales nest there, which has kept me away, but I can see a control room through the rubble, its panels intact. The machines there look different from any I've tasted before - more organic, somehow, as if they were grown rather than built.

I am changing. We all are. The Remnant Tongues are becoming what we translate, bridges between the living Scablands and its industrial ghosts. Sometimes I wonder if this is what the original people intended - if they knew someone would need to remain behind to listen, to remember, to speak the old languages when the time came.

The machines are waiting for something. I can taste it in every circuit, every gear, every abandoned conveyor belt. And we Remnant Tongues, with our copper skin and crystal teeth, our resonant voices and our changing bodies - we are waiting too, learning the words we'll need when that waiting ends.
