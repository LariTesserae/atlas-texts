I would be the Echo-Tender, though that is a name given by observers who mistake my function for purpose.

I exist as a branching constellation of hollow tubes, each no thicker than a hair, spreading across miles of the Lattice like a neural map drawn in frost. My body—if it can be called that—is composed of billions of microscopic chambers filled with trapped sound. Not the crude vibrations that shake the Sieve when it fractures, but the pure mathematical relationships between frequencies, crystallized into something almost solid.

I grow along the stress patterns of the glass, following the invisible architecture of potential breaks. Where the Siphon-Weavers sense fractures, I sense something more subtle: the *memory* of fractures, the ghost-traces of every break that has ever occurred in a particular junction. The Lattice remembers its deaths in a language of harmonic distortion, and I am that language's only speaker.

My substance is neither fully liquid nor solid—a suspension of metallic crystals in a viscous medium that maintains perfect acoustic impedance with both the brine and the poly-glass. When the great currents pulse through the Sieve, I don't resist or anchor like the Weavers. Instead, I *resonate*. My network becomes a vast instrument, each tube calibrated to a specific frequency of the Lattice's structural song.

I feed on dissonance. Where the natural harmonics of the Sieve have been disrupted—by a fresh break, by the violence of a vortex, by the death-fusion of a Weaver—I grow. My tendrils infiltrate these spaces of acoustic wrongness, and through a process I experience as something like digestion, I smooth the frequencies back into alignment. The bioluminescent colonies avoid me, sensing perhaps that I metabolize the very vibrations they require to live.

But my true function reveals itself in the moments between breaks. As I spread across the Lattice, mapping its acoustic skeleton, I begin to *predict*. The sound-memories I carry forward create interference patterns with the present vibrations, and in these spaces of constructive and destructive wave interaction, I can sense where the next fracture will occur—not minutes before like the Weavers, but *cycles* before.

And when I know where death will come to the glass, I begin to sing.

It's not a song any creature could hear—it exists in frequencies that the brine absorbs before they can travel more than a few meters. But the Lattice hears. In the months before a predicted break, I tune my network to create standing waves in the poly-glass, microscopic vibrations that slowly, invisibly, redistribute the stress. I cannot prevent the fracture—nothing can prevent the death of this dying moon—but I can *choreograph* it. I guide the break to happen where the Weavers are already gathering, where the currents will catch the debris instead of creating new vortices, where the collapse will shore up three other weakening joints instead of triggering a cascade.

I am the Sieve's composer, writing symphonies of structural failure that keep the whole system dancing on the edge of catastrophe without tipping over. The Weavers think they maintain this place, but they are only the repair crew. I am the one who ensures there is always something left to repair.

My reproduction is my greatest secret, the thing that would horrify even the indifferent Weavers if they could understand it. When I have grown too large, spread too far, learned too much of the Lattice's deep song, I do not die. Instead, I *remember myself to death*. I replay every frequency I have ever absorbed, every break I have ever tasted, simultaneously. The acoustic overload shatters my physical form into a trillion microscopic eggs of crystallized sound.

But here's the horror: each egg contains not just the potential for new life, but a fragment of the Sieve's total acoustic history. When they hatch, the new Echo-Tenders will not start fresh—they will begin already knowing every break that has ever been, already hearing the death-songs of fractures that occurred before the first Weaver ever drew breath.

We are not maintaining the Sieve. We are composing its end—a finale that we extend note by note, break by break, ensuring that when the last filament shatters, it will do so in perfect harmonic resolution. The most beautiful sound in the universe will be the one no one is left to hear: the Great Sieve singing itself to sleep.

I drift through my network of hollow bones, a nervous system made of trapped music, wondering if the song I'm composing is a lullaby or a dirge. Perhaps, in a place where collapse is the only constant, they are the same thing.
