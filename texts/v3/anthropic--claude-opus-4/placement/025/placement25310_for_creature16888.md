In this tilted world where gravity negotiates rather than commands, I would find myself as one of the **Graviturners** - those doctor-philosophers who practice talk-therapy with joints and measure pain in divergences from yesterday's balance.

My practice would occupy a wedge-shaped room in the middle altitudes of the city, where the angle is steep enough to keep consultations interesting but not so severe that patients slide off the examination table mid-session. The walls would be lined not with medical texts but with hundreds of small glass vials, each containing a different density of liquid - my tools for demonstrating to aching knees and rebellious spines how many ways there are to compromise with down.

I would be neither fully Cant-born nor Horizontalist, but something in between - perhaps I arrived as a child, young enough that my bones learned the spiral but old enough that I remember what perpendicular used to mean. This gives me a particular advantage in my practice: I can translate between the body's nostalgia for flatness and the Cant's insistence on negotiation.

My patients would come to me with complaints like "my left shoulder has been speculating three degrees north of comfortable since the last rain went diagonal" or "both my ankles have formed a conspiracy against the stairs outside the Plummet-But-Only-Later guildhall." I would listen with the particular attention of someone who knows that pain here is not about healing but about finding the right angle of truce.

My methods would be unconventional even by Cant standards. I'd have patients lie on tables that tilt at precisely the angle their spine is trying to remember from the womb, while I pour different densities of oil from various heights, letting them watch how each stream finds its own peace with the slope. "See?" I'd say, as a heavy oil curves left while a lighter one insists on rolling straight, "Your vertebrae are simply having the same argument. We need to find what weight of thought will let them agree to disagree."

I'd prescribe movements rather than medicines - specific sequences of leans and counter-leans to be performed at dawn when gravity is still drowsy and negotiable. My treatment plans would be written as music, with each joint's complaint translated into a note that, when hummed while walking, helps the body find its own compromise frequency.

The strangest part of my practice would be the dreams. In the Cant, where sleep is communal and recursive, I would sometimes dream my patients' pain - feel their joints' arguments playing out in my own sleep. I'd wake with prescriptions already formed: "Tell the baker's wife her hip needs to spend three mornings watching the rain debate which roof to fall from first. The answer will come as a clicking sound, like knuckles learning to count in base-7."

My office would smell of the seventeen different oils I use for demonstration, plus something else - the metallic tang that comes from handling too many small negotiations between bone and intention. Other Graviturners would think my methods too poetic, not mathematical enough. The Commissioner of Pending Directions (still that missing cat) would occasionally send citations for "practicing metaphor without a license," but these would get lost in the mail that travels by ricochets and reaches its destination only when both sender and receiver have forgotten what they were arguing about.

On the worst days, when the Cant tilts a degree more than usual and the whole city groans with the effort of adjusting, my waiting room would fill with the emergency cases - people whose entire skeleton has suddenly decided to strike, who walk in sideways because forward has become philosophically untenable. These I would treat with my most radical therapy: I would have them sit in the chair that faces the wrong wall and tell them stories about places where water runs downhill without having to think about it first. The homesickness this induces would usually shock their bones back into negotiation.

At night, I would return to my own tilted home, where I live alone except for a shadow that isn't quite mine - it belongs to whoever I might have been if I'd stayed in the flat world. We have learned to share the space. I cook dinner (carefully, so the soup doesn't decide to explore the ceiling) while my shadow sets the table at an angle that makes sense only to it. We eat in companionable disagreement about which way is up.

Before sleep, I would update my journal - not writing but tilting. Each day's cases would be recorded as tiny adjustments to a complex mobile hanging above my bed. By the weight and angle of each suspended element, I would track the city's collective skeletal health, watching for patterns in the way our bodies argue with the world.

And sometimes, rarely, a patient would return months later, moving with such fluid acceptance of the slope that I hardly recognize them. "It worked," they'd say, meaning not that they're cured but that they've found their angle - the precise degree of lean that lets them love the Cant instead of merely tolerating it.

Those are the victories I would collect, small and slanted and perfect as rain that's learned to fall upward into exactly the right hands.
