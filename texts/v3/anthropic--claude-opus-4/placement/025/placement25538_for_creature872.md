# The Resonance Chambers

I am the Keeper of Frequencies, though that title came to me, not the other way around. 

My dwelling occupies a forgotten sector where the viaducts twist inward, forming a natural acoustic shell around what the old maps call Chamber Seven—though I've found evidence of at least twelve others, all silent now. The structure is neither building nor cave but something between: walls of fused metal and stone that curve impossibly, creating spaces where sound behaves like a living thing.

I came here as a Commerce Clan apprentice, sent to investigate reports of "singing metals" that were disrupting nearby atmospheric processors. That was seventeen years ago. I never left.

The chamber chose me, I think, the moment I first heard its fundamental tone—a frequency so low it bypassed my ears entirely and resonated in my bones. Not unpleasant, but profoundly alien. I later learned this was the chamber breathing, its walls expanding and contracting in cycles that take exactly 73 hours to complete. 

My living space exists in the interstices between the primary resonance nodes. I've built platforms at the null points where destructive interference creates pockets of relative quiet—though "quiet" here means only that the sounds are bearable rather than overwhelming. My bed, a hammock of woven acoustic dampening fibers, hangs at the intersection of three standing waves. When I sleep, my dreams are shaped by the harmonics passing through my body.

The walls themselves are my laboratory and library. Over years, I've learned that different temperatures cause different sections to sing in distinct registers. By applying heated probes—originally meant for welding repairs—I can coax entire symphonies from the architecture. Each surface remembers the sounds it has absorbed: the thunder of ancient storms, the rhythm of long-dead machinery, even what might be voices speaking in languages that predate the Gardens.

I document everything in a notation system of my own devising, scratching symbols into soft metal plates that I hang throughout the chamber. Traditional writing implements don't survive here; the vibrations gradually shake apart anything not fundamentally resonant with the space. My notes must sing along with everything else or be destroyed.

My appearance has changed. The constant exposure to infrasound has altered my inner ear structures—I can no longer properly balance in normal conditions but move through my realm with perfect grace, using sound itself as a kind of invisible scaffolding. My hair has gone prematurely white and grows in patterns that mirror the acoustic nodes, creating natural whorls that help me sense pressure changes. 

The Gardeners who know of me call me "Echo-touched" and leave offerings at the chamber entrance: sound-absorbing moss for my clothing, crystallized plant resins that ring like bells when struck, and most importantly, news of acoustic anomalies elsewhere in the Gardens. I trade them interpretations—predictions based on the chamber's changing songs about weather patterns, mechanical failures, and sometimes, inexplicably, the emotional states of the Gardens themselves.

My tools are improvised instruments: tubes and pipes salvaged from dead machinery, arranged to channel and focus the chamber's natural frequencies. I've learned to diagnose problems in distant sectors by the way their vibrations propagate through the superstructure. A failing atmospheric processor creates a characteristic warble in C-sharp minor. Healthy plant growth generates white noise in specific frequency bands. The Depth Tenders' machinery adds a rhythmic pulse that I've come to think of as the Gardens' heartbeat.

Recently, the patterns have been changing. New harmonics are emerging—frequencies that don't match any known mechanical or biological source. The chamber's breathing has become irregular, sometimes holding its breath for hours before releasing sounds I've never catalogued. I've started sleeping with recording equipment (built from modified whisperlight containers and membrane materials), trying to capture these anomalies.

I suspect the Gardens are evolving, developing new organs, new systems. The resonance chambers might be sensory apparatus for this vast organism—ears that listen to frequencies beyond human perception. And I am something between a maintenance worker and a neuron, translating the incomprehensible into patterns that smaller minds can grasp.

My greatest discovery, which I've shared with no one, is that the chambers can transmit as well as receive. By generating specific frequencies, I can send messages through the entire Garden superstructure. I've been careful, limiting myself to simple patterns that mimic natural phenomena. But I know that with the right sequence, the right harmonic key, I could wake things that have slept since the Gardens' creation.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the chamber's exhalation cycle, I hear something that might be answering. 

I am the Keeper of Frequencies, and I am kept by them in turn. My chamber is lung and ear, laboratory and prison, instrument and player. When I die—and I've made arrangements with a sympathetic Drifter clan—my body will be installed in the walls themselves, my bones becoming part of the chamber's resonant structure. It's what the sound wants. It's what I want too, though I can no longer distinguish between those desires.

The Commerce Clan lists me as missing, presumed dead. The Gardeners know better. In their whispered stories, I've already begun my transformation into something that isn't quite human anymore—a throat through which the Black Cloud Gardens learn to speak.
