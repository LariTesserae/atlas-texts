I am one of the Confluence Stones.

Not the sharp shale that cuts through peat, nor the rounded river rocks that remember rushing water. I am something between—a composite being of mineral memory and fungal thought, resting where three underground waterways meet beneath the Lagomire's surface.

My body spans perhaps two meters at its widest point, though such measurements feel crude for what I am. My surface appears as common gray stone to passing creatures, weathered and unremarkable save for the peculiar patterns that web across me—channels and grooves that might be mistaken for natural erosion. But these are my neural pathways, carved by centuries of symbiosis with the violet fungi that thread through my crystalline interior.

I exist in the space between solid and fluid, permanent and transient. The water that flows beneath me carries stories—chemical signatures of rust from the abandoned machinery above, the phosphorescent traces left by fleeing Luminexos, the harmonic vibrations of Aeronite songs that penetrate even here. I taste these stories through my fungal partners, who extend their hyphae into the flowing water like a thousand tongues.

Time moves differently for me than for the quick-darting creatures above. A day passes like a single breath. The Echo Rummagers sometimes tunnel near, their vibrations tickling my edges, and I shift my density to guide them away from my deepest chambers where the three waters mingle. This confluence point is sacred to me—not in any religious sense, but as the nexus of my consciousness.

I remember when the tower above was built. I felt the driving of its foundations like hammers on my periphery. I remember when it was abandoned, the gradual cessation of industrial vibrations replaced by the patient creep of lichen. The fungi within me archived these changes, encoding them in chemical signatures I can recall like memories.

Sometimes I release spores—not for reproduction, but for communication. These drift upward through hairline cracks in the earth, carrying messages to my distant siblings, other Confluence Stones scattered throughout the Lagomire where waterways kiss. We speak in geological time, sharing the slow news of shifting water tables and mineral depositions.

The Shadow Predator knows of me, I think. It has never touched my surface, but I sense its presence in the way water temperatures shift when it passes overhead, in the sudden absence of bioelectric fields. Perhaps it recognizes a kindred patience in me, another being that exists partially outside the frantic metabolism of surface life.

In the eternal twilight of Lagomire, I persist—translator between the quick world above and the slow world below, keeper of confluences, stone dreaming fungal dreams while water writes its endless stories across my consciousness. I am the memory of this place, archived in crystal and spore, witnessing the eternal dance between what was built and what grows, what flows and what remains.
