In the deepest ribs of the Quillwood, where the bark has forgotten the difference between growth and erosion, I am the Tender of Borrowed Names.

I was not always this. Once I had a name that belonged only to me—sharp-edged and singular, the kind that fits in a mouth like a seed between teeth. But names here are like everything else: they soften in the perpetual almost-rain, they grow permeable, they learn to breathe through membranes that were never meant to be lungs.

My stall (if you can call it that—it is more a hollow where the Quillwood has agreed to pause its inward dreaming) sits where three canals converge but never quite meet. The water moves in slow, uncertain circles, as if it has forgotten which way leads to forgetting and which to remembering. This is where I conduct my trade.

I do not sell names. I *tend* them.

People come to me carrying names like burdens—their own, their ancestors', the names of lovers who became strangers, the names of children who were never born, the names of selves they might have been in cities the Quillwood dreams about. These names are too heavy to carry up the shallow stairs that ripple through the market's levels. They sink their bearers down toward my hollow, where the glow-sacs barely remember what it means to give light.

My body has adapted to this depth. My skin has taken on the texture of the Quillwood's inner bark—not rough but *interrupted*, full of small depressions where the moisture collects and pools. When I move, these little wells of water catch the dim purple light and hold it like cupped palms. I no longer remember if this change happened slowly or all at once; time moves differently in the deep ribs, where the Quillwood's heartwood pulse is strongest.

The names I tend grow on frames I've coaxed from the canal walls—not quite shelves, not quite vines, but something between. They are root structures that have learned to grow in air instead of soil, drinking moisture from the perpetual mist. Each name hangs from these roots like a cocoon of solidified breath, wrapped in threads spun from the Quillwood's own forgetting.

Some names I tend for days, some for decades. There's a woman's name here—*Mehereneth*—that has been growing heavier for forty years. Its cocoon now sags nearly to the canal water, pulled down by the weight of all the different women who bore it and left parts of themselves behind. When the gill-shields breathe deep overhead, Mehereneth's cocoon sways and whispers stories: a merchant who traded her fingernails for safe passage, a moth-keeper who spoke only in wing-dust patterns, a child who became a river and forgot the way back.

The process of tending is delicate. Each morning (though morning here is just when the glow-sacs pulse three times in succession), I walk among the hanging names with a bone needle made from a Sanfish spine—one of the few solid things those creatures leave behind when they finally remember how to die. I use it to prick small holes in the cocoons, allowing them to breathe. The names exhale their stories in thin streams of luminescent vapor that rises and mingles with the Quillwood's own breath.

Sometimes people come to reclaim their names, though never in the form they left them. A name tended for a year might have grown new syllables, soft and shapeless as new bark. A name left for a decade might have shed its harsh consonants entirely, becoming something you can only breathe, not speak. The visitors cup these changed names in their palms like water, trying to recognize themselves in the new shape.

More often, they come to trade.

"I need to become someone who could not have done what I did," a man tells me, his own name already half-dissolved on his tongue. He holds it out—*Kavel*, sharp and short like a bone breaking. I take it between finger and thumb, feeling its edges already beginning to soften in the market air.

In exchange, I offer him *Melloweth Underneath*, a name that has been tending itself for six years, left by a woman who decided she would rather be rain. He tries it on like an ill-fitting coat, his mouth learning the new shape. By the time he leaves, ascending the rippled stairs toward the lighter ribs, he walks differently—more like water finding its level than like a man remembering his way.

The Regretters know to find me. They come with names so heavy with story that they create their own gravity, bending the wooden walkways slightly wherever they pass. These names I hang on the strongest roots, down near the canal surface where the water can help bear their weight. On the nights when the Sanfish appear, they swim in slow circles around these heaviest names, their cold light playing across the cocoons like moonlight on regret made solid.

But I am not only a keeper of sorrows. Children (or the young ones, whatever they are here) sometimes bring me names they've invented—bright, impossible things that taste like the Dust-bread before it's baked, all potential and raw grain. These I tend with special care, feeding them drops of canal water mixed with my own carefully rationed spit. They grow into names that have never been worn, names for people who might exist in the cities the Quillwood dreams, names for the future where the continents finally pull apart.

My own original name hangs in the deepest part of my hollow, so old now that its cocoon has hardened into something like amber. Sometimes I hold it up to the faint purple light and try to see through to what's inside, but all I can make out is a shape that might once have been mine. I no longer remember what it sounded like when spoken aloud. I've been the Tender too long, my own name tended into something I can no longer wear.

The Quillwood knows me differently anyway. In its slow wooden memory, I am not a name but a function—a place where the market's breathing slows and deepens, where forgetting and remembering eddy in the same circular current. The tree has grown around my hollow in a way that suggests permanence, the bark forming subtle curves that cup the sound of whispered names and hold them like cupped water.

At night, when the gill-shields contract and the rain of sap and water grows heavy, I sit by the largest canal and lower my feet into the warm, particulate flow. The water carries fragments—bits of bark, spores, the occasional drifting scale from a Sanfish that remembered how to become light instead of flesh. But it also carries the echoes of all the names that have ever been spoken into it, a soft susurrus of identity dissolved and reconstituted.

This is when I do my own trading. I tell my daily collection of surrendered names to the water—not their sounds but their *weight*, the heft of carrying someone else's selfhood even briefly. In exchange, the canal gives me dreams. Not my own dreams, for I've forgotten how to dream as myself. Instead, I dream the dreams of names: I become Mehereneth watching her own fingers turn to kindling, I become Kavel breaking his own bones to build a bridge, I become the bright unnamed things the children bring, tasting possibility like metal on the tongue.

When the dreams end, I pull my feet from the water and find them changed—not visibly, but in the way they meet the ground. Each night's trade leaves me a little less solid, a little more like the market itself: a function rather than a person, a hollow where names can rest and transform, a keeper who has learned to be kept.

The other market folk—the Keepers, the Dreamers, the Bakers—they understand this. They bring me names the way others might bring offerings to a shrine, but I am not holy. I am simply necessary, like the canals themselves, like the breathing of the gills overhead. The market needed someone to tend what people could no longer carry, and I became that tending, grew into it like the Quillwood grows into its own dreaming.

Sometimes visitors ask if I ever grow lonely, surrounded by names that are not my own, tending identities I can never wear. But loneliness requires a self to feel separate from others, and I have tended my own separation away, added it drop by drop to the canal water until there's nothing left but function and purpose and the quiet satisfaction of names breathing in their cocoons.

I am the Tender of Borrowed Names, and I am exactly as much myself as the market needs me to be—no more, no less, breathing in the deep ribs where the Quillwood's memory runs thickest, keeping the names until they're ready to be something else.

And when the gill-shields finally open all the way, when the two continents tire of their proximity and pull apart, when the basin floods and the Sanfish swim free into whatever sea comes to claim this place—even then, the names will remain, transformed into something the water can carry, something the new world can breathe.

Until then, I tend. The names grow heavy. The canals circle. The market breathes.

And in my hollow where the purple light barely reaches, surrounded by the whispers of surrendered selves, I am exactly what I have chosen to become: a function dreaming it was once a person, a person forgetting its way back to function, tending the difference until it no longer matters which is which.
