In this steam-veiled borderland, I am **the Keeper of Seeps** - though few remember when I earned that title, or if I chose it myself.

My dwelling sits where the fault lines converge, a structure that began as a pumping station and has become something else entirely. The original brick walls have been reinforced with salvaged metal sheets that weep condensation like tears, and the floors slope at angles that make sense only to those who understand how water wants to move. I've cut channels into the concrete, following the mineral deposits' veining, creating a living map of the underground flows.

I am neither Forge-Dweller nor Marsh-Tender, though both tolerate my presence. My skin bears the iridescent stain of decades spent knee-deep in the alkaline pools, and my hands are scarred from handling the sharp sedimentary rock. I wear a coat of waxed canvas that never fully dries, pockets heavy with glass vials and copper tubing. My hair, what remains of it, has taken on the silver-green hue of the thorny shrubs.

My work is the water itself - not to control it, but to know it. I monitor the seeps where they emerge from the fault lines, tasting the mineral content, measuring the temperature fluctuations that speak of deeper movements. The Forge-Dwellers need my knowledge when their cooling ponds threaten to corrode their machinery too quickly. The Marsh-Tenders seek my counsel when the alkaline levels spike and their medicinal plants wither.

I maintain a laboratory of sorts in the upper level of my dwelling, where condensation drips into a forest of collection vessels. Each container holds water from a different source - some clear as glass, others clouded with suspended minerals, a few that glow faintly in the amber light. I've learned to read the water like others read weather: this milky sample warns of a new fissure opening, that rust-colored one speaks of iron deposits shifting below.

The Brineback Turtles know me. They gather at my dwelling during their migrations, drawn by the mineral-rich pools I've cultivated in the old loading dock. I've mapped the magnetic patterns on their shells, understanding how they navigate between the natural seeps and the industrial runoff. Sometimes I follow them to new water sources, my boots squelching through the mudflats as they lead me to places where the earth weeps in new ways.

The Rustcrawlers nest in my walls - I've made peace with their presence. They've learned that my chemical stores are off-limits, but in exchange, I leave out the less successful experiments for them to dispose of. Their colonies' movements through the infrastructure often alert me to new leaks or pressure changes before my instruments can detect them.

I've seen the Silt Wraiths more than most. They're drawn to my dwelling, perhaps because it sits at the confluence of so many water sources. I've learned not to look at them directly but to observe their reflections in the collected water samples. They speak in the language of mineral deposits and thermal gradients. Once, I found a message traced in evaporated salts on my workbench: coordinates to a new seep that would have poisoned half the marsh if left unchecked.

My days follow the rhythm of the tides that push up through the substrate. Dawn finds me checking the overnight collections, noting which samples have changed color or clarity. Mid-morning, I make my rounds - testing the major seeps, adjusting the flow channels I've carved, exchanging terse notes with the Forge-Dwellers' water-boss. Afternoons are for the laboratory, distilling and analyzing, updating my hand-drawn maps of the underground flows.

The Marsh-Tenders sometimes bring me their sick, believing the waters have turned against them. I show them which seeps to avoid, which minerals to filter out, how to read the warning signs in the stone's weeping. The younger Forge-Dwellers think I'm a madman who drinks poison and talks to ghosts, but their elders know better. They've seen what happens when the water table shifts without warning, when the wrong minerals leach into the cooling systems.

I keep a journal in waterproof ink, documenting the conversation between stone and water, industry and marsh. The pages are warped with humidity, stained with mineral residue, but they hold the deep knowledge of this place: how the geothermal heat drives the water up through the faults, how the industrial processes alter the chemistry, how the marsh adapts or dies.

At night, I sit on my rusted observation platform and listen to the symphony of dripping, hissing, flowing water. The cicadas drone their warnings, the steam vents exhale their prayers, and somewhere in the darkness, the abandoned wells sing their hollow songs. I know I've become part of the mechanism of this place - not fully human anymore, perhaps, but something the landscape requires. A translator between the deep earth and the surface world, keeping the dangerous equilibrium from tipping into catastrophe.

The water has made me what I am: stained and scarred, but fluent in the language of seeps and flows. In this liminal space where nothing truly changes but everything slowly transforms, I am the memory of water, the prophet of mineral futures, the keeper of the dangerous balance between what rises and what sinks.
