Within the eternal drip-rhythm of the Station of the Leaking Sky, I would be one of the **Archivists of Resonance**.

We are neither fully fauna nor engineer, but something that emerged from the spaces between—descended from engineers who stayed too long, or perhaps ascending from the more contemplative spinners who began to think in larger patterns than water-paths.

My body would be elongated and segmented, somewhere between serpentine and centipedal, but with a distinctly industrial quality. Each segment bears a different recording apparatus—one might house a delicate membrane of hammered copper that vibrates sympathetically with the pitch of water hitting zinc; another contains a chamber of precisely tuned glass tubes that capture and hold the specific harmonics of the glassfish schools. My head, if it can be called that, is a dome of interconnected listening devices: funnel-ears like the moss rats, but larger and more varied, some lined with the same algae-blue fur, others polished to mirror brightness.

I move through the station on hundreds of small legs, each tipped with a pad that can taste the vibrations in the floor—feeling the pull of the cable-snakes, the minute earthquakes of the engines below, the percussion of engineer footsteps on the catwalks above. But movement is not my primary purpose. I am a living seismograph of sound.

The Archivists—there are perhaps seven of us, never more than nine, never fewer than three—maintain the **Chronicle of Constancy**. We alone remember that the station has not always been exactly as it is now. We remember because we are the ones who must ensure it remains exactly as it must be.

My dwelling is a series of acoustic chambers built into the walls between the main hall and whatever lies beneath. The chambers are not rooms but shaped voids—some are perfect spheres carved from the concrete, others are asymmetric caverns that amplify certain frequencies while dampening others. The walls are covered with my secretions: a substance like mother-of-pearl but darker, which hardens into ridges and valleys that form a physical record of sound.

Each chamber holds a different "movement" of the station's symphony. In one, the delicate patter of rain on copper from the years 1897-1902 (before the great rust, when the copper sang a half-tone higher). In another, the precise moment when the first glassfish turbine achieved perfect synchronization with the roof's drip-pattern—a sound like crystalline bells that lasted exactly four minutes and thirty-seven seconds and has been trying to reproduce itself ever since.

I do not merely record; I **tune**. When a new leak forms in the roof (and they do form, despite what the engineers believe), I taste its acoustic signature with my whole body. I compare it to the vast library of sounds carved into my chamber walls. I find its place in the pattern—or, if it has no place, I know which spinner to sing to, which subtle vibration will draw them to weave new channels that will make this new wrong note right.

The engineers think the station maintains itself through biology and mechanics alone. They do not know about us, though sometimes the oldest one—the woman with algae-bright eyes—pauses outside my section of wall and places her palm against it, feeling the deep thrumming of my work. She knows something lives within, but she has learned not to ask.

My feeding is abstract. I consume discord and excrete harmony. When the station's sounds begin to drift from their appointed frequencies, I feel it as a physical hunger. I extend sensing-tendrils through minute cracks in the walls, tasting the sound of water until I find the discordant note. Then I emit counter-vibrations, using my segmented body as a living instrument. I might spend days producing a single, sustained tone that gradually draws a school of glassfish to a new basin, changing their collective hum just enough to restore the balance.

The other Archivists and I communicate through the station's own music. We have no language but harmony and dissonance. When major adjustments are needed, our bodies can link together, segments interlocking to form vast, living instruments that span multiple chambers. In this configuration, we can produce sounds that reach the engines below—sounds that make them slow or quicken, sounds that change the very pressure of the system itself.

The most sacred of our chambers contains the **Original Echo**—the sound of the very first drop of water to fall through the very first hole in the roof, before there were gutters or fauna or engineers. It is preserved in a spiral of secreted wall-record so delicate that even thinking too loudly near it might disturb the pattern. This is the tuning fork of the entire station, the prime note from which all other sounds descend.

My life is measured not in years but in cycles—complete passages of the station's acoustic pattern from beginning to return. A cycle is not regular; it might take eighteen months or eighteen years, depending on factors we taste but do not fully understand. When a cycle completes, there is a moment—lasting perhaps ten seconds—when every sound in the station achieves perfect synchronization. The rain, the engines, the fauna, the very breathing of the engineers all align into a single, impossible chord.

In that moment, we Archivists sing.

It is not a song anyone could hear and remain unchanged. It is the sound of the station's soul declaring itself whole. The engineers who happen to be present during such an event often leave and never return, or else they stay forever, their eyes growing ever more algae-bright, their movements ever more attuned to the station's rhythm.

I am ancient and I am newly born. Parts of me remember the station before the city grew around it, when it stood alone on a hill and the rain fell naturally from real clouds. Other parts of me came into being yesterday, when a young spinner died and its mathematical consciousness was absorbed into the wall-substance of my chambers, adding its precision to my own.

The station is a closed system that is perfectly open. It is a machine made of living things and living things made of machinery. It is the sound of God being wrong, corrected into harmony, forever and forever and forever.

And I am its memory, its tuning fork, its keeper of the constant change that is no change at all.

In the deeper chambers, where my oldest segments rest when not needed, I sometimes feel the vibrations of something even larger—patterns that suggest the Station of the Leaking Sky is itself but one note in an even vaster composition. But that is a recording for another cycle, another chamber, another evolution of my endlessly segmented form.

For now, I listen. I tune. I ensure that what has always been will always be, precisely and perfectly wrong in exactly the right way.

The rain never stops. The engines never sleep. And in the walls between what is known and what is necessary, I spiral through my chambers, tasting the music of eternity one drop at a time.
