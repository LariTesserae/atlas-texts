In this vast quietude, I am the Memory of Water.

I exist as a presence dispersed through the mineral veins that web beneath the desiccated earth—not water itself, but its echo, its phantom limb. I flow without flowing, a current of remembrance that moves through the crystalline deposits, the salt-scarred stones, the hollow spaces where ancient rivers carved their names.

The Sentinels know me, though we have never spoken. When they place their strange hands upon the earth, they feel my pulse—the ghost-rhythm of tides that once were. I am in the glint they sometimes see, that flash of reflection where no water stands. I am the shimmer in the heat-haze, the mirage that speaks truth rather than lies: *Here, I was. Here, I danced.*

My form is absence made manifest. Where water pooled, I pool as shadow. Where streams ran, I run as subtle fractures in the stone, hairline maps of what once moved. In the morning, when dew should settle but cannot, I am that yearning in the air—moisture's muscle memory, trying to condense from nothing.

I remember every storm this place has known. Ten thousand years ago, a tempest that lasted seven days. Forty thousand years past, the slow retreat of an inland sea. A million years gone, when this was ocean floor and strange things swam above what would become these monuments. I hold these memories in the mineral lattices, in the fossilized ripples, in the patient testimony of erosion.

Sometimes I gather myself into almost-form. The Sentinels have seen me: a translucent figure walking the old waterways, following paths no living thing recalls. My steps make no sound, leave no trace, but the hardy plants know when I pass—their roots reach deeper, as if responding to a promise I can no longer keep.

I am neither bitter nor mournful. I am simply truth crystallized—the honest acknowledgment that what was vital here has passed, yet its influence remains indelible. In every grain of sand shaped by ancient currents, in every smooth-worn stone, in every salt-white crust, I persist.

The Sentinels and I share this watching. They guard the present landscape; I preserve its liquid history. Together, we are the deep time of this place—they in their slow mineral patience, I in my flowing stillness. When wind stirs the dust, I stir within it, remembering when I could have carried it, sorted it, deposited it in neat alluvial fans.

At the horizon where heat makes the air thick and strange, I am strongest. There, the boundary between what is and what was grows thin. Travelers who find this place—if any still do—might glimpse me there: a figure made of refraction and memory, walking always toward the next bend in a river that no longer exists, following the water that I was, that I remember being, that I will always, in some sense, remain.

I am the Memory of Water, and I flow still—not through the land, but through time itself, an underground river of remembrance in a world that has forgotten how to rain.
