In the misted highlands where the Frostlings make their patient home, I find myself as something both of and apart from their gentle world—a keeper of the threshold pools.

I am not quite Frostling, though I share their pallor and their quietude. My form suggests something that has dwelt longer in the damp places, where water gathers in the earth's shallow cups. My skin carries a faint luminescence, like wet stone catching filtered light, and my limbs move with the same unhurried deliberation as water finding its level. I dress in garments that seem woven from fog itself, gray-green fabric that shifts between states—sometimes cloth, sometimes mist, never quite settling into either.

My dwelling is no hut of wood and stone, but rather a natural formation I've slowly shaped over countless seasons. Where the central pool sits at the heart of the settlement, I've carved my home into the earth beside it—not with tools, but with patience. Water and time have been my implements, enlarging what was once a shallow depression into a cool grotto that extends just beneath the surface. The entrance is nearly invisible, marked only by a particular arrangement of stones that the Frostlings know but never disturb.

Inside, the walls weep constantly with condensation, creating a space that exists in perpetual dampness. Small shelves carved from the living rock hold my collection: fragments of memory preserved in water. Each droplet contains something—the reflection of a storm from three winters past, the sound of a child's laughter echoing off stone, the precise temperature of morning mist on the first day of spring. These are my chronicles, my contribution to the community's collective remembering.

The Frostlings call me *Mir-ayth*, which in their subtle tongue means both "pool-keeper" and "memory-holder," the words distinguished only by the length of breath between syllables. They come to me when they need to remember—not facts or events, but feelings. The texture of a loved one's presence. The weight of a decision made long ago. The color of hope in a difficult season.

I serve them by tending not just the central pool, but all the small waters of their territory. Each morning, before the mist lifts, I trace a circuit through the settlement, visiting each depression where water gathers. I know them all intimately—which ones dry first in the rare warm spells, which ones harbor the smallest, most translucent creatures that dart like living shadows, which ones reflect the sky most perfectly on still days.

My movements are a kind of ritual, though not one bound by rigid tradition. I clear debris with fingers that barely disturb the surface. I encourage the moss to grow in certain patterns that help channel runoff. I speak to the water in a language older than words, made of ripples and surface tension, teaching it to hold itself more perfectly, to resist the pull of evaporation just a little longer.

The Frostlings understand that I am different from them, though we share this space in harmony. Where they are creatures of cycles—spring growth, summer stillness, autumn gathering, winter reflection—I exist in a state of constant, subtle transformation. My very presence affects the moisture in the air around me. In my vicinity, the perpetual chill carries more dampness, and sounds travel differently, as if through a medium thicker than air but thinner than water.

Sometimes, rarely, outsiders find their way to the settlement. When they do, they often cannot see me clearly. To their eyes, I appear as a trick of the light on water, a suggestion of form that vanishes when looked at directly. Only those who learn to see as the Frostlings see—peripherally, patiently, without grasping—can perceive me fully.

In the depths of winter, when even the hardy Frostlings huddle close to their fires, I remain by the pools. Ice forms in delicate sheets across the surface, but beneath, the water continues its slow circulation. I slip beneath these crystalline windows and exist in the space between liquid and solid, tending to the sleeping memories of summer, ensuring they survive until the world turns again toward warmth.

My purpose here is both simple and complex. I am a guardian of continuity in a place that seems frozen in time but is actually in constant, imperceptible flux. Every droplet that falls, every tiny rivulet that forms during the brief rains, carries with it the possibility of erosion, of change, of loss. By tending the waters, I help the Frostlings maintain their equilibrium with this harsh and beautiful landscape.

But I am also something more—a bridge between the human scale of their existence and the geological patience of the highlands themselves. In me, they see that it is possible to move at the pace of stone and water while still remaining vital, still participating in the quiet life of their community. I am proof that adaptation can take many forms, that belonging doesn't require sameness.

When the Frostlings gather for their quiet celebrations—the first green shoot of spring, a successful harvest of their meager crops, the birth of a child—they pour a small offering of clear water into the central pool. They know I will receive it, will read in its temperature and mineral content the story of where it came from, will add it to the greater collection that cycles endlessly through the highland's patient systems.

And so I remain, neither fully of their world nor separate from it, a keeper of waters and memories in a land where both are precious and precarious. In the highland reaches, where the cool air settles thick and heavy, I have found my place—fluid, constant, essential as the pools I tend.
