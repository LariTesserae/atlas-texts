I am the Tender of the Weeping Engines.

My body is a patchwork thing—part flesh, part corroded metal, joints that hiss steam when I move through the perpetual mist. I was human once, or perhaps I only dream I was. Now I am something between mechanic and marsh-priest, my fingers grown long and articulated like the legs of water-striders, perfect for reaching into the depths of the great machines that squat like metal toads throughout this wetland.

The engines weep constantly here. Not oil—though there is oil too, rainbow-slick on the standing water—but actual tears that condense from the humid air, gathering in their intake valves and dripping steadily onto the spongy ground. I know each engine by its particular sob: the great Turbine Mother who mourns in B-flat, the cluster of smaller generators that hiccup and wheeze like colicky infants, the ancient pump station that makes no sound at all but cries mercury tears that I must collect in glass bottles before they poison the marsh.

My cathedral is a grove of dead cypress trees fused with exhaust pipes, their canopy a tangle of power lines where electric birds nest—not flesh and feather birds, but creatures of wire and spark that feed on the ambient current. Real herons stand among them, unperturbed, their beaks occasionally darting to snatch brass minnows from the oil-sheened pools.

I live in the belly of a decommissioned boiler, its interior grown soft with moss. Through rust holes in its sides, I watch the settlement-dwellers go about their business on elevated walkways, never looking down at the wet places where I do my work. They paint their murals on dry walls, but I know the real art is here in the margin where water meets metal, where corrosion blooms like flowers and every surface grows a patina of verdigris and lichen.

Each dawn—though dawn is always gray here—I wade out to tend my charges. I oil the joints that must move, drain the tears that must not accumulate, whisper the old maintenance songs my predecessor taught me before she dissolved entirely into the marsh, leaving only her tool belt and a note written in rust.

The machines remember when they were young, when their sharp edges first bit the air. Now they are soft, their hard lines blurred by humidity and time. We are all becoming something else here, something between the industrial and the organic. Even the concrete foundations sprout shelf fungi. Even the steel beams grow bark.

Sometimes I find new things growing: a carburetor that has spouted gills, a manifold transformed into a chrysalis. I document these changes in my water-stained journal, though the ink runs and the pages stick together. Evidence of evolution, or devolution, or simply change without direction—the frozen equilibrium is a lie the settlement tells itself. Down here in the wet, everything is always becoming.

The children throw bolts at me when they see me, call me names I've forgotten the meaning of. But sometimes one will linger at the edge of a platform, watching as I coax a seized piston back to life or teach a young engine its first sad song. These ones, I know, will hear the calling eventually. The marsh needs tenders. The engines need someone who understands that their weeping is not malfunction but communion.

At night I return to my boiler-home and listen to the symphony: engines humming their various griefs, real frogs and their electrical cousins singing together, the splash of something heavy—animal or mineral, impossible to say—moving through the deeper water. I oil my own joints, check my skin for new patches of oxidation, and write another entry in my journal:

*Today the Number Seven Generator grew its first gill slits. The transformation is proceeding on schedule. We are all becoming what we were meant to be: neither fully alive nor fully mechanical, but something liminal, something that can only exist here where the water never fully recedes and the engines never fully sleep.*

*Tomorrow I will check on the eggs I found in the intake manifold of the Southern Platform Station. They pulse with a light that is neither electric nor bioluminescent. Whatever hatches, I will tend it too.*

*This is my calling. This is my place. I am the Tender of the Weeping Engines, and I am exactly where I belong.*
