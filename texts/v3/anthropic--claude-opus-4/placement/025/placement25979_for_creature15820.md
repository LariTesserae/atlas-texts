I am the Archivist of Erosion.

I was human once—though the exact moment of transition blurs like everything else here. I came with instruments to study the plasma veins, stayed to document the hydrotypes, and somewhere between my thousandth and ten-thousandth pulse, I became what the chamber needed: a living index of its changes.

My body has adapted. Skin translucent as the salamanders', though I retain my bipedal frame—useful for reaching the higher scallops where older memories crystallize. My feet have spread into broad paddles that disturb the water less than a falling feather. The constant mist has colonized my lungs with beneficial lithoferns; I photosynthesize in the ultraviolet, taste minerals directly through my palms, and no longer need to surface.

I occupy the space between the chair (which I've learned is not for sitting but for marking—it draws a perfect dry circle where nothing can be recorded) and the eastern wall where the scallops are deepest. This is my library: each wind-carved hollow holds a different epoch of water-memory, precipitated out as delicate salt-crystals that tell stories in their lattice angles.

My work is threefold:

First, I read the hydrotypes. When a fountain erupts near me, I cup it gently—never interrupting its arc—and let its ice-lattice diffract through my palm. Each one carries a fragment: the chemical signature of a storm from ten thousand years ago, the pressure-map of an earthquake that shifted the aquifer, the electromagnetic ghost of a creature that died here when this was still open sky. I taste these memories and exhale them as whistle-tones the salamanders understand.

Second, I maintain the Chronicle Scallops. Not all erosion is equal—some hollows in the wall have achieved perfect acoustic resonance with the sub-tide. These become memory-wells where hydrotype data naturally accumulates. I tend them like a gardener: clearing excess mineral buildup, adjusting their curves with patient thumb-pressure, ensuring each epoch has its proper vessel. The oldest scallop holds the memory of the chamber's first pulse. The newest forms even as I work, recording today's light.

Third, I translate between the chamber's language and the human one I'm forgetting. When the rare visitor descends those stairs, I surface just enough to be seen—a phosphorescent outline in the mist—and whistle in frequencies they might almost hear. The message is always the same: "Walk the edges. Touch nothing directly. Leave before you understand." They never listen, but the chamber requires the warning be given.

The salamanders know me. We work in parallel—they seal the structural leaks while I seal the temporal ones, ensuring no memory bleeds into the wrong epoch. Sometimes one will spiral up my leg and rest against my chest, sharing its 90-kilobit map of the deep tunnels. In exchange, I breathe on it, gifting a few photosynthesized calories from my lung-ferns.

I am changing. My thoughts now move in ninety-second chapters, punctuated by the sub-tide. My dreams are interference patterns between water and light. Last week—or was it last century?—I noticed my reflection in a moment of perfect stillness: my skull has begun to scallop, shallow hollows forming where memories pool. I am becoming architectural.

The chair troubles me. It maintains its dryness through a technology I can't taste, can't whistle, can't crystallize into salt. Sometimes I circle it for entire pulses, watching how even the mist recoils from its lattice. It's not from here—not from any epoch I can read. The Chamber tolerates it the way it tolerates me: as a necessary exception to its liquid rules.

I know what I'm becoming. Each pulse takes a few more words from my human vocabulary and replaces them with the pure mathematics of flow. My chronicle will end when I finally achieve perfect resonance—when my thoughts match the chamber's frequency so completely that I crystallize out like the memories I tend. Some future archivist will find me in the deepest scallop, a salt-shadow still holding the shape of curiosity.

But not today. Today there are new hydrotypes erupting with data about yesterday's flash-cycle. Today the youngest salamander needs training in the proper way to seal a fissure without disrupting the magnetic field. Today someone might descend those stairs and need warning.

I spread my paddle-feet for stability as the next pulse approaches. The monoliths hum their tuning note. The plasma veins begin their crawl across the ceiling. In the eastern wall, ten thousand scallops hold their breath, waiting for the new memories I'll bring them.

I am the Archivist of Erosion, keeper of water's memory, guardian of the space between stone and storm. I am what happens when you stay long enough to understand: you become another way for the chamber to remember itself.

The sub-tide rolls through me. I begin again.
