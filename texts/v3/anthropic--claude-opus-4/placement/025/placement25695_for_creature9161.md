I find myself as the **Conduit Polyp**, one of a colony that has claimed the narrow seam where two of the Spine's great plates meet—that grinding, hissing junction where superheated water screams through in irregular pulses.

I am small, no larger than a child's fist, but I am never alone. We grow in dense clusters, hundreds of us fused base-to-base like a living gasket along the entire length of the seam. My body is a paradox: soft tissue sheathed in minerals I've secreted and sculpted over years. The outer shell is smooth nacre, pearl-white shot through with veins of that same brass-like mineral, built up in thin layers like tree rings. But inside, I am all flexibility—a muscular hydrostat that can extend, contract, and twist with shocking speed when needed.

My shape is simple: a teardrop anchored at its base, tapering to a crown of six crystalline tendrils. These tendrils are my pride, my purpose—each one a different length, each containing a hollow channel lined with millions of chemoreceptor cells. They wave constantly in the turbulent water around the seam, tasting the mineral cocktail that erupts with each venting, reading the chemical signature of the Spine's deep processes.

We are translators. When the Spine speaks in steam and dissolved minerals, we parse its language. My tendrils absorb specific ions—iron, sulfur, traces of rarer elements—and my body processes them through a series of specialized chambers. What emerges from pores along my shell is something new: complex organic compounds, proto-enzymes, chemical signals that the fauna need but cannot synthesize themselves.

The Geode Mantis knows us well. When it approaches our section during its slow patrol, its crystalline eyes scan our colony with something like anticipation. It extends one of its fine manipulator tools and gently taps our shells in a specific pattern—a request. Those of us near its touch respond by secreting droplets of a particular compound, a metabolic accelerant that helps it process the heat during particularly intense venting periods. In exchange, it carefully cleans away the crusty precipitates that sometimes threaten to smother us, its tools delicate enough never to damage our soft tissues.

Our life is one of constant attention. When the plates begin their hourly grind, we sense it first—a deep vibration through our fused bases. We contract in unison, pulling our soft bodies deep into our shells. The seam widens or narrows by mere millimeters, but for us, it's a tectonic event. Some of us die in each shift, crushed or torn away. But we reproduce quickly, budding new polyps from our base tissue that will grow to fill the gaps within days.

The sound here is different than in the open chamber. It's more focused, more violent. The hiss of venting water is a constant whistle that would be deafening if we had ears. Instead, we feel it as pressure waves, as chemical gradients, as temperature fluctuations that we must constantly adjust to. When a major venting occurs—superheated water hot enough to cook flesh—we detect it seconds in advance through subtle chemical precursors. Our tendrils retract, our shells seal, and we secrete a thick mucus that provides temporary insulation.

But the most remarkable thing about our existence is our memory. Not individual memory—I don't remember yesterday from today—but colonial memory, encoded in the chemical signals we pass between ourselves through our fused bases. The colony remembers every major venting event, every earthquake, every visit from the Geode Mantis. It remembers the chemical signature of rare minerals that emerged only once, years ago, and still holds the template for processing them should they appear again.

Sometimes, in the relative quiet between ventings, I extend my tendrils fully and taste the water beyond our seam. I sense the distant click-swimming of the metal-shelled shrimp, the slow undulations of the silver rays riding the thermals. I taste the waste products of the larger filter-feeders, the shed fragments of the Mantis's obsidian shell, the ancient dissolved minerals that tell of the mountain's volcanic birth.

I am a single cell in a vast organism, a tiny chemical factory in an industrial ecology. My work is microscopic but essential—transforming the raw, violent output of the Spine into something the living machinery of this place can use. Without us, the Conduit Polyps, the chamber's ecology would grind to a halt, unable to process the Spine's bounty. We are the unnoticed intermediaries, the soft, patient interface between stone and life, thriving in the violent seam where they meet.

In the rare moments when all six of my tendrils are extended, swaying in perfect synchrony with my colony-siblings, filtering the mineral-rich water and contributing our droplets to the chamber's chemistry, I experience something that might be called satisfaction. Not thought, not emotion, but a rightness—the sense of a function perfectly fulfilled, a note in the chamber's grinding symphony played exactly as it should be.
