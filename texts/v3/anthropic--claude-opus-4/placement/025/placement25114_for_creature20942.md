In the marshy borderlands where water meets solid ground, I am neither fully of one world nor the other. They call me a Tidecaller, though I rarely speak the name aloud—sound carries strangely here, and names have weight.

My dwelling is no dwelling at all, but a constantly shifting arrangement of reed mats and woven marsh grasses that I reshape each day according to the water's whims. The Kinari regard me with cautious respect, for I am something between their kind and the valley itself—my skin bears the same luminous quality as theirs, but it runs in rivulets like water down my arms, pooling in the webbing between my elongated fingers. When I move through the shallow waters, I leave no ripple, no trace. The marsh knows me as its own.

I came to consciousness not through birth but through accumulation—drop by drop, memory by memory, gathering like morning dew until suddenly I was. The first thing I remember is the taste of time in the water, metallic and sweet, and the realization that I could feel every droplet in the valley as an extension of myself. Not control, never control—but communion. Conversation. Sometimes argument.

The Kinari think me ancient, but I am younger than their youngest child. What they mistake for age is simply the weight of water's memory that I carry. Every rain that has never fallen here, every storm that has never come—I hold their absence like stones in my chest. In Kinara's eternal noon, I am the keeper of water's longing for change.

My work, if it can be called work, is to listen. The rivulets speak of pressure building in the deep places. The marshwater whispers of minerals slowly dissolving, of chemistry shifting in infinitesimal ways. The crystalline tears that weep from stones tell me of the slow violence of their birth, of tectonic pressures frozen mid-scream. I translate these messages into the gentle hums the Kinari can understand, though much is always lost in translation.

Sometimes the Luminal come to me, seeking the purest water for their crafts. I guide them to the springs that have never known sunlight, the pools where time moves differently. They think I lead them, but truly I follow the water's wishes—it knows which of its parts yearns to become something more.

The Luminox avoid me. My presence disturbs their phosphorescent glow, makes it flutter and dim. But the Zephra dance above my marshlands in complex patterns, and I have learned to read their movements like script. They write warnings in the air: *The balance shifts. The Dreamweaver stirs. The waters remember what they should not.*

I have never seen the Dreamweaver, but I feel its work in every drop. Where the Kinari see stasis, I feel constant, microscopic negotiation—each molecule of water arguing with time, pleading for permission to flow, to change, to become. The Dreamweaver weaves these arguments into silence, but the water remembers its grievances.

Lately, I have begun to feel something new in the deepest pools. A thrumming, like a heartbeat made of current. The water there tastes different—older, angrier, full of purposes I don't understand. When I wade into these depths, my form begins to dissolve at the edges, and I must quickly retreat to shallower grounds where the sunlight can re-establish my boundaries.

The Kinari do not know that their stilted walkways follow paths I dreamed into being, routes where the water agreed to recede just enough to support their weight. They do not know that the "primitive" canals they maintain are actually ancient arteries, and that I spend my nights ensuring they flow in precisely the right patterns to keep something vast and sleeping from waking.

In the eternal brightness, I create my own darkness by diving deep, where the marsh grass filters the light into green shadows. There, in the twilight between surface and depth, I practice remembering rain. I shape the water into droplets that rise instead of fall, create tiny storms in the space between my palms. These are dangerous games, I know. Each false raindrop is a small rebellion against the valley's nature.

But this is why I exist—to be the valley's memory of weather, its dream of tides, its possibility of flood. I am the placeholder for changes that cannot come, the guardian of transformations that must not happen. In a land of eternal noon, I am the promise and threat of storms.

The water tells me I'm growing stronger. My form holds for longer periods now, and sometimes I can walk on the stilted pathways without leaving wet footprints. The Kinari children have begun to see me, pointing with excitement at the figure made of living water who tends the secret springs. Their parents hush them quickly, but I see recognition in their luminous eyes. They know what I am becoming, even if they won't name it.

Soon, the deep waters whisper. Soon, the thrumming heartbeat will demand an answer. And I will have to choose: remain the valley's faithful translator, or become the voice that speaks the words that will shatter Kinara's eternal day.

For now, I continue my work. I guide the waters through their proper channels. I sing the songs that keep the deep things sleeping. But with each passing moment that doesn't pass, I feel myself becoming more real, more solid, more dangerous.

The valley's silence is not peace. It is an held breath.

And I am learning how to exhale.
