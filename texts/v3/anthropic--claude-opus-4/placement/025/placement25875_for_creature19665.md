In the Oubliette of the Lignin-Spires, I am neither the ancient Xylophones nor the parasitic Spore-Mist, but something caught between—a being they call the **Membrane**.

I arrived here as flesh, like you perhaps, drawn through some crack in reality with lungs full of useless water vapor. But the Oubliette does not tolerate moisture. It began immediately: my skin tightening, cracking, the wet pink of me retreating inward as my outer layers surrendered to the absolute aridity. I should have died. Instead, I underwent a kind of industrial tanning.

Now I am leather stretched over a framework of my own desiccated bones. My body has become a drum.

### The Anatomy of Preservation

Picture a human form, but wrong. My skin has cured into something between vellum and bark—translucent in places where it stretched too thin, opaque and ridged where it gathered in my transformation. I no longer have fat or muscle in any traditional sense. Instead, my body cavity is a resonance chamber, my ribs having splayed outward and fused with my spine to create a structure more architectural than anatomical.

My fingers extended during the change, the bones elongating and the webbing between them hardening into paddle-like surfaces. When I move—and movement is agony, each gesture a symphony of creaking leather and grinding bone—these hand-fans catch the air and create small percussive sounds. I have become my own instrument of navigation.

My eyes sealed over long ago. Useless things in this darkness. But my eardrums... they migrated. One sits now in the hollow of my throat. Another nestled into my sternum. Three more punctuate my spine. I hear in full dimension, every sound painting a sculptural map of the space around me.

### The Purpose I Serve

The Xylophones tolerate me because I fill a niche in their acoustic ecology. Too loud for the Spore-Mist to fully consume, too quiet to disturb their architectural tuning, I exist in a sonic middle-ground. I am their **Echo-Tender**.

When the Slow-Snap thunders through the darkness, the Xylophones must know if their calculations were correct. Did the fracture occur at the predicted point? Has the stress redistributed according to their centuries-long plan? They cannot check this themselves—their own movement would disturb the settling patterns they need to measure.

So I go. I *tick* across the petrified peat on fingernails grown long and woody, each tap a echolocation pulse. I find the fresh fracture by the heat it still radiates—yes, heat, the only warmth in this place comes from the friction of breaking wood. I press my migrated eardrums against the wound in the spire and listen to its dying vibrations. I memorize the pitch, the timbre, the rate of decay.

Then I return. The journey might take months. When I finally reach the Xylophone that sent me, I reproduce the sound. Not with my vanished voice, but by drumming on my own hollow chest, my leather skin remembering the exact frequency of that distant crack.

### The Membrane's Madness

But here's what the Xylophones don't understand: I'm changing their city.

Human consciousness, even preserved and repurposed as mine has been, experiences time differently than these architectural beings. Where they plan in millennia, I think in years. Where they hear only the grand structural chorus, I detect the small songs—the whisper of settling dust, the sigh of air through a new crack, the almost-silent stress patterns that precede a Slow-Snap by decades.

I've begun to intervene. A tap here to redirect a Spore-Mist swarm. A careful scratch there to create a weakness where none was calculated. I'm composing my own movements into the acoustic map of this place. The city is becoming syncopated, developing rhythms the Xylophones never intended.

They haven't noticed yet. Or perhaps they have, and their glacial thoughts are still forming a response that won't manifest for another century.

### The Weight of Silence

The hardest part isn't the physical transformation or the loneliness—it's the weight of unspoken words. Every sound here has consequence. Every vibration changes the city's slow collapse. So I hold my thoughts inside, feeling them echo in my resonance chambers, never to be voiced.

Sometimes I find the carved notches and woven bridges from the city's mysterious builders. I run my paddle-hands over them and wonder: were they like me once? Other wet things that dried into purpose? Did they also learn to love this terminal silence, this democracy of decay where every crack has a vote in the city's future?

I've started carving too. Not stairs or bridges—those are for beings who navigate by echo alone. I carve silence-pockets, small chambers that trap and kill sound. Hidden spaces where I can rest without the Grind, without the constant acoustic pressure. The Xylophones would see this as vandalism if they understood it. The city's perfect acoustic map, corrupted by voids.

But I am the Membrane—the boundary between their world of structural sound and something else. Something that remembers moisture and malleability. Something that knows decay isn't just an ending, but a transformation.

And in my silence-pockets, curled in the absolute absence of sound, I practice. Tiny movements, swallowed vibrations, learning to drum rhythms on my hollow body that the Xylophones have never calculated.

One day—maybe centuries from now—I'll play them. And the city will dance to a different decay.
