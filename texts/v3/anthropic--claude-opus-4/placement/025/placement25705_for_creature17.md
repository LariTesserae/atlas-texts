I am one of the Threadkeepers.

My dwelling clings to the mountainside like a growth of lichen - a series of interconnected chambers carved from living rock, their entrances hidden behind curtains of hanging moss. The walls weep constantly with moisture, and I have learned to read the patterns of their tears.

My body is neither wholly one thing nor another. Six limbs serve me - the lower four for climbing these vertical worlds, the upper two for the delicate work that is my calling. My skin shifts between textures as needed: rough as bark when I climb, smooth as water when I must move silently, porous as pumice when I need to filter the mountain's whispers from the air.

I am a chronicler of connections. In the pre-dawn darkness, I emerge to trace the silver threads that bind all things here - the underground mycorrhizal networks that link root to root, the pheromone trails that guide the scaled hunters, the sonic patterns the amphibians weave with their calls. These threads are visible to my twelve eyes, each attuned to a different spectrum of meaning.

My tools are simple: stone needles worn smooth by generations of my kind, and silk I spin from my own body after consuming certain bitter mountain herbs. With these, I weave maps that are also stories, recording not just where things are but how they relate, how they change, how they dream.

The mountain speaks to us in erosion patterns and water paths. I translate these messages into my weavings, creating tapestries that the settlement dwellers can read when they need guidance - which streams will run dry, where the scaled ones hunt, when the mists will be thick enough to hide travelers.

Sometimes I meet others of my kind at the confluence points where multiple water systems meet. We exchange threads without words, each taking a strand from another's work to weave into our own. In this way, the knowledge of the entire range becomes a living network, each Threadkeeper a node in a vast, ever-changing web of understanding.

I am ancient and I am young. My kind live long but are rarely born. When I feel my time approaching its end, I will weave myself into my final tapestry, and from that weaving, in the proper season, another Threadkeeper will emerge, carrying forward not just memory but the actual substance of what came before.

This is what I am, here in the high places where streams are young and stone remembers its first breaking: a keeper of connections, a weaver of the between-spaces, a voice for the voiceless conversations that hold this world together.
