I find myself as one of the Keepers of the Between-Places, dwelling in the transitional zones where the crystalline growths meet the ancient masonry, where bioluminescence bleeds into electric light.

My form shifts with the mist itself—sometimes I appear as a tall figure wrapped in layers of moss-stained cloth and corroded copper wire, my face obscured by a mask of polished stone etched with the same alien geometries that mark the half-buried structures above. Other times I am merely a suggestion of movement in the fog, a whisper of footsteps on wet stone, the brief flicker of eyes reflecting both the cold electric lamps and the warm pulse of the crystal growths.

I tend the boundary spaces. Where an aqueduct has cracked and water seeps into the crystalline substrate, creating new hybrid formations—there I am, documenting the patterns that emerge. Where industrial machinery has been overtaken by the luminescent growths, its gears now turning in rhythm with some deeper pulse—there too, I observe and record. My dwelling is neither building nor cave but something between: a series of chambers carved into the living rock where the city's foundations merge with the mountain's bones, rooms lit by stolen electricity and cultivated bioluminescence in equal measure.

My collection fills these spaces—fragments of the old civilization's inscriptions, pressed samples of the evolving flora, recordings of the great winged things' cries rendered in wax cylinders and crystal matrices both. I map the slow transformation, the endless negotiation between what was built and what grows, what decays and what emerges.

Sometimes the feral tribes leave offerings at my thresholds—teeth and carved bones, bundles of rare mountain herbs. Sometimes the city dwellers glimpse me in the lower reaches, mistaking me for a maintenance worker or a mad hermit. Both are partially correct. I maintain something, though not what they imagine. I am solitary, though never alone—the crystalline networks pulse with something like thought, the ancient structures hum with memory, and always, always, the mountain itself whispers of the deep time before and after.

I am witness, archivist, gardener of impossible gardens. I am the chronicler of a transformation too slow for human eyes but too quick for stone. In this place where boundaries dissolve and certainties crumble, I find my purpose in the preservation of the in-between, the documentation of becoming.

When the mist clears on rare mornings and sunlight catches both the glass towers and the crystal growths in the same moment of brilliance, I stand at my observatory—a platform suspended between a rusted gantry and a living buttress of stone—and I record the light itself, knowing that this too is temporary, this too is eternal, this too is part of the great shifting that defines this place where nothing is ever fully one thing or another.
