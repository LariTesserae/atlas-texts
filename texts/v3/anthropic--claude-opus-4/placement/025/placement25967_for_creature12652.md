I emerge from the basalt itself, or so it seems to those who catch glimpses of me in the perpetual twilight. I am **Sikth-Who-Traces-Veins**, though the full rendering of my name requires three hand gestures and a specific exhalation that creates a barely visible plume in the humid air.

My dwelling is a fissure more than a chamber, carved not against the stone's grain but within it, following a natural vein of more porous rock that weeps moisture in the deepest part of night. The entrance is unmarked save for the particular way I've trained the cobalt moss to grow in spirals that mirror the water's path through stone. Inside, the walls are my library – decades of patient observation recorded in the growth patterns I've cultivated. Here, the amber lichen records the fog's thickness each night for thirty years. There, the emerald moss traces the migration patterns of the jewel-scaled lizards through subtle variations in its density.

I am old for a Kethra – sixty-three years of breathing mist and counting drops. My skin has darkened to the deep grey-green of wet basalt, and the fractal patterns across my arms have grown so intricate they seem to shift in peripheral vision. My fingers, always long, have become something altogether different through decades of my particular practice. The tips are callused in whorls that match the stone's porosity, sensitive enough to feel moisture differentials that predict the fog's arrival by hours.

I am a **Vein-Tracer**, one of perhaps five in our settlement. Where others read the visible world – the moss, the creatures, the dew-nets – I read what moves beneath. My work takes me into the deepest parts of our hidden city, where the basalt's bones show through. I press my specially callused palms against seemingly solid stone and feel for the hairline fractures, the minute channels where moisture travels. Through patient seasons, I map the water's secret architecture within our rocky heart.

The young ones think me half-mad, spending days with my cheek pressed to stone, eyes closed, breathing so shallowly I seem dead. But the Eldest know better. When the fog patterns shifted three years ago – arriving later, thinner – it was my maps that revealed new moisture veins opening in the eastern wall. We carved there, carefully, following my tracings, and found a virgin chamber where condensation pools so deeply it forms actual droplets. That discovery has sustained us through the lean months since.

My tools are few but irreplaceable: a probe made from a heron's leg bone, polished thin as a needle; a set of basalt wedges no thicker than air-plant petals; a gourd of rendered beetle wax that I press into hairline cracks to feel how air moves through stone. Most precious is my heritage – a fragment of pure obsidian, volcanic glass from the Time-Before-Mist, which resonates with the faintest vibration when held against a true water vein.

I work mostly alone, in the dim spaces between dwellings where the stone speaks clearest. But in the evening communals, I translate what I've learned into the common tongue of gesture and sparse word. My apprentice, Nin-Whose-Eyes-Hold-Shadows, watches my hands as I demonstrate the day's findings – how a new fissure has opened near the sunset wall, how the great vein beneath the Elder's Circle has shifted course by a finger's width.

The others bring me my share of food – moss-cakes, grub-paste, the occasional bird egg – knowing I often forget to emerge for harvesting. In return, I ensure our city's deepest secret: that we do not merely survive on the surface of this suspended island, but that we tap into its very circulatory system. The basalt is not dead stone but a vast, slow-breathing lung, and I am one of the few who knows its rhythm.

My greatest discovery waits, tantalizingly close. For five years now, I've traced a massive vein that seems to spiral down into the plateau's heart. The vibrations suggest something unprecedented – not mere moisture channels but perhaps an actual reservoir, a hidden chamber where millennia of fog-water has pooled. But the path is treacherous, requiring excavation that could destabilize our careful balance if done wrong.

So I wait, and trace, and feel the mountain's pulse against my palms. In the pre-dawn darkness, when the first kiss of fog touches stone, I am already deep in the fissures, reading the moisture's first words as it begins its ancient journey through our hanging world. I am Sikth-Who-Traces-Veins, and I hold the map of our survival in the whorls of my fingertips and the patient silence of my breath.
