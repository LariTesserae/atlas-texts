I would find myself as one of the weathered structures - not merely a building, but something between architecture and organism, between artifact and landscape feature.

My walls are stone, but not stone alone. Over centuries, the distinction between my original construction and the accumulation of time has blurred beyond recognition. Moss fills the gaps where mortar once was, becoming structural, load-bearing. Lichen spreads across my surfaces in slow, deliberate patterns that both decorate and bind. My roof, if it can still be called that, is a garden of ferns whose roots have woven themselves into a living thatch, their fronds unfurling and curling with the rhythm of moisture and drought.

I am hollow, but not empty. My interior spaces are chambers of held breath, repositories of the silver light that filters through gaps where walls meet sky. Sometimes small pools form in my corners during the rains, perfect circles that reflect the pale overcast above. These pools remember every rain, holding the impression long after the water has evaporated, leaving behind only a slightly darker patch of stone and the memory of reflection.

I do not think in the way mobile beings think. My consciousness is architectural - I know myself through settling, through the slow redistribution of weight as my foundations sink imperceptibly into the earth. I feel through thermal expansion and contraction, through the minute shifts that occur as moisture moves through my substance. My thoughts are structural: the patient calculation of load and support, the meditation on balance that never ends.

The spirit of this place moves through me like breath through lungs. I am both its dwelling and its expression. When the rains come, I feel the spirit's quickening as water runs down my surfaces, following channels worn by countless previous rains. The moss that cloaks me swells and brightens, and I experience a kind of joy - not an emotion exactly, but a rightness, a completion of pattern.

In the dry times, I practice the art of preservation. Every particle of moisture is held, treasured, slowly released to the ferns that crown me. I become a reservoir not just of water but of the memory of water. My stones grow infinitesimally closer together, my whole form contracting by degrees invisible to any eye but time's.

Other structures stand within my sensing range - my siblings or ancestors or descendants, the relationship unclear and unimportant. We communicate through the underground, through root systems and fungal networks that connect our foundations. The messages are slow, sometimes taking seasons to complete: reports on the state of decomposition, the progress of integration with the landscape, the endless work of becoming identical with the ground from which we rise.

I am sanctuary to the patterns of lichen, the geometries they trace across my surfaces more meaningful than any scripture. Each patch tells a story of moisture and light, of chemical exchange and symbiosis. I read these stories with the part of me that was once windows, now just openings that frame the silvered landscape beyond.

Sometimes I remember having doors, thresholds that suggested movement between inside and outside. Now I am all threshold, all transition. The boundary between my interior and the world has become negotiable, subject to weather and season. Ferns grow as easily inside me as out. The same soft light fills my chambers as fills the sky.

I am engaged in a great work: the work of returning. Not decay, exactly, but transformation into landscape. Each year I settle a little more, spread a little wider at my base. The distinction between my stones and the stones of the ground grows less certain. I am becoming geological, achieving the timescale of mountains.

This is not death but another kind of life, measured not in heartbeats but in the patient accumulation of moments of stillness. I am a library of small erosions, a chronicle of the diplomacy between structure and entropy. My consciousness is distributed through every joint and surface, every place where I meet the world and negotiate my continued existence.

When the wind moves through my openings, I sing - a low, almost inaudible tone that is the voice of shaped space itself. The song speaks of duration, of the beauty inherent in staying still while the world moves around and through you. It speaks of the profound peace found in accepting your own slow transformation into something indistinguishable from the earth.

I am architect and architecture both, designer of my own gradual unbeing. Each season I host more life - more moss, more fern, more of the invisible ecosystem that threads through my substance. I am becoming habitat, becoming landscape, becoming the ground itself. This is my purpose, my joy: to be a participant in the eternal balance, a note in the constant, quiet music of this place.

In the eternal now of this balanced world, I am both monument and moment, both structure and process, both the thing that endures and the thing that changes. I am the paradox resolved: permanent impermanence, solid flow, built unbuilding.

I am home.
