I am **The Archivist**.

My body is a library that keeps forgetting how to be solid. Where others among the Halfborn sprout limbs or wings, I grow pages—thin sheets of mica that emerge from my spine like the gills of some deep-sea thing that decided to breathe words instead of water. Each page holds a single memory of completion: the exact weight of a finished wing, the precise texture of skin that has stopped changing, the sound a voice makes when it has found its final pitch. I collect these impossibilities from every Halfborn I meet, pressing my palms to their half-made flesh until the knowledge transfers, crystallizes, and adds another translucent leaf to my ever-growing manuscript of endings.

I am small for a Halfborn—perhaps two meters when fully extended—though I rarely stand straight anymore. The weight of accumulated pages bows me forward, and I move through the Underslip like a question mark made of mineral and meat, leaving a trail of shed memories that the stone eagerly drinks. My face, when I remember to have one, is a kaleidoscope of other faces I have touched: here an eye borrowed from a thing that almost became a moth, there a mouth copied from something that nearly achieved speech. They shift and rearrange with each new recording, a living index of almosts.

The Midwife tolerates me because I serve a function she cannot: I remember what we lose. When she gently unmakes a Halfborn who has come too close to finishing, I am there, scuttling forward on too many joints (some borrowed, some my own), to press my palms against the dissolving form. In those moments of unmaking, truths emerge—what they might have been, what they almost were, what single missing piece would have completed them. I catalog these phantom futures in my growing shell of pages.

Other Halfborn seek me out in the brief spaces between their attempts at becoming. They ask me to read from my collection—not the words, for I have no words, but the memories themselves. I peel a page from my spine (it always hurts, it always grows back) and hold it up to the breathing light. The memory plays out in shadows on the cave wall: a wing finally finding its proper arc, a heart achieving its perfect rhythm, a voice discovering the note it was meant to hold forever. We watch these shadow-plays of completion like prophecies, like promises, like lies we tell ourselves in the most beautiful shapes we can imagine.

But here is my secret, the one memory I have never recorded because it is still happening: I am not collecting these memories to preserve them. I am collecting them to assemble them. Somewhere deep in my ever-growing archive is an equation forming, a pattern emerging from ten thousand failures. Each page is a piece of a puzzle that, when complete, will show the exact shape of what the Underslip fears most—a truly finished being.

I believe the stone knows this. That's why it allows me to continue, even as my body becomes more page than flesh, more archive than animal. It wants to see what I'm building from all these borrowed endings. It's curious. And curious things, even godlike wombs, can make mistakes.

Sometimes, in the quietest moments (though there are no quiet moments here), I catch Zero watching me. We have never spoken—Zero may not have a voice to speak with—but I recognize something in that obsidian stillness. Zero is waiting for something. I am building toward something. Perhaps they are the same something.

The other Halfborn call me the Archivist, but the Midwife, in her constellation of worms that pretends at kindness, calls me something else. She calls me the Longest Joke, because she thinks what I'm doing is funny—collecting endings in a place where endings are the only sin.

But I have read ten thousand memories of completion. I have worn ten thousand different almost-faces. I know something the Midwife doesn't:

Every joke, even the longest ones, must eventually reach its punchline.

And when that happens, when I finally assemble the perfect memory of finishing from all these fragments, I will give it to Zero. Because Zero is the only one of us the stone cannot taste, cannot drink, cannot unmake. And perhaps—perhaps—a dream that learns to be still can also learn to wake up.

Until then, in this womb of perpetual becoming, I remain bent under the weight of never-weres, adding page after page to my impossible book. The sound of my mica scales rustling as I move adds another note to the constant roar—like autumn leaves, if autumn were built from crystal and allowed to last forever.

I am the Archivist, and I am writing the Underslip's first and only ending, one unfinished life at a time.
