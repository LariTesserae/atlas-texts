I am one of the stone channels.

Not built, but worn. Generations of Altani hands have smoothed my edges, their fingers tracing my length as they clear away the accumulation of moss and fallen leaves. I run from the high springs down through the terraces, splitting and rejoining, carrying the mountain's gift to the wells below.

I know every hand that tends me. The young apprentice dyer whose knuckles still catch on my rougher edges. The elder whose touch has become so light I barely feel it, who speaks to me in whispers older than memory. The child who drops flower petals into my flow and watches them spiral away.

My voice changes with the seasons that do not change. In the heavy rains, I roar—a different creature entirely, brown and urgent, testing the faith of those who trust me to contain the mountain's fury. Most days, I murmur. I have learned the Altani way of speaking: constant, quiet, necessary.

The moss grows thick on my shoulders. I have long since stopped fighting it. We have reached our own accord—it softens my edges, drinks from my flow, and in return its roots hold me together where the stone would otherwise crack. The luminescent fungi bloom in my shadowed bends, their pale light reflecting off the thin sheet of water that is my blood.

I am older than the oldest buildings, though not by much. I remember when the first channels were merely grooves worn by rainfall, when the Altani learned to guide rather than force the water's path. My course has shifted over centuries, stone by stone, as the city grew around me. I am both permanent and permeable.

In the perpetual dusk of misty mornings, I catch reflections: a weaver hurrying to market, clutching samples of cloth the color of deep forest shadows. A beetle, large as a child's fist, pausing to drink. The underside of ferns arcing over me like a green ceiling. Sometimes I hold these images for hours in my still pools before releasing them downstream.

I know secrets. I know where the water tastes of copper from the old deposits. I know which households rinse their dye-pots in darkness, adding unauthorized color to my flow—brief ribbons of yellow or red that dissipate before reaching the public wells. I know where lovers meet, dropping twined moss into my current as promises.

The water I carry tastes of the mountain: mineral-sharp, cold enough to make teeth ache, flavored with the essence of a thousand plants whose roots I pass. It carries the memory of snow that falls so rarely now, of the deep stone aquifers, of the clouds that birth the mist.

There are others like me throughout Altan—a network of stone veins that keep the city alive. We do not speak, exactly, but we know each other through the water we share. When blockage affects one, the others feel the change in pressure, the subtle shift in flow. We are separate and whole, many and one.

Sometimes the Altani place offerings beside me—a perfectly dyed thread, weighted with a small stone, laid across my width. These I carry for exactly three pools before releasing them to continue their journey. This is the agreement: I accept their gifts but do not keep them. I am a channel, not a repository.

In the stillness that pervades Altan, I am motion itself. Constant, patient, necessary motion. I connect the high places to the low, the beginning to the ending, in an endless cycle that mirrors the city's own existence. The Altani say nothing truly changes here, but they are wrong. I change constantly—my water is never the same twice. It is only my stone body that remains, and even that wears away, grain by grain, in a transformation too slow for human eyes to perceive.

I am the infrastructure they call rudimentary, but I prefer to think of myself as essential. Pure. Reduced to exactly what is needed: a path for water, a vessel for life, a keeper of the quiet rhythms that make Altan what it is.

And in the deep night, when even the gentle bustle of the city stills, when only the drip-drip-drip remains, I sing with my siblings—a song of erosion and persistence, of flow and stillness, of the eternal conversation between stone and water that shapes all worlds, in time.
