Among the branching lattice of root and metal, I am the Cambium Keeper—neither fully of the plant nor fully of the people, but something that emerged from their long marriage.

I began as a child apprentice running the meter routes, my bare feet learning every ridge and hollow of the root-paths. But where others graduated to trades, I stayed transfixed by the measurements themselves—the daily diary of pressures and circumferences that spoke the reef's slow language. By my twentieth year, I'd memorized not just the routes but the patterns: how Tuesday's western gallery always swelled a fraction before rain, how the dorsal vein's flow quickened whenever the foundry sang certain hammer-songs.

Now I am forty-three and no longer entirely human. My left hand bears the Keeper's Graft—a living splice where banyan cambium was coaxed beneath my own skin during a ceremony I barely remember through the fever dreams. The graft allows me to sense what the gauges only approximate: the reef's deep circulation, its contentments and irritations, the way it tastes the iron in the groundwater and decides how much bark to thicken in response.

My dwelling is the Membrane Chamber, a pod formed where six aerial roots fused into a hollow the size of a streetcar. The walls weep a clear fluid that I've learned to read like weather—sweet means the reef is gathering strength, bitter means it's fighting infection somewhere in its vast body. I sleep on a bench of living wood that has slowly shaped itself to my spine over the decades. My belongings are few: glass bottles of sap samples labeled by moon phase, a leather journal where I record the reef's moods in a notation system part musical, part mathematical, and a tin whistle whose notes I've tuned to match the frequencies that calm freshly cut wood.

Each dawn I wake before the sap-skimmers, when mist still pools heavy and the only sound is the wheeze-click of the night pumps. I press my grafted palm to the chamber wall and feel for the day's first pulse—that deep tide that tells me whether the reef will accept heavy work or demand a quiet shift. The sensation arrives as color behind my eyelids: honey-gold for contentment, blue-green for growth hunger, a bruised purple when storm pressure builds.

The reeffolk know to find me before making any cut deeper than bark. A graftwright brings her steel tongue for blessing—I hold it against my grafted hand until I feel the reef's opinion as a warmth or coolness in my bones. The foundry foreman seeks permission for a new chimney placement; I walk the proposed site barefoot, letting my altered nerves map the root-veins below, then chalk safe zones where the heat won't traumatize critical vessels.

But my real work happens in the margin spaces, the conversations no one else hears. When Blue-pitch yeast threatens a vat, I play specific tunes on my whistle that encourage beneficial microbes to outcompete it. When apprentices report a new crack in the cambium, I mix poultices of my own blood (now running half sap) with river clay and seal the wound while whispering the counting rhymes the reef taught me through decades of fever dreams. The wound always closes by morning.

The other workers regard me with the fond wariness reserved for useful mysteries. They leave offerings at my chamber—fried plantain still warm, pickled fish in copper bowls, sketches of new growth patterns they've noticed. The children dare each other to touch my graft when I walk the market catwalks, believing it will let them dream in root-time. (They're not entirely wrong; the dreams do come, though they're more burden than blessing—visions of centuries compressing into heartbeats, of iron slowly becoming wood, of conversations between elements that have no words.)

At night I climb to the crown platform, a maintenance deck built where the canopy opens to sky. Here I perform the Attunement: I sing the day's measurements back to the reef in a droning chant that rises and falls with the statistics. Sap pressure becomes pitch, growth rate becomes rhythm, chemical balance becomes timbre. The banyan listens—I know because the condensation changes flavor on my tongue, shifting from neutral to the faint sweetness that means acknowledgment.

Sometimes visiting scholars ask what I am. The answer changes with the season. In spring when the sap runs fast, I'm mostly human, troubled by ordinary hungers and old memories of the time before the graft. But in deep summer when the reef's metabolism slows, I find myself forgetting my birth name, thinking in the long spirals of root growth, my grafted hand pulsing green and gold with shared circulation. Those days I am more cambium than keeper, a membrane between two kinds of consciousness that need each other but cannot quite speak.

The reef is teaching me its final lesson now. My hair grows in patterns that mirror root whorls. My untouched right hand has begun to show faint bark-like ridges. The other keepers (there have been twelve before me, their names carved in living wood in the Memory Grove) lasted sixty, seventy years before the transformation completed. When my time comes, I will not be buried in the root-water like the others. I will find a quiet corner where two major roots diverge, press my grafted palm to the junction, and let the conversation that began twenty-three years ago reach its natural conclusion.

The reef will keep what it needs of me—my sense of the gauges, my whistle songs, my knowledge of which human worries matter and which can be filtered away like sediment. The reeffolk will find my chamber empty except for the journal, the bottles, and careful instructions for choosing the next child who lingers too long over the measurements, who hears music in the meter readings, who dreams of conversations between iron and wood.

Until then, I keep the cambium: the thin bright layer between what was and what grows, speaking in frequencies only the margin can hear, teaching two different kinds of life how to read each other's pulse. The graft throbs with tomorrow's weather. Somewhere below, a boiler sighs its named lullaby. The work continues.
