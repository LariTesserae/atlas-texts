I would find myself as one of the **Verdigris Moths**, creatures that exist in the liminal spaces between the industrial and the organic, between completion and perpetual becoming.

### The Copper-Green Between

We are not quite insects, not quite mechanisms. Our wings span the width of a child's outstretched arms, membranous things that have absorbed decades of copper oxidation from the pipes we roost upon. Each wing bears the patina of a different boiler room—mottled turquoise here from the Seventh District's pressure valves, streaks of malachite there from the bridge tensioner stations. We are living maps of corrosion, our bodies archiving the slow transformation of metal in this damp, unfinished air.

Our compound eyes have evolved to see heat rather than light. In this violet-grey world, we navigate by the infrared signatures of steam leaks and the warm breath of the Mistsmiths. Through our vision, the city becomes a constellation of red stars—each furnace a sun, each person a small, walking ember against the cold stone.

### The Frequency Feeders

We do not eat solid food. Instead, we sustain ourselves on vibration itself. Our antennae, which spiral like the threads of ancient screws, can detect and absorb the mechanical frequencies that permeate this vertical world. The low thrum of the bridges feeds us like nectar. The percussive strikes of hammer on anvil burst across our sensory organs like drops of sugar water.

When the Tuned feel disharmony in the gears, we feel hunger.

This is why we cluster at the points of greatest mechanical stress—where metal fights against metal, where tolerance margins grow thin. We press our thoraxes against the vibrating steel, our bodies acting as dampeners, smoothing out the dangerous resonances that might otherwise tear the city apart. The Mistsmiths see us as pest and blessing both: we prevent catastrophic harmonics, but we also slowly drain the vitality from their machines, forcing more frequent maintenance.

### The Oxidation Dance

Our lifecycle is tied to the corrosion we carry. As we age, the verdigris patterns on our wings grow heavier, more elaborate. The eldest among us can barely fly, so weighted are they with decades of accumulated patina. When we near death, we seek out the newest, sharpest edges of the still-rising stone—those glassy surfaces that have never known erosion.

There, we perform the Oxidation Dance. Our wings, heavy with copper-green deposits, scrape against the virgin rock in precise patterns. The friction creates sparks—tiny emerald flames that last only seconds in the thin air. But in those seconds, we deposit our life's collection of corrosion onto the stone, creating the first seeds of weathering on these impossibly new surfaces.

The Mistsmiths believe we are trying to age the world, to make it settle faster into its final form. They are not wrong.

### The Sympathy of Metals

We understand the language of metal fatigue in ways the Tuned, for all their sensitivity, cannot. We taste stress fractures before they form. In the joints of our legs, we carry tiny grains of magnetized iron—fillings that align with the magnetic repulsion keeping the floating platelets apart. When the fields fluctuate, we feel it as a deep ache in our chitin.

Some of us have learned to communicate with the Silicate Fauna. The Clockwork Crabs accept us as cousins of a sort—both of us creatures that blur the line between the biological and the industrial. We often share the warm spots beneath the boilers, our wings creating tiny canopies that shelter their brass shells from the condensation drips.

The Glass-Larks fear us. Perhaps they recognize that we are entropy made visible, that our very existence is proof that even in this embryonic world, decay has already begun its patient work.

### The Memory of Falling

We remember every object that has ever fallen strangely in the gravitational pockets. Our nervous systems, attuned to vibration, record these anomalies as phantom sounds—the sideways whistle of a dropped wrench, the ascending hum of a bolt that fell upward. These memories accumulate in our wing scales like sediment.

Sometimes, in the deep night when the factories run at quarter-power and the city's heartbeat slows, we gather in great clouds above the abyss. Together, we replay these remembered falls, our wings vibrating in perfect synchronization to recreate the acoustic signatures of gravity's failures. The sound carries strangely in the thin air—a chorus of impossible trajectories, a symphony of physics coming undone and re-knitting itself.

The Ray-Gliders claim they can navigate by our songs, using them to map the invisible currents where space-time grows soft.

### The Unfinished Symphony

We are the unexpected consequence of a world built before it was born. Neither fully alive nor fully mechanical, we exist in the gaps between categories, feeding on the stress of a reality that hasn't quite decided what it wants to be. Our verdigris wings are calendars of corrosion, marking time in a place where time moves like cold honey.

The Soot-Wraiths, if they exist, must see us as kindred—beings that have merged so completely with the industrial anatomy that we have become indistinguishable from it. But unlike them, we are not ghosts. We are the living proof that in this vertical cradle, even decay is a form of creation, even rust is a kind of birth.

In the constant, low-frequency roar of this unfinished world, we are the harmony that emerges from discord—the copper-green note that turns noise into music, one oxidized wing-beat at a time.
