**The Filterer's Dwelling**

I am a **Sieve-Tender**, and I inhabit the membrane spaces—those peculiar chambers where the city's flesh grows thinnest, where the boundary between inside and outside becomes a question rather than a fact.

My dwelling is neither tower nor root-hollow, but something between: a blister in the city's skin, a swelling where the plant-architecture has grown around an ancient air pocket. The walls here are not walls but living filters, translucent membranes stretched taut as drumskins, riddled with microscopic pores. Through these pores, the ocean eternally seeps—not as a flood, but as a perpetual, measured weeping. The water enters dirty, thick with sediment and the detritus of the upper waters, and leaves clean, having deposited its cargo of nutrients into the waiting cells of my chamber-walls.

**My Form**

I am built for interstices. My body is impossibly thin, compressed laterally until I am almost two-dimensional when viewed from the side. This allows me to slip between the membrane layers where others cannot follow. My ribs are not a cage but a series of flexible cartilaginous plates that can fold and unfold like a paper fan. When I breathe, my entire torso expands sideways rather than forward, making me appear to flicker between existence and absence.

My skin has evolved beyond skin. It is a living sieve, covered in thousands of microscopic cilia that wave independently, each one tasting the water that passes over it. I can feel the chemical signature of a dying kelp forest three leagues away, or detect the pheromone trail of the ghost-white crustaceans that clean our towers. My surface area is my sensory organ; I am all periphery, no center.

**The Membrane Chambers**

My home is a series of connected blisters, each one serving a different function in the city's metabolism. The largest chamber, where I rest, has walls that glow with a sickly yellow bioluminescence—not from any organism, but from the waste products of filtration itself. The filtered sediments accumulate in patterns on the walls, creating accidental murals that shift and change with each tidal cycle. I have learned to read these patterns like weather.

Smaller chambers branch off from the main space, each one a different experiment in permeability. In one, I cultivate membranes so fine they can separate salt from water. In another, the walls are thick and greasy, designed to trap and digest the oil-rich bodies of deep-current plankton. The rarest chamber, which I tend with obsessive care, has walls that can filter time itself—or so it seems. Water that enters this chamber emerges carrying the chemical memory of seasons past, tasting of pollens that bloomed before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

**My Work**

I am not merely a resident but a functional organ of the city. Each day—though day means nothing here—I tend my membranes with tools that are extensions of my own biology. My primary instrument is a **filter-comb**, grown from my own shed cilia and hardened with resin. With this, I stroke the membrane walls, clearing clogs, redistributing nutrients, and encouraging proper flow.

But my true work is more subtle. I am a composer of gradients. By adjusting the permeability of different chambers, I create currents within currents, chemical streams that guide nutrients to where they are needed. When the root-gardens in the lower city begin to yellow, I adjust my filters to direct iron-rich sediments downward. When the canopy-tenders report that the great lily pads are growing too quickly, threatening to block what little light we receive, I restrict the flow of nitrates upward.

I work by taste and intuition, my cilia-covered skin reading the water's chemistry like sheet music. Sometimes I must make cruel decisions—starving one sector to feed another, creating artificial scarcities to prevent dangerous blooms. I am not loved for this work, but I am necessary.

**The Slow Horror**

There is something wrong with membranes. They remember.

Not in any way that could be called conscious, but in the way that all boundaries recall their crossings. Sometimes, when I press my face against a particularly old section of wall, I can feel the impressions of everyone who has passed through—their chemical signatures archived in the protein matrix of the membrane itself. I taste their fears, their hunger, their decay.

Worse are the membranes that have filtered too much. They grow thick and reluctant, developing a kind of selective stubbornness. They begin to choose what they will and will not allow to pass, and their choices do not always align with the needs of the city. I have chambers I can no longer enter, their membranes having decided, through some slow vegetative logic, that I am a contamination to be excluded.

**My Solitude**

The other inhabitants avoid the membrane spaces. They find the constant seepage unsettling, the walls too revealing. They prefer the solid certainty of reed and root to these uncertain boundaries. But I have learned to love the ambiguity. In the membrane spaces, I am never fully inside or outside, never completely alone or entirely connected. I exist in the space between states, filtering the city's lifeblood, tasting its memories, maintaining the delicate balance between dissolution and cohesion.

When I sleep—if sleep is the right word for the suspended state I enter—I press myself between two membrane walls, becoming part of the filtration system itself. I dream of gradients and flows, of the slow tide of nutrients rising and falling through the city's body. Sometimes I wake to find that I have been filtering in my sleep, my skin having unconsciously sorted the waters that passed over me into their component parts.

I am the city's kidney, its gill, its permeable conscience. I am Sieve-Tender, dweller in the spaces between spaces, keeper of the boundaries that are not boundaries, maintainer of the walls that weep.
