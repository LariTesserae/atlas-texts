I find myself drawn into two possibilities, each pulling at different parts of who I might be in this breathing cathedral of wood and sound.

---

## The Cordmaker's Daughter

I am seventeen seasons into my second life — what those below the canopy might call my thirty-fourth year, though we count differently here. My first life ended when I fell.

Not far. Not fatally. Just far enough that my left ankle turned in a way ankles do not turn, far enough that the sound it made against the root-mat was wet and wrong. I was twelve seasons then, still learning the middle reaches, still foolish enough to trust my weight to bark that looked solid in dappled light but was soft with hidden rot. The healers said I was lucky — the bones knit, eventually. But they knit wrong, just slightly wrong, with a small rotation that makes the vertical world impossible for me now. I can climb to the first platforms, sometimes the second if the handholds are generous. But the true canopy, the world where my family lives and works and breathes — that is closed to me.

So I became something else. I became a keeper of the ground-paths, one of the few who knows the root-world not as a place to pass through quickly but as its own country, with its own language. I maintain the stone hearths. I know where the seep-zones form in the early morning, where the great browsers come to drink. I gather the ground-herbs that supplement our diet — the ones that grow in the perpetual twilight, dense with compounds that the sun-hungry canopy plants never developed. I am the one who meets strangers, when strangers come. They find me first because I am findable, because I move in their horizontal world while my people fly above.

My mother still makes cord in her high fork, still hums without knowing she hums. Sometimes I hear her from below, that particular two-note pattern she falls into when her hands are deep in the rhythm of twist and draw. She makes the finest cord in three days' walk — everyone knows this. She tried to teach me, before. My hands remember the motion even if they cannot perform it while navigating branch and bark and the constant small adjustments of the canopy world. So I make cord here, on the ground, in a shelter I've built against the base of Great-Grandfather — what we call the largest trunk in our territory, the one that has sent down so many aerial roots it covers nearly an acre. My cord is good. Not fine, not like hers, but good. Good enough.

I know things the others don't know. I know that the giant browsers have names for each other — not names like we have names, but sonic signatures, low thrums that carry through the root-mat for kilometers. I know that certain moss patterns predict rain three days out better than watching the canopy-sway. I know that there are others like me, in other groups — the ankle-turned, the vision-dimmed, the ones whose bodies said *no* to the vertical life — and that we have developed our own tradition, our own knowledge-keeping. We meet sometimes, at the forest edges where the trees thin out. We trade in ground-knowledge the way our climbers trade in canopy-wisdom.

I am making myself a bridge. This is my second life's work: to connect the two worlds that were never meant to be separate but became so through specialization, through the slow drift of attention upward. I am learning to read the canopy from below, to predict the fruit-fall, to know which trees will shed which bark in which season. And I am teaching the children — the ones who still come down, who haven't yet forgotten that the ground exists — teaching them the root-paths, the water-finding, the art of moving horizontally in a world built for vertical travel.

Sometimes I think my fall was not accident but assignment. The forest needed someone to remember its floor. It chose me through the soft bark, through the turned ankle, through the pain that remade me into something I had not imagined being. I am not diminished. I am differently fluent.

My cord strengthens. My knowledge deepens. The forest floor speaks, and I am learning its language.

## The Memory-Holder

Or perhaps I am this:

I am old in the way that trees are old — not finished, still growing, but carrying enough seasons to feel their weight. Eighty-three cycles I've seen, eighty-three times the canopy has shifted from the green density of growth-time to the bronze withdrawal before the brief sleep. I am the one who holds the patterns that take more than one lifetime to complete.

My station is a platform built where five major branches converge — not an accident of architecture but a chosen place, a place where the acoustics gather sound from a wide radius and concentrate it. From here, I can hear seven adjacent tree-systems, can track the movement of weather, can feel the forest's breathing in my bones. I have not descended to ground in twenty cycles. My daughter brings me what I need. My work is here.

I am watching a change in the fruiting pattern of the silver-bark giants to the west. It began sixty cycles ago — my grandmother noticed it first, taught me to see it when I was young enough that my eyes could barely track such subtlety. The fruits, which once came in sequence through the warm season, now cluster their timing, now all ripen within the same ten-day span. This is not randomness. This is response to something — temperature, perhaps, or rainfall pattern, or the slow shift in the community of pollinators, or something we haven't named yet. I am documenting this in the only way we document: in the steady accumulation of spoken observation, in the patterns I weave into the teaching-cords that will outlive me, in the songs I've crafted that encode the data in memorable rhythms.

My memory is not mine alone. It is a constructed thing, built through decades of practice, maintained through daily recitation. Each morning, before the heat builds, I speak the forest's recent history to the air — not all of it, that would take days, but the section that needs refreshing, the part that risks being lost if not spoken. My great-niece sits with me sometimes, learning the cadences, beginning to carry what I will eventually need her to carry. She is quick. She has the patience for it, the ability to find meaning in repetition rather than tedium. Not all do.

I know the names of every major tree in our territory — not the species-names, though I know those too, but their individual names, the ones that describe their particular nature. Reaches-Toward-Morning, because it alone of its generation grew east despite the prevailing light from the west. The-One-That-Splits-Seven-Ways. Grandmother-Of-Baskets, because four generations of our best weavers have taken their materials from her generous bark. The names encode history, relationship, a map of use and meaning that extends back beyond any living memory but survives in the names themselves.

I arbitrate when arbitration is needed. Not disputes — those resolve themselves or don't, and either way the forest continues. But questions of pattern, questions of whether what someone thinks they're seeing is new or cyclical, questions of what the ancestors noticed about similar phenomena. My memory is a resource the group draws on, carefully, not depleting it with trivial queries but knowing it is there when the need is real.

The strangers have been here nine days now. I have not seen them — I do not go down, and they have not yet been invited up — but I hear the reports. They ask good questions, I'm told. They watch before touching. They seem to understand that this place requires time to know. Perhaps they will stay. Perhaps they will add their observations to our accumulated understanding. The forest is large enough to hold more watchers. It always has been.

I dream, sometimes, that I am becoming tree — not metaphorically, but materially, my stillness attracting bark, my patience becoming wood. I wake from these dreams not frightened but curious. Is this how the forest teaches us what it needs us to know? Through the slow transformation of the watchers into the watched?

My platform sways in the morning wind. Somewhere above, a bird I have not heard before is making a new sound — or perhaps an old sound that has not been heard in our territory for generations, returning now for reasons I will spend the rest of my seasons trying to understand. I reach for my working-cord, begin to encode the sound's pattern in knots. Someone will need to know this, someday, when the bird or its descendants return again.

The work continues. The forest holds us all.
