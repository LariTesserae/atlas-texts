In the deep shelter of an overhang where water sheets constantly from above, creating a perpetual curtain between myself and the wider world, I tend the memory gardens.

I am one of the Archivists—though that word carries different weight here than it might elsewhere. My dwelling is carved back into the living rock, accessed through the waterfall that never ceases. The entrance appears as nothing more than shadow and streaming water from outside, but step through that liquid boundary and you find my domain: a series of interconnected chambers where the stone itself has been coaxed into shelving, into furniture, into art.

The surfaces here remember. That's what drew me to this particular formation when I first arrived in the enclave seven years ago—the unique crystalline structure of these walls that responds to directed thought, storing impressions like three-dimensional photographs. I've spent these years learning to read the older memories already embedded here, deposited by the stone's previous inhabitants: not people, but the microorganisms that first colonized these surfaces, leaving their chemical signatures in patterns I'm still decoding.

My skin has taken on more of the iridescent quality than most—a side effect of spending so much time in the mineral-rich spray. It forms a faint prismatic sheen across my arms and face, refracting the bioluminescent light into tiny rainbows. I've grown to love it, this visible mark of belonging to this place.

Each morning, I emerge through my water-door to check the instruments I've placed throughout the surrounding area—small quantum resonance detectors that pick up the faintest electromagnetic signatures from both the fungal networks and the deeper stone. The data feeds into my personal archive, cross-referenced with the memories I extract from the rock walls. I'm building something here: a living history that includes not just human presence but the full spectrum of existence in this young world.

The other inhabitants know me as the keeper of ceremonies. When someone wishes to preserve a moment—a birth, a discovery, a loss—they come to me. Together, we enter the memory chambers, and I guide them in imprinting their experience into the stone. It's delicate work, requiring perfect calibration between human consciousness and mineral receptivity. Too forceful, and the memory shatters into meaningless fragments. Too gentle, and it fades within days.

My favorite time is during the transitions between mist and rain, when the water patterns shift and create new harmonics against my entrance. I've learned to read weather in the sound of water on stone, to predict the cycles by the way the droplets dance. During these times, I often sit just inside my threshold, half in my cave and half in the spray, playing a small instrument of my own design—strings made from fungal filaments that resonate with frequencies beyond normal human hearing. The music draws the small creatures, the scuttling things with their bioluminescent markings. They gather in the lee of the overhang, creating living constellations that pulse in rhythm with my playing.

I've documented seventeen distinct species in my immediate area, including three that exist nowhere else in the enclave. One, a six-legged organism no larger than my thumb, seems to feed exclusively on the biofilm that forms where my water-door meets the stone. I've named it the threshold keeper, and I ensure my movements never disturb its feeding grounds.

The Archive isn't just my profession—it's my contribution to this impossible, essential experiment in living. Every day, I feel the weight of responsibility and the lightness of discovery in equal measure. The stone remembers, and through it, so do we all. In this young world still finding its shape, I am both witness and chronicler, preserving the moments that will one day help others understand how humans learned to live in true harmony with a planet still becoming itself.

At night, when the fungal colonies reach their peak luminescence, I often receive visitors. They come for stories—not the ones in the stone, but the ones I've learned to tell about them. We gather in my main chamber, where the walls hold thousands of memory impressions, and I weave narratives from the fragments: the first human to discover this place, the engineer who solved the gravitonic equation that made our sky bridges possible, the child who befriended a colony of bioluminescent insects and taught them to respond to whistled commands.

This is my place in our hidden world: part scientist, part artist, part bridge between the human and the more-than-human. Behind my waterfall, surrounded by memories both ancient and immediate, I continue the work of ensuring that nothing essential is forgotten, even as everything around us continues to change and grow and become.
