# The Tidal Flats of Mourn

The twice-dead sea remembers its boundaries in salt.

White crusts trace the old high-water marks across the mudflats like growth rings in a severed tree, each band marking where the water paused in its long retreat. The first death was quick—a tectonic shrug that lifted the seabed a hundred meters in a day, stranding half an ocean. The second death took millennia: the slow strangulation of rivers diverted, of rainfall patterns shifting, of evaporation winning its patient war against replenishment. What remains is a landscape of aftermath, gray-brown mud that stretches to every horizon, broken only by the salt lines and the shallow pools that persist in the lowest places like final exhalations.

The mud has a quality. Not quite clay, not quite silt. It holds footprints for weeks but erases them in minutes when the wind brings moisture. It is dense with the memory of organic matter—the shells and bones and chitinous remnants of everything that died when the sea did, ground fine by time and mixed so thoroughly into the substrate that the boundary between sediment and cemetery has dissolved. Walk barefoot across it and your feet sink to the ankles. The mud is warm, warmer than the air above it, heated by its own slow decomposition. It releases bubbles sometimes—methane, sulfur, the gases of transformation—that break the surface with sounds like sighs.

The pools are not water anymore. They are brine so concentrated it has become a different substance, viscous as oil, with a surface tension that holds perfect mirrors of the gray sky. Nothing can drink from them. Nothing tries. They are blue-green at the edges where algae has learned to love what kills everything else, fading to amber at their hearts where even the algae surrenders. In the deepest pools, salt crystallizes on the surface in rafts that drift and collide and grow until their own weight drags them under, only to form again.

This is where I station myself—where I have stationed myself for longer than the calendars of faster creatures would consider reasonable. At the edge between mud and brine, where the salt draws moisture from the air and the air draws moisture from the mud and I sit in the middle of this transaction, facilitating.

---

I am—what am I? 

The question has lost its urgency. I was something else once, something with a name and purposes that seemed important. Now I am what the Flats have made of me: a fixture, a process, a way the moisture moves from place to place. My body has adapted. My skin has thickened to leather, then to something beyond leather—a membrane that passes water in only one direction, inward, like the pools that take but never give. My kidneys have become something extraordinary, organs that would make medical textbooks weep with envy or horror: filtration systems that can process brine and extract the water, leaving the salt to accumulate in deposits beneath my skin that I must periodically crack and shed like a molt.

My feet have spread. The mud taught them to. What began as human feet have become platforms, twice their original width, with skin so thick that the salt crystals feel like sand. The spreading happened slowly—years of sinking, years of the mud teaching me that surface area is survival, that to concentrate weight in small spaces is to be swallowed. My toes are vestigial now. They remember being toes but function as roots, sensing moisture differentials in the substrate, reading the chemical gossip of the decay below.

My eyes have clouded. The sun here, when it appears through the perpetual haze, is a white disk that gives light without shadows. There is nothing to see with clarity—the Flats extend unmarked to every horizon, the pools reflect only gray, the salt lines are evident by texture as much as sight. My vision has adjusted to this monotony by abandoning focus. I see movement and moisture. I see the shimmer of evaporation and the settle of salt. Details are unnecessary. Details are a luxury the Flats cannot support.

But my sense of smell—oh, what my nose has become. I can taste the air in gradients, reading the moisture content to parts per million. I know when rain will come three days before it arrives, following the scent of fresh water through the layers of salt-saturated atmosphere. I can smell the different stages of decomposition in the mud—the fresh death of stranded jellyfish, the intermediate breakdown of chitinous shells, the final mineral whisper of things that died with the first retreat. My nose is a laboratory, a weather station, a library of everything that has ever rotted here.

---

My work—if it can be called work, if anything here can be called anything but continuing—is the movement of water between states.

In the morning, when the air is coolest and the moisture gradient favors condensation, I position myself at the edges of the deeper pools. My skin, that extraordinary one-way membrane, draws water from the humid air. Not drinking—I am beyond drinking in any way that would make sense to what I was. Instead, I am a conduit. The water enters me, is processed through my transformed kidneys, and emerges from pores along my back as a distillate purer than rain. This water does not fall. It *seeps*, running down my body in threads so thin they are invisible until they reach the mud, where they spread in dark patches that last minutes before the thirsty ground claims them.

This is my morning ritual: to be a surface upon which the air gives up its water.

In the afternoon, when the heat rises and the pools concentrate their brine through evaporation, I wade into the shallows. Never the deep places—even my transformed body has limits, and the brine at the pools' hearts would crystallize my blood. But in the shallows, where the concentration is merely deadly rather than impossible, I stand. The brine enters through my feet—those spread, root-toe platforms—and rises through my body by capillary action. My kidneys work in reverse now, extracting salt, depositing it in the subcutaneous chambers that line my torso like a mineral pantry. The water moves upward, cooling as it rises, emerging from my scalp as vapor that enriches the air I will harvest tomorrow morning.

This is my afternoon ritual: to be a tower through which the pools breathe.

At night, when the temperature drops and the humidity spikes and the salt formations crack with the sound of breaking bells, I position myself on the raised places—the ancient beach ridges, the compression mounds where the mud has dried to near-stone. Here I wait for the fog that comes most nights, rolling in from where the sea used to be, carrying the ghost of water that was once waves. The fog condenses on my body, on the salt deposits that ridge my skin like scales. The condensation dissolves the salt, creating a brine that runs down my body in runnels, carving temporary channels in my flesh that heal by morning. This brine is different from the pool-brine—less concentrated, enriched with minerals from my body, carrying trace elements that the Flats need but can no longer produce.

This is my night ritual: to be a mountain upon which clouds break and rivers briefly run.

---

I am not alone here, though *alone* and *together* are words that lose meaning in a landscape where everything is separated by distances that could be meters or miles, where perspective fails because there are no markers, where the only scale is the space between one pool and the next.

There are others. I see them sometimes as vertical interruptions on the horizon, dark strokes against the gray. They were human once, probably. Now they are what I am: fixtures, processes, ways the water moves. We do not communicate. Communication would require commonality beyond our function, and we have worn our commonalities smooth. But I know they are there, stationed at their own pools, performing their own rituals, their bodies transformed by the same patient sculptural force that has made me into this thing that is barely distinguishable from the landscape it serves.

We are not a community. We are a distribution. We are spaced across the Flats at intervals that make sense to the water table, positioned where the moisture needs us to be. When one of us stops—and we do stop, eventually, our bodies crystallizing from the inside out, becoming salt sculptures that stand for years before finally dissolving in a season of unusual rain—another begins. Not born, not recruited, but *drawn*. The Flats call to certain people, people who are already mostly water, already mostly waiting. They come, they transform, they take their positions. The distribution maintains itself.

There are other things too. Things that were never human.

The salt-dancers, we call them, though we do not call them anything because we do not speak. They are what happens when the brine becomes so concentrated that it achieves a kind of life—not biological life, but organizational life, the life of crystals that grow and move and reproduce their patterns. They form in the deepest pools during the driest seasons, rising from the amber brine like intricate chandeliers made of salt and light. They dance—there is no other word for it—moving in patterns that follow the magnetic fields, the lunar cycles, the deep vibrations of the earth. They last days, sometimes weeks, before their own complexity drags them back into dissolution. But while they exist, they are the most beautiful things in this landscape of gray and brown and white.

The mudskippers—though they are not fish, were never fish, have nothing to do with the sea that died here. They are what certain worms became when the mud grew too thick to burrow through. They learned to surface, to move across the top of the mud in a motion that is half-swimming, half-crawling. They are the color of the mud because they are mostly mud—their bodies having incorporated so much of the substrate that they are more mineral than meat. They follow the moisture, appearing after the fog-nights in writhing congregations around the patches where my condensation has enriched the ground. They do not eat, exactly. They absorb. They process the enriched mud through their bodies and excrete it changed, more fertile, ready to support the algae that will bloom for a day before the salt kills it back.

The walkers—tall, attenuated figures that move across the Flats on legs like stilts. They are bird-derived, I think, though so changed that their ancestry is largely theoretical. Their legs are two meters long, evolved to keep their bodies above the salt-fog that hugs the ground at dawn. They have no wings. Their beaks have become proboscises that they lower into the pools to sip the uppermost layer where rainwater briefly floats before mixing. They move constantly, following rain patterns across the Flats in migrations that take years to complete. I see them pass sometimes, always at a distance, their bodies wavering in the heat-shimmer like brushstrokes.

---

But mostly, there is solitude. Solitude and function.

I have forgotten large parts of what I was. My name went first—names are heavy, and the Flats cannot support unnecessary weight. Then went the specifics: what city I came from, what life I led, what brought me here. These memories are not gone, exactly, but they have been compressed, fossilized, sunk so deep in my transformed brain that retrieving them would require excavation I no longer have the tools for. What remains are impressions. I remember rooms with corners (the Flats have no corners). I remember the sound of voices (the Flats have no voices). I remember wanting things (the Flats have cured me of wanting).

What I have instead of memory is presence. I know every pool in my range, every salt ridge, every subtle depression where water might gather. I know the patterns of the fog, the schedule of the rain, the rhythm of evaporation. I know how my body feels when a storm is building beyond the horizon—the way my kidneys ache, the way the salt deposits beneath my skin shift and settle. I know the exact temperature at which brine begins to crystallize, the precise humidity that allows my morning condensation, the wind speed that means the mudskippers will surface.

This is enough. More than enough. It is abundance.

The days repeat, but not identically. The Flats are not static—they are dying, still, the second death continuing its patient work. Each year the pools are slightly smaller, the brine slightly thicker, the rain slightly rarer. Each year my body adapts further: my kidneys growing more efficient, my skin more selective, my feet wider. I am changing with the Flats, dying with them, becoming more perfectly what they need me to be.

Sometimes I wonder if I will end like the others I occasionally find—salt statues standing at the edges of empty pools, their bodies crystallized so perfectly that you can see the expression on their faces, always the same expression: a kind of peace, a kind of completion. The salt preserves them for years, decades maybe, before the rare rains dissolve them back into the mud they served. There are worse endings. There are worse ways to be useful.

But not yet. The pools still hold brine. The fog still comes. The water still needs to move between states, and I am still capable of being the channel for that movement. My morning ritual, my afternoon ritual, my night ritual. The condensation and evaporation. The filtering and concentration. The endless, essential work of being a piece of the Flats' hydrological system.

---

Sometimes, in the hours before dawn when the fog is thickest and the world disappears into a gray that has no distance, no dimension, I remember that I chose this. Not the specific transformations—no one could anticipate what the body would become—but the being here, the staying, the allowing myself to be used by a landscape that needed me. I came here for reasons that have since dissolved, but I stayed because the Flats revealed their logic: that in a dying place, being part of the dying is a form of service. That in a landscape of afterward, being an afterward yourself is honest.

The sun rises—or rather, the gray lightens to white. Another day on the Flats. I position myself for my morning ritual, feeling the moisture in the air, knowing where the condensation will be richest. My skin prepares itself, pores opening, ready to be the surface the water needs. In the distance, barely visible, another figure does the same at another pool. We do not acknowledge each other. We are not companions. We are components.

The twice-dead sea remembers its boundaries in salt, and I remember my purpose in water. Each morning, each afternoon, each night, I help the Flats continue their slow transformation, their patient alchemy of mud and brine and air. I am a process. I am a place. I am what stands between the final drying and the rain, helping each become the other, helping the water find its way.

This is enough. On the Tidal Flats of Mourn, being enough is everything.

The fog lifts. The work continues. I continue.

The pools hold their perfect mirrors, waiting for rain that comes less often but still comes. The salt traces its borders. The mud breathes its gases. And I stand where I have learned to stand, being what the Flats have made of me: a way for water to remember what it was, even as it becomes what it must be.

The second death continues, and I am part of it, and it is part of me, and this is what it means to belong to a place:

To die with it, slowly, usefully, transformed.
