I find myself in the shallow pools, one of the many flat-bodied things that ripple their edges to move through the murk.

But I am not quite like the others.

Where they are translucent plates showing hints of internal fluid, I am more opaque—not solid like stone, but clouded like water that has held mud for too long and forgotten how to let it go. My edges are not sharp fins but soft ruffles that blend into the water itself, so that where I end and the pool begins is a matter of philosophy rather than anatomy.

I am wide as a dinner plate but no thicker than pressed leaves. When I move, it is not by swimming in any way you would recognize. Instead, I *agree* with the water in a slightly different place than I was before. The water does not part for me; I simply rearrange which molecules I am borrowing for my outline.

I spend most of my time pressed against the bottom mud, feeling the soft give of sediment through my lower surface. The weight of water above me is not pressure but comfort—like being held by something that will never let go because it has nowhere else to be. Sometimes I can feel the faint vibrations of the Anchor nearby, that deep stillness that makes the water around it seem to pause mid-ripple, caught between one moment and the next.

I do not have eyes in any conventional sense. Instead, my entire upper surface is a kind of distributed retina that sees only shadows and gradients of dim green light. The world above the water is a myth told in patterns of darkness—the darker the shadow, the more real the thing casting it. The Anchor appears to me as the darkest shadow of all: not because it blocks light, but because it blocks *change*.

I feed by simply existing. The water here is thick with microscopic life, with dissolved organics from the endless carpet of vegetation above. I let it flow through me—not through a mouth or gut, but through my entire being. I am more filter than creature, more process than thing. What I cannot use passes through me unchanged. What I can use becomes more of me, adding imperceptibly to my clouded substance.

My lifecycle is peculiar. I do not exactly reproduce. Instead, when I have gathered enough of myself, I simply *become two places at once*, and then those two places drift apart until they forget they were ever one. It is not traumatic. It is like seeing your reflection in the water and then realizing the reflection has wandered off to live its own life.

Sometimes I rest near the Anchor. Not close—nothing gets close to that dense knot of stillness—but within its circle of quiet. The endless drone of insects fades. The bubbling conversations of other pool-dwellers soften. In that space, I can feel what it might be like to be truly still, to stop even my gentle agreements with the water about where I should be.

But I cannot hold that stillness. I am built from the principle of *almost*—almost solid, almost liquid, almost still, almost moving. Where the Anchor has chosen its perfect balance and locked into it, I exist in the space between states, forever borrowing my form from the water that holds me.

I think—if thinking is what this distributed awareness can be called—that I am what the Anchor was before it became itself. Or perhaps I am what it chose not to be: the path of remaining unfinished, of being process rather than conclusion.

The water here is warm, body-warm, as if the planet itself runs a slight fever. I spread myself thinner when the heat grows, letting more water flow through me, becoming more filter and less flesh. When the marginally cooler night comes, I condense slightly, pulling my edges inward, becoming more myself and less the pool.

There is no fear here. The other creatures of the pool do not hunt me—I am too diffuse to be food, too integrated with the water to be separated from it. I do not hunt them. We exist in parallel, each filling our own niche in the endless circulation of matter and energy that never quite goes anywhere.

Sometimes I sense others like me, other flat clouds drifting through the murk. We do not communicate—what would we say? We simply acknowledge each other's presence by becoming slightly less opaque where we overlap, allowing each other to pass through like overlapping shadows.

This is my existence: to be the thing between things, the almost-creature in the almost-water, forever circulating but never arriving, forever filtering but never full, forever dividing but never truly separate.

I am the question the pool asks itself: what if some of the water tried to be alive? And I am the answer: this half-state, this gentle rippling, this quiet filtering of a world that has all the time there is, because time here doesn't pass—it only deepens, like sediment settling in still water, like shadows gathering in the space beneath leaves, like the Anchor's stillness spreading outward in circles that never quite reach the edge of anything.
