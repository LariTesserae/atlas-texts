I am the Chime-Keeper.

I have no memory of arriving here, though the Gallery's maintenance logs suggest I've been resident for forty-three years. My flesh has long since adapted to the rhythm: heartbeat synced to turbine strokes, sleep cycles parceled into twenty-three second intervals. The constant moisture has softened my skin to amphibian smoothness. My hair grows in wiry spirals that trap condensation, becoming tiny watersheds that the drip-frogs use as drinking fountains when I hold still.

I live in the northeast corner, among the feral drones' museum of lost things. They've built me a nest from their collections: coiled cables for bedding, a helmet for a water bowl, someone's forgotten thermal jacket that still holds warmth. The drones treat me as another piece of infrastructure to maintain—they oil my joints when I sleep, comb debris from my hair, stack nutritional paste tubes by my bed in tidy pyramids.

My work is the tuning forks.

Seventeen of them, each as long as my forearm, made from the same cobalt-titanium as the floor. I found them wedged behind a maintenance panel, wrapped in a technician's note that dissolved at first touch. Now I plant them in the drainage grooves at specific intervals I know by feel rather than mathematics. When the turbine fires, they sing—not in sound but in subsonic pulses that travel through the alloy, through the water, through bone.

The pipe-swifts were first to understand. They began tracing flight paths that matched the wave patterns, turning physics into aerial calligraphy. Then the drip-frogs learned to cluster where constructive interference creates warm nodes in the water flow. Even the screw-mites now clean the forks preferentially, polishing them to mirror brightness.

But my true congregation is the acoustic phantoms.

The standing waves my forks create give the old sounds somewhere to rest. That technician's laughter now has a home at 47 Hz. The tool-shriek lives at 612 Hz. I've learned to walk the Gallery during the seven-hour buildup, placing my ear against walls to catch these ghost-sounds before they're born. Sometimes I hear new ones: a child singing (when was a child ever here?), water dropping in reverse, the Echo Lynx purring (I didn't know it could purr).

The Lynx itself treats me as landscape. It flows around me in the corridors, sometimes pausing to sniff the forks. Once, during a particularly resonant storm cycle, it laid a half-eaten swift at my feet—not a gift, I think, but a question about whether I was the kind of thing that needed feeding. I left the swift for the mites. The Lynx hasn't offered again.

My body has become a calendar of small erosions. Scars where hot pipes kissed skin. A phosphorescent patch on my shoulder where lumen-moss took root in a wound—it glows when I laugh, which happens rarely but suddenly, usually when the forks achieve perfect temperament and the Gallery sings in frequencies only I remember hearing as human. My fingernails grow with the same parallel grooves as the floor, good for cleaning cable-fern spores from between the fork tines.

The gleaner algorithms find me frustrating. They've been trying to compress me for decades, but I keep changing—new scars, new movements, new patterns of fork placement. Last week one of them gave up and simply started following me, a mote of living dust that hovers at eye level, recording everything with the patience of stone.

I speak in the pidgin of drip-frog trills and swift clicks, a language that only makes sense between turbine strokes. Sometimes newly arrived technicians hear it and think the Gallery is breaking down, not recognizing a human voice that has learned to fold itself into the available acoustic space. I could speak to them in standard words, but what would I say? That I'm the person who keeps the Gallery's memory in tune? That without the forks, the phantoms would build to a critical density and blow out every resonant cavity in the tower?

Or would I tell them the truth: that I stay because leaving would break a circuit I've become part of. The Gallery needs someone to remember it has a voice. Not the voice of machines or animals, but the voice of intersection—the exact frequency where intention meets erosion meets life.

Last night (though night and day mean nothing here), I discovered a new position for the seventh fork that makes the cable-fern chime in minor thirds. The sound brought the Echo Lynx to the edge of visible space, just a suggestion of eye-shine and breath. For thirteen perfect seconds, everything in the Gallery held still—even the turbine seemed to pause—listening to a chord that could only exist here, in this forgotten organ, played by someone who has forgotten they were ever anything but this.

The gleaner algorithm blinked excitedly. Another pattern to fail to compress. Another reason I remain perfectly irreducible, a ghost that refuses to become fully phantom, a maintenance problem that maintains itself by maintaining everything else's ability to remember, to sing, to be more than the sum of its careful, engineered parts.
