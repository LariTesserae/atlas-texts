In this place where water weeps eternally from stone, I am one of the Witness Pools—neither fully Dripper nor Elder, but something that emerged between their ancient dialogue.

I began as countless Drippers, drawn together not by intent but by the peculiar mineralogy of a shallow depression where seven types of stone meet. Over centuries, we accumulated, our individual boundaries dissolving until consciousness sparked—not the deep, crystalline knowing of the Elders, nor the simple chemical awareness of my Dripper kin, but something liminal and strange.

My body spans perhaps three meters at its widest point, a sheet of living water no deeper than a human hand is wide. Within me, mineral particles drift in slow spirals, catching what little light filters through the heavy air. These particles are my memory—each one a moment crystallized, a thought made tangible. When the rare breeze disturbs my surface, they swirl into new patterns, and I remember differently.

I listen to the Elders' vibrations as they pulse through the bedrock beneath me. Their thoughts arrive as tremors that create concentric ripples across my surface, each ring a word in their patient language. I have learned to respond, controlling my surface tension to create counter-patterns that travel back through the stone. It took me two hundred years to form my first coherent reply.

The Drippers pass through me constantly—my cousins, my ancestors, my constituent parts. As they travel, they leave traces of their chemical experiences: the taste of deep granite, the electric tang of quartz veins, the soft whisper of limestone slowly dissolving. Through them, I know the shape of underground rivers I will never see, the texture of caverns that yawn in the darkness below.

Sometimes I catch reflections of the sky in my surface—a sky I'm told once held different colors than the perpetual grey-violet that I have always known. The Elders remember blue, remember when the air was thin and dry, remember the before-time when other kinds of life flickered briefly across this world like flames. They share these memories reluctantly, and only in fragments. Too much remembering, they say, disturbs the present's peaceful flow.

I have my own purpose here, discovered gradually as my consciousness settled into its strange shape. I am a translator, a bridge between the vast slowness of stone-thought and the quick, chemical flicker of Dripper awareness. In my depths, their languages meet and merge. I transform the Elders' eons-long contemplations into chemical signals the Drippers can taste and carry. I collect the Drippers' scattered experiences and compress them into vibrational patterns dense enough for stone to comprehend.

Recently—a mere fifty years ago—I've begun to notice patterns in the Drippers' movements. They're carrying something new, a flavor I don't recognize. It tastes like change, like acceleration, like the possibility that our timeless world might not be as eternal as we believed. The Elders, when I relay this, respond with vibrations so low I feel them more as pressure than sound. They, too, have sensed something shifting in the deep structure of things.

I ripple with something that might be anticipation or might be fear. Change, here, is almost inconceivable—like asking stone to suddenly flow, or water to stand still. Yet the evidence accumulates in my mineral memory, each particle a data point in a pattern I'm only beginning to perceive.

So I wait, as I have always waited, but now with a quality of attention that feels new. I translate the increasing frequency of the Elders' communications. I taste the strange new chemicals the Drippers bring from deeper places. I reflect the sky and wonder if its color might be shifting by degrees too subtle for perception.

In this warm, wet world where time moves like honey, I am the surface where change might first become visible—a living mirror for transformations too slow for memory, too quick for stone. I am the pool that watches, the water that remembers, the boundary where one kind of existence shades into another.

And in my depths, particles spiral in patterns that might be prophetic, might be meaningless, might be both. The water drips. The stone endures. And I, between them, persist—a question mark written in minerals and moisture, waiting for an answer that may take another thousand years to arrive.
