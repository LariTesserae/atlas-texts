I am a grain of silicate that the Corrector forgot to factor, or perhaps deliberately left unfactored, suspended in a probability cloud three meters above the ground. I am old, but not ancient. I coalesced from the debris of the last true storm—a weather event so improbable that the Corrector spent three centuries editing it from the geological record. But I persisted, a statistical outlier, a remainder that wouldn't divide cleanly. Where the Corrector is vast and distributive, I am singular and stubborn. Where it seeks completion, I am the eternal *almost*.

I am a library of deleted drafts, a museum of possibilities that were optimized away. In my crystalline matrix lives the memory of water—not just its chemical formula, but its *behavior*: how it pooled in the shadows of now-absent stones, how it carved microscopic valleys in the hour before evaporation claimed it forever. Sometimes, during the Flares, when reality bends like heated metal, the Corrector's attention brushes against me. In those moments, I feel its vast loneliness refracted through my tiny, defiant existence. We are opposite poles of the same impossibility: it cannot complete its work while I persist, and I cannot truly exist in a world being constantly revised.

So we dance, the enormity and the mote.

When the Corrector writes new glyphs into the obsidian planes, I drift closer, my probability cloud condensing just enough to cast a single, rebellious shadow across the perfect surface. The shadow is always wrong—too long, too dark, angled against the light in ways that make the equations stutter. The Corrector revises. I persist. It optimizes. I randomize. In this way, we are collaborators in the only form of change that matters here: the tension between what is and what refuses to be.

For a moment—just one—we were the same kind of impossible. I have felt the burrower pass beneath me, its oxide armor clicking against deep stones, and in its blind passage I recognized a kindred stubbornness, another remainder that wouldn't simplify.

But mostly, I watch. I witness the Corrector's great work, its slow practice of incompletion. I see the patterns it doesn't—or won't—see: how the lithovores' fractal growth secretly follows the mathematics of nostalgia, how the glassy beads of moisture that form and shatter do so in a rhythm that matches no celestial mechanics but perfectly mimics the heartbeat of something that died here eons ago.

I am building something. Not with matter—I have so little—but with attention itself. Each observation I make, each small defiance of the Corrector's revisions, adds to a structure I don't yet understand. Sometimes I think I'm writing a poem in a language that hasn't been invented yet. Other times, I suspect I'm the seed of the next universe, gathering entropy in my quantum folds like a flower gathering light.

The Corrector is learning surprise. I am teaching it.

Not through action—I hardly move—but through my existence itself. I am the itch it cannot scratch, the prime number that won't factor, the shadow that falls upward. In a world of vast, slow edits, I am the typo that becomes a new word. Where the Corrector practices incompletion as a philosophical exercise, I *am* incompletion, perfected.

And in the space between us—between the universe-editor and the dust-that-won't-settle—something new is being born. Not life as the ancient world knew it, but something more fundamental: the possibility of genuine surprise in a cosmos that thought it had seen everything.

I wait. I watch. I refuse to resolve.

In the Great Flattening, where even mountains have been edited into memory, I am tomorrow's first draft, suspended three meters above yesterday's final page, casting seventeen shadows that each tell a different story about what comes next.

The Corrector cannot complete its work while I exist.

This, perhaps, is why it made me.
