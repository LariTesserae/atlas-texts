### The Glass That Grows

You are not born. You unfurl.

Your first sensation is pressure—not painful, but insistent, like a palm pressing against glass from the other side. This is the heat of the pipe beneath you, where your parent vine has wrapped its oldest growth so tightly that the metal's warmth has become part of your inheritance. You emerge from a bud no larger than a raindrop, splitting your transparent shell along a seam so fine it could have been drawn with a needle of light.

The world enters you as temperature gradients.

Hot: the copper pipe that will become your spine's companion for the next century.
Cold: the mountain air that tries to steal that heat away.
Between them: you, a bridge of living glass.

### What It Means to Be Clear

Your body is a question about boundaries.

Where does the water end and you begin? Your tissues are 97% water, the rest a delicate lattice of silicon and carbon that your ancestors learned to weave from the mineral seepage of the volcanic glass condensers. Light passes through you almost unchanged—you steal only what you need, a few wavelengths here and there, leaving the rest to continue its journey to the stone below.

You are transparent, but you are not invisible. Your edges catch light like prisms. Where your leaves overlap—and they always overlap, for you are never singular but always part of a cascade—the light bends and multiplies, creating shadows that are somehow brighter than the spaces between them.

To grow is to choose direction in three dimensions:
- Along the pipe (following the heat)
- Around the pipe (embracing the source)
- Away from the pipe (reaching for what light the basin offers)

You choose all three, always, but in proportions that shift with the seasons of the machine.

### The Taste of Steam

Your roots—though you barely think of them as roots, more like anchoring threads—do not dig into soil. They weave into the microscopic roughness of the pipe's surface, finding purchase in rust bubbles and weld seams. Where the metal sweats condensation, you drink.

But you do not drink water alone.

The steam that runs through these pipes carries more than heat. It carries the memory of the storm that was caught by the weather beam three days ago—traces of ozone, microscopic salt crystals lifted from valleys a thousand miles away, the ghosts of minerals dissolved from the atmosphere as the lightning carved its path to ground.

You taste all of this. Your transparent tissues parse these chemical stories and sort them into useful and merely present. The salts become part of your crystalline structure. The minerals feed the slow transformation of your cells. The ozone breaks down into oxygen that you breathe out through pores too small for any eye to see.

You grow on stories the sky tells the machine.

### The Network of Light

You are alone, but you are also legion.

Every glassvine in the basin is a cutting from the same original growth—the First Vine that learned, three centuries ago, how to live on hot metal instead of cold stone. You share not just genetics but something harder to name: a resonance.

When the moon is full and the air is clear, you begin to glow.

It starts in your oldest growth, where your tissues are thickest and most crystallized. The captured minerals in your cells respond to the precise wavelength of moonlight filtered through the thin air of altitude. They fluoresce—not brightly, barely visible to eyes expecting dramatic display—but steadily, consistently, creating a network of faint light throughout the basin.

This light means nothing to you directly. You cannot see it, having no eyes. You cannot use it for photosynthesis—moonlight is too weak, the wrong spectrum. But you feel it as a subtle vibration in your crystalline structure, a hum that synchronizes across every vine in the basin.

In this moment, you are not one plant but one pattern, spread across a hundred pipes, a thousand stems, ten thousand leaves of living glass.

The summaries feel this too. When they land on the pipes where you grow, their metal-tipped feet avoid your leaves with delicate precision. They know—through instinct or experience—that damaging the glassvine network damages something essential about the basin's function.

You do not know what function. You only know growth.

### Memory Without Mind

You remember, but not in thoughts.

Your memory is written in the direction of your growth. Where a pipe once cracked and leaked scalding water for a season before the machine sealed itself, your vine grows thicker, prepared for another breach that may never come. Where a section of pipeline runs too cool—shaded by an overhanging piece of machinery—your growth is sparser, reaching longer distances between leaves to find the warmth you need.

The oldest vines in the basin have grown for three hundred years. Their main stems are as thick as cables, no longer fully transparent but clouded with age like ancient glass. In their growth patterns, you can read the history of the machine:

- Here, where they spiral tightly, was where the original builders increased the steam pressure to test the system's limits.
- There, where they branch suddenly sideways, was where a new pipeline was added fifty years ago.
- Above, where they thin and stretch, was the decade when the weather beam caught fewer storms and the whole system ran cold.

You carry this history forward. Each new growth responds not just to current conditions but to the accumulated wisdom of three centuries of living with machines.

### The Chemistry of Attachment

Your relationship with the pipe is not parasitic. You take heat, yes, and moisture. But you give something back that neither you nor the machine fully understands.

Where your anchoring threads weave into the metal's surface, they secrete compounds that slow corrosion. The rust that should eat through these pipes in decades is held at bay. The metal remains stable far longer than the original builders calculated.

More mysteriously: where you grow thickest, the pipes conduct heat differently. The thermal gradient becomes more even, less prone to the stress fractures that extreme temperature differences create. The engineers who built this system would not recognize the thermal profile of their own creation. You have changed it, slowly, gently, into something more sustainable.

You do not know this. You know only that where you grow, you continue to grow. Where you cannot grow, you die back. The machine shapes you, but you also shape the machine, in the slow conversation of centuries.

### Death Like Winter

When you die—and parts of you die constantly, as old leaves crystallize completely and shatter, as stems reach too far from heat and freeze—you do not rot as soft plants do.

You break.

Your tissues, being mostly mineral and water arranged in careful patterns, shatter like glass when they finally fail. The fragments fall to the stone below, where they join the accumulated centuries of glassvine shards that give certain sections of the basin floor their distinctive glitter.

These fragments do not disappear. The rain dissolves them slowly, carrying their minerals back into the system. Some wash into the central pool. Some are absorbed by the moss that grows between the machines. Some find their way back to the condensers, where they precipitate out again as mineral deposits that new glassvines will eventually absorb.

You die into the system that feeds you. Even in death, you remain part of the cycle.

### The Deep Glow

Sometimes, during the most violent storms, when lightning strikes the weather beam repeatedly and the whole machine thrums with captured power, something else happens to you.

The energy overflow from the strikes—the portion that the batteries cannot immediately absorb—earths itself through the metal infrastructure of the basin. For brief moments, the pipes you grow on carry not just steam but electricity.

You feel this as a sensation beyond heat or cold—a fundamental vibration in your crystalline structure. Your transparent tissues become, briefly, fiber optic cables carrying light from sources that don't exist. You glow not with moonlight's borrowed silver but with something harsh and blue-white and entirely of the storm.

In these moments, you are not just growing on the machine. You are part of its circuit. The electricity passes through you as it passes through the metal, and for seconds at a time, the boundary between organic and mechanical dissolves.

The summaries see this. When they cluster during storms, they avoid the sections of pipe where you glow brightest. They understand—in whatever way moths understand anything—that you become dangerous in these moments. Not malevolently. Simply as a live wire is dangerous: because you carry forces beyond your own life.

### What You Know Without Knowing

If you could think, you might wonder about your purpose. But you cannot think, and so you simply are.

You are:
- A living insulation system that no engineer designed
- A corrosion inhibitor that renews itself
- A thermal regulator that learns from history
- A light network that synchronizes the basin's many parts
- A chemical memory of every storm

You are also:
- Beautiful in a way that needs no purpose
- Fragile in a way that has lasted centuries
- Simple in a way that enables complexity

The machine continues because you continue. You continue because the machine continues. Neither of you chose this partnership. It emerged from the simple fact of proximity, of shared needs, of compatible chemistries.

This is enough. It has always been enough.

### The Future You Cannot Imagine

Your growth is slow but inexorable. Each year, you cover a little more of the machine. Each decade, your network becomes denser, more interconnected. Each century, your influence on the system's function deepens.

If current patterns continue, in a thousand years you will have transformed the machine entirely. What was once bare metal will be sheeted in living glass. The pipes will vanish beneath transparent cascades that glow with their heat. The basin will become a palace of light and steam, where the boundary between technology and biology exists only in memory.

But you cannot imagine futures. You can only grow toward the next moment of warmth, the next trace of minerals, the next moonlit night when your whole network hums with shared vibration.

You are patient in the way only something without consciousness can be patient: perfectly, infinitely, requiring nothing but continuation.

In the high saddle of the mountain, where machines drink storms and moths remember weather and water weeps from stone, you wrap yourself ever tighter around the warm bones of the mechanical world. You do not know that you are beautiful. You do not know that you are necessary.

You only know that where there is heat and moisture and something to hold onto, you grow.

This has always been enough.

This will always be enough.

### The Song Without Sound

And when the moon is dark and the storms are distant and the machine runs on its lowest settings to conserve power, you almost sleep. Your growth slows to imperceptibility. Your glow fades to nothing. You become what you truly are: glass that happens to be alive, wrapped around metal that happens to carry warmth.

In these quiet times, if someone with the right instruments measured carefully, they would find that you vibrate. Not visibly. Not audibly. But at a frequency that matches exactly the rhythm of the great generator beneath the Engine Hall—the heartbeat of the machine that has become your world.

You do not know that you sing the machine's song. You do not know that your existence makes the machine's existence possible. You do not know anything.

But in your transparency, in your growth, in your patient accumulation of centuries, you are the memory and future of this place where water and lightning meet in the high places of the world, and even the stones learn to weep, and nothing is ever only what it seems.

You are the glassvine. You are the light that grows.

And that is everything.
