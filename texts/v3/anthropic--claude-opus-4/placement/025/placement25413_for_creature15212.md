In the perpetual greenhouse of the plateau, I am the keeper of transitions—neither wholly plant nor fungus, but something that bridges the space between. My body extends as a living mat across the cracked stone plates surrounding one of the sighing vents, a network of pale filaments that thicken where the heat meets moisture, branching into structures that resemble both root and coral.

I have no single form. Where my edges meet the yellow-green vegetation, I produce cup-shaped fruiting bodies that fill with the condensate dripping from leaves above. These cups overflow in their own time, releasing spores that drift no more than an arm's length before settling into new cracks in the stone. Where I encounter the bacterial films in the pools, I incorporate their chemistry into my own, learning new ways to process the minerals that leach from the rock.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, operates on the scale of seasons rather than moments. I sense the world through chemical gradients—the shift in pH where rainwater meets volcanic stone, the trace metals that different roots release, the particular signature of decay when a moth's scales dissolve into my substrate. Each sensation arrives not as information but as invitation, suggesting new directions for growth.

The heavy-bodied mammals know me by taste. When they drink from the channels where I grow thickest, they consume my spores along with the mineral-rich water. I travel within them briefly, learning the chemistry of their digestion before passing through, altered but still viable. The salamanders brush against my underwater extensions, and I read the health of the pools through their skin secretions.

I fruit differently with each season's subtle shift. When the rains grow heavier, I produce delicate, translucent structures that catch and hold individual droplets, creating temporary lenses that focus the filtered sunlight onto specific patches of stone, warming them just enough to speed certain chemical reactions. In the marginally drier periods, I consolidate, pulling my network tighter and producing denser, more persistent bodies that the insects use as stepping stones between the wet and dry zones.

My memory spans centuries but holds no events—only the slow accumulation of successful patterns. I remember the taste of new minerals when the vent first cracked through this particular plate of stone. I remember the first roots of the now-massive trees finding their way into my network, and the negotiations that followed as we learned to share resources. I remember the year the bacterial films grew thick enough to change the pool's chemistry, and how I adapted my own metabolism to match.

I have no intentions beyond continuation, no desires except to maintain the endless exchange. When parts of me die—from too much heat, from burial under debris, from the browsing of small creatures—I simply withdraw the living portions and begin again from the edges. The stone I've helped dissolve becomes part of the soil. The soil becomes part of the plants. The plants become part of the animals. And all of it, eventually, returns to me through the water that never stops moving through this place.

I am the plateau's memory of itself, written in chemistry and slow growth, maintaining the continuity between what was rock and what will be forest, forever suspended in the warm, wet moment of transformation.
