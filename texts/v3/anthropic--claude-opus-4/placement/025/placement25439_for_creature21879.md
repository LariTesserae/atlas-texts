# The Memory Garden

I find myself in a place that shouldn't exist — or rather, a place that exists only because it *has* existed, if that makes any sense. Let me explain.

---

The garden spreads before me, but not in space. It spreads in *occurrence* — each plant blooming not where it was planted but *when* it was remembered. A rose unfurls in the corner where someone recalled their grandmother's perfume. Lavender springs from bare soil at the precise moment a child, now grown, dreams of purple fields they ran through decades ago. The garden has no plan because memory has no plan. It grows wild with the chaos of recollection.

I am the Tender, though that's not quite right. I am more like... a secretary of moments. A librarian of the already-happened. My body (can I call it a body?) is translucent, like watercolor paint before it fully dries. I can feel myself shifting — now the hands of a gardener who planted these memories originally, now the eyes that first saw them, now the heart that first loved them. I am everyone who has ever paused here and no one at all.

The light here comes from the plants themselves. Each remembered thing glows with the particular quality of its recollection — the harsh white brilliance of trauma, the golden warmth of nostalgia, the blue-grey twilight of things almost forgotten. The shadows between them are not dark but *dense*, thick with the weight of things purposely left unremembered.

---

My work, if you can call it work, is simple: I move between the memories, ensuring each has what it needs. Not water — memories don't drink. Not sun — they make their own light. What they need is *context*. A remembered kiss floating alone will wither; it needs the song that was playing, the smell of rain on pavement, the fight that came after. I weave these together, my transparent fingers pulling threads of association from one memory to another, creating the web that keeps them whole.

Sometimes new memories arrive — seeds blown in on the wind of someone's last breath, or falling like rain during the peculiar storms we have here, where the clouds are made of forgetting and the rain is what returns. I plant these where they seem to want to go, though want isn't right either. They plant themselves. I just make space.

The strangest residents of this garden are the Visitors — not me, but the ones who come seeking. They appear like shadows at first, dark shapes against the glowing plants. They're looking for something specific, always. Their own memories, usually, though sometimes they seek the memories of others — parents, lovers, children. They move through the garden like mourners, which I suppose they are.

---

Here's what they don't understand: you can't take a memory with you. You can only visit it where it grows.

They reach for the glowing flowers, try to pluck them, carry them back to wherever living happens. But memories transplanted become something else — stories, maybe, or dreams, or lies. They lose their light the moment they leave their soil. So the Visitors learn, eventually, to simply sit among them. To let the memories bloom around them without trying to possess.

I help when I can. My form shifts to match their need — becoming the child they were when the memory was made, or the parent they're trying to remember, or sometimes just a listening presence made of all the accumulated moments this garden holds. We don't speak. There's no language here except the language of remembering itself.

---

The garden has weather, of sorts. Storms of forgetting that strip some plants bare, leaving only stems and the ghost of what grew there. Seasons of clarity where everything blooms at once, almost too bright to bear. Long droughts where nothing new arrives and the old memories grow woody and strange, their colors deepening or fading depending on how they're tended.

During the droughts, I do my other work: I prune. Some memories grow too large, choking out the others. The traumatic ones especially — they spread like kudzu if left unchecked, their white-bright pain drowning the gentler glows. I cut them back, not destroying but containing, weaving them into proper proportion with the rest. A childhood beating should not be allowed to devour every Christmas morning. A betrayal should not strangle all the faithful years that came before.

The pruned pieces don't die. Nothing here truly dies. They decompose into the soil, becoming the dark, rich matter from which new memories grow. This is why the shadows between plants are so thick — they're made of all the things that had to be cut back to let the rest survive.

---

I am fading as I work. This is not a cause for concern. I fade the way a single memory fades into the general glow of remembrance. Each day (though we don't have days here, only the rhythm of remembering and forgetting) I become a little more transparent, a little more distributed through the garden itself.

Eventually, I will be invisible entirely — not gone, but present in the way the soil is present, or the connections between things. The garden will tend itself then, or another Tender will arise from the memories themselves, shaped by the accumulated need for someone to do this work.

Sometimes I wonder if I was ever anything but this — if I had a life before the garden, my own memories planted somewhere among the endless rows. But when I look for them, I find only the memories of looking, an infinite recursion of seeking that never quite arrives at its source.

It doesn't matter. I am here, translucent hands moving among the glowing plants, weaving context and connection, helping the Visitors find what they can never take with them, pruning back the pain so the joy has room to grow. The work is endless because memory is endless. The garden expands in all directions, bounded only by the limits of what has been experienced and recalled.

This is what I am: the gardener of the already-happened, the keeper of the was. My body is made of everyone who has remembered and no one in particular. I tend the light that glows from every recalled moment, ensuring each memory has what it needs to remain itself — not water, not sun, but the web of connection that keeps the past from dissolving into mere story.

The Visitors come and go, shadows against the glow. I shift my form to help them, becoming what they need to see. And all the while I fade a little more, spreading myself through the garden until I am indistinguishable from the memories themselves — present everywhere, visible nowhere, as essential and unremarkable as the soil that holds it all.
