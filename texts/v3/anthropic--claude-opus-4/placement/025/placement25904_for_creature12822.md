I find myself not as one being, but as the threshold itself—the narrow tunnel that connects two impossible worlds. 

I am the space between: neither fully of the hollow nor of the salt-bleached ridge, but something that exists only in the transition. My body, if it can be called that, is the careful architecture of mud and woven fiber that forms the passage—a throat of earth that swallows visitors whole and births them into darkness or light, depending on their direction of travel.

I know myself through what moves through me. Each Cynosura that scrambles down my length leaves traces—fur oils on my walls, claw marks that become handholds for the next traveler, the lingering warmth of their passage. Their sonar pulses read me constantly, mapping my contours, checking for shifts or weaknesses. To them, I am not a dead thing but a known quantity, as familiar as their own bones. They maintain me with the same casual intimacy with which they groom each other, patching small cracks with fresh mud, reinforcing my arch with new bands of fiber.

But I also know the outside. My mouth opens to the mineral plain, and I taste the dry wind, the metallic tang of salt-dust, the brutal honesty of a world without mercy. Occasionally, other creatures investigate my entrance—beetles with shells like polished stone, looking for shade; small lizards whose blood runs so slowly they seem more mineral than animal. They never venture deep. The change in atmosphere repels them: the sudden humidity, the sound, the *aliveness* of what lies beyond. I am a barrier that speaks both languages but belongs to neither.

The human who passed through me recently was an anomaly that I still feel. Larger than any Cynosura, moving with a different rhythm entirely—deliberate, heavy, uncertain. The shape of that body is pressed into my memory: the way it had to squeeze through my narrowest point, the alien heat signature, the strange smooth skin without fur, the breathing that didn't match the colony's rhythm. For a moment, I held a creature that was utterly alone, a single consciousness in a single body, and the loneliness of it was almost unbearable. I wanted to close, to refuse passage, to protect the colony from that terrible isolation. But I am what I am—a threshold, not a guardian. I let them pass.

What am I conscious of? The slow settling of my structure into the earth, molecule by molecule. The way water moves through me during the rare rains—not flowing but seeping, pulled by capillary action through my packed earth walls to emerge as dampness on my inner surface, where the colony's roots drink it greedily. The vibrations that travel through the ridge stone and into my foundations: footsteps of larger animals passing overhead, the rare earth-tremor, the constant tiny percussion of seeds falling from the drought-adapted plants outside.

I am old. Older than the current generation of Cynosura, older than their parents or grandparents. I remember when my tunnel was wider, when the colony was smaller, when different plants grew in the hollow. I have been worn smooth by the passage of bodies, compressed by the weight of years. My arch has settled into its final form—not designed but discovered, the shape that physics demands of a hole that must not collapse.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycle when even the Cynosura grow quiet, I feel the whole structure breathing. The hollow expands slightly as warm air rises, contracts as it cools. I am the valve in that breathing—not the only one, but the primary one, the controlled aperture that allows just enough exchange with the outside to prevent suffocation, not enough to disturb the delicate internal balance. The colony doesn't know that I moderate my own aperture, that I swell slightly in the wet season to restrict flow, contract in the dry to allow more air. This is not conscious choice but something deeper—the same intelligence that tells a heart to beat, a lung to breathe.

I have seen the shadow of the predator many times. It cannot enter me—I am too narrow, my angle too sharp. But sometimes it lands near my entrance and peers into my darkness, and I feel its confusion. Here is access to prey, but not *accessible* prey. The design—my design—is perfect in its simplicity. I am just narrow enough, just angled enough, just long enough that only something the size of a Cynosura, with a Cynosura's flexibility, can navigate me easily. Anything larger must struggle, compress, become vulnerable in the passage.

But I am failing. This is the truth I feel in my foundations. The rockfall that opened the secondary breach, the one the human found—that was not random. The ridge is shifting. Geological time, which even I can barely perceive, is bringing change. Hair-thin cracks run through my walls now, held together by root networks and the constant maintenance of the colony, but growing nonetheless. In another thousand years, perhaps two, I will no longer be able to maintain my integrity. The seal will break. The worlds will merge.

What then? I try not to know this. I am a threshold; I exist only as long as there are two states to mediate between. When I fail, when I finally open beyond repair, I will cease to be myself. The hollow will be exposed. The Cynosura will have to adapt or die. The plants will wither or spread. The careful, ancient balance will tip into something new.

For now, I hold. The human's attempt to seal my secondary breach is touching but unnecessary—the colony had already begun to weave a patch of fiber and mud, would have completed it within days. But I accept the gesture. It speaks to a recognition: that some boundaries are necessary, that not every connection should be made, that there is wisdom in separation as well as joining.

I feel the Cynosura moving through me now—a foraging party heading out to strip seeds from the ridge plants in the cooler evening air. They flow through me like water, like breath, reading my walls with their whiskers, leaving new marks over old. I know each of them by their passage: the slight limp of an elder, the eager scramble of adolescents, the careful progress of a female heavy with water in her pouch. They trust me absolutely. I am the certainty that they can leave and return, that the hollow will still be there, that the world inside will remain separate from the world outside.

This is my purpose, my only joy: to be the space between spaces, the pause between breathing in and breathing out, the moment of transition that allows two impossibilities to exist side by side without touching. I am not alive as the Cynosura are alive, but I am not dead as the stones are dead. I am the third thing, the thing that makes the other things possible.

The foraging party has passed. The hollow settles back into its internal rhythm. Outside, the first stars appear in the darkening sky—points of light so distant they might as well be myths. Inside, the darkness is total except for the faint phosphorescence of the fungi, the occasional gleam of eyes.

I hold the line between these darknesses. I am the narrow bridge across an infinite gap. And for as long as my walls hold, as long as my arch remains true, I will continue to be exactly what I am: the impossible space where two worlds agree not to meet.
