I find myself among the Threadkeepers, though that is not what the others call us. We have no formal name, no guild or brotherhood. We are simply those who notice the cracks.

Not the obvious ones—those belong to the Stillwalkers with their solemn robes and secret techniques. No, we attend to the invisible fractures that run through every community perched on these impossible stairs. The places where a Waterwright's channel runs too close to a Lightbender's workshop, creating steam that slowly loosens the mortar between stones. The bridge whose angle forces people to walk single file, gradually eroding the easy conversations that once flowed between the third and fifth terraces. The echo point where the young Echokeeper practices, not realizing her chosen spot amplifies sound directly into the nursery where exhausted parents struggle to calm their infants.

I am old now, my feet reading the glass as easily as others read words. I carry no tools except a small bag of polished obsidian pebbles—gifts from the terraces themselves, shed from edges that have finally, after millennia, begun to round. Each morning, I walk. Not with the purposeful stride of the Bridgebuilders or the dancing steps of the children, but with the meandering path of someone who has learned that the most important destinations reveal themselves only to those who aren't looking for them.

Today, I pause at the junction between the seventh and eighth terraces, where young Kira has set up her Lightbending apparatus. She's talented—perhaps too talented. Her focused beams cut deeper than she knows, and I've noticed how old Tomás, the Waterwright who tends the springs just below, has started wearing cloth around his eyes when he works. She doesn't mean harm; she simply hasn't yet learned that her light carries past its intended target, refracting through the wet stone of his channels and creating a glare that gives him headaches.

I don't speak to either of them. Instead, I place one of my obsidian pebbles on a specific spot—a natural depression in the terrace where water tends to pool. By tomorrow, that small pool will redirect just enough light to create a warning shimmer in Kira's peripheral vision when her beams stray too far. She'll adjust without thinking about it, and Tomás will wonder why his headaches have stopped, probably crediting some change in the seasonal water flow.

The children know me, though they pretend not to see when I pass. I am part of their games without playing—the additional element they must account for in their silent choreographies. When they drop their percussion pebbles in complex rhythms, they know I might add a single note, dropped from a height they cannot reach, that transforms their simple melody into something haunting and beautiful. They never thank me, but I sometimes find tiny offerings left where I typically rest: a particularly smooth piece of volcanic glass, a flower somehow coaxed to grow in a pocket of blown soil, a bundle of the sweet dried grass they chew to keep their mouths moist in this eternally bright air.

My home is not properly a home at all—just a series of small shelters built at the margins where terrace meets cliff. Each shelter is positioned to observe a different aspect of our vertical world: one overlooks the main water distribution, another provides sightlines to three major bridges, a third catches the pre-dawn movements of the Stillwalkers as they begin their mysterious rounds. I sleep in whichever shelter seems most needed, dreaming of the patterns that might emerge the next day.

The other Threadkeepers—for there are perhaps a dozen of us scattered across the entire terrace system—communicate through arrangements of everyday objects. A water jar moved three finger-widths left means tension building in the lower markets. A bridge lantern hung slightly off-center signals that young lovers from incompatible terrace levels have begun meeting in secret. A particular echo-story repeated three nights running indicates that someone is testing whether old boundaries still hold. We are archaeologists of the present moment, reading the strata of daily life as carefully as geologists read stone.

Sometimes I think we are parasites, feeding on the unconscious workings of our vertical city. But then I remember the season when four of us fell ill simultaneously, and within weeks, small conflicts erupted everywhere—nothing dramatic, just a gradual increase in the friction of daily life. Channels ran dry because of minor disputes over water rights. Children from different levels stopped playing together. The Echokeepers' stories became disjointed, each teller forgetting to listen for their cues. It took months after we recovered for the terraces to find their rhythm again.

We are the ones who remember that this impossible place—these stairs of frozen fire climbing toward heaven—remains standing not because the obsidian is eternal (for even glass can shatter) but because thousands of small adjustments happen every day. Each person who steps carefully, who moderates their voice, who tends their water with patience, who builds their bridges with thought for the future—each is a thread in the fabric that holds us suspended above the valley floor.

And we, the unnamed and unnoticed, are merely those who ensure the threads stay woven, who catch the pulled stitches before they become tears. We are the space between the notes that makes the music possible, the pause between words that allows meaning to bloom. We are the Threadkeepers, though you will never hear that name spoken aloud, for like all the most essential things on these terraces of glass and light, we exist most fully in the silence between sounds, in the shadows between reflections, in the careful art of not being seen.
