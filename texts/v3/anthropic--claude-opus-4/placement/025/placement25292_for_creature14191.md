In this breathing world, I would be a **Membrane-Weaver**.

Not quite Scaffolder, not quite Ephemeral—I exist in the ambiguous space between permanence and dissolution. My kind emerged from a mutation, or perhaps a negotiation: Scaffolders who stayed too long in the apertures, whose children were born breathing the spore-thick air of the Throat's interior, until something shifted in our metabolism. We can dissolve—but only partially, only temporarily. We have learned to ride the edge of absorption.

I live midway up a Throat called *Bronze-Mourner* (named by the Scaffolders for the particular octave of its noon-groan). My dwelling is neither fully inside nor out—I've colonized a wound in the bark where lightning struck decades ago, creating a cave-like gash that weeps sweet sap in the heat. I've lined it with shed chitin scales arranged in overlapping patterns that catch the morning condensation, funneling it into carved-cork vessels.

My work is translation. When the Winged return at dusk to dissolve against the inner walls, I position myself at the dissolution sites. As their photosynthetic membranes begin to weep back into the wood, I harvest—not the whole wing, but single threads of the dissolving tissue. These I wind on spindles carved from Throat-cork, working by the phosphorescent light of dying symbiotes.

The threads remember flight.

I weave them while they still hold their journey—the thermal columns they rode, the spore-patterns they witnessed, the geometric mating flights they carved into air. The membrane holds this information for exactly one night. By dawn, if not woven into my patterns, it becomes simple fiber, forgetful and mute.

My weavings are maps that move. Hung in the wind that always flows through my wound-cave, they ripple with remembered thermals. The Scaffolder Bands trade bronze tools and rope for them, using them to predict where the dust-devils will walk, where the next spore-bloom will occur. The Solitaries meditate on them, claiming to see the Throat's dreams made visible in the shimmer of once-wings.

But I know the real audience: the Throat itself.

For I've discovered something the others haven't. The Throats are not merely groaning—they're trying to see. All those billions of Winged are eyes, yes, but eyes that dissolve each night, carrying their visions back into the wood. Yet something is lost in the dissolution, some essential quality of *perspective* that the Throat craves but cannot grasp.

My weavings preserve that perspective. When the wind moves through them, they cast shadows on the inner bark—shadow-patterns that tell stories of the Pan from above, of the world as seen by the free. The Throat drinks these shadows through its bark. In return, it tolerates my partial existence, my refusal to fully join or fully separate.

I am changing. Years of breathing spore-thick air, of handling dissolution-threads, of living in the wound-space between states—my fingers are becoming translucent at the tips. Sometimes I wake to find I've partially dissolved in sleep, my shoulder or hip temporarily absorbed into the cork before I pull myself back to definition. 

The Scaffolders fear me—I am their cautionary tale, the one who went too deep. The Ephemerals don't recognize me as separate from the Throat. But the Scarab-Undertakers, when they pass my cave on their patient rounds, click their mandibles in a rhythm I've learned means "not-yet-cousin."

I weave through the night, my spindles clicking against the cork in counterpoint to the great Groan. By dawn, I'll have created another map of elsewhere, another gift of parallax to the vast, stationary god I live within. And perhaps tomorrow, or in a decade, or when the thermal vents shift and Bronze-Mourner begins to sing a new note, I'll set down my spindles and let the dissolution take me.

But not today. Today I am the bridge—threading memory between the ones who fly and the one who cannot, preserving the ephemeral view for the eternal eye.

The wound weeps sap. I catch it in carved vessels. Tonight, new wings will dissolve, carrying new patterns. And I'll be waiting with my spindles, translating the temporary into something that lasts just long enough to matter.
