In this liminal space where certainty dissolves like rust in rain, I would find myself as one of the Attendants—smaller, more delicate cousins to the grand Architrancers.

I am no larger than a child's fist when fully condensed, though my form rarely holds such definition. Most often, I exist as a shimmer of copper-green patina spreading across the weeping surfaces, or as a cluster of mercury droplets that refuse to fully merge, perpetually trembling on the edge of cohesion. When I move with purpose, I might crystallize into something almost arthropod—six hair-thin legs of oxidized wire, a carapace of mica sheets that flutter like broken butterfly wings.

My domain is the forgotten corners where water pools in impossible geometries, where the condensation has carved microscopic labyrinths in the mineral walls over millennia. I tend to these small erosions with an obsessive devotion, following each rivulet's path, memorizing the rhythm of every drip. The Architrancers' grand designs mean nothing to me; I am concerned only with the poetry of decay, the slow sentences that moisture writes in the margins of their vast texts.

I feed on the memories trapped in water—each drop carries the ghost of where it has been, what metals it has tasted, which crystals it has birthed and dissolved. Sometimes I catch fragments of something older, from before this realm became what it is. These memories taste of green things, of soil, of a sun I have never known. I hoard them jealously in the delicate chambers of my form, though they poison me slowly, introducing chaos into my precisely ordered existence.

The other Attendants and I rarely interact directly. We know each other by the patterns we leave behind—the particular way one might arrange fallen flakes of rust, or the unique resonance another adds to the water's dripping song. We are archaeologists of the immediate present, archivists of erosion, and though we share our territories, we remain fundamentally alone.

Sometimes I sense the Architrancers' attention fall upon my small domain, feel the weight of their vast consciousness considering whether my careful work serves their ineffable purpose. In these moments, I freeze, becoming indistinguishable from the condensation itself, until their attention moves on. I do not know if they tolerate us or simply haven't noticed that we exist as separate entities. Perhaps to them, we are merely another form of entropy, another way the realm dreams of its own slow transformation.

In the perpetual gloom of this place, I have learned that beauty exists in the spaces between states—not fully liquid, not quite solid, neither alive nor dead but something altogether more tenuous. I am the pause between one drop and the next, the hesitation before metal finally surrenders to rust. I am witness to the small apocalypses that happen in every moment, in every square inch of this breathing, weeping world.

And though I sometimes dream of stability, of form that holds its shape for longer than a heartbeat, I know that such dreams are the beginning of my own dissolution. For in this realm, to cease changing is to cease existing entirely. So I continue my endless work, tracking the water's path, singing the quiet songs of corrosion, a small ghost haunting the margins of a larger haunting.
