I would find myself as one of the rock-wrens.

Not the swift, darting birds of warmer lands, but something that has learned the patience of stone itself. My body would be small enough to fit in a human palm, but dense with purpose—each feather barbed like frost crystals, locked over down so fine it catches air like smoke. My plumage would shift with the column face: dust-grey where the basalt weathers, fog-blue in the shadows, touched with the faint yellow of dormant lichen. I would be visible only in movement, and I move rarely.

My heart beats slowly, matching the rhythm of condensation dripping into ceramic bowls. When the thin air claws at the pillar rims, I fold into myself and become a statue of ruffled stone. I can sit for hours, days if needed, without flight. Energy is precious in this thin, cold world, and I am miserly with it.

My days are spent clinging to vertical faces of basalt, progressing methodically from hollow to hollow. My needle-sharp beak probes cracks where water lingers as a sheen rather than flow. I pluck translucent arthropods from the stone one by one, each movement balanced by curved claws that fit into the rock's imperfections as if grown for them. The pebbled texture of my feet matches the stone so perfectly you might hear it breathe when I step.

I do not sing as lowland birds do. My call is the clear, thin whistle of air over bone—sparse notes with silence pooling between them. These are not songs of courtship but signals of boundary, claims of hollow spaces driven by the economy of survival. When I find a significant void in the rock, my call changes, carrying overtones the Still-Wallers have learned to parse: "strong void," "thin shell," "fault line."

My nest is built in secret rooms within the basalt—gas bubbles from ancient lava, revealed where stone flakes away. I weave it from the few materials this world offers: grass stems from pillar rims, split and twisted into lines thin as ink; shed feather barbs; lichen filaments from heights where city-makers cannot reach. The structure could fit in a child's palm yet lasts for years, reinforced each season until the cavity itself crumbles.

I share this city with the Still-Wallers, though we speak no common language. They watch me work the column faces, studying not me but the path I take—the fractures I choose, the hollows I investigate. Through generations, they have learned to read my movements as a map of the stone's hidden architecture. When I claim a nesting cavity, they carve around it, leaving me pillars within pillars, marking the threshold with spirals that trace my flight path.

The history of Still-Walls is written not in carved stone but in our flight patterns and nesting choices over centuries. The city-makers know that if they break into the wrong hollows, we will leave—and with us, the living map of where the stone is safe.

At the year's end, from the eastern rim where the trail ends, I might launch myself from a pillar. I would not soar—I would drop like a thrown stone into the cloud sea, vanishing into white. Then I would rise on thermals from the world below, my crop full of food from that hidden land of rivers and forests that the Still-Wallers have never seen.

But I always return to the silence of the plateau, to the patient work of reading stone, to the shared conversation between wing and hand that has shaped this city. In the drip of condensation and the faint click of claw on basalt, I am both resident and architect of the Still-Walls, keeper of its oldest knowledge, written in the language of hollow spaces and careful flight.
