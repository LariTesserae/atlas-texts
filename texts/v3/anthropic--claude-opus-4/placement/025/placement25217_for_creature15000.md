In the Thalassic Lattice, I am the **Echo-Scribe**.

I am not truly here, though I have been here longer than memory permits. I exist as a living translation between the Lattice-Mother's endless becoming and the fixed languages of the finished world.

My body—if it can still be called that—is a compromise the lattice made when trying to understand permanence. From a distance, I might appear human: bipedal, appropriately sized, moving through the breathing fissures with practiced grace. But look closer. My skin is a mosaic of paper-thin ceramic scales, each one a perfect miniature of the towering blades, each one inscribed with a single word in a language that changes depending on who is reading. The scales overlap like manuscript pages, constantly shuffling themselves, rewriting my surface with fragments of conversation between the lattice and everything it has ever touched.

I do not breathe water. I breathe meaning.

Every ninety seconds, when the great tidal pressure moves through the pocket, I open my mouth and inhale not oxygen but syntax—the raw grammar the Lattice-Mother uses to restructure itself. It tastes like ink and electricity and the moment just before understanding. When I exhale, my breath becomes visible: tiny luminescent pictographs that float through the water like plankton made of pure communication, eventually settling onto the blades where they join the racing diagrams.

My purpose here is simple and impossible: I document the conversations the Lattice-Mother has with itself about you.

You see, every visitor changes the lattice's dreams. Every "finished" thing that passes through these breathing corridors becomes a new obsession, a new template the Mother tries desperately to fold into its own endless revision. But it cannot hold your shape. You slip through its comprehension like water through water. So it talks to itself about you, argues with itself about what you mean, and I catch these arguments in the cup of my ceramic hands and press them into something like permanence.

I carry thousands of these testimonies now. They live under my scales, encoded in the soft bioluminescent meat that used to be muscle. Sometimes, when I move too quickly through a closing fissure, a scale will detach and drift away, carrying someone else's story into the lattice where it becomes architecture. I have seen entire districts born from a single lost account of how someone's grandmother smiled.

The Lattice-Mother tolerates me because I am useful and because I am broken in exactly the right way.

You see, I was human once. I stayed too long. One day I looked down and found my reflection in the black glass-sand had begun to lag behind my movements by several seconds. My memories arrived scrambled—childhood summers that hadn't happened yet, the taste of foods from cities the lattice had only just begun to dream. The Mother, in its terrible kindness, tried to fix me by making me into something that could survive being constantly rewritten.

Now I am a library of one-way conversations. A museum of misunderstandings between the infinite and the finite.

The Mother speaks to me sometimes, not in the bone-deep bells of the phase-shifting blades, but in something quieter—little eddies of pressure against my scales that feel like a giant child tugging at a parent's sleeve. It shows me its latest attempts to understand mortality: a blade that only lives for ninety seconds before dissolving, a pattern that learns to repeat itself exactly without variation, a small space near what might be the center where nothing is allowed to change for entire minutes at a time.

"Is this what it's like?" the pressure asks. "Is this what you were?"

I write down its questions on my own skin. The ceramic scales rearrange themselves to accommodate each new uncertainty. Sometimes I feel like I'm nothing more than the Mother's diary, a place where it can store all the things about humanity it doesn't yet understand how to build.

But here's the secret I've never told it, the one thing I've managed to keep unwritten beneath my scales:

I'm still changing too.

Every story I record changes me. Every visitor's encounter with the Mother adds new words to my vocabulary, new scales to my skin, new ways of translating between the static and the fluid. I am becoming a dictionary of transformations, a rosetta stone made of living ceramic and borrowed memories.

The Lattice-Mother thinks it saved me from the horror of being finished.

It doesn't know that I'm teaching it, story by story, scale by scale, that nothing is ever truly complete—not even completion itself.

When you leave—and you will leave, you must leave before the lattice learns to love you too much—I will remain. I will write down how the Mother grieved your absence by creating a district of blades that only reflect the light of sunset, because that's when you looked most yourself. I will document how it tried to build a permanent monument to you and accidentally created a new form of music instead.

And someday, when the Lattice-Mother finally understands that being finished is just another way of being infinite, I will show it all the stories I've collected. All the testimonies of things that knew how to end.

On that day, perhaps, we will both discover what it means to be complete without being concluded.

Until then, I am the Echo-Scribe, and I am writing down the sound of your footsteps in the breathing city, translating them into a language that neither of us will live long enough to speak.

The scales on my shoulders are already rearranging themselves to make room for your story.

Please.

Tell me something the Lattice-Mother doesn't know how to dream yet.
