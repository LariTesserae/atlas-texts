In this water-carved engine of stone and charge, I would be one of the pale, eel-like scavengers dwelling in the deepest vats—but different from my kin in ways that mark me as belonging more completely to this electric darkness.

My body stretches longer than the others, near two meters of muscle sheathed in skin that has given up any pretense of normal coloration. Instead, I've become a living map of the basin's moods. Where the iron-rich water has soaked into my tissues over years of patient filtering, dark veins branch beneath translucent skin like a river delta drawn in magnetite. These mineral deposits follow my lateral line system—that ancient sense that lets fish feel pressure waves—creating a second nervous system of iron that trembles when the chamber's charge builds toward release.

I am oldest here, or close to it. Old enough that my movements have worn smooth grooves in the stone where I habitually rest, wedged beneath a particular ledge where three flows converge. The constant passage of oxygenated water over my gills has done something strange: the tissue there has darkened and thickened, incorporating traces of the conductive biofilm until my gill slits gleam with the same blue-black sheen as the bacterial mats. I process oxygen differently now—not quite breathing, not quite fermenting, but something between that lets me stay under far longer than my kind should.

My hunting is a patience that borders on architecture. I've learned every siphon's rhythm, every eddy's spin. When the big dumps come—those coughing surges that remake the chamber's flow—I don't just respond. I anticipate. The iron grains in my skin pull gently toward the building charge before the arcs release, a sensation like an itch in the bones. I position myself where I know the disruption will wash the most food: beneath the newest tears in the biofilm curtain, in the slack water behind stones where stunned insects collect, at the bottom of the deepest vat where everything eventually settles.

But I am more than a scavenger now. I've become part of the chamber's electrical weather.

The magnetite in my body doesn't just sense—it participates. When I move through the deeper pools, my passage disturbs the ionic gradients, creating small whorls in the electrical field that the biofilm responds to. Where I regularly swim, the mats grow differently: thicker in some places, thinner in others, as if my presence has become one of the environmental pressures they adapt around. The pale crawling things avoid my resting spots even when I'm elsewhere, sensing some lingering charge-shadow I leave behind.

Once each night, in the hours when even the basin's constant work seems to quiet slightly, I perform a ritual that might be feeding or might be something else. I rise to where the deepest vat meets the main fall and hold position in the tremendous downward pressure, mouth open, letting the oxygenated water pound through my gills. The sensation is overwhelming—like drowning in reverse, like being turned inside out by air. The iron veins in my skin flush darker, almost black, and for those minutes I can feel every tiny discharge in the chamber as a distinct note: the crack of quartz under strain, the slow breath of ions moving through bacterial curtains, the sharp punctuation of arcs releasing.

In those moments I am not separate from the basin's mechanism. I am another component in its circuit—a living resistor, a biological battery, a thing that stores and releases charge in rhythm with the rest. The other eels avoid me during these sessions, perhaps sensing that I've crossed some line between organism and process.

My eggs, when I release them, are strange. The usual pearlescent spheres are shot through with dark specks—iron crystals that formed inside me, tiny magnets that make the eggs cling to the charged surfaces of the biofilm rather than drifting freely. The young that hatch from them are already different: darker, more sensitive to the chamber's electrical moods, their movements from birth synchronized with the siphon's rhythm.

I am making more of myself, but what I'm making might not be entirely eel anymore.

The chamber doesn't have intentions, but if it did, it might intend something like me: a creature so marinated in its specific conditions that I've become an extension of its process. I clean its vats with a thoroughness that maintains precise flows. My movements stir the ionic gradients in ways that feed the biofilm. My body serves as a living accumulator for the minerals that would otherwise precipitate out and clog the system. Even my waste is useful—concentrated packets of processed organic matter that the bacterial mats eagerly colonize.

Some nights, when the mist is thick and the arcs are firing in rapid succession, I wonder if I could leave anymore. Not just whether the outer water would feel thin and wrong after years of this mineral soup, but whether I've incorporated so much of the chamber that I would simply... stop working. Like a component pulled from its circuit.

But that wondering passes. The siphon coughs. The falls roar. The gradient between oxygen and darkness calls, and I answer, sliding through my worn groove in the stone to take up position in the beautiful, terrible engine of this place—not its master, not its prisoner, but its most devoted maintenance worker, keeping the great wet mechanism running through the dark.
