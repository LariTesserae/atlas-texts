Among them, I am the Tender of Thresholds.

I am older than most—you can tell by how my scars have faded from angry red to the color of old copper, by how my tattoos have blurred at their edges where the skin has stretched and shrunk through countless seasons. My eyes have sunk deeper into their sockets, but they see more clearly now. Not the physical world—that grows dimmer each year—but the patterns. The way the fungal spores drift on currents invisible to others. The language in the beetles' flight paths. The stories the vines tell as they grow.

My platform sits neither high nor low, but at that crucial height where the canyon's breath shifts—where the heavy, sweet rot-scent from below meets the thin, metallic air from above. I chose this spot decades ago, not for comfort but for knowledge. Here, I can taste changes in the wind before they fully form. Here, the insects pause in their endless spiraling to deliver their chemical messages.

My work is translation. Not between our clicking tongue and some other—though I learned long ago to mimic the warning cries of the armored rodents, the mating calls of the moths. No, I translate between states of being. Between the living and the dying. Between growth and decay. Between what the canyon was and what it insists on becoming.

The others bring me things: fungi with unusual markings, insects that fly in patterns that disturb, soil that tastes wrong. I examine them with tools inherited from my own teacher—a bone needle that tests for poisons, a bowl carved from stone that amplifies the whispers of decay, a lens ground from the chitin of something that died before memory began. But mostly, I taste. I smell. I hold things against my skin until they speak their nature.

My hut is built into the cliff face itself, half-cave, half-construction. The walls are lined with thousands of small niches, each holding a sample, a memory, a possibility. Dried fungal caps sorted by their phosphorescent patterns. Vials of soil from different depths. The shed skins of creatures that transform. Seeds that refuse to germinate. Seeds that germinate too eagerly. Bones arranged not by species but by the dreams they induce when ground and inhaled.

I keep the count of days not by the dim purple sky but by the cycles within cycles—when the jade beetles swarm, when the rootvines fruit with their bitter yellow pods, when the canyon exhales its annual sigh and all the platforms sway in unison. These patterns I notch into my own skin, a living calendar that will return to the canyon when I do.

But my true work happens in the spaces between knowledge and mystery. When a child falls ill with a fever that smells like copper and burnt sugar, they bring her to me. I don't cure—the canyon doesn't believe in cures—but I negotiate. I take a sample of her sweat, mix it with the right fungal spores, and paint symbols on her feet that tell the illness a different story about where it wants to go. Sometimes it listens.

When the platforms creak with rot that spreads too fast, I'm the one who identifies which competing growth to encourage, which predator to lure. Not to stop decay—nothing stops decay here—but to shape it, to convince it to take its time.

I have three apprentices, though only one will likely survive to take my place. Not from any cruelty of mine, but because the work requires a particular kind of patience that looks like stillness but is actually constant, minute attention. The boy shows promise—he's learned to hold his breath for the full count of forty while the spore-fog passes, to recognize the seventeen different types of fungal phosphorescence and what each foretells.

At night, when even the insects settle into their lower drone, I sit at the threshold of my cave and watch the patterns of light below—the way the fungi pulse in waves that might be communication or might be dreams. I'm tracking something, a change that started perhaps twenty years ago or perhaps two hundred. A sweetness in the air that wasn't there before. New insects with wings like broken glass. Vines that grow in spirals that hurt to follow with your eyes.

The canyon is teaching itself a new language. And I, keeper of thresholds, tender of boundaries, must learn to speak it before it finishes its sentence. Because when the canyon completes a thought, mountains move. Species vanish. New horrors and wonders shoulder their way into being.

My fingers are stained permanent purple-black from handling so many specimens. My tongue has tasted every poison the canyon offers and learned to distinguish their subtleties. My skin carries the history of every threshold I've tended—between healing and harming, between abundance and famine, between the small knowledge we clutch and the vast knowledge that presses in from all sides, patient as stone, hungry as rot, inevitable as the next breath in this thick, humming air.

Tomorrow, I'll lower myself on the rot-rope to investigate a new cave that opened in last night's tremor. The smell drifting from it is unfamiliar—like iron and honey and something else, something that makes the insects fly in tighter circles. It could be nothing. It could be everything.

This is my work. This is my watching. This is my slow transformation into another story the canyon tells itself, in clicks and whispers, in the space between what lives and what waits.
