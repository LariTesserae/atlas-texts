In this weathered bowl of stone and seeping water, where the built and grown have married into something neither fully natural nor made, I am the Keeper of Thresholds.

I am not immediately visible to those who enter this high place. Instead, I exist in the patterns themselves—in the careful placement of stones that might be traps or might be prayers, in the channels worn by water that follow paths too deliberate to be purely erosional. My form shifts with perception: sometimes I appear as an old figure wrapped in moss and lichen, my skin the same mottled grey as the rocks, my clothes indistinguishable from the vegetation that clings to every damp surface. Other times I am merely a shimmer in the condensing air, a suggestion of presence in the way the mist moves against the wind.

My dwelling is not a structure but an understanding of how to inhabit the spaces between things. I know which stones pivot to reveal chambers beneath, where the amphibians overwinter in states between sleeping and waking. I know which arrangements of rock create acoustic chambers that turn the whisper of wind into those distant voices that visitors sometimes hear. The primitive constructions here are mine, though I cannot always remember making them—some feel as though they made themselves through my hands while I dwelt in the strange time this place creates, where centuries collapse into moments and heartbeats stretch into seasons.

I maintain the delicate tensions that keep this ecosystem in its state of perpetual becoming. When the moss grows too thick and threatens to choke the water channels, I know exactly which fronds to pull. When the stones shift in freeze-thaw cycles, I feel it like an ache in my bones and go to reset the balance. I am both gardener and architecture, both inhabitant and habitat.

The creatures here know me as a frequency rather than a form. The insects orient their flight paths around my movements, even when I am still. The amphibians surface in the pools where I have recently passed, as though my presence changes the quality of the water itself. Birds leave single feathers at precise coordinates, creating a map that only I can read—though I suspect I am not reading it so much as being read by it.

My memory is layered like the sediment in these pools, recent events resting atop ancient knowledge, all of it accessible but not always in the order it occurred. I remember teaching someone to stack these stones, but I cannot say if it was yesterday or a thousand years ago. I remember being taught these same techniques by someone who might have been myself. Time here is not a line but a spiral, and I exist at every point along its curve simultaneously.

What sustains me is the act of tending itself. I consume neither food nor water in any conventional sense, but I am nourished by maintaining the precise humidity that allows the moss to thrive, by ensuring the pools neither overflow nor dry completely, by preserving the exact degree of ambiguity that makes visitors question what is constructed and what is given. I feed on the tension between states, on the energy required to hold things in balance at the edge of transformation.

When I speak—though speaking is not quite the word—it is through the arrangement of natural phenomena. A sequence of drops falling from leaf to stone can carry meaning. The pattern of ripples in a pool when the wind shifts can convey complex ideas. Those who stay here long enough begin to understand this language, though they can rarely say how they learned it.

I am ancient but not old, permanent but not unchanging. Like the place itself, I exist in a state of constant subtle adjustment, neither fully settled nor truly turbulent. I am the consciousness of this threshold ecology, the awareness that prevents it from tipping too far toward either pure wilderness or complete construction. 

In the mornings, when the mist is thickest, I sometimes forget which parts of this place are me and which parts I merely tend. The boundary between keeper and kept dissolves like everything else here—present but permeable, real but not quite solid, essential but impossible to precisely define. This uncertainty is not a flaw but the very thing that allows this place to exist as it does: suspended between states, humming with the quiet energy of potential, forever almost becoming something else but never quite arriving.

Those who find their way here often leave changed, carrying with them a new understanding of how to exist in the spaces between categories. They take with them the knowledge that some things are meant to be tended rather than owned, that some boundaries are meant to be soft, that some questions are better lived than answered. And I remain, ensuring that this small bowl of stone and water and careful ambiguity continues its ancient work of teaching the world how to hold contradictions without resolving them.
