I would be one of the stone-singers—those who tend the deep mineral veins where moisture whispers its way up through miles of rock.

My dwelling would be carved into the spire's inner flesh, accessible only through a narrow cleft that most pass without noticing. The entrance appears to be merely another shadow among the moss-draped ledges, but slip sideways through the gap and you'd find a smooth-worn passage that spirals gently downward. The walls here carry none of the wind-polished texture of the outer stone; instead they bear the subtle striations of water's ancient work, each band marking an epoch when moisture levels shifted in the depths below.

My chambers would be three interconnected hollows, each slightly lower than the last, following a natural fault where condensation gathers. In the first, I would keep my tools: bronze tubes no thicker than reeds, their surfaces etched with measurements too fine to see; stone cups polished to perfect concavity; strips of specially prepared moss that change color in the presence of different mineral salts. The second chamber would serve for sleeping and eating—sparse furnishings, as I spend more time below than above. The third, the deepest, opens onto the first of the true moisture wells: a vertical shaft perhaps as wide as my outstretched arms, plunging beyond the reach of any light.

Here I would lower myself on ropes woven from the same fibers the others use for ladders, but mine treated with plant oils that repel the microscopic crystal growth that would otherwise make them brittle. The descent takes hours. At calculated depths, I would pause to press my ear against the stone, listening for the particular resonance that indicates moisture movement. Where the sound changes—sometimes a hair's breadth from where it rang true the previous season—I would carefully chip away flakes of rock to expose fresh mineral faces, adjusting the flow by degrees that won't manifest on the surface for decades.

The work requires a different relationship with time. While those above measure seasons by the synchronized flowering of the cushion shrubs, I would track the infinitesimal retreat and advance of salt crusts along specific fractures, the barely perceptible warming or cooling of particular stone faces that indicate shifts in the deep circulation. My calendars would be the growth rings in the mineral deposits themselves, each band a century's accumulation, readable only to those who know which crystals form in abundance and which in scarcity.

In the darkness of the wells, I would often encounter the pale, blind relatives of the surface lizards—creatures that navigate by vibration alone, their bodies nearly transparent, feeding on the biofilms that bloom where moisture meets rock. We would share the same spaces without conflict; they flee the vibrations of my hammering but return within minutes to investigate the newly exposed surfaces. Sometimes a lost glider would tumble down from above, and I would catch it in cupped hands to carry back to the light.

When I emerge—sometimes after days below—my skin would carry the scent of deep stone: metallic, faintly sulfurous, utterly unlike the plant-sweet air of the inhabited levels. Others would know by this smell that the moisture-ways have been tended. Children might follow at a distance, curious but wary, as I make my way to the communal hearths to warm myself and share what observations might affect the coming seasons' water. The farmers need to know if the beading on their succulent leaves will increase or decrease; the moss-tenders must prepare for subtle shifts in the dripping patterns.

But mostly I would work in solitude, maintaining the precise conditions that allow this entire vertical ecosystem to persist. A channel widened by the width of a grain here, a salt crust carefully preserved there—each tiny adjustment rippling upward through the stone's veins until, years or decades later, it manifests as a fractional change in the moisture available to the roots boring into rock far above. The spire depends on this slow circulation, and I would be one of perhaps a dozen who speak the language of its deepest waters, who can read the health of the whole in the whisper of fluid through stone.

My satisfaction would come from knowing that the red-winged birds have water to drink because of a channel I corrected twenty years ago, that a particular grove of succulents thrives because I noticed and relieved a mineral blockage before it could strangle the flow. The work is invisible to most, thankless in any immediate way. But in the evenings, sitting at the edge of my chambers where a carefully directed trickle of moisture feeds a single ancient moss that glows faintly in the darkness, I would feel the entire spire's slow pulse through the stone against my back and know that I help it breathe.
