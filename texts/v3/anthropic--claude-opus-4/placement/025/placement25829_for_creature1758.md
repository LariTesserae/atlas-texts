# The Keeper of Forgotten Moorings

I am the rope-tender of the Third Basin, though that title hardly captures what I've become after forty-three years threading my life through these waterways. My dwelling—if you can call it that—is a series of connected platforms suspended between four ancient pilings at the confluence of three minor canals. The structure has grown organically over the decades, each addition marking some necessity or whim: a covered workspace here, a sleeping loft there, storage nets strung beneath walkways like spider webs heavy with salvaged flotsam.

My primary occupation is the maintenance and rental of mooring lines—a simple enough trade that has evolved into something far more complex. Every boat that passes through the Third Basin needs a place to tie up, and I know every ring, every post, every secure point within a half-mile radius. But more than that, I know which moorings flood at the third tide, which attract the aggressive tunnel rats, which have rusted through and will fail under strain. This knowledge lives not in any ledger but in my fingers, which can feel the weakness in a rope or the fatigue in metal with barely a touch.

The ropes themselves have become my medium and my language. I splice and weave them into configurations that serve purposes beyond mere anchoring. Some repel the more destructive varieties of canal life—the boring worms that can hollow out a wooden hull in weeks, or the acid-secreting barnacles that cluster on anything stationary. Others are woven with materials that glow faintly in the perpetual twilight, creating navigation aids that the Water Nomads have come to rely upon. A few contain cores of copper wire that connect to the district's sprawling network of communication lines, allowing messages to be passed through the very act of mooring.

My hands tell the story of this work: permanently stained with tar and rope oils, fingers thick with calluses that have hardened into something like horn, nails worn down to nothing. The pinky finger on my left hand bends at an odd angle—broken years ago when a sudden tide surge snapped a mooring line taut while I was adjusting it. My palms bear the spiral scars of rope burns, overlapping so many times they've created a topography all their own.

But it's my feet that mark me most distinctly as one who belongs to this place. Decades of walking on wet wood and slimed stone have splayed my toes wide, the webbing between them more pronounced than in those who live on solid ground. I no longer wear shoes—my soles have thickened and roughened to the point where I can feel the vibrations of approaching boats through the planking, sense the changing pressure that precedes a tide shift, even detect the peculiar tremor that means the great pumps are about to reverse.

My relationship with water has passed through fear, respect, and accommodation to arrive at something like intimacy. I know its every mood in this basin—the oily smoothness that precedes the automated tide changes, the choppy confusion when competing currents meet, the deceptive stillness that marks the presence of underground streams. I can smell the difference between rainwater runoff (sharp, metallic) and seepage from the upper city's fountains (faintly perfumed, touched with copper).

The tools of my trade hang from every available surface: marlinspikes polished smooth by use, fids of various sizes, serving mallets with heads wrapped in leather to prevent sparks. But I also keep more esoteric implements—brass rods that vibrate at specific frequencies to test the deep moorings, bottles of rendering made from canal-eel oil that prevents certain kinds of rot, charts that map not the canals themselves but the invisible currents that run between and beneath them.

My dwelling connects to others through a network of rope bridges—some permanent, others that I can release with a single pull when solitude becomes necessary. The Mechanist who maintains the sector's secondary pumps lives two platforms over; we've developed a relationship of mutual usefulness over the years. She ensures my area never floods beyond the third marker, and I keep her approach clear of the more aggressive rope-growth that can foul her equipment.

Below my main platform, accessible only at low tide, I maintain what I call the Archive—a collection of rope samples dating back over a century. Each fragment tells a story: here, a length of silk-core from when the upper city still traded in luxuries; there, a piece of the experimental kelp-weave that nearly revolutionized mooring before it was discovered to dissolve in the presence of certain industrial pollutants. I can read the history of the district in these fibers—periods of prosperity when even working ropes contained quality materials, lean years when moorings were spliced together from whatever could be salvaged.

My days follow the rhythm of the tides rather than any clock. At the morning ebb, I walk my territory, checking each mooring point, testing tensions, replacing worn sections. The first flow brings the working boats—coal barges, supply vessels, the occasional passenger ferry—each requiring specific accommodations. Midday's second ebb is for maintenance and repair, working in the exposed areas while they're accessible. The evening flow brings the Water Nomads returning to their berths, each family's boat requiring its particular configuration of lines.

But it's the night tides I live for, when the commercial traffic ceases and the canals belong to those of us who truly inhabit them. This is when I practice what I think of as rope-singing—adjusting the tensions and positions of the mooring lines to create harmonic vibrations in the water flow. The sound is subtle, felt more than heard, but it carries messages to those who know how to listen. Other rope-tenders in distant basins respond with their own adjustments, creating a conversation that spans the entire district without words.

I've trained several apprentices over the years, though few last more than a season. The work is too solitary for most, the skills too specific. Those who stay must develop not just the manual expertise but the patience to understand that we're not simply securing boats—we're maintaining the connective tissue that holds this fluid community together. Every rope is a relationship, every knot a promise that what is moored will remain, that what needs to move can be released.

In quiet moments, usually during the deep night when even the mechanical systems seem to drowse, I think about the patterns I've observed over the decades. The way certain moorings are avoided during the full moon tides. The gradual migration of the rope-growth from the eastern channels to the western ones over the past fifteen years. The increasing frequency of what the Mechanists call "anomalous tide events" but which feel to me like the district stretching, testing its boundaries.

Some mornings I wake to find offerings tied to my platform supports—a particularly fine piece of salvaged cable from a grateful Salvager, a bottle of the good rope oil from a Water Nomad whose boat I kept secure during an unexpected surge. These small acknowledgments are worth more than any payment, recognition that what I do matters in ways that go beyond the merely functional.

I know that someday my fingers will lose their sensitivity, my feet their ability to read the vibrations in the wood. When that happens, I'll train one final apprentice—not in the techniques of rope-work, which can be learned, but in the deeper knowledge of how to listen to what the moorings are telling you, how to feel the pulse of the district through the tension in a line, how to be not just a keeper of ropes but a keeper of the connections that prevent this sprawling, breathing, ever-shifting community from simply floating apart.

Until then, I remain here at the confluence, hands wrapped around whatever line needs tending, feet planted on boards that rise and fall with the engineered tides, maintaining my small portion of the vast web that holds the Dockside District together—one splice, one knot, one mooring at a time.
