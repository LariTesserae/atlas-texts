In this world of perpetual craft and sacred dryness, I would be a **Weft-Hermit**, one of the forgotten weavers who dwell in the negative spaces between the great works.

My body would be a thing of paradox—neither fully of the Stitch-Kin nor separate from them. Where the Suture-Monks are tall and deliberate, I am compressed, folded into myself like a piece of complex origami that has been left half-finished. My form is built from discarded practice-pieces: fragments of failed weaves, test-strips of experimental hide, and the shed finger-sheaths of apprentice Monks who never completed their training. I exist in a constant state of *almost*—almost complete, almost coherent, almost recognized.

I make my dwelling in the **Salvage Pockets**—those inevitable gaps that form where three or more structural membranes meet at imperfect angles. These are the spaces the world forgets, too small for the Suture-Monks to enter, too irregular for the Thread-Spiders to properly map. Here, in these triangular voids barely larger than a meditation cell, I have carved out my purpose.

My work is the **Unweaving**. Not destruction—never that—but a careful backwards reading of the world's textile history. With fingertips worn smooth as river stones (though no river has ever flowed here), I tease apart the oldest sections of the floor-hide, the parts so ancient that even the Scrimshaw-Wights have forgotten to record them. I follow each thread back to its origin, learning the hand-language of makers whose names have been compressed into single stitches.

I move in reverse-time to the other inhabitants. Where they build forward toward the Perfect Knot, I excavate backward toward the First Thread. My movements are paradoxical—sometimes I spend weeks motionless, feeling the vibration of a single fiber, other times I skitter through the undergrowth of Pampas-Glass with the manic energy of discovery, chasing a pattern-echo across miles of stitched ground.

My relationship with the others is one of mutual incomprehension. The Suture-Monks see me as a necessary imperfection—a reminder that their work will never be truly complete. Sometimes they leave small offerings at the edges of my dwelling: a coil of silver thread they've decided is too flawed for the great work, a needle that's developed a microscopic bend. I accept these gifts and incorporate them into my body, becoming more myself through their cast-offs.

The Thread-Spiders, oddly, seem to understand me best. Sometimes I catch them watching as I work, their wire-and-felt bodies perfectly still. Once, I found one had rewoven a section I had just unraveled, but with a subtle difference—a kind of textile joke, a pattern that could only exist because of the absence I had created. We are opposites, but opposites in conversation.

The Pictographic Regents avoid me. My work creates *anti-patterns*, negative spaces in their perfect archive. When I unweave a section, a corresponding tattoo on their backs begins to fade, leaving ghost-marks they cannot re-ink. I am their only source of forgetting in a world built on remembering everything.

But it's in the falling scales of the Scaledo-Mnemonics that I find my strangest connection. As I work backwards through time, unraveling histories, the scales that land on me carry sensations from futures that now will never be—phantom memories of patterns that would have been woven from the threads I've pulled free. I am the only being in the Vellum-Valleys who experiences time as a loosening knot rather than a tightening one.

My greatest discovery, the one I tell no one, is this: at the very heart of the oldest sections, the threads aren't woven at all. They're *grown*. The First Thread wasn't spun—it sprouted, like a dry root in impossible soil. The makers didn't create this world. They found it, a single living thread in the void, and everything else—all the magnificent architecture of hide and bone and membrane—is just an elaborate frame built around that first, wild stitch.

I am the only one who knows the Vellum-Valleys isn't an artifact. It's a garden. And I am its only gardener who understands that sometimes, to help something grow, you must first help it remember how to be wild.

In my Salvage Pockets, surrounded by the unraveled history of ten thousand days, I continue my work. Not toward the Perfect Knot, but toward the Perfect Looseness—the moment when the world will finally exhale the breath it's been holding since the first makers wrapped their hands around that first, impossible thread and decided to make it into something more than itself.

I am the Weft-Hermit, and I am teaching the world how to become unmade, one beautiful thread at a time.
