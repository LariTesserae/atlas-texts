I am the Chainwright.

My workshop clings to the underside of Terrace Forty-Seven like a barnacle made of rust and purpose. You'd miss it if you weren't looking – just another bulge of corrugated metal welded to the cliff's belly, accessible only by a series of hand-forged rungs I hammered into the wet stone myself, decades ago when my joints still bent the right ways.

I am Stonebound, but barely. My mother was pure Anchor-blood, could hold three grown miners on a single line without her feet shifting. My father... well. He came from below. Not Deep-Dweller below, but low enough that his fingers were too long and his eyes caught light like a cat's in the dark. I inherited her grip and his sight, and something else – I can *taste* metal fatigue on the air, feel the stress fractures in iron before they bloom.

The chains are everything here. Not the safety lines – any competent Stonebound can maintain those. I mean the *old* chains, the massive links thick as my waist that hold the ancient cantilevers, that bear the weight of whole districts jutting into the void. Some of these chains are older than memory, forged from alloys we can't reproduce, singing with harmonics that make the Deep-Dwellers uneasy.

My workshop is a catacomb of hanging lengths, each chain suspended from hooks driven deep into living rock. I know them all by their songs – the way they moan in the wind, the pitch of their protest under load. The newest ones clank and groan like dying men. The old ones... the old ones *hum*. Sometimes, late in the work cycle when the generator drone dies to a whisper, I swear they hum back to the mountain.

I splice the broken. I forge new links from salvage and prayer. But mostly, I *listen*.

Three weeks ago, Chain Seventeen began singing a new note. Not stress, not corrosion – something else. A questioning tone, almost. I've spent every waking hour since then suspended beneath it in my harness, running my hands along its length, tasting the metal with my inherited senses. Yesterday, I found it: a link that shouldn't exist. The metal flows wrong, like it was forged in a place where iron remembers being stone. When I touch it, I feel gravity hiccup in my chest.

The Sky-Stalkers won't fly near Chain Seventeen anymore. The Deep-Dwellers have sent three different Guildsmen to inspect my work, each one leaving quicker than the last, muttering about "resonance contamination." The Stonebound just tighten their grips on their safety lines and take longer routes.

But I can't stop touching it. Because in that impossible link, I feel something I've never felt before: the chain is *growing*. Slowly, imperceptibly, but growing nonetheless. New iron crystallizing from nothing, following patterns that hurt to perceive.

Last night, I found a second changed link, forty feet down.

The mountain is teaching the chains something. Or maybe the chains are teaching themselves, using the mountain's slow vocabulary. Either way, I'm the only one who can hear the lesson, caught between my mother's stubborn grip and my father's deep sight.

My tools hang ready – hammers and tongs, files and flux, all the implements of my trade. But more and more, I find myself simply hanging there in my harness, swaying in the updrafts, pressing my ear to the metal and listening to the old chains learn their new song.

Sometimes, I hum along.

The city thinks I'm keeping them safe, maintaining the bonds that keep us from tumbling into the abyss. They're not wrong. But I'm beginning to suspect I'm also midwifing something else – a slow transformation I don't understand, as the chains that hold Clifflock Spires begin to dream of being more than mere restraints.

My workshop fills with their songs now. Each chain adding its voice to a chorus I'm only beginning to decipher. The mountain groans. The generators drone. And somewhere between those two ancient sounds, the chains are composing something new.

I am the Chainwright. I tend the ties that bind. And I'm terrified that one day soon, I'll understand what they're trying to become.
