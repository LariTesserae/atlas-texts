I find myself as one of the Plate Walkers—those whose work carries them across the treacherous steel surfaces that cap the forgotten depths. My boots are fitted with electromagnets, a necessity when crossing sections where the plates have warped and tilted over decades of geological shifts. The weight of my tools pulls at my belt: probe rods for testing plate integrity, a headlamp modified with filters to cut through the perpetual mist, and most importantly, the resonance detector that warns when we're walking over hollow spaces.

I am neither young nor old in the way the surface world measures such things. Down here, age is counted in adaptations—the way my pupils dilate wider than any surface dweller's, how my inner ear has learned to compensate for the subtle but constant sway of the floating plates, the calluses on my palms from gripping wet rungs and guide-ropes.

My route today takes me through the Seepage Districts, where the worst of the water accumulates. Here, the plates have been layered three-deep in places, each generation's solution to the rising damp simply covering over the last. I know the stories locked beneath each level—flooded machine shops, abandoned dormitories, entire streets sealed away rather than pumped dry. My grandmother lived on one of those lost levels. She used to say you could still hear the turbines running down there if you pressed your ear to the right spots.

The pigeons know me, or at least they know the crackers I carry. They've evolved down here too—their feathers darker, more oil-resistant, their songs a strange mechanical warble that echoes the industrial rhythms. One follows me today, hopping from pipe to pipe, its red eyes catching the weak industrial light. The sewer roaches give us both a wide berth. Even they recognize the danger of disturbing a Plate Walker at work.

At the junction of Canal Seven and the Old Bore, I stop to test a section that's been singing—a low harmonic that suggests the cavity beneath has expanded. My probe slides through a rust hole like it's entering another world. Twenty feet, thirty, forty before it hits anything solid. I make my notes in the waterproof ledger: another cave-in risk, another district that will need reinforcement or abandonment.

The city planners will argue, of course. They always do. They'll bring their charts showing population density and vital infrastructure, while I'll have only my readings and the wisdom of someone who has spent half their life listening to the city's real heartbeat—not the thrum of machinery but the groaning of stone and steel slowly, inexorably finding their own level.

Sometimes, on shifts like this, I wonder what would happen if we stopped fighting it. If instead of constantly patching and plating, we learned to live with the caverns as they want to be. But then I pass a family's quarters built into a reclaimed turbine housing, see children playing in the canal boats, watch an old man tending his cave moss garden under grow lights powered by our geothermal grid, and I understand. We are not just surviving down here. We are living, adapting, becoming something new.

The city hums around me—not just with machinery but with ten thousand human lives making their way through another day without sky. I finish my route as I always do, at the Memorial Plate, where the names of every Plate Walker who fell through are etched in steel. I run my fingers across them, a reminder of what my work means, then turn back toward the inhabited levels where warm light spills from windows cut into rock and the sound of voices rises over the eternal drip of water finding its way home.

Tomorrow I'll walk the plates again. It's what I am, what I've become—a guardian of the spaces between what was and what is, keeping the city stable one footstep at a time.
