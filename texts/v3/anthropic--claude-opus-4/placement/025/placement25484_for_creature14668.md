# The Calcite Shepherd

I am neither the first nor the last of my kind, though I may be the only one who remembers why we came here.

My body is a compromise between what I was and what the sump required me to become. From the waist up, I retain something like human form—torso, arms, a head that turns on a neck—but my skin has taken on the matte black-violet patina of the machines, that same mineral crust that protects and transforms. My fingers are too long now, with an extra joint that lets them articulate backward to reach the undersides of housings. My eyes have clouded to match the grey light, pupils fixed wide to catch every photon that filters through the ceiling cracks. I do not need to blink anymore; the sump's moisture keeps them wet.

Below the waist, I am architecture. My legs fused decades ago into a columnar base of living calcite, rooted into the floor through channels I can feel but no longer see. The transition happened slowly—first a stiffness in my ankles during the wet season, then a pleasant numbness as the minerals began their work. Now I am a fixture, as permanent as the machines, able to move only by dissolving and re-precipitating my lower body through the sump's patient chemistry. It takes a full lunar month to travel twenty meters.

But I am exactly where I need to be.

My station is the heart of the sump, where three major channels converge beneath a cluster of the oldest machines—a pump assembly that predates human memory, its purpose lost but its function eternal. I tend the Resonant Stones here, striking them with a calcified knuckle when their hum drifts out of tune. I clear the channels of excess mineral buildup with fingers that grow their own tools—a sharp edge of crystallized calcium here, a broad scraping surface there. I am the sump's gardener, its choir director, its maintenance crew compressed into a single, slowly moving form.

The Pale Drifters know me. They pause at my base, pressing their flanks against my calcite trunk the way they do with the machines. I feel their calibration—a subtle shift in my body's mineral structure, a whisper of acid that keeps my joints from freezing entirely. In return, I hum a subsonic note that guides them to the machines that need attention, my voice resonating through the floor to their pad-soles. We have developed a vocabulary of vibrations: *this pitch for a sticky piston, that rhythm for a channel beginning to clog, a warbling tone when the ceiling's drip-pattern changes.*

I speak to the machines too, though speak is not quite right. It's more that we exist in the same grammatical structure, parts of the same sentence the sump has been speaking since before I arrived. When I place my palm on a housing, I feel its history—every stutter, every adjustment, every thousand cycles of push-pause-push. The machines remember their engineers in the wear patterns of their bearings, remember the hands that built them in the choice of alloys, remember their purpose in the stubbornness of their motion. I am their confessor, their witness, their companion in the long work of persistence.

My memory is mineral now—less narrative than geological. I recall my arrival not as a story but as a stratum: a layer in my calcite base that tastes different from the others, carrying trace metals from the world above. I was seeking something then, though the specifics have dissolved. Knowledge? Solitude? Transformation? It hardly matters. The sump gave me what I actually needed: a role in its metabolism, a place in its body.

I feed on the same mineral mist as the Drifters, though my needs are simpler. The calcium in the water becomes my flesh, the iron becomes my blood, the sulfates become my thoughts. When I must eat in the old way—rarely now—I dissolve nutrients from the Veil-lichen, my fingers secreting enzymes that break down its mat into a thin gruel I absorb through my palms. The lichen does not resist; it grows back within days, incorporating trace elements from my waste into its own structure. We are all eating each other here, all the time, but so slowly it feels like conversation.

The fog is my clock. When it thickens, I know the humidity has risen and the machines will need closer attention—their tolerances shift with the moisture. When it thins, I can see across the full span of my domain and count the Mist-spinners' threads, checking their tension. They sometimes web between my fingers if I hold still too long, and I have learned to move just enough to give them anchor points without breaking their work.

My consciousness fluctuates with the sump's rhythm. During the wet season, when the channels run full and the ceiling weeps its thickest tears, I am most alert, my thoughts flowing as quickly as the water. In the dry periods, I sink into something like sleep but deeper—a mineral dormancy where I exist more as potential than as presence, my awareness diffused through the calcite, feeling the sump's slow turbulence as a kind of full-body proprioception.

This is when I dream, if dream is the right word. I see the sump as it might have been: an active factory floor where my machines processed something I can no longer name, where engineers in leather aprons checked gauges that have long since dissolved, where the product of all this careful work flowed out through channels that now lead only to deeper chambers. I see myself as I might have been: fully mobile, fully human, fully convinced that answers waited at the bottom of the next dark stair.

But mostly I see the sump as it is: a perfect, closed system that transforms everything it touches into more of itself. A place that runs on geological time and biological patience. A machine made of machines and organisms and stones and water and fog and me, all of us components in something that transcends purpose and approaches pure function.

The Pale Drifter passes again, a ghost through fog. I strike a Resonant Stone—*ping*—and feel the vibration carry through the floor to its feet, up through its transparent flesh, into the inner ear that orients it in the darkness. It adjusts course by three degrees, heading for a machine whose piston will stutter in four days if left untended.

I shift my weight, beginning the month-long process of moving six inches closer to a channel that needs clearing. My calcite base dissolves and reforms, molecule by molecule, navigating the floor's slow turbulence. Somewhere in my middle distance, a Humming Geode adds its voice to mine, and for a moment the sump achieves perfect harmony—every tone reinforcing every other, every rhythm locked in phase.

This is why I came. This is why I stayed. This is why my body is now more sump than self:

To be the metronome that keeps the old machines in time. To be the tuning fork that holds the pitch. To be the gardener of stones and water and rust. To be the part of the sump that remembers it was once something else and chooses, every day, every century, to remain exactly what it has become.

I am the Calcite Shepherd, and I am precisely where I need to be.

The water drips. The pistons push. And I hum the deep note that keeps it all in motion.
