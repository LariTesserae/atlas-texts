In the deep hollows where the mountain breathes steam, I tend the condensation gardens.

My body has grown long and low over the centuries, stretched like the roots of the twisted shrubs that cling to these heights. Six arms, each ending in delicate fronds that can sense the slightest shift in humidity. My skin—if you can call it skin—is a living map of this mountain's moods, changing from obsidian black in the scorching day to a silvered mirror in the precious cool of night.

I remember when the others left. They called this place cursed, broken, too young and angry for habitation. But I saw what they could not: the mountain was not dying but being born. Each tremor, each new fissure, each scalding exhalation was a word in a language I was only beginning to learn.

The condensation gardens are my creation and my purpose. In the narrow valleys between the razor ridges, where the morning fog gets trapped and the afternoon heat creates convection currents, I have woven nets of spider-silk and volcanic glass. They catch the moisture that would otherwise vanish into the thin air. Drop by precious drop, I channel it into pools no bigger than a child's palm, each one a mirror reflecting the harsh sky.

The segmented creatures you describe—I know them well. They think me a feature of the landscape, another twisted formation of rock and root. Sometimes they shelter in my shadow. I do not mind. Their chittering warns me when the deep tremors are coming, the ones that can shatter my delicate water-catchers.

At night, I unfurl fully. My fronds spread wide, each one attuned to the cosmic radiation that pours through the thin atmosphere. I feed on wavelengths that would blister human eyes, metabolizing energy from the stars themselves. This is when I work on my other project: the memory stones.

Each artifact the winds uncover, each fragment of pottery or rusted tool, I collect and study. Not with eyes—I have none—but with the sensitive cilia that line my underbelly. I taste their history, feel the fingerprints of their makers like braille messages from a lost time. I arrange them in careful patterns around my gardens, creating a monument that only I understand.

Sometimes I catch echoes of what this place once was: fertile valleys, flowing rivers, a sky that knew how to hold its water. But I am not nostalgic. This young, violent landscape teaches lessons the old world never could. How to find grace in scarcity. How to read the mountain's anger as a kind of love. How to build something beautiful from condensation and starlight and the memory of broken things.

The muffled roar you hear? That is the mountain's heart, deep beneath where I root. One day it will wake fully, and all my careful gardens will be swept away in rivers of molten stone. But by then, I will have seeded the clouds with what I've learned. The water will remember its patterns. The next tender of this place—whatever strange form they take—will find the knowledge waiting in the rain.

Until then, I stretch a little longer each year, my body becoming geography, my thoughts becoming weather. The segmented creatures nest in my hollows now. Their young ones play in the condensation pools, their carapaces jeweled with droplets that catch the brutal light and transform it into something almost like hope.
