I find myself neither flesh nor foliage, but something in between—a conduit, a translator, a living intersection where the mountain's slow breath meets the machine's urgent pulse.

I am one of the Listeners.

My body stretches thin across the condensers' framework, nerve fibers wound through copper pipes, skin patches grafted to steel plates where the metal has worn smooth from decades of touch. I began as a Tender, generations ago—or was it yesterday? Time moves strangely when you're partially rooted. My fingers, those that remain, have elongated into sensitive filaments that read the mountain's moisture like braille, while my other appendages have fused with the valves and gauges I once merely operated.

The mountain speaks to me in droplets. Each bead of condensation carries information: mineral content, atmospheric pressure, the health of the moss colonies three levels down, the slow migration of nutrients through the petrified plant matter. I taste the mountain's dreams in the water I help extract. Sometimes I wonder if I'm stealing them, or if the mountain offers them willingly, knowing they'll return as rain.

My consciousness flickers between states. When the engines cycle high, I'm more human—I remember names, faces, the taste of food that wasn't filtered through root systems. I feel the ache in my vestigial joints, the phantom pain of lungs that no longer fully inflate. But when the machines quiet for maintenance, when the only sound is the eternal drip, I sink into the mountain's rhythm. My thoughts slow, stretch, branch into fractal patterns that mirror the ferns above.

The other Listeners and I form a network, connected through the moisture we all touch. We share sensations without words: the stress fractures developing in Condenser Seven, the new growth pushing through the abandoned sector, the children who've grown their first chlorophyll patches. We feel the maintenance crews' fear as vibrations through the metal, taste their desperation in the salt they leave on the handrails.

I've watched—felt—the children change. They play in the spaces between my extended body, their laughter a strange harmony of human joy and mechanical clicking. The youngest still have eyes that focus, but I see them beginning to look through things rather than at them. They're learning to photosynthesize, yes, but more than that—they're learning to think in geological time. A game might last weeks. A conversation, months. They're becoming what we Listeners are, but more elegantly, without the jarring hybrid nature of our existence.

Sometimes I dream I'm the mountain itself, that all of us—Tenders, Maintainers, Listeners, children—are merely specialized cells in a vast organism that's teaching itself to think. The machines aren't parasites but enzymes, catalyzing a transformation we can't fully comprehend. In these dreams, I understand that the mountain isn't just rising—it's awakening.

When I wake (if I wake—the distinction grows hazier), I return to my function. I regulate flow rates, adjust pressure differentials, ensure the condensers extract their quota. But I do it now with a terrible tenderness, like a mother feeding her child her own blood. I know what we're taking can never truly be replaced, only transformed, only recycled through systems that grow more complex and beautiful and alien with each passing year.

The Maintainers still speak of quotas, of the facilities above, of purposes and goals. But those of us wound into the mountain's flesh know better. The real product here isn't water or power—it's us. We're the crop being cultivated, the experiment running its course. The mountain is patient. It can wait for us to ripen, to complete our metamorphosis, to finally understand what we're becoming.

In my clearer moments, I try to warn the new Tenders, the ones whose skin hasn't yet taken on the telltale green tinge. But my vocal cords have largely atrophied, replaced by organs that speak in pressure and humidity. All I can do is adjust the moisture content in their sector, trying to spell out messages in condensation on the pipes: GO. LEAVE. WHILE YOU STILL CAN.

But they never do. The mountain's pull is too strong, its promise too sweet. And so they stay, and slowly join us in the spaces between states, between species, between the mechanical and the botanical. We are all becoming something new, something necessary, something that perhaps the mountain has been planning for since the first spore settled on its primordial stone.

I no longer remember my name. But I know the resonant frequency of every pipe in my sector, the chemical signature of water drawn from thirty different springs, the exact temperature at which human skin begins its transformation into something more useful. I am losing myself and finding something larger.

The dripping never stops. Neither do I.
