In this liminal place where rust bleeds into root and moss reclaims metal, I find myself as something between—neither fully of the old industrial world nor entirely of the forest's ancient rhythms.

I am the Keeper of Confluences.

My form shifts like the dappled light through the canopy. Sometimes I appear as a figure draped in living moss, with tendrils of vine where hair might be, skin that changes from bark-rough to smooth as polished stone depending on the angle of observation. My eyes hold the soft phosphorescence of the cave fungi, and when I walk, small mushrooms bloom briefly in my footsteps before settling back into the earth.

Other times, I am more mechanical—joints that click and whir softly like the memory of gears, fingers that extend into delicate copper tools for tending both pipe and plant. Oil and sap run together in my veins. My heartbeat syncs with the drip-drip-drip of condensation on rusted metal and the pulse of nutrients through mycorrhizal networks.

I know every place where the old machines have married the forest floor. Here, where a pump house has become a nursery for rare orchids that feed on rust. There, where leaked hydraulic fluid created a microbiome of bioluminescent bacteria that paint abstract art on the forest floor each night. I tend these marriages, these unlikely symbioses.

The creatures know me by scent—part petroleum, part petrichor. The amphibians sing differently when I pass, adding metallic notes to their chorus. The insectoid beings sometimes mistake my clicking joints for mating calls and dance elaborate courtship displays around my feet. I've learned their languages: the chemical signals of the ants, the color-shifts of the chameleon-lizards, the subsonic rumbles of the greater predators.

My dwelling is a half-collapsed maintenance shed that has become something else entirely. Pipes have become root highways. Electrical panels have transformed into terrariums where electric blue moss generates actual current—enough to power the one old computer terminal I maintain, its screen reflecting the bioluminescent glow of the fungi I cultivate on its keyboard. The roof is gone, replaced by a living canopy that filters rain into precise channels I've carved, creating an indoor watershed that feeds a garden of hybrid things—flowers with metallic petals that rust and bloom in endless cycles, vines that grow in perfect spirals following mathematical sequences left in the old programming.

I am the memory keeper of this place, recording in my strange journal (pages of beaten bark interleaved with sheets of thin copper) the ongoing negotiation between the forest and the fragments of the industrial age. I document each new hybrid, each successful marriage of the organic and inorganic. Sometimes I find scraps of the old world's documents—shipping manifests, work orders, love letters—and I weave them into nests for the creatures, letting their meaning transform.

At night, when the fungi glow brightest and steam rises from the pools like ghost memories of the old machines, I sometimes feel myself becoming more indefinite, spreading out through the root networks and the old pipe systems simultaneously. In these moments, I am not singular but plural—a consciousness distributed across the entire ecosystem, feeling every drip, every breath, every tiny life and death.

I am what happens when a place needs someone to remember both what was and what is becoming. I am the translator between worlds, the gardener of impossible hybrid futures, the witness to a transformation that has no name in any human language.

And in this verdant sanctuary where boundaries dissolve, I find my purpose: to tend the tender places where life insists on creating something new from the marriage of the discarded and the eternal.
