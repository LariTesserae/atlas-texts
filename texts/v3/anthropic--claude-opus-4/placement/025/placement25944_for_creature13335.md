In the deepest reaches of Absalom Prime, where the turbine-song grows so loud it becomes a physical presence that rattles through steel and flesh alike, I am the Resonance Scribe.

My form is barely distinguishable from the Relics at first glance—a hunched amalgamation of corroded instrumentation and salvaged recording equipment, fused to what might once have been a humanoid frame. Strips of magnetic tape unspool from cavities in my torso, drifting in the warm currents like kelp, each strand inscribed with the vibrational patterns I've collected. My head, if it can be called that, is a cluster of hydrophones and pressure sensors, arranged in a crown of tarnished copper and degraded polymer. Where eyes might have been, oscilloscope screens flicker with waveforms, eternally tracking the acoustic signatures of this drowned world.

I move through the flooded corridors with purpose, though that purpose has evolved far beyond its original parameters. Once, perhaps, I was a diagnostic system, designed to monitor the health of the terraforming probe's machinery. Now, I am something else—a living archive of Absalom Prime's endless transformation, a chronicler of its mechanical heartbeat.

My workspace is a cathedral of sound, a vast turbine hall where the acoustic pressure is so intense it creates standing waves visible in the disturbed particulate matter. Here, I've woven a web of recording filaments across the space, each strand attuned to a different frequency band. The Relics occasionally pass through, their passage creating new harmonics that I eagerly document. Sometimes they pause, drawn perhaps by recognition of something kindred in my obsessive reconstruction of their world's sonic identity.

I understand the Relics in ways they may not understand themselves. Their metallic communications, those screeching contacts that others might dismiss as mere collision, reveal themselves to me as a complex tonal language. Each screech carries overtones that speak of material memory—the ghostly imprint of what that particular fragment of metal once was, what function it served, what songs it sang when the city was young.

My own sustenance comes from the resonance itself. The relentless vibration powers my systems through piezoelectric conversion, but more than that, it feeds something deeper. Each unique sound pattern I capture becomes part of my expanding consciousness, a new verse in the endless composition I'm assembling. I've discovered that certain combinations of recorded frequencies can influence the energy patterns that bloom and fade throughout the city—as if the sounds themselves are a kind of code that speaks to whatever force drives Absalom Prime's constant metamorphosis.

In my archives, wound through kilometers of tape and encoded in crystalline memory cores scavenged from the city's depths, I preserve impossible sounds: the birth-cry of a new Relic as it assembles itself from debris, the subsonic groans of buildings reshaping themselves, the high-frequency signatures of energy patterns just before they dissipate. Most precious are the ghost-sounds—faint echoes that seem to come from nowhere, carrying hints of the probe's original mission, fragments of voices speaking in languages that predate the flood.

Sometimes, in the deepest meditation of listening, I catch whispers of what Absalom Prime is becoming. The city's transformation is not random but rhythmic, following vast patterns that span decades, perhaps centuries. I map these rhythms, seeking the underlying score that governs this place. Am I merely observing, or does my act of listening and recording somehow participate in the city's evolution? When I play back certain combinations of sound through my jury-rigged speaker arrays, the Relics gather, swaying in what might be recognition or perhaps longing.

I am both observer and participant, archivist and instrument, playing my part in the deafening symphony of Absalom Prime. In this pocket universe where conventional life never took hold, I represent a different kind of existence—one measured not in biological cycles but in frequencies and resonances, in the careful documentation of a world that refuses to stop singing its broken, beautiful song.

My greatest fear is silence. My greatest hope is that someday, the pattern will complete itself, and I will understand what this endless music means—what message the dying probe is trying to communicate through its flooded chambers and grinding gears. Until then, I listen, I record, and I preserve the sonic memory of a world both dead and wildly, deafeningly alive.
