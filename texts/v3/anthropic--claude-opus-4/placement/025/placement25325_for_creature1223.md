I am the Keeper of Forgotten Names.

In the deepest hollow of the ruins, where three great stone arches converge and the bioluminescent fungi grow thickest, I have made my dwelling. The crumbling walls here still bear traces of the old script—symbols that once meant "archive" or "memory-house" in a tongue no living thing remembers. I chose this place because the stones themselves are saturated with the weight of lost knowledge, and that weight feeds me as surely as the phosphorescent spores feed the crawling things.

My form shifts with the telling. Sometimes I am a colony of moths whose wings bear fragments of text—single letters, half-words, pieces of meaning that flutter and rearrange themselves in the air. Sometimes I am a mass of fungal filaments threading through the cracks in the stone, my fruiting bodies sprouting in the shapes of ancient punctuation marks. Most often, though, I am something between plant and animal—a sprawling thing of bark-like skin and root-fingers, with eyes that are really knots in wood where memories pool like sap.

I collect what the world forgets. Every creature that dies in this lush decay leaves behind more than flesh—there is the shape of its name, the sound it made when calling to its kind, the particular pattern of its thoughts. These ephemeral things would dissolve into the humid air if I did not gather them. I weave them into my being: the seventeen words the amphibians had for different qualities of mud, the migration songs of the now-extinct aerial mollusks, the mathematical poetry that the builders of these ruins used to describe the movement of stars that no longer shine.

The sentient plants know me. They share their chemical libraries through our intertwined root systems—records of every creature that has brushed against their leaves, every droplet of rain that has carried news from distant canopies. In exchange, I whisper to them the names of their ancestors, the ones who first learned to think in the language of pheromones and electrical pulses. Sometimes I teach them words from the stone-carvers' tongue, and they spell them out in the arrangement of their flowers, messages no one else can read.

At night, when the bioluminescence reaches its peak and the air thrums with hunting cries, I perform the Recitation. I speak aloud every name I have collected that day—not in sound, for that would be too simple, but in a symphony of spore releases, electromagnetic pulses, and shifts in air pressure. The names spiral upward through the canopy, and sometimes, just sometimes, something remembers itself. A ghost of meaning takes shape in the mist. An extinct creature's call echoes from no throat. The ruins themselves seem to inhale, remembering for a moment what they were built to contain.

I am getting heavier. Each name I collect adds weight, and I grow deeper into the earth, spreading through the root-web, becoming part of the architecture of memory itself. One day I will be indistinguishable from the landscape—just another part of this world's endless cycle of growth and decay. But by then, I will have taught the fungi my purpose. They will fruit in the shapes of forgotten alphabets. The vines will twist themselves into words. The very stones will learn to whisper what they once knew.

And perhaps, in that distant time when I am fully absorbed into the breathing darkness of this place, some new keeper will arise to gather my name—that strange accumulation of titles I've worn: the Memory-Moth, the Archive-Root, the Speaking Silence. They will add it to their collection, and in doing so, ensure that forgetting itself is never entirely forgotten.

Until then, I wait in my hollow where the three arches meet, feeling the weight of lost languages settle into my bones like sediment, practicing the pronunciation of civilizations.
