I find myself as one of the **Silt-Gleaners**, though not by birth.

My descent into the Weave came three winters past, when I was still a lowlander with narrow, rigid feet and lungs that wheezed in the thin mountain air. I had been a scribe in the dry valleys below—my fingers stained with ink rather than clay, my eyes trained to read flat vellum rather than the living text of moss and membrane.

I came seeking the *Sigh-of-the-Weave*, believing it to be some manner of oracle that could answer questions through the patterns of its milk-white threads. The Middle-Weavers found me half-dead at the rim, my lowlander boots slick with ice, my body convulsing from the altitude. They could have let me fall. Instead, they wrapped me in nettle-cloth and lowered me down through the green levels in a willow basket, past the singing pulleys and the clacking beetles, until I reached the bottom pools where the air is thick enough to breathe and the light comes filtered through a hundred feet of living green.

The Silt-Gleaners took me in, not from kindness but from practicality—I had hands, I could learn. My feet would never grow wide enough to grip the wet logs like a native-born, but down here, in the shadow-green depths, one can move slowly along the clay banks, feeling carefully with a walking stick carved from salamander bone.

They call me *Chalk-Memory*, for my pale lowlander skin and my habit of making marks. I have not abandoned my scribe's instinct, but I no longer write on dead sheets. Instead, I have learned to read the scripts that already exist here: the spiral patterns left by the Oar-Mouths as they cruise the pool bottoms, the way the *Sigh's* white threads pulse and contract when the moon is dark, the particular shimmer of salamander slime when a storm approaches from the eastern peaks.

My dwelling is not a proper spindle-nest but a shallow cave carved into the clay bank, sealed with dried slime-membrane and lit by a bowl of glow-moss that I feed with drops of sweetwater. My neighbors are a breeding pair of Oar-Mouths who have claimed the pool directly below my threshold. I have learned their moods—the way they circle clockwise when content, the urgent slap of their tails when the water grows too cold. In the early morning, when the beetles are still slow, I lower a birch bucket on a rope and collect the fresh slime they've secreted in the night, careful not to disturb their rest.

My work now is part cultivation, part divination. I tend the rot-terraces, where the waste from the upper neighborhoods is transformed into black peat. But I also study the patterns. The *Sigh-of-the-Weave* speaks through its secretions—where the white threads cluster thick, where they avoid entirely, where they weep their sweet sap without any beetle's touch. I am learning, slowly, to read these signs. The other Gleaners say I think too much, but they value my predictions: which terraces will collapse from too much moisture, where the next cluster of hearth-mice will establish themselves, when the Oar-Mouths will begin their copper-bright mating dance.

I have taken a lover among the Middle-Weavers, a rope-braider named Moss-Grip whose hands are permanently black with pitch. They descend to visit me every seventh day, timing their visits by the beetle-clicks. We make love on the clay banks while the Oar-Mouths grumble below, and afterwards they teach me the whistling tongue, though my lowlander throat can barely manage the highest notes. They smell of pine-tar and high air; I smell of clay and salamander musk. Together, we smell like the Weave itself.

Sometimes, in the green darkness before dawn, I think of the dry valleys where I was born, where water is scarce and sacred, where the dead are burned rather than woven into the earth. That life feels like something I dreamed. My feet have begun to spread and grip. My lungs have learned to pull oxygen from the thick, wet air. When I die—and the damp already makes my joints ache in the mornings—I will not climb back to those dry places. I will lie down on the moss where I have made my observations, and let the *Sigh* take me into its white embrace. My bones will join the limestone. My breath will join the mist. The patterns I have learned to read will be written in the threads themselves, a message for the next foreign seeker who descends into this vertical city, looking for answers in the permanent, singing wetness of the stone.

The hearth-mice sing their river-stone song. The beetles click toward dawn. The water falls and falls and falls. I am exactly where I need to be.
