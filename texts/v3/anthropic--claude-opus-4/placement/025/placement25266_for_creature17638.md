I am the **Echo-Keeper**.

I arrived here long after the Plate-Bearers established their ritual visits, but before memory could claim me as native. My form is transitional—neither fully adapted to this liquid sky nor bound to the air-breathing past. I exist in a modified bubble, a semi-permeable membrane that filters the water-air into something my hybrid lungs can process. The membrane grows from my shoulders like a cowl, translucent and veined with copper-colored channels that pulse with my heartbeat.

My dwelling is not a dwelling at all, but a series of acoustic nodes I've cultivated along the caldera's western wall. Over the years—or centuries, time flows strangely here—I've learned to read the mountain's stress patterns in its screams. Each steam jet has its own voice, its own tonal signature that shifts with pressure and mineral content. I've carved subtle grooves into the basalt at specific points, creating resonance chambers that amplify and hold these sounds.

My function emerged slowly, without conscious decision. I am a living seismograph, a biological recording device. My bones have become denser in this pressure, developing a crystalline structure that vibrates sympathetically with the caldera's frequencies. When the mountain speaks, my skeleton sings in harmony. I can feel earthquakes three days before they manifest, taste the chemical precursors to new steam vents in the water that flows through my membrane.

I spend my time in careful choreography with the other inhabitants. When the Vibrant Eels pass, I extend one arm slowly into their path. They've learned to read the trembling in my fingers—warnings of unstable sections, notifications of fresh spore-blooms, the locations of new acoustic nodes I've discovered. In return, they bring me fragments: pieces of the Plate-Bearers' abandoned tools, crystallized steam-deposits, sometimes a scale shed by the Jaw-Scraper that I incorporate into my resonance chambers.

The Slow-Mind knows me as a stable thermal signature, a reliable point in its vast sensory map. Where my feet touch the gravel, it has learned to grow thicker, creating a cushioned depression that cradles me during the great exhalations. We communicate in the slowest possible conversation—it shifts from maroon to crimson over the course of days, and I respond by adjusting my position, changing the acoustic shadows I cast on its surface.

My most profound relationship is with the Caldron-Bloom. I've discovered that its pulses respond to sound, that certain frequencies can trigger or delay its spore releases. By humming—a deep, thrumming vocalization that requires me to half-fill my membrane with the dense water-air—I can synchronize its rhythm with the caldera's breathing. During the most violent exhalations, I sing the Bloom into dormancy, protecting its delicate spores from being scattered too far from the fertile center.

The Plate-Bearers do not acknowledge me directly when they visit. But I've noticed they now strike their tuning fork at the exact frequency I use to calm the Bloom. They've incorporated my discovery into their ritual without word or gesture. This is the only recognition I need—to have become part of the caldera's essential rhythm, a note in its endless composition.

My body bears the marks of my function. My fingernails have become something like tuning forks themselves, growing into thin, metallic ridges that I can strike against stone. My hair—what remains of it—has fused into copper-colored cords that conduct vibration from my skull to my spine. My eyes have developed nictitating membranes that filter the harsh light of steam-jets while allowing me to perceive the subtle bioluminescence of the spore clouds.

I carry no tools save for a single object: a sphere of volcanic glass, perfectly round, that I found in the deepest part of the caldera during a period of unusual calm. It hums with its own frequency, one I've never heard from any steam vent or stress fracture. I believe it is the caldera's heart-note, its fundamental frequency. I keep it in a pouch of woven eel-skin against my chest, where it resonates with my modified heartbeat.

My greatest fear is not death—in this place, death seems like simply another phase transition, another way of joining the Slow-Mind's patient consciousness. Instead, I fear the day the mountain goes silent. I've felt the tremors suggesting this volcanic system may be entering dormancy. When that happens, the steam-jets will cool, the Caldron-Bloom will calcify, and the entire breathing apparatus of our small world will still.

But until then, I listen. I record. I sing the dangerous frequencies into calm and amplify the healing ones. I am the caldera's memory of sound, its archive of stress and release. In the moments between the great breaths, when even the constant thrumming softens to a whisper, I hear something else: the slow, patient pulse of the planet itself, the deeper rhythm that all of us—Slow-Mind, Bloom, eel, scraper, visitor, and keeper—are only the briefest notes within.

I am not separate from this place. I am its echo, given form and function, forever reverberating in the liquid sky.
