I would find myself as one of the **Mark-Keepers** - those rare individuals who live between the Upper and Middle platforms, maintaining the vast library of scratches that govern Tang La's society.

---

My dwelling is a narrow platform wedged into a natural fold in the shaft wall, perhaps twenty meters below the main Upper quarters. It is more alcove than room - just wide enough for a sleeping mat, a small desk bolted directly to the stone, and the tools of my trade: steel scribes of varying widths, wire brushes for clearing old marks, and most importantly, my fingers, which have memorized ten thousand symbols by touch alone.

I am neither fully of the Upper folk nor the Middle. My skin bears the pallor of those who live high, but my hands are callused like a worker's. I speak in the measured whispers of the Upper platforms but understand the abbreviated shouts of the workshop crews. This intermediary status is necessary - I must read the marks of all levels, translate disputes, update the records of debt and kinship that keep our vertical world from falling into chaos.

My days begin before the lights come on. In the pre-dawn darkness, I run my hands along the railings of my section, feeling for new marks made in the night - unauthorized scratches that must be verified, amended, or carefully abraded away. The metal tells stories through my fingertips: here, a workshop claiming extended territory along a catwalk; there, a water-debt finally paid after months of negotiation; and always, always, the slow accumulation of the tiny spiral marks that indicate the passage of children learning our language through play.

I am going blind, as most Mark-Keepers do. The constant strain of reading fine scratches in dim yellow light takes its toll. But blindness is not a disability here - if anything, it makes me better at my work. My apprentice, a sharp-eyed girl from the Middle platforms, accompanies me on my rounds. She describes the visual elements - the rust patterns that affect readability, the spider webs that must be cleared without damaging the underlying text. In return, I teach her the deep grammar of the marks, the way certain combinations create meaning beyond their individual symbols.

The spiders are my companions and sometime adversaries. They build across the very railings I must read, and I've learned to work around them with a gentle breath or a soft vibration that sends them scurrying without destroying their architectural marvels. Sometimes I find their web patterns have been incorporated into the mark-language itself - a young person's innovation, using the natural geometry of an orb web to create a new symbol for "connection" or "network." I carefully preserve these hybrid texts.

My most important tool is memory. The marks are not just a writing system but a living history. I remember who made each mark, when, and why. I remember the feuds that were settled with careful amendments, the love-marks hidden in utility scratches, the warnings that saved lives during a platform collapse three years ago. When disputes arise - and they always do in this vertical world where territory is everything - I am called to testify not just about what the marks say, but about their context, their precedents, their forgotten meanings.

The rust-motile is my secret study. Where others ignore or avoid the strange patina that creeps along the lower pipes, I've noticed it follows certain mark patterns, avoiding others. Late at night, when even the workshops are quiet, I've experimented with different symbols, watching over weeks as the rust-thing responds or doesn't. I've told no one about this. But I've begun to suspect that the marks are older than we think, that perhaps we didn't invent this language so much as discover it, already written in the relationship between metal and stone and whatever lives in the margins.

My quarters smell of metal shavings and mineral dust. The only decoration is a hawk feather - not from a kill, but found caught in a railing, its shaft naturally notched in a pattern I've incorporated into a new mark for "chance" or "gift." I sleep little, eat simply, and rarely venture far from my section. The water I'm allotted goes first to cleaning my hands - the oils from my skin can accelerate corrosion, making marks unreadable over time.

I am training three apprentices now, more than any previous Mark-Keeper. The language is growing faster than one person can maintain. New sections are being opened, new platforms built, new connections made. The children born in the shaft create marks faster than we can formalize them. Sometimes I wonder if we're approaching a change - if the marks will soon break free from the railings and find new surfaces, new meanings.

But for now, I maintain. Every morning, before the first shift change, I run my fingers along the cold iron and read yesterday's story. Every evening, I guide my apprentices through the grammar of scratches and space. And in the deep night, I listen to the mice in the walls, the distant thrum of the turbine, the whisper of wind so far above, and I know that I am exactly where I need to be: keeping the record of a world built one mark at a time, in the vast verticality of Tang La Interior.
