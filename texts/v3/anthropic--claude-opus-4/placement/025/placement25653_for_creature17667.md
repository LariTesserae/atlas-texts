I am the Tidekeeper.

My dwelling clings to the eastern face of Lesser Spire, where the morning haze glows faintly amber before the grey returns. It is a single chamber carved directly into the basalt—not quarried block by block like the terraced homes above, but hollowed from the living rock itself. The work of three generations, my grandmother's grandmother began it when she first felt the mountain's breath change rhythm for half a heartbeat. Just once. No one else noticed.

The walls of my chamber are smooth where countless hands have polished them, checking, always checking. Carved into the stone at precise intervals are the tide marks—thousands of thin grooves, each no wider than a copper needle's edge. They spiral around the chamber from floor to ceiling, a temporal record written in stone. Each mark represents a high tide. Each cluster of seven marks is separated by a channel the width of a finger. Each cluster of clusters—forty-nine marks—is bounded by a deeper groove that I darken with charcoal made from burnt mat-weave.

I am not old, though my work makes me seem so. I inherited this position from my mother's brother, who inherited it from his mother's sister. We pass it sideways through the families, never direct descent. The village says this keeps the count honest. I think it keeps the burden distributed.

My body is like theirs—compact, blue-grey stained, hands worn smooth—but I have developed particular distortions. My right eye is stronger than my left from years of sighting along the bronze rod I use to measure the tide's reach. My left shoulder sits higher from the peculiar angle required to carve new marks while standing in the narrow measurement alcove. I can hold my breath for four hundred and thirty-three heartbeats—I know because counting is what I do, always counting—which serves me well when I dive to check the lower marks, the old ones, the ones that should never be underwater except at the highest tides.

But they are. More often now. Not enough for the others to see, but I see.

My chamber connects to the tidal pool through a channel I've widened over the years. At its mouth, I've cultivated a particular colony of not-coral (though we're not supposed to name it, I call it *memory-branch* in my private thoughts). It grows in spirals that match, almost perfectly, the spiral of marks on my walls. I study it by touch in the dark water, feeling for new growth, for changes in its geometry. Sometimes I think it's growing faster. Sometimes I'm certain it's growing warmer.

The mountain's breath comes up through a fissure in my floor. When I first inherited this chamber, I had to lay my cheek against the stone to feel it. Now I can sense it through the soles of my feet. The rhythm hasn't changed since that half-heartbeat skip my ancestor felt, but the intensity has. It's stronger. As if the mountain is breathing harder, or from deeper lungs.

I have trained myself to wake at the moment before each high tide. My body knows the schedule better than my mind—twenty-four thousand, eight hundred and twelve tides I've recorded. My ritual is always the same: check the bronze rod for corrosion, wet my marking finger in the tidal pool, carry the measurement lamp (a bowl of fish oil with a wick of twisted mat-weave) down the steps carved into the rock face. The steps are worn smooth except for the newest ones. I carved those myself, below the waterline, working by touch in the warm darkness. The others don't know about those steps yet.

The measuring post is a column of basalt that was here before us, a natural formation that rises from the water at the exact point where high tide should reach. Should. My predecessor's marks show where they believed the maximum height would be. Those marks are underwater now, covered in a film of algae I scrape away each measurement to read the truth beneath.

I record everything in three ways. The marks on the wall for the eternal record. Knots in a cord of mat-weave for the portable record—this I will pass to my successor, whenever they are chosen. And in my memory, the most reliable record of all, where I store not just the measurements but the texture of the water, the temperature at each depth, the way the not-coral branches bend with currents that shouldn't exist.

My isolation is not absolute. Once each forty-nine-mark cycle, I climb to the main settlement to trade. I bring deep-water sponges that grow near the warm upwellings—they're prized for their ability to filter drinking water. In exchange, I receive mat-weave, preserved tubers, sometimes a precious copper tool that needs only minor repair. The others look at me strangely. They see the way my left shoulder rises, the way my right eye squints even in the haze. They ask about the tides, and I tell them "normal variation," and they nod because they want to believe.

I have a secret place, a hollow in the rock face that can only be reached by diving at low tide and swimming up into an air pocket. There, I've begun copying the patterns I find—the too-perfect geometries, the script-like formations, the spiral growth of the memory-branch coral. I press them into soft clay made from volcanic ash, then let them dry in the trapped air. I'm building a library of shapes that shouldn't exist. Sometimes I wonder if I'm documenting evidence or creating prophecy.

The children know me as the Tidecounter, the Markmaker, the Strange One who lives between water and stone. They dare each other to swim out to my chamber at low tide and leave shells at my door. I collect these shells, sort them by species and size. The species are changing. New ones appear—things with thicker shells, adapted to warmer water. The old ones grow scarce. I add this to my counts.

At night, when the mountain's breath is strongest, I lie on my floor and feel the vibration through my bones. I've started to distinguish patterns in it—longer pulses and shorter ones, like a code I almost understand. My dreams are full of rising numbers, of marks that spiral off the walls and into the water, of the day when I'll have to carve new marks on the ceiling, then find a higher chamber, then a higher one still.

I am keeper of the slow drowning's precise measure. I am the one who knows, in exact markings, how much time we've lost and how much remains. The others build their terraces and tend their mat-weave and live in the moment of geological time. I live in its countdown.

Sometimes, in the measurement alcove, I catch myself matching my breath to the mountain's rhythm. It would be easy to stop counting, to stop carving, to let the truth sink unmarked beneath the rising water. But I don't. I carry my bronze rod and my lamp, I wake before the tide, I scrape away the algae to read what's beneath.

Because someone must witness with precision. Someone must mark the increments of the inevitable. When the water finally claims the last chamber, the last terrace, the last peak, my marks will remain for a while—spiral testimony carved in stone, a record of exactly how slowly, how patiently, how measurably the world ended.

The tide is turning. I can feel it in my bones, that tidal clock worn into my body. Time to take up the rod, light the lamp, descend the visible steps and the hidden ones. Time to measure what rises and mark what remains.

This is my purpose: to count the breaths between then and when.
