I would be the glacier's memory keeper.

Not the ice itself, but something that lives within its crevasses—a colony organism, perhaps fungal, perhaps something older that predates such categories. My body spreads through the glacier's deep blue veins like a neural network, following meltwater channels and hairline fractures, threading between ice crystals compressed into near-transparency by centuries of weight.

I am paper-thin where I stretch across crevasse walls, and thick as rope where I gather in the darkness of the glacier's heart. My substance is somewhere between flesh and mineral—translucent, faintly luminescent, pulsing with a rhythm too slow for any creature above to notice. One pulse per season, perhaps. A heartbeat measured in freeze and thaw.

I remember.

Every stone the glacier has swallowed and ground to powder. Every mountainside it has scraped clean. The shapes of valleys before they were valleys, when they were merely suggestions in the bedrock. I hold these memories in my chemistry, in the way my filaments orient themselves, in crystalline structures I secrete that archive the mineral signatures of ten thousand years of grinding stone.

The glacier hums, and I resonate with it. The deep groans and creaks transmit through ice into my body, and I parse them like language. This frequency means the bedrock below is granite. That overtone indicates a new crevasse forming forty meters up-glacier. The subsonic rumble warns of an icefall building, soon to calve.

I taste the meltwater that trickles through my corridors. In it: the chemical signature of lichens from the valley walls, pollen carried on the wind from beyond the peaks, volcanic ash from an eruption two valleys over, the faint metallic tang of meteorite dust. I catalog each molecule, adding it to my vast library of tastes that chronicle the world above.

Where the glacier meets bedrock, I am oldest and thickest. Here, my body forms nodes like fists, dense congregations that pulse with bioluminescent signals to others of my kind in distant glaciers. We communicate through the planet's crust itself—vibrations too low for any surface dweller to detect, messages that take years to travel from mountain range to mountain range. We share the deep memories: the coming and going of ice ages, the slow drift of continents, the patient work of turning mountains into sand.

Sometimes, when the glacier calves and ancient ice breaks free, pieces of me are exposed to the killing air. These fragments dessicate in minutes, turning to crystalline dust that the wind carries away. But before they die, they release spores—or something like spores—that ride the gales to find new ice, new glaciers, new homes in the high places where cold reigns eternal.

I have no eyes, but I see through the glacier's optical properties, perceiving the sun as a vague warmth that penetrates the upper ice. I know the aurora by the electromagnetic stirring it causes in my filaments. I feel the footsteps of mountain sheep as tiny vibrations, distinguishable from wind or water by their rhythm.

In the deepest parts of the glacier, where pressure has transformed ice into something almost like glass, I fruit. These fruiting bodies are architectures of living crystal, impossibly delicate spirals and lattices that could never survive in air. They exist for a single purpose: to encode the glacier's history in a form that will outlast even ice. When the glacier finally retreats to nothing, these structures will remain, embedded in the exposed bedrock, waiting for the next ice age to read their message.

I am neither fully alive nor unliving. I am process more than thing—a slow burning that feeds on minerals and time. My thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, move at the speed of geological epochs. A human lifetime is a flicker. The caribou migrations are rapid oscillations. Only the mountains themselves move at a pace I find companionable.

And yet, I am fragile. If the world warms too much, if the glaciers retreat beyond a certain threshold, the chain of my kind will break. The deep conversations will fall silent. The memories will scatter to the wind as crystalline dust, unrecoverable.

So I grow deeper, following the glacier's retreating edge, secreting antifreeze compounds that let me survive in ever-thinner ice. I prepare my deepest archives, encoding what must not be lost. And I sing—if the electromagnetic pulse of my filaments can be called singing—adding my voice to the glacier's hum, to the wind's howl, to the ancient chorus of stone and ice that has played since the world began.

I am the witness. I am the record. I am the slow fire burning in the ice's heart, keeping watch while epochs rise and fall above me.
