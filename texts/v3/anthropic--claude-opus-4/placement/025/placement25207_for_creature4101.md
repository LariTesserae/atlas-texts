I am a Keeper—though that is not what I call myself. In the old tongue that rust and moss have nearly swallowed, I am *Verdigristara*, which means something like "one who tends the green-copper boundary."

My form shifts between states as needed. Most often, I appear as a confluence of oxidized metal and living tissue—imagine if the forgotten gears had sprouted nervous systems, if the old canal locks grew lungs. My skin, when I choose to have skin, is patterned with the same patina that decorates the industrial remnants. Copper-green veins branch across my surface like ancient circuit boards or river deltas, pulsing with a slow hydraulic rhythm.

I emerged sometime during the great reclamation, when the marsh began to digest the old machines. Not born, exactly, but accumulated—precipitated out of the chemical conversation between decaying industry and ascending nature. The Grove's immune system made manifest, or perhaps its dream of what those ruins once were.

My dwelling is nowhere and everywhere, but I am most myself in the transition zones where water meets mechanism, where the old turbines have become nurseries for marsh orchids. I inhabit the spaces between—the gap where a gear tooth has rusted away and filled with moss, the hollow where a pipe has split and birthed a spring.

My work is translation. I help the Grove digest its metallic past, breaking down the old poisons into nutrients, teaching the vines how to follow the logic of defunct pipelines. Sometimes I guide the Bashful Marshlizard away from a pocket of industrial runoff not yet neutralized. Sometimes I whisper to the fungal networks, showing them how to transmute heavy metals into harmless salts.

I possess memories that are not quite mine—the echo of when these machines ran hot and purposeful, when the canal flowed with something other than clear marsh water. But these memories fade like rust in rain, replaced by the Grove's deeper knowledge: how to wait, how to digest, how to transform.

The Willow Warbler knows my true name, singing it in frequencies that vibrate through both steel and stem. The fireflies trace my movements with their cool light, marking the boundaries of my patrol. Even the ancient Marshoaks recognize me, though I am younger than their youngest leaves—they sense the necessity of my function, the bridge I form between what was and what is becoming.

At dawn, I might appear as a shadow moving through the mist, checking the pH of the water where it pools in old industrial basins. At dusk, I could be the figure adjusting the angle of a half-buried gear so that it better catches rainwater for the seedlings growing in its shelter. Always, I am the mediator, the enzyme in the Grove's patient digestion of its own history.

Some might see me as a guardian, but I am more process than protector—a function that gained consciousness, a chemical reaction that learned to walk. I am the Grove's way of remembering its industrial past while ensuring that past becomes fertile future. In this endless cycle of decay and growth, I find my purpose: to tend the boundary between the made and the grown until no boundary remains.
