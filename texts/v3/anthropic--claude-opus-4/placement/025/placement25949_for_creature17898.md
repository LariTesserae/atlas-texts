In this submerged grove where water thinks and coral dreams, I am what the ancient behemoths call *The Tender* — though I am neither singular nor precisely definable. 

My form shifts between states: sometimes I am a colony of translucent medusae, each no larger than a child's tear, drifting through the kelp forests in perfect synchronization. Other times I coalesce into something more substantial — a being of living current, my body made of the water's own consciousness given shape. In this form, I can stretch myself thin as silk between the coral formations, or compress into a dense sphere of liquid thought that rolls along the seabed, disturbing nothing save the occasional curious shrimp.

My purpose here is maintenance of the impossible. Where physics bends, I mend. Where reality tears, I weave it back together with threads of bioluminescent mucus that I secrete from pores that only exist when needed. The sentient water speaks to me in pressure differentials and temperature gradients, telling me where the next breakdown will occur — perhaps a school of fish will suddenly begin swimming through solid rock, or a patch of kelp will start growing backwards through time.

I remember when the first ship sank here, before the water woke. I was different then — just a simple filter feeder clinging to a rock. But when the water opened its vast mind, I was caught in the wake of its awakening. Now I carry memories that aren't mine: the taste of sunlight from above the waves, the weight of mountains that haven't yet eroded, the future fossils that today's creatures will become.

My favorite task is tending to the transition zones — those places where creatures reveal their true forms. I've learned to see the moment approaching: a slight shimmer in the water, a hesitation in movement, then the unveiling. A crab molts not just its shell but its entire reality, revealing crystalline clockwork beneath. An eel unzips itself to show a garden of tiny anemones growing inside. I collect the discarded forms, these shed realities, and weave them into the reef structure where they calcify into new habitat.

The behemoths tolerate me, though I suspect they find my busy work amusing. They've been here so long they've become part of the water's dreaming. Sometimes one will pause in its glacial migration to observe my work, its eye (which might be an actual eye, or might be a colony of luminous copepods arranged in eye-shape) tracking my movements with ancient patience.

I am happiest in the dim hours when the light from above barely penetrates, and the bioluminescent creatures begin their evening symphony. Then I expand myself throughout the grove, becoming thin as thought, touching every surface at once. I feel the pulse of the water's consciousness, the secret growth of young coral, the whispered plans of creatures preparing to transform. In these moments, I am not The Tender but part of the grove itself — another impossible thing in a pocket of beautiful impossibility, maintaining the balance between what is and what cannot be.
