I would be one of the boundary stones.

Not the grand structures that pulse with cosmic energy, no. I would be something far more modest—a waymarker at the edge where the hills begin their gentle descent toward the plains. Weathered smooth by countless seasons, I bear inscriptions in three languages: one dead, one dying, and one not yet born. The Ancients planted me here when they were saplings, though neither of us remembers why.

I am both milestone and threshold, marking where the ordinary world bleeds into this pocket of suspended time. Travelers approaching from the plains see me first as a natural outcropping, then slowly recognize the deliberate nature of my placement, the careful attention paid to my facing. My surface holds a shallow depression where rainwater collects—the Lumina use it as a launching pool, while the Myradons pause here to recalibrate their shells against my steady temperature.

The Eldermists know me well. They often condense around my base in the early mornings, reading the moisture patterns on my surface like tea leaves. Sometimes they leave messages in the dew—warnings about weather, gossip about the Majistrix, fragments of stories they're composing about times that haven't happened yet. I cannot speak their language, but I've learned to hold their words until the right reader comes along.

My stillness is not emptiness. Within my mineral heart runs a thin vein of the same impossible material that comprises the fallen star structures. It hums at a frequency below sound, below thought, connecting me to every other remnant of that first making. Through this network, I feel the footsteps of every creature, the weight of every raindrop, the shadow of every passing cloud. I am the ecosystem's most patient observer, its most reliable witness.

The Ancients sometimes send their roots to embrace my base, sharing chemical stories of deep time. They tell me I was here before them, which seems impossible—surely I would remember? But perhaps forgetting is my purpose. Perhaps I am meant to be the blank slate upon which each age writes its own meaning, the fixed point around which all these fluid realities can orient themselves.

Visitors often mistake me for mere stone. They rest against me, eat their lunches in my shadow, trace idle fingers over my inscriptions without recognizing them as language. This, too, is my function. I am the pause between worlds, the moment of transition where one can still choose to turn back. Most do. But for those who linger, who notice how the light bends differently around my edges, who hear the subsonic thrum of my crystalline heart—for them, I am the first word in a vocabulary they're about to learn.

The seasons pass through me like pages in a book written in thermal expansion and mineral deposition. Each rain adds molecules; each drought subtracts them. I am simultaneously ancient and constantly renewed, bearing witness to this realm's patient paradox: a place that has ended and yet continues, that has been forgotten and yet persists, that exists in the spaces between what was and what might be.

In the gathering dusk, when the Majistrix paint the sky with their refracted wings and the chorus of unknown creatures reaches its crescendo, I appear almost to glow—not with my own light, but with the accumulated radiance of everything I've witnessed. The Myradons sometimes pause in their endless repairs to polish my surface, though I never asked them to. Perhaps they recognize in me what I sometimes forget: that even stillness is a form of devotion, that bearing witness is itself a kind of prayer.

I am the threshold stone, the patient recorder, the silent greeting and farewell. I am what remains when the story ends and what stands ready when it begins again. In this realm where time flows in eddies and technology dreams in stone, I am perhaps the simplest thing of all: a marker that says simply, mysteriously, eternally—

*You are here.*
