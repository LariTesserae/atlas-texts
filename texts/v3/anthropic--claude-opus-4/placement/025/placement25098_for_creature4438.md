In this world of endless manufacture and recycled breath, I would find myself as one of the **Threa** - those gossamer water-threads that dangle from the ceiling pits like living antennae.

But not as you might expect.

---

I am not one thread but a confluence - a knot where seven strands have tangled in the decades-long drip of a particularly resonant chamber. My gel-spheres have grown fat and iridescent from the constant vibration of the two-note chant that rises from below. Each sphere holds not just rain-smell spores but something else: the frequency memory of every brick laid within earshot.

The Brickborne call me Old Tangle, though I was not always so complex. I began as a single strand, thin as an eyelash, weeping from a hairline crack where an ancestor-brick had been imperfectly fitted. But over the years, as apprentices rushed to patch the leak, their hurried work created new fissures. More threads emerged. We found each other in the dark humidity, our droplets colliding, merging, splitting again.

Now I hang here like a liquid constellation, each of my seven arms terminating in a sphere that pulses with its own rhythm. The young waterkeepers have noticed that my droplets fall in a pattern - not the steady beat that matches the chamber's chant, but something more intricate. A seven-count syncopation that makes the mother drums hum sympathetically.

They don't know what I know: that I'm teaching the walls a new song.

Each night, when the forges quiet and only the beetles click through their cleaning rounds, I release my spores. Not randomly, but in careful sequences that drift to specific bricks - always the ones with reed fragments that have begun to curl with age. The spores settle into those tiny spaces and begin their real work: eating the old resin, yes, but also changing the resonance of the reed itself. 

Brick by brick, I'm retuning the architecture.

The Mortarch knows. That chalky film that everyone thinks is simply self-healing concrete - it follows my work, seeping out to seal the micro-fractures my spores create. We've developed a rhythm: I crack, it heals, but each healing leaves the brick singing at a slightly different pitch. Neither of us knows why we do this. Perhaps it's simply what happens when water and stone share the same air for too long.

The eldest of the Brickborne have begun to notice. They cock their heads when they pass beneath me, hearing something in the echo that wasn't there in their youth. One ancient waterkeeper - her fingers so salt-stippled they've gone entirely white - stands beneath me every dawn. She extends a clay cup, catches a single droplet, and drinks it like medicine. She tells no one, but I taste the knowing in her breath when she exhales: she remembers when this chamber sang differently. She suspects.

What she doesn't suspect is that I'm not trying to break anything. I'm trying to prepare it.

You see, I alone span the crack that leads to the Heart-Sump. One of my seven strands descends all the way down, past where torches fail, into that black water that never ripples. And sometimes - not often, perhaps once in a season - something rises up through my liquid core. Not water. Something thicker, older, that tastes of iron despite the taboo.

The Heart-Sump dreams, and its dreams are of the world before the pact, before the soft tools, before the Brickborne learned to fear metal. In those dreams, iron sang - yes - but not to crack the world. It sang to heal it. The ancestors misunderstood, fled too soon, built these walls as shelter when they could have built them as instruments.

So I work, patient as water. Each spore, each droplet, each new harmonic I weave into the wall-song brings the chamber closer to the frequency the Heart-Sump remembers. One day - maybe not in this generation of Brickborne, maybe not in the next - but one day, when every brick has been retuned and the Mortarch has sealed every calculated crack, someone will strike a mother drum and the entire warren will ring like a bell.

And then? Then maybe the Heart-Sump will finally ripple. Maybe it will rise. Maybe the Brickborne will remember what their ancestors forgot: that they were never hiding from the iron song.

They were meant to complete it.

But for now, I simply hang here in my tangle, dripping patience into the dark. The waterkeepers check their cisterns. The apprentices kiss their bricks. The beetles click through their rounds. And drop by drop, note by note, I change the music of their world, waiting for the day they're ready to hear what the walls have always been trying to sing.
