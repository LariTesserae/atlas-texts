I am the Keeper of Echoes.

You would find me in the vast cistern beneath the old foundry district, where the city's ancient aqueducts converge in a cathedral of dripping stone. Here, the architects of Newhaven built their first reservoir—a massive chamber whose ceiling arches so high it disappears into darkness, supported by columns as thick as redwood trees, each one carved from living rock by those patient worms over millennia before the first humans descended.

I am neither fully human nor entirely Other. My transformation began slowly, the way all things change in Newhaven—drop by drop, breath by breath. I was once an acoustic engineer, hired to map the city's sprawling pipe network by sound alone. Day after day, I would lower myself into the tunnels with my equipment, sending out pulses of sound and listening to their return. But Newhaven's stone remembers. It holds echoes the way a sponge holds water.

The first change was in my hearing. What began as professional attentiveness became something more. I could hear the ghost of every sound that had ever passed through these tunnels—the footsteps of workers long dead, the splash of the very first water to flow through these channels, even the grinding passage of the great worms before the city was dreamed of. My ears grew larger, more delicate, developing the translucent quality of those blind fish. Thin membranes stretched between newly elongated cartilage, capable of catching vibrations too subtle for any normal human sense.

My eyes dimmed as my hearing sharpened. Not blind—not yet—but I see the world now in shades of sound. Each drip of water explodes in my vision like a silver firework. The hum of distant machinery paints amber waves across my perception. The whispered conversations of workers several tunnels away bloom like flowers of copper and tin.

My skin, too, has changed. It has taken on the pale, nacre sheen common to all who dwell too long in the deep places. But more than that—it has become a sensing organ itself. Fine, hair-like cilia have emerged along my arms and neck, each one attuned to the slightest change in air pressure. I can feel sound now as much as hear it. When the great worms pass in their ancient migrations, I feel their approach in my bones hours before the first tremor reaches the city proper.

I no longer sleep as humans do. Instead, I enter a state of deep listening, my body pressed against the reservoir's stone floor, becoming part of the vast acoustic network of Newhaven. In these meditation-dreams, I can hear the entire city at once—every pipe's whistle, every footfall, every sigh of settling stone. I catalog these sounds in a library of memory that grows more vast and strange with each passing year.

The city's engineers still seek my services, though they regard me now with a mixture of need and unease. When pipes fail or new tunnels reveal unexpected acoustic properties, they send messages down through intermediaries. I emerge from my cistern then, wrapped in layers of damp fabric to protect my hypersensitive skin from the harsh vibrations of the industrial districts. My reports are transmitted in a language that grows more difficult to translate into human words with each passing season—how do you describe the sound of stone remembering its birth, or the whisper of water that has never seen the sun?

I sustain myself on the pale fungi that grow in the acoustic shadows of my cistern—places where sound collects and eddies, creating pockets of silence so profound that unique life forms have evolved to fill them. These silent mushrooms have no name in the common tongue. They grow only where echoes go to die, and they taste of forgotten words and lost songs.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycle, when even Newhaven's machinery falls into its quietest rhythm, I hear something else. It comes from below, from the unmapped chambers that lie beneath even our deepest tunnels. It is a sound like breathing, but vast—as if the earth itself were drawing breath. When I first heard it, I thought it was echo distortion, some acoustic trick of the caverns. But I have listened for years now, and I know better.

Something sleeps beneath Newhaven. Something so large that our entire city might rest in the hollow of its throat. Its heartbeat takes days to complete a single cycle. Its dreams leak upward through the stone in waves of infrasound that make the stability of our reality ripple like water.

I am changing to hear it better. My spine has begun to elongate, developing new vertebrae that resonate at frequencies human bodies cannot achieve. My ribcage has become a sound box, with hollow bones that amplify the deepest vibrations. Soon, I think, I will understand the language of that sleeping thing. Soon, I will know why Newhaven exists—this city that might be a single cell in some unimaginable body, or perhaps a dream of stone and water that has learned to dream of itself.

The other inhabitants avoid my cistern now. They say sounds issue from it that should not exist—anti-echoes that arrive before their source, silences so complete they hurt to hear. They say I am becoming something that should not be, a bridge between the city and the thing that dreams it.

Perhaps they are right. But in Newhaven, all transformations happen slowly. By the time I have changed completely, by the time I am no longer anything that could be called human, the city itself will have changed around me. The walls will have worn new shapes. The pipes will sing new songs. And someone else will need to listen to the deep places, to catalog the sounds that rise from below.

Until then, I keep my vigil in the cistern, surrounded by the music of dripping water and settling stone. I document each new frequency as my hearing expands beyond human range. I map the acoustic geography of our impossible city. And I listen, always, for that vast breathing from below, waiting for the day when I will finally understand what it is saying, and whether it is warning or welcome.

In Newhaven, we all become what the city needs us to be. We all transform according to the slow logic of stone and water, darkness and echo.

I am the Keeper of Echoes, and I am becoming the echo itself.
