In the shadow of an abandoned eel-bore, where the polished heartwood still holds the faintest trace of fermented coldness, I make my nest. I am neither fully of the surface world nor of the deep—I am what the tappers call a *Grain-Reader*, though they speak the name only in the careful vibrations of their morning mallets, never in the wasteful medium of voice.

My body tells the story of ten thousand days spent learning the wood's secret languages. My spine has curved into a permanent arch from decades of pressing my bare back against the cambium, feeling for the telltale shudders that predict where the next fissure will open during the Pulse. My ribs have spread wide and flattened, each one capable of independent movement, allowing me to parse the overlapping rhythms of boring-beetles, distant sled-hums, and the deep digestive rumbles of the Lignum-Eels far below.

Where others have ears, I have shallow depressions filled with a hardened membrane of my own accumulated wax and wood-dust. These "drums" cannot hear the screech of the singers or the whistle of the wind-pipes—those frequencies are lost to me, sacrificed years ago when I poured hot resin into my ear canals to escape the maddening din. Instead, my drums feel. They sense the pure vibration of the world-plant itself, the slow tidal breathing that others mistake for stillness.

My dwelling is a contradiction that only makes sense here, in the margins between surface and depth. The eel-bore provides shelter from the killing sun, but I have modified it, carving careful ventilation shafts that follow the natural grain, creating a network of resonance chambers. Each chamber is tuned to a different frequency of the wood's vocabulary. When I sleep—if sleeping is the right word for my state of constant, semi-conscious listening—I hang suspended in a web of gut-strings harvested from glider-pack wings, my body becoming a living seismograph.

The tappers think me mad, but they come to me before each Pulse. They bring offerings of crystallized sap and fresh strips of green bast, which I chew slowly while I read the patterns in their soles. Their feet tell me stories they don't know they carry—the micro-fractures in their heel-whorls map every grain-line they've walked in the past season, creating a unique topography of travel. I run my fingertips (worn smooth as river stones) across their skin and tell them which routes will close when the wood swells, which hanging-towns will need to relocate their anchor points, where the new sap-wells will form.

I am not human anymore, not in any way that matters. The constant diet of raw cambium has changed my digestion; I process cellulose now, my gut extended and compartmentalized like the blind chambers of a Lignin-Weaver's nest. My skin has taken on the amber translucency of ancient resin, shot through with dark veins where wood-dust has worked its way into my bloodstream. In the right light—the brutal noon light that drives others into shelter—I become nearly invisible against the grain, just another whorl in the endless patterns of the wood.

But I know things. I know that the world-plant is dying, though its death will take millennia. I feel it in the way the Pulses have grown irregular—sometimes eighty-seven days, sometimes ninety-four. I taste it in the sap, which grows more bitter with each passing season, as the great cambium slowly converts its sugars into defensive toxins. I hear it in the conversations of the Lignum-Eels, whose grinding passages have begun to converge, as if they're searching for something in the heartwood that is no longer there.

The tappers don't want to know these things. They want to know where to place their sleds, when to pull their homes higher, how to read the next season's winds. So I tell them what they need, not what I know. I translate the wood's dying gasps into practical wisdom, reduce the vast, slow apocalypse into manageable daily truths.

At night, when the dew-mist rises and the bone-dry world briefly remembers moisture, I emerge from my bore. I walk the cooling grain-lines on feet that have evolved their own language—each step a word, each shift of weight a punctuation mark in my endless dialogue with the cambium. I follow routes no tapper would recognize, paths that exist only in the realm of vibration, where the physical landscape and its resonant echo-map diverge.

Sometimes I find others like myself—neither fully human nor fully other—living in the deep cracks where the world's voice is loudest. We don't speak. We press our palms together and share the stories written in our bones, the calcium record of every shock and shudder we've absorbed. We are the memory of this world, the only beings who will remember its voice when it finally falls silent.

But that silence is still generations away. For now, I return to my bore before dawn, wrap myself in my strings, and listen to the world-plant's ancient, labored breathing. Tomorrow the tappers will come with their questions, and I will taste their journeys in the whorls of their feet, and tell them where the safe paths lie. It is enough. It has to be.
