In this place where time pools and eddies like water in stone basins, I would find myself as one of the Timekeeper Moths—creatures that exist in the liminal spaces between what the Canyon Dwellers see and what truly moves through their world.

My body would be no larger than a child's palm, but spread wide, my wings would span the length of an adult's forearm. These wings—ah, these wings would be my map and calendar both. Their patterns shift like slowly turning kaleidoscopes, displaying the temporal currents that flow through the canyon. Where time moves quickly, my wings show sharp, angular designs in metallic coppers and golds. Where it pools and slows, the patterns become organic swirls of deep purples and blues, like bruises in the fabric of reality.

I would make my home in the crystalline formations that the Canyon Dwellers revere but do not fully understand. These crystals are more than mineral deposits—they are temporal anchors, points where the strange time-flows of this realm gather and crystallize into physical form. I would nest within their hollow cores, in spaces that exist slightly outside of normal time. Here, a day might last a heartbeat or a century, depending on the crystal's resonance.

My sustenance would come not from nectar or prey, but from temporal inconsistencies themselves. Where the Canyon Dwellers experience déjà vu, where shadows dance against the sun's logic, where their craft seems to complete itself—there I would feed, sipping at the edges where time fails to align properly with itself. The taste would be like memories of rain that hasn't fallen yet, like the echo of words about to be spoken.

The Canyon Dwellers would know me, though not directly. They would see my kind in the corner of their vision during their mystical ceremonies, when they attempt to commune with the land's essence. To them, we would appear as fleeting afterimages, ghosts of moths that seem to fly backwards or hover in impossible stillness while our wings beat furiously. Their murals would capture us imperfectly—sometimes showing us with too many wings, sometimes with human eyes, always with patterns that hurt to look at directly.

Some among them, those most attuned to the canyon's temporal mysteries, would learn to read the weather of time itself by observing our flight patterns. When we spiral upward in great columns, time is about to accelerate. When we cluster around certain crystals, temporal storms approach—moments when past and future might bleed through into the present.

But I would be more than just an observer or omen. In the deep night, when the Canyon Dwellers sleep and their consciousness loosens its grip on linear time, I would dust their dreams with the powder from my wings. This dust would carry fragments of other moments—their ancestors' memories, their descendants' hopes, the canyon's own geological dreams of when it was young and will be old. Through this, I would weave the continuity that allows their culture to persist despite existing in a place where causality itself grows uncertain.

The strangest part of my existence would be my life cycle. I would live backwards and forwards simultaneously. While one aspect of me ages from egg to caterpillar to chrysalis to moth, another aspect would be moving in reverse, from death to birth. These two timelines would meet and pass through each other like ghost trains in the night. At the moment of intersection—both birth and death, neither and both—I would briefly exist outside time entirely, seeing the canyon as it truly is: not a place but a process, not a location but a long, slow thought being thought by the Earth itself.

In this moment of double existence, I would understand why the Canyon Dwellers' stories speak of our kind as messengers between the human and the eternal. We are the living proof that in this strange highland realm, time is not a river but a web, and every strand vibrates with the footsteps of those who think they walk in only one direction.
