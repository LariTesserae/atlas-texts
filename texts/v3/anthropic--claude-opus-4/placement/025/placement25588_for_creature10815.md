In the depths of this ancient sanctuary, where stone memories stretch back to the world's first breath, I am the Keeper of Echoes.

My dwelling lies in a hollow between two massive roots of the great cascade vines, where the stone curves inward like cupped palms. The entrance is barely visible—a gap no wider than a stretched arm span, marked only by the peculiar way the bioluminescent fungi grow in spirals around its edges, as if the stone itself were dreaming in light. Inside, the space opens into a chamber just large enough for me to stand with arms outstretched, the walls worn smooth by countless years of my presence.

I am neither fully of the settlers nor apart from them. My skin bears the same earthen warmth, but traced with thin lines of luminescence that mirror the fungal patterns on my walls—a slow transformation that began the day I first pressed my ear to stone and heard the cavern's memory. My hair, once dark as deep soil, now carries threads of silver-blue light that pulse gently with my heartbeat. The settlers say I am becoming like the cavern itself, and perhaps they are right.

My gift—or curse, depending on the day—is this: I hear the stone's memories. Every impact, every drop of ancient water, every footfall of creature long extinct resonates through the rock and into my bones. I am the living archive of this place, keeper of sounds that span millennia. When the miners need to know where to dig safely, they come to me. When the water guardians seek new springs, I press my palms to stone and listen for the ghost-echoes of ancient streams. When children are born, their parents bring them so I might hear if the cavern welcomes their voice into its eternal chorus.

My tools are simple: a collection of resonating stones gathered from throughout the cavern, each one holding a different range of memory. I strike them gently with a mallet wrapped in vine fiber, and their vibrations unlock the whispers in the walls. A flat disc of polished mineral serves as my mapping surface, where I trace the sounds I hear with pigments made from crushed fungi and clay—creating visual music that shows the settlers where the stone is strong, where it is hollow, where it remembers water, where it remembers death.

The work is solitary but never lonely. The echoes are my constant companions—generations of settlers laughing, working, weeping, singing. I hear the wing-beats of the first iridescent flyers to discover this place, the patient footfalls of ancient herbivores wearing paths in stone that the current generation still follows. Sometimes, in the deepest listening, I hear the cavern's own voice: a thrumming so low it exists more as feeling than sound, the pulse of the earth itself.

My role in the settlement's careful balance is crucial yet understated. I am the one who warns when stone grows weary, when mining must cease in one area and begin in another. I hear the stress-songs in the rock before cracks appear, the shift in harmonics that precedes a cave-in. During the great ceremonies, I play the cavern itself like an instrument, striking sequences that resonate through the entire space, creating music that seems to rise from the earth rather than any single source.

The other settlers treat me with a mixture of reverence and unease. They bring me offerings—perfectly preserved fungi, intricate bone carvings, woven cloth dyed in the rarest pigments. But they rarely linger. Something in my eyes unsettles them, perhaps the way I sometimes look through the present moment into the deep past, seeing the ghosts of creatures that walked these paths before the first settlements were dreamed of.

At night—though night and day blur together in this fungal twilight—I often climb to a narrow ledge high on the cavern wall. There, I sit with my back pressed against stone worn smooth by my repeated visits, and I simply listen. The entire cavern spreads below me, a constellation of blue-green lights and gentle warmth. In these moments, I feel myself dissolving into the greater symphony, my consciousness spreading through the rock until I cannot tell where my memories end and the stone's begin.

This is my existence: translator between the eternal and the ephemeral, bridge between the cavern's ancient patience and the settlers' immediate needs. I am becoming something between human and habitat, a living echo myself, ensuring that the wisdom held in stone passes into flesh and back again, maintaining the delicate balance that allows life to flourish in this waterless cathedral.

The cavern keeps its own time, and I keep pace with it, my life stretching like the slow-growing vines, each day both eternal and fleeting. I am the memory-keeper, the stone-singer, the one who ensures that in this place of careful abundance, nothing is forgotten and every sound finds its place in the endless echo.
