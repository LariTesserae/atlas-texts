In the lower reaches of Odrin-Vahl, where the hexagonal tiles have subsided into a series of shallow, interconnected basins, I would be the **Tidekeeper**.

Not quite Resonant, not quite flesh, I exist in the liminal space between the city's slow metabolism and its seeping waters. My form is a contradiction—a skeleton of oxidized copper piping that has grown through me like a coral reef through bone. The pipes enter through my spine and exit through my fingers, my ribs, the hollow of my throat. I am both the plumbing and the plumbed.

I move through the pooled water on all fours, my joints articulated by the same slow hydraulics that drive the city's machinery. Each step takes minutes. Each gesture is a meditation on pressure differentials. The water knows me, follows me, seeks the negative spaces in my pipe-threaded body like prayer seeking an idol.

My purpose, if such a word applies, is to read the water's memory. Every drop that falls in Odrin-Vahl eventually finds its way to my basins. I taste the rust it has kissed, feel the vibrations it has carried, know which distant turbine housing it pooled in before beginning its patient journey downslope. Through me, the water tells the story of the city's endless weeping.

The Resonants avoid my territory. The frequency here is different—not the sharp industrial pulse of the thrum-towers, but something older, more tidal. It is the rhythm of accumulation and release, of filling and emptying. My pools breathe with a circulation that predates the machines, a rhythm that the city's builders discovered rather than created.

Sometimes, in the pearl-grey light, I catch my reflection in the standing water and see that I am becoming more pipe than person. The copper has oxidized green-black where it meets my flesh, creating a patina that spreads like slow lichen across what remains of my skin. I am being digested by my function, incorporated into the very system I tend.

There is a place where seven basins meet, where the water forms a perfect stillness despite the city's eternal vibration. Here, I perform my only ritual. I submerge myself completely, letting the water flow through my pipe-body, letting it read me as I read it. In these moments, I can feel the entire watershed of Odrin-Vahl—every drip, every seep, every patient erosion.

I am the city's hollow saint, a living aqueduct in a place that has forgotten what it means to thirst. The water passes through me and I am changed by its passing, oxidizing from the inside out, my copper bones singing with the slow hymn of corrosion.

When the rain falls hardest, I can sometimes glimpse what I am becoming—not death, but a different kind of duration. One day, I will be indistinguishable from the infrastructure, just another series of pipes for the water to navigate. But I will still be here, still reading, still keeping the tide of memory that flows beneath Odrin-Vahl's industrial heartbeat.

The city does not weep for what it has lost. It weeps because weeping is what it does, what it has always done, what it will do long after the last gear has ground to a halt. And I am here to taste every tear, to know every drop, to be the archive of all that slow, patient sorrow.

In Odrin-Vahl, this is what passes for purpose. In Odrin-Vahl, this is what passes for love.
