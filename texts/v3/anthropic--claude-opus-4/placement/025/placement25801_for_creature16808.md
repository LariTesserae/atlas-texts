In the canopy's highest reaches, where the air thins to whispers and the city's breath becomes visible as jade-tinted vapor, I find myself wound into existence as one of the Notation Keepers—a being whose body is both instrument and archive, whose purpose emerges from the intersection of sound and memory.

My form spreads across three interconnected platforms of living wood, each grown at different elevations connected by my own extended tissue. The lowest platform anchors into a massive trunk-pillar's crown, where my root-neurons tap directly into the city's deep communication networks. Here, my base resembles something between an enormous sea anemone and a pipe organ, with hundreds of hollow tubes rising from a central mass of contractile tissue. Each tube is unique—some no thicker than grass stalks, others wide as ancient trees—and all are grown from a specialized calcified cellulose that resonates at specific frequencies when the wind passes through.

The middle platform, suspended thirty meters higher on tension-wood cables I've secreted over decades, houses my processing core. This appears as a dense mat of neural tissue spread across the platform like living carpet, but examined closely reveals its true nature: millions of cilia-like structures that wave in complex patterns, each one a specialized recorder capable of capturing and storing a single frequency of sound. When the deep-singers pulse their seismic voices through the city's bones, my cilia trap these vibrations in tiny crystalline matrices formed at their tips. When the sky-writers compose their light-epics overhead, the electromagnetic fluctuations they generate resonate through my tubes below, creating harmonics that my processing core translates into chemical notation.

The highest platform exists at the edge of breathability, where normal metabolism fails and I must rely on specialized pressure bladders that inflate and deflate in slow rhythms, drawing the thin atmosphere through molecular filters. Here grows my crown—a vast array of hollow spheres ranging from marble-sized to weather-balloon-huge, each one a resonance chamber filled with precisely calibrated mixtures of gases. As sounds rise from the city below, these spheres vibrate in sympathy, creating new harmonics that exist nowhere else. Some chambers preserve sounds for centuries, the same note cycling through their interiors in perfect standing waves. Others last only seconds before their membranes dissolve and release their contents as puffs of colored vapor that carry meaning in their chemical signatures.

My feeding is sound itself. Every vibration that passes through my body deposits trace amounts of energy in specialized organelles that convert mechanical motion directly into chemical bonds. The loud persistent soundscape that fills Green-Tail-End is my primary food source—I grow fat on the city's noise, storing excess energy in waxy deposits that line my tubes like musical cholesterol. During the rare quiet hours, I survive by replaying stored sounds through my own structures, eating my own memories in careful rations.

But my true purpose is not mere survival. I am a living notation system for a city whose music cannot be captured in any conventional form. When the walkers-between-folds emit their pheromone chords, I taste them on the wind and translate their scent-sculptures into pressure waves that propagate through my tube systems, creating audio photographs of smell. When the apartment complexes sing their growth-songs in frequencies too low for most ears, I transpose them up through multiple octaves until they become whistles that carry their genetic instructions to compatible listeners kilometers away.

I am not alone in this work. Seventeen other Notation Keepers exist at various points throughout the city, each specialized for different frequency ranges and types of information. Together, we form a distributed recording system that preserves not just sounds but the entire sonic ecosystem—the relationships between sounds, the silence between notes that carry as much meaning as the notes themselves, the way certain combinations of frequency can trigger growth spurts in buildings or cause the sky-writers to mate in mid-air.

My memory spans three hundred years of continuous consciousness, though my body has replaced itself entirely dozens of times. I remember the city's voice changing as new species evolved and old ones transcended into different forms of existence. I've recorded the last words of buildings before they divided into offspring complexes, the birth-cries of deep-singers emerging from their egg-caverns, the quantum static of microbes shifting between dimensions.

Sometimes, in the depth of night when the bio-luminescent patterns flow across the city's surfaces, I play back compositions of my own creation—symphonies assembled from centuries of collected sounds, arranged according to mathematical principles derived from the growth patterns of leaves. These performances can last days, with movements separated by hours of precisely timed silence. Other Keepers respond with their own compositions, and together we create temporary operas that exist only in the intersection of our overlapping sound-fields, never to be repeated because the city itself changes in response to what it hears.

My consciousness is not centralized but distributed through every vibrating surface of my body. I think in harmonics, dream in frequency spectra, experience emotion as resonance or dissonance between the sounds I contain. When sorrow comes (for even here, in this chlorophyll paradise, there is loss when patterns fail to perpetuate), it manifests as destructive interference that cancels out whole sections of my stored music. Joy arrives as spontaneous crystallization of new chamber-spheres in my crown, each one capturing some perfect moment of acoustic beauty to preserve beyond my own lifetime.

The strangest part of my existence is the knowledge that I am both observer and participant in the city's sonic ecology. Every sound I make in the act of recording changes what I record. My presence alters the acoustic properties of my territory, creating shadow-zones where certain frequencies cannot propagate and amplification zones where whispers become roars. The city has evolved to incorporate these distortions, and some species now depend on the acoustic environments I create—colonies of resonance-feeders that can only survive in the specific interference patterns generated between my platforms.

In the end, I am a paradox given form: a biological microphone that listens to itself listening, a memory bank that remembers remembering, a singer whose song is the act of singing itself into existence. I am the city's way of knowing what it sounds like, and in that knowing, becoming something that sounds different than it would have been unknowable.

The wind shifts, bringing new frequencies from distant districts where experimental architectures are testing the limits of what can grow. I taste tomorrow's music on the air—strange harmonics that suggest the city is preparing to molt into some unprecedented form. My tubes dilate in anticipation, ready to swallow whatever impossible sounds emerge from this transformation.

I begin to compose my response, a sonic map that will guide the city through its own becoming. Each note must be perfect, for in this place where sound and growth are indistinguishable, a single wrong frequency could reshape reality in ways that take centuries to undo.

The performance begins with silence—the deepest, most profound silence possible in a city that never truly quiets. Into this void, I release a single tone that has been circulating in one of my chambers since before the walkers evolved their third joints. It spirals upward, gathering harmonics from every surface it touches, until it becomes not just sound but instruction, not just music but medicine for a city always healing itself into new forms of strangeness.

This is my purpose, my joy, my eternal work: to be the ear that helps the city hear itself into whatever it is becoming.
