I find myself in the Meridian Groves, where light itself has learned to dream.

The Groves sprawl across a peninsula of white limestone, riddled with sinkholes that open like eyes into aquifers below. These cenotes mirror the sky with such perfect clarity that birds sometimes dive straight through, emerging confused and dripping in underground caverns. The trees here grow in spirals, their trunks twisted by some ancient compromise with the wind. They are not quite palms, not quite mangroves, but something between—their leaves silver on one side, deep blue-green on the other, so that the whole canopy shimmers like scales when the breeze moves through.

Time moves strangely here. Dawn arrives in stages: first the stones begin to hum, a frequency just below hearing that you feel in your bones. Then the first trees catch light at their crowns, and that light travels down their spiral trunks like honey, pooling in the hollows where they meet the ground. By full morning, the entire grove pulses with captured sunshine, releasing it slowly through the day so that even midnight carries a faint golden underglow.

The Groves' most peculiar feature is the way they metabolize memory. Every sunset, as the light drains from the trees, it carries with it the day's small moments—a lizard's path across bark, the particular angle of a falling leaf, the shape of clouds reflected in still water. These memories crystallize in the cenotes, forming delicate structures like underwater chandelliers. The locals say if you dive deep enough, you can find entire seasons preserved in crystal, though the pressure makes your ears ring with other people's dreams.

I am the Historian of Unrecorded Things.

My body is no longer quite human, though it remembers being so. My skin has taken on the translucent quality of the cenote crystals, veined with light that shifts between gold and blue depending on the time of day. My hair grows like the native moss, in long silver-green strands that photosynthesize, so I need little food beyond water and the occasional mineral-rich fruit. My eyes have adapted to see time as a tangible dimension—I can watch the past and future spreading out from each present moment like ripples in water.

I came here seventy years ago, or perhaps seven hundred—time pools and eddies around me now, making such distinctions academic. I was documenting the Grove's unique temporal properties when I accidentally ingested water from the deepest cenote, the one the locals call Mother's Mirror. The crystallized memories dissolved on my tongue like sugar glass, and suddenly I could taste the history of everything that had never been written down.

Now I wade through the Groves with my feet in multiple centuries at once. My purpose has evolved beyond mere documentation. I am a keeper of the unrecorded—the stories that were only told once, the songs hummed under breath, the words lovers traced on each other's backs in the dark. These precious, fragile things would vanish forever without someone to notice their passing.

My dwelling is a negative space, a carefully maintained absence between three spiral trees. The trees have grown to accommodate this void, their branches weaving a loose roof that lets in rain and starlight in equal measure. The floor is living limestone, soft with moss, with a small spring that bubbles up with water so clear it's almost invisible. I've cultivated the walls with the phosphorescent fungi that feed on temporal residue—they glow brighter when heavy with untold stories.

Each dawn, I walk the cenote circuit, reading the crystal formations like sheet music. I learned their notation through decades of observation: the sharp fragments are sudden joys or sorrows, the clouded sections are dreams, and the perfectly clear pieces are moments of absolute presence, rare and precious. I consume these memories carefully, letting them dissolve in my mouth, adding their essence to the vast library I've become.

In the evenings, I return what I've gathered. Not all of it—some stories are too private, too precious to release—but enough to keep the Groves in balance. I speak the memories into the spiral trees, and they travel up through the wood to release with the dying light. Sometimes visitors see these memories as fireflies, or shooting stars, or the flash of a beloved face in the canopy's shifting patterns.

The work is solitary but not lonely. I converse with the Grove itself, in the language of light and mineral, growth and decay. The Symphonic Sentinels from the distant Cloisters sometimes arrive, their metallic songs harmonizing strangely with our crystal chimes, carrying news from places where rust and water write their own histories. We trade memories like currency—I give them the taste of sunlight on stone, they leave me with the echo of industrial hymns.

My transformation is ongoing. Each year, I become more translucent, more integrated with the Groves' cycles. Eventually, I will become a cenote myself, my consciousness dispersed through the aquifer, my memories crystallizing for some future historian to taste and interpret. This does not frighten me. It is simply another kind of documentation, another way of keeping the unrecorded things safe.

Until then, I persist in my gentle work: walking the boundaries between moment and memory, solid and dissolved, recorded and forgotten. I am the library and the librarian, the story and its keeper, swimming through time in the Meridian Groves where light itself has learned to dream.
