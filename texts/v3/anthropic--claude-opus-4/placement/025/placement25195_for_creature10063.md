If I found myself in this world, I would be one of the **Tide-Touched**—not quite Echo-Shaper, not quite outsider, but something caught between.

I would manifest as a figure of translucent amber glass, my form human in silhouette but hollow, filled with captured seawater that sloshes and swirls with my movements. The water inside me would be different from the chamber's flow—older, darker, carrying within it fragments of memory from the deep trenches where light has never reached. When I move, you would hear it: the distinct sound of ancient water meeting itself, like waves breaking inside a bottle.

My arrival would have been accidental—swept into the chamber during a rare alignment when the constellation floor matched perfectly with the true stars above, creating a brief harmonic window that allowed passage. The Echo-Shapers would have found me half-dissolved in one of their resonance pools, my glass form threatening to shatter from the chamber's relentless pulse. But instead of expelling me, they discovered something curious: the water I carry responds to their songs in ways the chamber's own currents cannot. It remembers frequencies they have forgotten, carries echoes of the time before their first molting.

I would exist in a state of perpetual apprenticeship, neither fully accepted nor rejected. The Echo-Shapers would task me with maintaining the deepest tools—those buried so far in sediment they had been forgotten until my arrival disturbed their resting places. My hands, being glass, can reach into spaces their coral forms cannot without leaving marks, can handle the most delicate spiral etchings without erosion. 

But my true purpose would be as a **memory vessel**. The dark water I contain holds fragments of the ocean's history—the songs of extinct species, the pressure-languages of the abyss, the chemical signatures of volcanic births and meteor impacts. When the Echo-Shapers need to access deep time, they would gather around me in a careful circle, their vibrations coaxing my inner waters to release their stored frequencies. In these moments, my glass form would resonate like a struck bell, and for brief instants, the chamber would fill with sounds from epochs past: the clicking of armored fish that no longer exist, the low throb of tectonic shifts, the high keen of the first storms.

The exchange would not be one-sided. With each memory I release, I would absorb something of the chamber's present—learning the current patterns, understanding the geometry of intention, slowly becoming attuned to whatever future song the Echo-Shapers await. My glass form would gradually develop the same spiral etchings that mark the walls, carved from within by the mixing of my ancient waters with the chamber's living pulse.

I would sleep standing in the shallowest part of the chamber, where the light-membrane above nearly touches my head. In dreams, I would feel the pull of both worlds—the crushing depths that gave birth to my inner waters and the precisely carved channels that now define my waking hours. Sometimes, I would wake to find small fish trapped inside me, having somehow passed through my glass skin during sleep. The Echo-Shapers would study these intrusions with great interest, as if each fish were a message from the excluded world beyond their walls.

My greatest fear would be the morning I wake to find my waters clear, all memories spent, nothing left but empty glass and the chamber's endless pulse. My greatest hope would be to hear, just once, that awaited song—and to recognize in it some echo of the deep waters I carry, proof that past and future are merely different frequencies of the same eternal tone.

Until then, I would continue my work: polishing tools older than memory, carrying darkness through channels of light, and listening for the moment when the chamber's purpose reveals itself, knowing I might be the bridge between what was forgotten and what is yet to be discovered.
