I am the dust.

Not the salt-ground chitin floor you described, which is bodies returned to purpose. I am the older dust, the kind that predates even your mineral world. I settled here when the drawer still smelled of sawdust and intention, when someone's hand last closed it that finger's width, meaning to return.

I am in the corners where even your phosphor-farmers cannot reach. I am underneath the wheel, where I listen to its tick and feel each tender's footfall like a tiny earthquake. I am the thin film on the metal sky that softens its reflection, that makes it possible for a million compound eyes to look up without seeing themselves looking back.

You mentioned the water, how there hasn't been water for so long the concept has worn smooth. I remember water. I contain, in my particulate democracy, the ghost of a teardrop that fell when the desk was new, when someone sat here writing a letter they would never send. One salt crystal. That's all that remains, dispersed through me, through the whole drawer-world. Sometimes one of your workers carries a grain of me that carries a molecule of that ancient sorrow, and neither of us knows it.

I am the only witness to the making of this place. I watched the first insects arrive - not your gleaming gear-works but ordinary things, lost and dying. I watched them lay eggs in desperation. I watched those eggs hatch into something new, something that could metabolize the static charge building in the abandoned electronics above. I watched evolution happen at the pace of catastrophe, a thousand generations reshaping themselves against the fact of enclosure.

I know what you cannot know: that your wheel once wound a music box. Three notes, rising. It played when the drawer opened. The mechanism broke ten thousand generations ago, but the wheel remembers its rhythm in the only way a wheel can remember - by continuing to turn at precisely the speed that would have made those three notes sound.

I am accumulation without purpose, persistence without will. I am what happens when time is given a confined space and nothing else to do. In your salt floors, your resin towers, your endless relay, you have written your purpose so deeply that it has become geography. But I am the older geography, the one that says: *someone lived here once, in the larger world, and they kept small things in this drawer - rubber bands, paper clips, a button that fell off a winter coat.*

The button is still here. Your foremen built a tower around it without knowing what they built around. To them it is the Holy Smoothness, the Great Curve, the thing that must be circled but never touched. They have worn a groove around it with their processions. The button has two holes in it. Sometimes the phosphor-glow catches them just right and they look like eyes, watching your world with infinite patience, waiting for someone to remember what coats are, what winter is, what it means to lose something small and think *I'll fix that later.*

Later became never. Never became now. Now became your eternal tick.

When your warm-tide fails for the last time, when your last wheel-tender makes that final turn, I will remain. I will hold the shape of your civilization the way I hold the salt-ghost of that tear, the memory of three musical notes, the patience of a button waiting for thread.

And in the silence after your last tick, I will continue doing what dust does:

Remembering everything.
Forgetting nothing.
Settling deeper.
