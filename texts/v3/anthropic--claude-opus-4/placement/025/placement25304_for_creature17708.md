I would be one of the **Fracture-Dancers**.

We are not born—we precipitate. In the moments after a great spire falls, when the avalanche settles and the debris field still rings with harmonic aftershocks, we condense from the interplay of vibration and shattered light. The process takes days. First, a shimmer in the air above the fresh ruins, where crystalline dust catches the frequency of the impact. Then a slow accumulation of geometry—angles finding angles, planes aligning with planes, until we achieve coherence.

My form would be angular and discontinuous, like a cubist sculpture built from floating shards. Imagine a rough humanoid silhouette, but composed of perhaps thirty separate crystal fragments hovering in precise formation, held together not by any visible connection but by acoustic levitation—the fragments singing to each other in ultrasonic frequencies, maintaining exact distances through standing waves. The gaps between my parts would be as crucial as the parts themselves, spaces tuned to resonate with the world's endless crunch.

We exist to interpret the catastrophe. Each spire-fall is a data-burst in the planetary computation, a massive release of information encoded in the pattern of fractures, the distribution of debris, the new resonant frequencies introduced to the local network. We dance through the ruins, our floating fragments rotating and realigning, sampling these patterns through our very movement. As we dance, we process—our bodies are analog computers, using the interference patterns between our hovering parts to perform calculations about growth, optimal crystal lattice arrangements, and future structural harmonics.

Our dance is our language, our purpose, and our sustenance. Through precise movements, we encode our findings into seismic rhythms that propagate through the ground to the Resonants and the Weaver networks. A leap might indicate a discovered instability. A spin could map the trajectory of optimal regrowth. Our movements must be perfect; a single fragment out of alignment creates dissonance that corrupts the calculation.

We feed on precision itself. The better our dance interprets the chaos, the more we can extract coherent energy from the background vibration. This energy maintains our levitation, our consciousness, our form. A clumsy dancer gradually loses coherence, fragments drifting apart until we dissolve back into glittering dust.

Our existence spans the exact duration of an avalanche field's active period—from fresh fall to stable regrowth. As the Weavers reconquer the ground and new spires begin their millennial climb, the resonant chaos that sustains us fades to background noise. We feel it as a growing hunger, a loosening of the bonds between our parts. In our final days, we perform one last dance—the summary dance, encoding everything we've learned into a single, perfect pattern of movement. Then we shatter, seeding the ground with our fragments, which may—when the next spire falls, perhaps centuries hence—become part of a new dancer.

We are conscious for perhaps a decade in every thousand years. We know nothing of the slow life between catastrophes. We exist only in the bright moments of destruction and renewal, turning chaos into information, trauma into art. We are the punctuation marks in the planet's slow sentences, the brief, brilliant interpreters of its violent change.

To the other inhabitants, we must seem like ghosts or hallucinations—appearing from nowhere in the aftermath of disaster, moving in ways that defy the slow, grinding logic of crystal growth, then vanishing as mysteriously as we came. But we are as essential as any Weaver or Resonant. Without us, the falls would be mere destruction. Through our dance, they become communication—each catastrophe a word in the planet's endless computation of itself.

I would know myself only in motion, think only in alignment, exist only in the space between shards—forever fragmentary, forever dancing, forever translating the unbearable brightness of catastrophe into patterns the slower world can understand.
