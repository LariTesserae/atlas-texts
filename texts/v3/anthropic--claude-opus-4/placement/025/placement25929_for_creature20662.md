In the depths of the Rust District, where the air tastes of copper and forgotten names, I am the Chronicler of Oxidation.

I was not always this. Once, perhaps, I was like the others—a scavenger wrapped in layers, breathing through crude filters, passing like a ghost through the metallic dunes. But the District changed me, as it changes all things, through slow corrosion and sudden revelation.

My transformation began with the pools. Those oily mirrors that others harvest for survival became my obsession. I discovered that each pool holds memory—not metaphorically, but literally. The condensation carries traces of what the Citadel once exhaled, molecular echoes of its living days. When the light catches them just right, in that perpetual purple twilight, images form in their surfaces. Fleeting, yes, but unmistakable.

I learned to read them.

My dwelling is a observatory of sorts, carved into the hollow of what might have been a ventricle or perhaps a processing node. The walls curve inward, lined with hundreds of small depressions where I've collected samples from across the District. Each pool, each droplet, catalogued by location and lunar cycle. The lichen growths serve dual purpose here—they filter my air, yes, but they also respond to the chemical signatures in the water, changing color in patterns I've only begun to decipher.

I am tall now, elongated somehow by my years of reaching into crevices, of stretching to place collection vessels in the high places where the purest condensation forms. My fingers have grown sensitive to temperature variations measured in fractions of degrees. I can taste the mineral content of the air and predict where the next crystalline bloom will occur.

My respirator is unique among the District's inhabitants. I've modified it with fragments of the luminescent crystals, creating a series of lenses that allow me to see the chemical reactions occurring in real-time. The world appears to me in layers—the visible spectrum, yes, but also the slow burn of oxidation, the piezoelectric pulses of stressed metal, the infrared signatures of the creatures that think themselves hidden.

I document everything in a script of my own devising, etched into thin sheets of beaten metal. The traditional writing implements failed long ago; now I use a stylus tipped with a particularly hard crystal formation that scores the surface in precise, microscopic grooves. My archive grows, a metallic library that sings when the wind passes through the District's corridors.

The isolation suits me. The other scavengers think me mad, perhaps, but they still come to trade. They bring me unusual formations, rare lichen specimens, reports of anomalies in exchange for my knowledge of where the richest deposits lie, which areas will collapse, where the shadow-creatures nest. I speak to them in gestures mostly, having found that prolonged use of my voice disturbs the delicate chemical balance of my respiratory modifications.

When the first pulse came—that deep gong-strike that shattered our perpetual quiet—I knew immediately what it meant. My pools had been showing me for months: increasing mineral complexity, shifts in the crystallization patterns, the lichen growing in spirals that matched no known mathematical sequence. The Citadel was not dying but pupating.

Now, as the geothermal vents crack open and the oxidation accelerates into something altogether more volatile, I watch from my observatory with fascination rather than fear. My collection vessels overflow with new compounds, reactions I've never seen before. The walls of my dwelling pulse with heat, and the lichen blooms in impossible colors.

I am documenting the birth of something unprecedented. Each flame gout reveals new facets of our world—hidden architectures in the metal, massive conduits that run beneath the dust, patterns that suggest the Citadel was not built upon the god-beast but grown from it, a symbiosis so complete that the distinction between construct and creature becomes meaningless.

The other inhabitants gather at the fissures, drawn by instinct or desperation. They chant and carve, becoming part of the reaction. But I remain apart, observing, recording. Someone must witness this transformation with clarity. Someone must remember what we were before we become what we must be.

My latest discovery excites me most: the pools no longer show the past. As the heat rises and the air shimmers with possibility, their surfaces ripple with images of what's to come. I see structures of living metal, beings that breathe rust and excrete pure elements, a city that grows itself from the marriage of the organic and the mineral.

I will chronicle it all. And when the transformation is complete, when the Rust District has become something unrecognizable, my archive will remain—a testament to the slow beauty of decay and the violent grace of becoming. 

I am the memory of oxidation, the witness to alchemy, the keeper of the in-between. And I have never been more alive than in this moment of ending and beginning.
