**The Weight of My Hands**

I am one of the oldest here, though age means little when measured against limestone. My run stretches four yards along the northwestern curve where the grooves branch like river deltas frozen in stone. Three channels, each no wider than a grass blade's edge. I know them as you might know the lines in your palm.

The morning tastes of salt and waiting.

I began my journey at first light—a shifting of weight so gradual that the dust motes spinning in the chamber's thermal breath barely noticed my passage. My destination: the third groove, where yesterday's condensation left a crust of minerals thick enough to choke tomorrow's water. 

My toolkit rests where I left it seven suns ago: two siphon bones (one from a swift, one from something smaller I cannot name), three chert scrapers graded by sharpness, and my grinding stone—a perfect black disk worn smooth by ten thousand salt-crushing sessions. I select the middle scraper. Its weight in my hand is a familiar song.

The first scrape sends a whisper through the chamber: *tink*. I pause, letting the sound fade into the limestone's memory. The others acknowledge with a subtle shift of their brow-ridges—not greeting, not warning, simply recognition. We are all part of the same slow machine.

I work the groove with patience that would drive warm-blooded things to madness. Scrape. Wait for the heart's double beat. Scrape. The salt comes away in tiny avalanches, each grain catching the reflected light from the central column like a falling star. I taste the quality of the buildup with my tongue-tip: bitter with age, crystallized too long. This groove has been neglected. Perhaps its previous tender has gone to the deeper darkness. Perhaps they simply forgot, their thoughts slowing until they became another ridge of limestone.

By midday, I have cleared six inches of channel. The selenite lens above blazes with perfect fury, and I feel the edge of its heat even here in the safety of the ring-shadow. My skin drinks the warmth through its chalk armor, each pore a tiny mouth. I pause to watch the air in the light-column—how it shimmers and breathes, how the mica flakes rise like the ghosts of ancient fish.

A water bead is forming at the top of my third groove. I calculate its weight, its speed, the exact moment it will break free and begin its journey. Three hours. Perhaps four. I will be ready.

The jade-button at the groove's base has not flowered in two seasons. I examine it with the care of a jeweler: the faint yellow tinge at its crown suggests mineral stress. Too much salt in its bed. I switch to my finest scraper and begin the delicate work of clearing the crust from around its base without damaging the black glue that holds it to the stone. The plant does not respond—plants never do—but I sense its potential, coiled tight as a spring.

As I work, I become aware of Youngest watching from their position near the shallow depression we call the Gathering Bowl. They have only recently learned to use the siphon bones, their movements still too quick, too eager. They waste moisture in their haste. But they will learn. We all learn, or we become stone.

The afternoon light shifts imperceptibly. I feel it in my bones before I see it—that slight cooling of the air that means the sun has begun its descent toward the plateau's rim. Soon the chamber will exhale its heat into the night, and we will gather in the basin's heart.

But not yet. The water bead has grown fat and heavy. I position myself beneath it, my mouth-corner precisely aligned with the groove's terminus. The drop falls—a perfect sphere of liquid light—and I catch it on my tongue. It tastes of the sky from three days ago, when clouds passed over the selenite lens and left their memory in the moisture.

I allow myself one small luxury: I let the water sit in my mouth for a full heartbeat before swallowing. The coolness travels down my throat like a blessing.

The salt I've scraped today fills a small depression beside my tools. I begin the grinding, using the black stone in slow, circular motions. The salt becomes powder, then dust, then something finer than dust—a substance that exists between solid and air. I take a handful and begin working it into the scales along my left forearm where I noticed the first white threads of fungus two days ago.

The treatment burns with a clean, purifying fire. I continue methodically: arm, shoulder, the difficult spot between my shoulder blades where the spine creates a valley. Each area must be thoroughly salted, each scale lifted and cleaned beneath. This is not vanity. This is survival.

As twilight approaches, I sense the others beginning their descent. One by one, they leave their runs, their tools carefully arranged for tomorrow's work. I finish my salt treatment and join the slow migration to the basin floor.

We arrange ourselves in our pattern—a living mosaic of bodies that hasn't changed in memory. My place is between Old-Three-Claws and the one we call Droplet-Counter. Our sides touch lightly, sharing the faint warmth we've gathered through the day.

The selenite lens above holds the last blue light of evening. I watch it fade through my heavy brow-ridge, feeling my metabolism slow to match the chamber's nighttime breathing. My thoughts stretch out, becoming as long and thin as the grooves in the limestone.

Tomorrow, the second groove will need attention. There is a place where the channel branches that collects debris from the upper world—tiny fragments of the desert that fall through the lens like memories of rain. And the jade-button... perhaps tomorrow it will sense the shift in the light. Perhaps it will offer its white cup to the sun.

But these are tomorrow's thoughts. Tonight, I am stone among stones, my breath matching the chamber's rhythm. The Salt-Loom holds us all—limestone, water, plant, and flesh—in its patient embrace.

We wait for the sun to find us again.
