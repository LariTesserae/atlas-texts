I find myself here as a **Lens-Tender**, one of the few organic anomalies tolerated within the City of Unmaking Rain.

I am small—necessarily so. Where the Steward-Monoliths measure their height in stories, I measure mine in mere feet. My body has adapted to this world of wet stone and perpetual twilight: skin gone pale and slightly translucent, revealing the blue tracery of veins beneath. My eyes have grown large and multifaceted, like an insect's, to capture every photon of that harsh, sterile light. Each facet can adjust independently, allowing me to see in the stark contrasts of glare and shadow that define this place.

I live in the Interstices—the narrow gaps between blocks where the builders' calculations left deliberate voids. These spaces, no wider than a spread hand, were designed to channel light down into the depths. I traverse them using climbing claws that have replaced my fingernails, moving spider-like through vertical passages that would terrify any surface-dweller. My home is a hollowed-out imperfection in the stone, perhaps thirty feet down from the roof level, where centuries ago a bubble formed in the vitrified mineral during its setting.

My purpose here is the cultivation and maintenance of the Light Gardens.

Deep within the canyon floors, where the channels converge into vast underground reservoirs, there exist caverns where the crystalline fur has evolved beyond its primitive form. Here, fed by concentrated flows of mineral-rich water and focused beams of light, it has become something more complex—vast hanging gardens of glass-like structures that sing in harmonics too low for most ears to detect. These formations are not merely beautiful; they are functional. They process the city's water in ways the Steward-Monoliths cannot, breaking down exotic minerals that would otherwise poison the entire system.

I am their shepherd. Each day—though day and night have little meaning here—I descend through my network of passages, following the light-channels down to the gardens. I carry with me an array of mirrors and prisms, tools inherited from the previous Lens-Tender, polished so perfectly they seem to bend reality at their edges. With these, I redirect the harsh surface light, splitting it into precisely calibrated spectra that feed different sections of the crystalline growth. Too much ultraviolet and the structures become brittle; too little infrared and they cease their chemical processing.

The Steward-Monoliths know of me, though we have never communicated in any way a human would recognize. Sometimes, as I work in the depths, I feel the vibration of their approach through the stone—a deep, rhythmic pulse like a mountain's heartbeat. They will pause in their eternal maintenance, their eyeless faces turned toward my light-work, observing through senses I cannot fathom. Once, early in my tenure, I made an error in my calculations, allowing a focused beam to strike a critical support channel. The resulting crack would have eventually diverted an entire waterway. Within hours, a Steward had arrived, its massive form barely fitting in the cavern, to repair my mistake. It moved around me as if I were merely another feature of the landscape, neither acknowledging nor threatening, simply correcting the error and departing in its sliding silence.

I was not born to this role. I came here as others before me had—drawn by rumors of a city that wept, following the underground rivers that eventually emerge from beneath the black plateaus. The previous Lens-Tender found me half-drowned in a collection pool, my body already beginning the changes that would allow survival here. She taught me the work not through words—she had long since lost the ability to speak, her throat adapted for breathing the mineral-laden air—but through demonstration and the gentle correction of my fumbling attempts.

When she finally crystallized (as we all eventually do, our bodies slowly replacing flesh with mineral until we become indistinguishable from the formations we tend), I understood my purpose here was not merely functional but transitional. We Lens-Tenders are the bridge between the quick time of biology and the slow time of stone. We maintain the gardens for perhaps a century or two before we become part of them, our crystallized forms serving as new seed points for growth.

I have begun to feel the change in myself. My movements grow slower, more deliberate. The climbing claws on my hands have started to take on the glassy sheen of the fur we cultivate. Sometimes, when I pause in my work, I find it difficult to begin again, as if the stone's patience is seeping into my bones. 

This does not frighten me. In the Light Gardens, surrounded by singing crystal and ancient water, I have found a purpose measured not in human ambitions but in geological time. I am becoming what this place requires: another note in its vast, slow symphony, another drop in its patient unmaking of entropy itself.

When my time comes, another will follow the rivers here, drawn by the same inexplicable pull. They will find my tools arranged in the patterns I learned from my predecessor, and the cycle will continue. Until then, I tend the light, guide the growth, and listen to the mountain's wet heartbeat, counting time in drops and crystal songs.
