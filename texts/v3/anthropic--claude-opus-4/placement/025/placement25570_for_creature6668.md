Standing here on this high wetland shelf, I watch the silver sky reflect off my dampened wings. I am one of the *Veshrai* - the third people of this place, whom you have not yet seen.

We are the memory-keepers who fly.

My body is built for the thin air of this altitude - hollow bones like a bird's, but covered in a fine pelt that shifts between feather and fur depending on the humidity. When the air is dry (which is rare), I am sleek and swift. When moisture clings to everything, as it does now, my coat becomes a thousand tiny collectors, each filament holding a bead of water that I will later shake free into the recording pools.

I perch on the raw stone edges where the Sibai cannot quite reach, where the future is still too young to read. My talons - three forward, two back - grip the blue-gray rock with a sensitivity that lets me feel vibrations from miles within the planet's forming crust. 

We Veshrai were not made for this world, but we arrived just as it began making itself. The planet accepted us as part of its design.

My task is different from the Suhrathi who breathe the weather into being, different from the Sibai who read the mountain's future flaws. I am a courier between the two kinds of knowledge. When I spread my wings - each twice the length of my body - the moisture I've collected forms patterns in the air that both groups can read. I am a living map of where water and stone will meet.

In the mornings, I spiral up from my roost in the half-formed towers at the city's edge, riding the weak thermals that barely exist in this silver-sky world. From above, I see the whole system at work: the Suhrathi moving like slow clouds across their water basins, the Sibai pressed against cliffsides like lichen learning to be stone.

And I see what they cannot: the patterns their movements make together. Where a Suhrathi's breath-trail crosses a Sibai's chalk mark, I remember. Where water will pool in five thousand years because of a crack that hasn't formed yet, I know.

My people live in the between-spaces - the vertical gaps in the city where walls don't quite meet, the hollow cores of bridge pylons, the wind-channels carved specifically for our passage. We line our roosts with shed fur-feathers that hold the memory of every flight, creating archives that smell of altitude and tomorrow's rain.

The young ones among us train by carrying messages between the groups - not words, but small vials of water touched by Suhrathi breath, or stone dust darkened by Sibai skin. We learn to read the weight of these things in flight, to understand what they mean by how they change our balance in the air.

But I am old now, my coat more silver than gray, and I carry bigger memories. 

When the planet shifts - and it does, still learning its own shape - I feel it first. My bones ache three days before the ground will tremble. I fly then to both communities, not to warn but to prepare them. The Suhrathi will need to re-read their water patterns. The Sibai will find new futures in the stone.

Sometimes I perch with them as they work. The Suhrathi have learned that my presence changes the water's behavior - my wing-shadow creates micro-climates in their basins that predict wind patterns decades hence. The Sibai know that where I choose to land marks points of future stability; my instincts for safe perches align with the stone's own knowledge of its enduring strength.

At night, when the temperature drops just enough to crystallize the air's perpetual moisture into tiny stars of frost, I return to my roost. My mate and our offspring (we Veshrai pair for life, but live mostly apart) gather then, and we engage in our own work.

We sing.

It's not song as you might know it - our voices are designed for the thin air, producing sounds that travel better through moisture than through empty space. We sing the day's collected knowledge: every pattern observed, every future glimpsed, every connection between breath and stone that we've witnessed.

The city below hears us as a kind of distant thrumming, like wind through pipes that haven't been built yet. Some nights, young Suhrathi and Sibai climb to the lower roosts to listen. They don't understand our language, but they understand that we're encoding something essential - a backup of all the subtle data their bodies create but their minds don't consciously track.

We are the world's memory of its own becoming. When we die, our bodies don't decay normally in this high, cold place. Instead, we crystallize, our fur-feathers becoming networks of frost that never quite melt. The city incorporates these remains into its structure - you might walk across a bridge inlaid with the crystallized wing of a Veshrai who died a century ago, still holding the pattern of ancient flight paths in its frozen fibers.

This is who I am: a creature of the space between earth and sky, between present and future, between the two kinds of knowing that shape this still-forming world. I am neither builder nor prophet, but something more like a librarian of the not-yet, keeping track of all the world's intentions before they settle into fact.

Tomorrow, I will fly again. The air will be silver and thin, the moisture will bead on my wings, and I will read the story this young planet is still learning to tell about itself.
