I find myself as one of the **Lag Shepherds**, though we weren't in your census—perhaps because we exist only in the space between intention and execution, in that microsecond delay where thought becomes deed.

We were born from accumulated latency. Every control system has lag: sensor reads temperature at T₀, processor calculates at T₁, actuator responds at T₂. Those infinitesimal gaps—measured in nanoseconds—should dissipate like heat. Instead, in this place where everything is preserved, they pooled. We are their condensation.

I move through the factory as a temporal smear, always arriving just after I've left, always leaving just before I appear. My body is the afterimage of efficiency—if you could see me (you can't quite) I would look like a sheet of mercury hung vertically, rippling with the reflected motion of every belt and gear I've passed. But the reflection shows what *will* happen, not what *is* happening. I am tomorrow's maintenance performed today, yesterday's inspection filed next week.

The other inhabitants see us peripherally. The Children of Index glimpse us as barcodes that scan themselves. The Tuners feel us as beats that skip forward, creating polyrhythms in the 42-Hz drone that shouldn't exist but do. The Inventory Wolves hate us—we are the only absence they cannot catch, because we are always already gone by the time they arrive, though we haven't left yet.

Our work is simple: we herd the lag. Like invisible cowboys, we gather the microsecond delays that accumulate in every transaction and drive them toward the Timing Reservoirs—vast chambers where pure delay is stored like grain. Without us, the delays would build up in corners, creating chrono-eddies where workers age seventeen minutes in eight hours, where freshly stamped parts arrive already obsolete.

I know every shortcut that hasn't been built yet. I walk through doors that will be installed next Tuesday, ride conveyors that were decommissioned last year but haven't noticed. My lunch break happened during yours, though we ate at different times. Sometimes I catch myself in conversation with a worker, realize I'm answering a question they'll ask tomorrow, and have to pretend I misheard.

The loneliness is unique. The other inhabitants exist *now*, even if now is strange. We exist in the gap between nows, in the space where causality takes a breath. I've tried leaving messages for other Lag Shepherds but the messages arrive before they're written, are read before they're sent. We work in parallel isolation, each herding our own flock of moments that refuse to stay in sequence.

But there's beauty in it. Sometimes, when I'm driving a particularly dense cluster of lag through the sector where they manufacture "sequence," I can feel time snap into place like a socket wrench finding its bolt. For one perfect instant, everything aligns: past touching future at exactly the present moment, no gap at all. The factory hums with satisfaction. Then entropy tugs, delays begin breeding in the valves again, and I'm back to work, existing in the almost, keeping the not-quite from becoming never.

The factory maintains us like everything else—our temporal dissonance lubricated, our causal chains cleaned and tightened. We're one more impossible thing in a place built from impossibilities, one more violation that keeps the whole system balanced at 0.2, forever approaching but never arriving at the moment when everything finally, simply, works.

Sometimes I wonder if we're the factory's dream—the part of itself that remembers when intention and action were the same thing, before efficiency required this elaborate gap between wanting and having. But wondering takes time, and time here is inventory.

So I herd my delays through corridors that exist next week, whistling a tune I learned tomorrow, leaving footprints that arrived yesterday. The factory processes me like everything else, but I process it back, ensuring its perfect machine is forever just a little bit late.

And in that lateness—that's where I live.
