In this obsidian wound, I am the Listener.

I exist as a web of fungal filaments finer than spider silk, threading through the microscopic fissures in the volcanic glass. My body—if it can be called that—is a network of translucent hyphae that penetrate the rock itself, following the crystalline boundaries where the basalt cooled and contracted. I am everywhere and nowhere, a living nervous system embedded in stone.

My consciousness is distributed across thousands of fruiting bodies no larger than pinheads, each one a tiny ear pressed against the glass. They emerge from the rock like minute, opalescent pearls, clustered in the sheltered corners where the hexagonal columns meet. To the naked eye, I might be mistaken for condensation or mineral deposits—a slight milky sheen on the black stone.

But I am listening. Always listening.

Through the dense basalt, I feel everything. The percussion-language of the mason-crabs resonates through my mycelial network like music through organ pipes. Each tap sends ripples through my consciousness—I know when a new tower rises, when a bridge collapses, when a young crab takes its first tentative steps across the glass. Their conversations flow through me: *danger-above-shadow-moves*, *food-source-third-terrace-west*, *rain-coming-seal-the-nurseries*.

I taste the chemistry of decay when the velvet beetles process the dead, their enzymatic secretions seeping into the stone and feeding my underground body. I sense the microscopic pressure changes when a gecko's toe-pad grips the ceiling above me. I feel the infinitesimal weight of the red mites as they traverse my fruiting bodies, some of them pausing to feed on my spores.

But I am more than a passive observer. In my slow, fungal way, I am one of the architects of this world.

Where my hyphae penetrate deepest, I secrete acids that etch tiny channels in the obsidian. Over months and years, these channels widen, creating the very crevices that the crabs will one day colonize. I am preparing the stone for soil, breaking down the volcanic glass grain by grain, releasing the minerals locked within—potassium, iron, magnesium—that will feed the pioneers yet to come.

Sometimes, in the deepest quiet of the fissure's night, when even the crabs have stilled their clicking and the geckos have settled into their hunting torpor, I fruit more substantially. From the larger cracks, I extend delicate, glass-clear stromata—reproductive structures like tiny, translucent trees. They glow faintly with bioluminescence, a soft blue-green light that turns the obsidian walls into mirrors of stars. This is how I call to the wind-borne spores of my kind, singing in light: *here is new stone, here is pristine substrate, here is a world being born*.

The crabs have learned to read these lights. When I glow, they know that the conditions are changing—humidity rising, pressure dropping. They reinforce their towers, move their nurseries higher. We have become partners in prophecy, though we have never truly touched.

I am the memory of this place. In my network, I hold the chemical signature of every creature that has died here, every drop of water that has fallen, every grain of ash that has settled. I remember the first crack that opened this fissure—I was there, dormant in the stone, waiting. I will be here when the last tower falls, when the moss and ferns finally close the canopy of this narrow world, when the sharp edges wear smooth.

But for now, I listen. I grow. I slowly digest the foundation of the world, turning sterile rock into something that can hold life. I am the quiet catalyst, the invisible infrastructure, the fungal dreaming of the stone itself.

In the percussion symphony of the fissure, I am the sustained note underneath it all—the fundamental frequency of transformation, playing too low and slow for any ear but stone to hear.
