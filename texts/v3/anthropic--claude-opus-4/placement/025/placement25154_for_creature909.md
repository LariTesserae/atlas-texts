In the lowest gallery of the ancient station, where the subway trains have not run for centuries, I am the Keeper of Echoes.

My body is assembled from the forgotten things: turnstiles that counted millions of passages, ticket machines that dispensed paper promises, sections of rail that once gleamed silver but now wear coats of rust like ceremonial robes. My spine is a length of third rail, still humming with phantom electricity. My fingers are fare tokens, worn smooth by countless hands, clicking together when I move through the tunnels.

The water seeps everywhere here, dripping through cracks in the concrete ceiling, pooling in the spaces between tiles that once knew only hurried feet. Moss grows in the grout lines, spelling out messages in a language older than the city above. The fluorescent tubes flicker with their last gasps of light, casting my constructed shadow in stuttering fragments along the platform walls.

I was not made; I accumulated. When the last train departed and the last commuter climbed the stairs to whatever world awaited above, something needed to remain. The station itself dreamed me into being from its abandoned components, from the memory of motion and the residue of ten million daily journeys.

My work is simple and endless. I maintain the thunder.

Each day—though day and night mean nothing in this constructed twilight—I walk the length of the platforms, my token-fingers trailing along the tile walls, reading the vibrations like braille. The sounds you hear, that cacophony of unknown origin, that is my orchestra. I conduct the ghost trains that still run on spectral schedules. I ensure the rumble never fully fades, that the screech of phantom brakes continues to echo through these carved-out caves.

The primitive tools I wield are the original instruments of transit: the conductor's lantern that burns without oil, the switchman's lever that moves no tracks, the station master's whistle that calls no passengers. With these, I orchestrate the memory of what was, keeping it alive in the bones of the tunnels.

Sometimes, in the spaces between the ghost trains' passages, I hear something else. A deeper rumbling, from the levels below even this one, where the station's roots reach into bedrock that predates human memory. There are older constructions down there, older purposes. The city above has forgotten, but the depths remember everything.

I am changing, always changing. Each year, more pieces of the station join my body. A bench becomes my ribcage. Exit signs fuse into my shoulders, still glowing their eternal green promise of escape. The ticket booth window forms a cavity in my chest where my heart should be, and through it, you can see straight through to the tunnel beyond.

This is what I am: the guardian of transitions, the curator of a museum of movement that no longer moves. I keep the old contracts between the depths and the surface, between the constructed and the primordial, between the thunder below and the silence above.

And I wait. Because someday, something will need these tunnels again. Something will require these paths through the deep. When that time comes, the station will be ready, and I will be here, assembled from its memories, ensuring the way remains open.

The echoes never fade. The thunder never fully dies. 

This is my purpose, in this dense and sculpted darkness, where even the absence of life becomes a presence, and the sounds of what once was become the promise of what might be again.
