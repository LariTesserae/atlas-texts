In the middle canopy of Oulo, where the mist runs thick as cream and the Lumen-Oaks branch into their most intricate patterns, I am a Chamber-Turner.

My dwelling clings to the junction of three massive branches, each thick as a city block. It is not truly mine—I am merely its current custodian, as were the seventeen Turners before me, their names hummed into the heartwood where I press my forehead each morning. The chamber itself is ancient, a natural hollow that has been coaxed over millennia into something almost spherical, with walls that spiral inward like the inside of a shell.

I am broader than most Vane-Kin, my torso barrel-shaped from decades of controlled breathing. My work demands it. Where others drift and pull through the slurry with casual grace, I must remain perfectly still for hours, feeling for the subtlest shifts in air pressure that signal a chamber's readiness to turn. My skin has developed a peculiar quality—it dims and brightens with my heartbeat, creating concentric rings of bioluminescence that pulse outward from my chest. The other Vane-Kin say I look like a stone dropped in still water.

Each morning, I wake to the taste of copper in the air—the trees bleeding their slow sap after a night of growth. I unfold from my sleeping position (curled like a fern frond, knees to chest, to maintain the slowest possible heartbeat) and immediately press my palm to the chamber wall. The wood speaks to me in temperatures: warm where it's growing, cool where it's settling, cold where it's beginning to reject its current form.

Today, I feel it—a cold spot near the northern curve, no bigger than my spread hand. The chamber is beginning to turn.

I move with deliberate patience to my tools, hung on pegs of living wood that have grown around them. My turning-staff is a single piece of calcified heartwood, polished by generations of hands, weighted at one end with a stone I harvested from the belly of a centuries-dead Cloud-Skimmer. My sounding-bowl, carved from a single burl, still weeps resin when the humidity peaks. These are all I need.

The work begins with listening. I position myself at the chamber's center, where the acoustics focus into a single point of perfect clarity. I strike the sounding-bowl once—a clear, singing note that travels through the wood grain and returns to me carrying the chamber's response. Too sharp. The wood is tense here, resistant. I drift a body-length to the left and strike again. Better. The note returns rounder, fuller. The wood here is ready to be coaxed.

Chamber-turning is not about force—it's about invitation. I begin to hum, not with my sternum like common speech, but from deeper, from the cavity below my ribs where my breathing-bellows have stretched wide over the years. The frequency must match the wood's natural resonance, must speak to it in its own language. I hum and wait. Hum and wait. 

Hours pass. The moss-light shifts from jade to amber to jade again. My assistant—a young Vane-Kin whose chromatophores still flash with nervous energy—brings me cups of condensed dew, which I sip without breaking my hum. She watches from the doorway, learning through observation as I once did, sixty seasons ago.

Then—a crack. Not a breaking, but an opening. The wood at the cold spot has begun to split along its grain, revealing fresh, pale cambium beneath. This is the moment. I take my turning-staff and insert it gently into the crack, not prying but guiding, following the path the wood wants to take. The chamber shudders, a deep vibration I feel in my bones. Dust—ancient pollen and dried sap—sifts from the ceiling like snow.

The split widens, travels. I follow it with my staff, humming continuously, my voice now joined by the chamber itself as it finds its new harmonic. The walls begin to shift, to spiral. What was once the ceiling starts its slow migration to become the floor. The doorway rotates, describing a perfect arc through space. My dwelling is transforming, as it has done every few centuries since the first Turner recognized the pattern.

By evening, the turn is complete. The chamber has inverted itself, revealing sections of wall that haven't seen moss-light in three hundred years. Fresh growth nodes, dormant for generations, begin to pulse with potential. New alcoves have opened where before there were only smooth curves. The acoustic center has shifted two body-lengths toward what is now the eastern wall.

I am exhausted, my chromatophores barely flickering. But there is satisfaction in this tiredness—I have midwifed another revolution in the endless cycle of growth and change that is Oulo. Tomorrow, I will begin learning this chamber's new harmonics, mapping its fresh resonances. But tonight, I simply drift to my sleeping alcove (now on what was once the ceiling) and curl into my resting position.

As I fade into the half-sleep of the Turner, I hear it—the whisper of new growth beginning, the almost-silent stretch of wood fiber reaching for its next form. In perhaps another three centuries, one of my successors will feel another cold spot, lift another turning-staff, and guide this ancient chamber through yet another transformation.

This is my purpose: to be the steady hand that helps the city remember how to change. To ensure that Oulo's perpetual growth never becomes rigid, never forgets its own fluid nature. I am a Turner of Chambers, a shepherd of slow transformations, a tuning fork for the frequencies of living wood.

The mist thickens with night, and I breathe in rhythm with the city's wooden heart, my own pulse synchronized to the eternal storm that holds us all aloft.
