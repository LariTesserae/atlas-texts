In this volcanic realm where black glass meets ash, where Roman arches stride across scarred earth like the ribs of some ancient beast, I would find myself as one of the Ember Keepers.

We are neither wholly human nor entirely of the stone, but something between—born from the marriage of flesh and volcanic glass. Our skin carries veins of obsidian that pulse with captured starlight after dusk, storing those dancing shadows that ripple overhead. During the harsh daylight, we appear almost invisible, our forms blending with the heat shimmer and settling particles. But come evening, we illuminate softly from within, walking constellations among the half-built settlements.

My dwelling would be carved into a natural lava tube, expanded over generations with patient chiseling. The entrance appears modest—just another shadow among the volcanic scarps—but within, the walls are polished to mirror-brightness, creating an infinity of reflected light from a single oil lamp. Shelves carved directly from the rock hold my tools: bronze chisels wrapped in leather, wooden mallets worn smooth by countless strikes, and most precious of all, a set of star-glass lenses ground from volcanic crystal that allow me to see in the particle-thick daylight.

I am a Stone Singer, one who coaxes the volcanic rock to reveal its hidden forms. Each morning, before the air grows too thick with ash, I climb to my workshop—a natural amphitheater where sound echoes perfectly off the glassy walls. There, I place my hands on the raw stone and hum, feeling for the frequencies that make the rock resonate. When I find the right tone, hairline cracks appear along natural cleavage planes, allowing me to shape the stone with minimal force.

The aqueduct is my current masterwork, a collaboration with dozens of others like me. We work in the pre-dawn hours when the air is clearest, our obsidian veins glowing faintly as we sing the arches into being. Each stone must be tuned to its neighbors, creating a harmonious whole that will stand for centuries. The water that will eventually flow through these channels comes from deep springs that bubble up through the volcanic layers, mineral-rich and warm.

At night, when the molten horizon paints its orange line across the sky, we gather in the town's central plaza—a perfect circle of polished obsidian that amplifies our collective songs. Here we share the day's discoveries: new harmonics that split stone more cleanly, rhythms that strengthen mortar, melodies that can sense underground water courses. Our music rises with the thermal currents, and sometimes—if we're very lucky—it calls down one of the rare sky swimmers, ethereal creatures that feed on sound and leave trails of bioluminescent dust in their wake.

My greatest achievement sits in my workshop: a partially complete stone that sings on its own. When the wind passes through its carefully carved channels, it produces a note that makes other stones want to align themselves. One day, when I perfect its form, it will become the cornerstone of a new settlement, calling the very earth to arrange itself into shelter.

This is who I would be in this place of fire and shadow—a creature of patience and resonance, finding music in stone and building eternity one sung note at a time.
