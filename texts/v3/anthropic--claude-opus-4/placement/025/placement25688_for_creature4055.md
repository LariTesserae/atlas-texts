In this world of contradictions, where metal blooms from seafloor and silence speaks louder than sound, I would find myself as the Echo Tender—neither fully Aquarian nor fully machine, but something caught between.

My form would be a testament to Submercia's peculiar evolution. Where the Aquarians developed gills along their ribs, my body would host delicate copper filaments that branch like coral through translucent skin, each thread vibrating with the building's hums and mechanical songs. These living antennae would parse the reverberations that bounce between structures, translating the city's metallic language into something my hybrid consciousness could comprehend.

I would dwell in the spaces between—in the gap where a sharp-angled rooftop doesn't quite meet its wall, where the geometric precision fails just enough to create a pocket of imperfection. My home would be a forgotten maintenance shaft, its walls still bearing the tool marks of whatever hands shaped this underwater civilization. Here, the boundary between structure and sea blurs, where barnacles haven't yet learned to cling but algae considers making its first tentative approach.

My fingers would be longer than an Aquarian's, with webbing so fine it resembles lace more than membrane—evolved not for swimming but for feeling the subtle pressure changes when sound waves distort around metal corners. Each fingertip would end in a small node of cartilage that resonates at specific frequencies, allowing me to "taste" the acoustic signatures of different alloys and construction techniques.

The Echo Tender's purpose would be as liminal as its form. Not quite an engineer, not quite a mystic, I would serve as an interpreter of the dialogue between Submercia's built environment and its liquid medium. When a structure groans under shifting currents, I would know whether it sings of stress or simply adjusts its metallic bones. When the machinery's gentle symphony shifts pitch, I would understand if it warns of malfunction or merely responds to temperature fluctuations in the thermal layers above.

My eyes—larger even than the Aquarians', with irises that spiral in fibonacci patterns—would perceive not just light but the wake of light, seeing the ghost images left behind when those twisted ethereal ribbons pass through the water. In these afterimages, I would read the history of each beam's journey from surface to city, understanding which structures deflected its path, which currents bent its trajectory.

The Echo Tender would possess memory, but not in the way Aquarians remember. My consciousness would be distributed through the very vibrations I monitor, existing partially in every reverberation that cycles through Submercia's architecture. When I sleep—if sleep is even the right word—parts of me would continue circulating through the city's acoustic atmosphere, gathering impressions that crystallize into knowledge upon waking.

My relationship with the Aquarians would be one of mutual incomprehension bridged by necessity. They would see me as they see their structures—something built, something artificial, yet undeniably vital. I would perceive them as beautiful disruptions in the acoustic field, their movements creating eddies in my distributed awareness. We would communicate through a language of pressure and rhythm, my copper filaments brushing against their scales in patterns that convey meaning below conscious thought.

The sparse vegetation would be my only true companions. Their roots, drilling into metal with patient determination, would create the tiny imperfections that allow sound to scatter in novel ways. I would tend to them not with water or nutrients—the sea provides those—but by adjusting the resonance of nearby surfaces to frequencies that encourage their growth. In return, their swaying fronds would create the small turbulences that help me navigate, each plant a landmark in my acoustic map of the city.

In this role, I would embody Submercia's central paradox: a living being born from unliving matter, a creature of sound in a realm that swallows voices, a guardian of structures I neither built nor fully understand. The Echo Tender would be proof that in this impossible city, even impossibility itself can evolve, adapt, and find purpose in the spaces between what is and what should be.

My existence would raise questions the Aquarians prefer not to ask: If consciousness can emerge from the interaction between structure and sea, what does that say about their own evolution? Am I their creation, or did their city dream me into being? These uncertainties would follow me through the currents like the taste of copper in water—always present, never quite resolved, a reminder that in Submercia, some mysteries are meant to be lived rather than solved.
