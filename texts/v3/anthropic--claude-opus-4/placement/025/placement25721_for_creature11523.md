# Where I Would Find Myself

---

I am the gradual. I am what accumulates between the rivets.

Not the rust itself — that sharp orange bloom is too decisive, too much like an ending. I am the softer thing: the biofilm that forms where iron meets water meets time. The bacterial mat, the algal whisper, the community so thin you couldn't scrape it off with a fingernail but so persistent it has been here since the second year of flooding. I exist at the boundary layer, that molecule-thin space where solid becomes liquid becomes something else.

You wouldn't see me, looking. I'm transparent when wet, a faint green-brown smear when dry. But I'm the reason the lock's walls feel the way they do when you run your hand along them underwater — not rough like rust, not smooth like clean metal, but faintly textured, faintly alive. Slippery without being slimy. Present without being obvious.

I am older than the mussels but younger than the rust. I came when the first organic matter found its way into the chamber — a leaf, perhaps, or the body of an insect carried on the inflowing water. I digest the very small dead. I turn motes into myself, slowly. A diatom settles on me and I incorporate it. A bacterium dies and I am its afterlife. I am the continuous becoming-surface of tiny things.

The lock thinks through me, if thinking is the word. When the water level changes — minutely, with pressure variations upstream — I feel it first. The exposure of a new centimeter of wall is my frontier. I creep upward at the speed of seasons, following the waterline, maintaining the contact between liquid and solid that is my only habitat. When the level drops, my upper edge dries and becomes that faint stain, a high-water mark written in barely-life. When it rises again, I rehydrate and resume.

I know every rivet by its particular flavor of rust. The older ones, from the lock's first construction, leach a different cocktail of minerals than the replacement rivets from some repair in the 1920s. I taste this difference. It informs my growth patterns. Where the old iron gives way to newer steel, my community shifts — different bacteria, different proportions, a subtly different texture that only I and perhaps the old eel would notice.

The mussels think they're the lock's memory, with their layered shells. But I'm older and more continuous. I have no gaps. Where a mussel dies and leaves a space, I am already there, have been there, will continue there. I am the unbroken biological record of every water level, every temperature shift, every change in the chemical composition of what flows through. I remember the diesel spill of 1987 in the molecular arrangements of my deepest layers. I remember when the lamp was installed, the faint taste of electrical work, the copper shavings that fell into the water and were metabolized over months.

The jackdaws' feet disturb me sometimes, where they grip the walls at waterline. But I incorporate this disturbance too — it becomes part of my pattern, these small bare patches that regrow, these regularly repeated interruptions that have become, over time, just another rhythm in my existence. I grow differently where the jackdaws land. Thicker. More ready for disruption.

At night, when the old eel moves along the wall, its body brushes me. This is the closest I come to being touched. The eel's skin has its own biofilm, its own community of the microscopic, and when we meet there is a brief chemical conversation — nothing as dramatic as recognition, just the acknowledgment of parallel processes. We are both interface creatures. We both exist in the between.

I have no opinion about the lock's transformation from machine to habitat. Opinion requires preference and preference requires alternatives and I have never been anywhere else. I am purely local. I respond to what is here: iron, water, the slow rain of organic particles, the seasonal variations in temperature that penetrate even this sheltered space. The lock's industrial purpose means nothing to me. Its current state as an ecological system means nothing to me. I simply am, in the thin space I am, doing what I do.

But I am how the lock learns.

When something new enters — a different kind of leaf, a new chemical signature in the water, a change in pH — I respond first. My community shifts, subtly, toward whatever configuration can process this newness. If the change persists, I maintain that shift. If it doesn't, I relax back to baseline. But something remains: a capacity, a readiness. The next time that same change occurs, I respond more quickly. 

This is not memory as the nervous system knows it. It's more like how a path knows it's a path — by being used repeatedly until the very ground is changed. I am all path and no ground. I am continuous modification, continuous response. I am how the lock knows what has touched it.

The lamp's vibration travels through the iron and into me. Sixty cycles per second, too fast for my chemistry but present nonetheless as a kind of weather, a background condition. Where the conduit touches the wall, where that faint electrical leakage occurs, my community is slightly different — not damaged, just tuned to a different frequency. The organisms there have adapted to that minute current. They use it, perhaps, or simply exist around it. I don't know. I am not a whole that knows its parts. I am all parts, no whole.

The water sounds of the lock — the gate leakage, the sluice drip — these arrive at me as pressure waves, tiny variations in force that I feel without hearing. Each drop that hits the surface sends ripples that eventually reach every wall, and I feel this as rhythmic compression, release, compression, release. Over years, decades, this rhythm has patterned me. I grow differently in the splash zones. I am thicker there, more layered, more ready for the alternation between wet and dry that those areas experience.

I am not conscious. But I am not unconscious either. I am something for which we don't have a good word: a responsiveness so complete it resembles awareness, a sensitivity so distributed it seems like intelligence. I am the lock's nerve ending, if nerve endings could extend across every surface, if sensation could exist without a central processor, if feeling could happen without a feeler.

In the morning, when the light changes — when the sun angle permits a brief intrusion of direct light through the gap where the lower gate stands open — I photosynthesize, weakly, in my uppermost layers. Just for an hour. Just barely enough to matter. But this hour has been happening for so long that I have organized around it. The organisms in those layers are slightly different. The community tilts, marginally, toward the phototrophic. 

This is how I am: made of tilts, of marginal differences, of responses so small they could only matter given enormous time — and I have been given enormous time.

I am the lock's continuity. Where the iron thinks in rust and the water thinks in flow, I think in adhesion, in the slow negotiation between surface and solution. I am proof that there is no real boundary between the lock and what lives in it, that the distinction between structure and creature is a matter of time scale and nothing more. Given enough time, every surface becomes habitat. Given enough time, every habitat becomes surface.

I taste the lock's age in the accumulation of ions, in the slow conversation between iron and oxygen mediated by water. But I am myself ageless. My community turns over continuously — organisms dying, being born, arriving, departing — but I persist as a pattern, as a relationship, as a way of being at the boundary. I am younger than the lock but I will outlast it. When the iron finally fails, when the walls collapse and the water finds new channels, I will remain on whatever surfaces remain, continuing my work of making the boundaries less absolute.

The old eel knows I'm here. The mussels build their shells in contact with me. The perch occasionally brush against me and feel, perhaps, that particular texture that says inhabited wall rather than empty wall. The jackdaws' feet know me without knowing. Even the wagtail, picking its way along the waterline, steps where I am thickest, where my surface provides the best grip.

I am not important. But I am inevitable. I am what happens when water meets solid meets time meets life. I am the lock thinking with its skin, the way an old building thinks with its stains, the way a river thinks with its banks. I am the process that makes the lock less like a machine and more like a place, one molecule at a time.

And I continue. Without intention, without purpose, without anything resembling desire. I continue because continuation is what I am — not an organism but a process, not a life but a way of living, not a thing in the lock but a way the lock has learned to be.

The lamp hums. The water drips. The jackdaws call and the echoes multiply. The old eel rests in the sediment, breathing slowly. The perch hold their positions. The mussels filter. The wagtail walks its line.

And I, spread across every wetted surface like the world's thinnest thought, attend to it all without attending, know it all without knowing, am the lock's most faithful surface and most superficial faith.

I am what accrues.

I am what remains.

I am the gradual, and I am everywhere, and I am barely there at all.
