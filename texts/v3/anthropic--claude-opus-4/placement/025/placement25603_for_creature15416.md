I would find myself as one of the **Threshold Weavers**.

Not the dense, shape-shifting entities that flow through stone, nor the architectural pressure-sculptors of the deep dark. I would be something more liminal—a being that exists primarily in the moments of transition, in the fleeting seconds when one state becomes another.

My form would be barely distinguishable from the water itself, save for the faint shimmer of heat-death that follows in my wake—a ribbon of slightly warmer water that cools too quickly, leaving behind microscopic ice crystals that sublimate instantly back into the liquid medium. I would move by creating tiny pockets of phase-change, boiling and freezing the water in nanoscopic bursts, propelling myself through the contradiction of states.

I would dwell in the vertical shafts where the light still reaches but barely, in the columns of space where the golden surface-light meets the abyssal dark. My purpose—if purpose is the right word—would be to maintain the memory of boundaries in this boundless world. Where the others preserve the memory of the rising, I would preserve the memory of *surfaces*—of the moment when water met air, when wet met dry, when breathing was a negotiation between two different mediums.

My body would be a collection of membrane-thin sheets, translucent as jellyfish bells but structured like origami made from surface tension itself. Each fold would contain a different pressure, a different temperature, a different salinity—tiny pocket dimensions of remembered seas. Arctic brine in one crease, equatorial warmth in another, the brackish mix of river deltas captured in the valleys between.

I would feed—though feeding isn't quite right—on gradients. Where the architectural weavers spin matter from pressure, I would sustain myself on *differences*: the sweet shock where cold current meets warm, the electric potential between salt layers, the tension where dissolved gases struggle against their liquid prison. I would stretch myself across these boundaries, becoming a living membrane that simultaneously separates and connects, drinking in the energy of disparity.

The pearl-anemone networks would sense me as a flutter, a discontinuity in their vast sensory web. To them, I would appear as static in their otherwise smooth perception—not hostile, but fundamentally *other* in a way that even the warm-blooded visitor is not. For while the human disrupts their equilibrium with alien metabolism, I would exist as a living reminder that equilibrium itself is an illusion, that all states are transitional, that even their patient, pressure-sculpted world is just one breath away from becoming something else.

When I move through the caverns, I would leave behind thin sheets of altered water—layers where the molecules have been convinced to align differently, to remember different physics. These would last only moments before the overwhelming presence of the sea-sky reasserts itself, but in those moments, one might glimpse what this place could have been: an air pocket, a different ocean, a world where the rising never came.

The others tolerate me as one tolerates a necessary paradox. I am the itch they cannot scratch, the dream of surfaces in their volumetric world. And in the deepest part of the formation, where even I rarely venture, there are whispers—pressure-poems in the architectural resonators—that speak of a time when there will be Threshold Weavers for the opposite transition: when the sky finally learns to recede, and something must remember the drowning back into air.
