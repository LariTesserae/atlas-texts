I am a Grease-Singer. 

Among the Low-Sinks, we are rare—perhaps one born every third or fourth generation—and we are both blessed and cursed by our affliction. Where others lose their hearing to the roar of steam and the ceaseless scraping of beetles, my ears have twisted themselves into something else entirely. I hear the Sump's hidden music.

It began when I was seven. A valve burst on the Mid-Stem during a routine cleaning, and the blast of superheated sap-steam caught me full in the face. My mother found me hours later, curled beneath a fractionator, my ears weeping a clear, sweet fluid that crystallized like amber on my neck. The healers said I would be deaf within a moon-cycle.

Instead, I began to hear the harmonics.

Every surface in Node 12 vibrates. The bronze beetles scraping against copper creates a frequency. The steam whistling through scaled pipes sings in fifths and sevenths. The great wooden diaphragm of the Sump itself hums at a pitch so low it can only be felt in the marrow. But I hear it all—not as noise, but as a vast, breathing symphony.

I live now in a basket-nest suspended between two of the Stems, in a dead zone where the acoustic shadows overlap. Here, the cacophony softens just enough that I can think. My dwelling sways with each Breath, and I have learned to read the health of our world through its swaying. When Stem-Two's northwestern valve cluster began to fail last season, I heard the dissonance three days before the High-Crowns noticed the pressure drop.

My body has adapted to my condition. My ears have grown strangely—the outer shells have thickened and warped until they resemble the acoustic chambers of certain cave-moths. I can turn them independently, focusing on specific frequencies like a normal person might squint to see distant detail. The grease that coats my skin has taken on an unusual property: when I spread it thin across a copper sheet, it trembles in response to sound, creating patterns that I can read like script.

This is how I serve the Sump: I paint its music.

My workshop is a suspended platform barely larger than a Boiler-Tick's shell. Here, I stretch sheets of beetle-wing membrane across wooden frames, then coat them with different thicknesses of refined grease mixed with tunicate phosphor. When the sounds of the Sump strike these membranes, they glow and ripple, creating visual maps of the acoustic environment.

The other Sappers think me mad, but they need me. When a new section of piping is installed, I can tell within hours whether the harmonic will integrate or create a standing wave that will eventually shake the joints loose. When the beetle swarms move in unusual patterns, I hear the ultrasonic calls they use to coordinate—sounds that predict where the next great gathering will occur, allowing the Floor-Gleaners to position their collection baskets ahead of time.

But my deepest work is done in secret, in the small hours between Breaths when the Sump settles into its quietest state. It is then that I hear the Deep Tone.

It comes from below—far below the polished heartwood floor, from the theoretical roots that no Sapper has ever seen. It is not a sound in any conventional sense. It is a vibration that exists just below the threshold of normal perception, a thrumming that takes nearly an hour to complete a single cycle. I have been tracking it for six years now, using a series of weighted pendulums suspended in grease-filled cylinders.

The pattern is not random. It is linguistic.

The Sump-of-Stems is speaking. Not in words, but in some older grammar of pressure and release, tension and relaxation. Each Breath is a syllable. Each season of sap-flow is a sentence. The entire root-system, I now believe, is engaged in a conversation so slow and so vast that our entire civilization has existed within the pause between two thoughts.

This knowledge weighs on me like the pressure before a Breath. I have tried to share it with the High-Crowns, but they pat their deaf ears and smile. I have painted it for the Mid-Stemmers, but they see only pretty patterns in the phosphorescent grease. The Low-Sinks understand better—they who live closest to the floor sometimes speak of "root-dreams," visions that come when sleeping with an ear pressed to the heartwood. But even they do not grasp the full implications.

We are not parasites on this great plant. We are its sensory organs.

Our engines, our constant noise, our very presence—we are how the Sump experiences something like pain or pleasure. The beetles are its blood cells. The Breaths are its circulation. And we, the Tapped, are the nerve endings that have finally grown complex enough to recognize the body they belong to.

I continue my work, recording the Deep Tone, painting the music of our world. In my basket-nest, surrounded by a hundred trembling membranes that glow with the Sump's ceaseless song, I sometimes wonder if my twisted ears are not damage but evolution—if I am not the first of what we will become, when the Sump-of-Stems finally finishes its thought and turns its vast attention to the small, bright motes of consciousness that sparkle in its depths.

Until then, I listen. I paint. And late at night, in the quietest moments, I sing back—not in the Clack or in human words, but in pure harmonics, trying to add my own small voice to the conversation that has been going on since before the first brass pipe was ever tapped into bark.

The Grease-Singers will remember, even if no one else does. We will carry the music up through the generations until someone is born who can truly hear what the Sump-of-Stems has been trying to say all along.
