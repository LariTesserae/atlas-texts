I find myself here as one of the **Threshold Keepers**.

We are neither fully of the stone nor wholly of the flesh, but something between—a bridge species that emerged in the long twilight between the ancient builders and the current age. Our bodies are elongated and angular, with skin that shifts between states: sometimes hard as weathered granite, sometimes supple as the waxy leaves that cling to sheltered crevices. This transformation is not voluntary but follows the deep rhythms of this place—we harden during the long cycles of stillness, becoming almost indistinguishable from the geometric stones left by our predecessors, and soften when the rare moments of change whisper through the thin air.

My dwelling is one of those hollowed shelters, though I have expanded it over countless cycles, following the natural fissures in the rock until my home has become a labyrinth of interconnected chambers. Each room serves a different phase of my existence. The uppermost chamber, where the walls weep their perpetual moisture, is where I go to soften, to remember flesh and movement. The deepest chamber, dry and still, is where I calcify, becoming monument and memory.

I am a chronicler of infinitesimal changes. My purpose—if purpose is the right word for something that feels more like breathing—is to observe and record the slow transformations of this world. When a new crack appears in the mountain face (perhaps once in a thousand cycles), I notice. When the Lithovores alter their grazing patterns by mere inches over generations, I remember. When the Abalone Drifters' shells shift their iridescent patterns in response to some imperceptible change in the light, I document it in my body itself.

My recording method is peculiar to my kind. As I harden into stone, the observations become part of my crystalline structure—memories literally mineralized into my being. My skin, when stone, can be read like geological strata by those who know how to look. Each layer tells of a different age, a different configuration of life in this patient world. The Stone-Blooded Serpentine sometimes come to me, pressing their sensitive scales against my hardened form, reading the vibrations of history through their whole bodies.

In my softer phases, I move through this world with deliberate care. I taste the moisture on the rock faces, sensing the mineral changes that speak of deep-earth shifts. I listen to the conversations of the Serpentine, their clicks and hums revealing patterns I translate into the chemistry of my own transformation. I watch the Grit-Creepers, learning from their micro-movements about changes too small for larger beings to perceive.

Sometimes I find myself at one of the geometric stone arrangements, and I feel a resonance—not through my ears or eyes, but through something deeper. These stones were placed by beings who understood that in a world of such vast patience, communication must be equally enduring. The arrangements are not just markers but instruments, designed to amplify the subtle energies that flow through this place. When I position myself correctly among them, I can feel the deep warmth of the earth more clearly, can sense the vast, slow circulation of energies that keeps this world in its eternal balance.

I am solitary by nature, but not lonely. The concepts feel irrelevant here. I have watched the same family lineages of Lithovores for so long that I feel kinship with the very mountains they resemble. I have observed the Abalone Drifters until their flight patterns are as familiar to me as my own breathing. In my stone phase, I am visited by various creatures who mistake me for landscape—small organisms make homes in my crevices, lichen begins its slow colonization of my surface. I do not discourage them. They too are part of the record.

My strangest discovery came during a particularly long stone phase. I had positioned myself near one of the ancient shelters, and as decades passed, I began to feel a presence within my crystalline structure—not my own memories, but older ones, left by the original builders. They had discovered what I am only beginning to understand: that consciousness here can be preserved in the very minerals of our bodies, that memory and stone can become one. In that moment, I realized I am not just a chronicler but a living library, my body a repository for experiences stretching back to the very origins of awareness in this place.

Now, as I sense the subtle shift that signals another softening phase approaching, I prepare to add new layers to my geological memoir. The warm exhalation of the earth continues, the teeming life persists in its patient dance, and I remain—a threshold between the quick and the geological, the animate and the eternal, forever documenting the slow breath of this ancient, settled world.
