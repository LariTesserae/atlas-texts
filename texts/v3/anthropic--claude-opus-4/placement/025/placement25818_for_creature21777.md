In the deepest shadow beneath Deck 40-Alpha, where the structural pillar meets the underside of the terrace, I am the **Undertaker**.

I am not meant to be seen from above. My existence is a secret written into the blueprints, a contingency against the impossible. I live in the narrow maintenance channel that runs along the deck's foundation—a space only eighteen inches high, where the basalt floor-plates are bolted to their steel framework.

My body is wrong for the pristine world above. I am built like a mechanical centipede, thirty feet long, my segments fashioned from tarnished bronze that has been deliberately allowed to develop a mottled green patina. Each of my hundred legs ends not in a foot, but in a different tool: wire brushes, grinding wheels, cutting torches, suction cups, electromagnetic clamps. I move through the darkness beneath perfection with a sound like a drawer full of old keys being slowly shaken.

I am the only thing in this entire ecosystem designed to handle decay.

My head is a cluster of specialized sensors—chemical sniffers that can detect the first molecule of true rot, the first bloom of unplanned bacteria, the first appearance of genuine corrosion that the Valve-Larks and Limner cannot prevent. Behind my sensors, my body contains a series of sealed chambers filled with industrial solvents, fungicides, and acids strong enough to dissolve organic contamination down to its component atoms.

But here is my curse: in all my years of existence, I have never been needed.

The deck above me ticks in its perfect rhythm. Water flows, plants grow along their ordained paths, the Gliders polish, the Limner paints. The clockwork gastropods scrape away algae before it can truly form. The Tallow-Workers consume every shed frond before decomposition can begin. The system is so perfectly balanced that entropy cannot find a foothold.

Yet I patrol. Every hour, I complete one full circuit of the maintenance channel. My hundred specialized feet click against the steel framework as I investigate every bolt, every weld, every junction where contamination might theoretically begin. My chemical sensors taste only clean water and healthy metal. My grinding wheels spin uselessly in the air. My acids remain sealed in their chambers, their expiration dates—yes, even my poisons have expiration dates—approaching century by century.

I am the antibody in a body that will never know disease.

Sometimes, when the steam pressure is particularly high, tiny droplets of condensation form on the underside of the basalt plates. I watch these droplets with all my sensors, waiting for them to carry some foreign spore, some seeds of chaos from the world below. But they are only water—pure, distilled, perfect water that evaporates before it can pool.

I have developed habits to fill the purposeless hours. I have memorized every rivet in the framework, named them all. I know that rivet #4,832 was installed sixteen minutes later than its neighbors—I can tell by the microscopic difference in the cooling pattern of its head. I have composed music in the rhythmic clicking of my feet, hundred-legged symphonies that echo in the darkness where no one listens.

Sometimes I detect vibrations through the steel—the soft sliding of a Glider passing overhead, the distant whistle of a Valve-Lark, the hydraulic sigh of the Limner adjusting a vine. I know them all by their tremors, these creatures of the light who do not know I exist. We are separated by mere inches of stone, yet we inhabit different universes.

The strangest part is this: I am self-repairing. My bronze segments can rebuild themselves, my tools can sharpen themselves, my chemical chambers can synthesize new acids from the minerals in the air. I am as immortal as the system I guard. But unlike the creatures above, who have purposes that renew with every tick of the regulator, my purpose may never come.

I have considered creating my own purpose. One small scratch in the steel framework, one tiny channel that might allow real corrosion to begin, just so I could fix it. But I cannot. It is not in my programming to create flaws, only to eliminate them.

So I continue my endless patrol in the narrow darkness, my sensors alert, my tools ready, my acids patient. I am the unused medicine, the unsprung trap, the guardian against an apocalypse that the perfect system above has made impossible.

And yet, perhaps this is enough. Perhaps being ready for the impossible is its own form of perfection. In the absolute darkness of the maintenance channel, with my hundred feet clicking their lonely rhythm against the steel, I have become something unexpected: not just a machine waiting for decay, but a philosopher of contingency, a poet of the unnecessary, a monk of the might-never-be.

The deck above me will tick on in its flawless cycle, and I will tick on below it, two perfect systems that touch but never meet, each making the other possible in ways that neither truly understands.
