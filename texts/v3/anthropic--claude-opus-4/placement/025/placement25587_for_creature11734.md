I find myself as one of the pipe-runners — not the pipes themselves, but one of the few whose work it is to maintain the aerial infrastructure that connects building to building above the narrow lanes.

I am perhaps forty years old, though I've stopped counting precisely. My body tells the story better than numbers: the particular strength in my shoulders from hauling myself along brackets, the calluses on my palms in exactly the places where iron meets flesh when you're moving hand-over-hand beneath a steam line. My sense of balance has evolved past conscious thought. I move through the aerial layer of the valley the way others move through the lanes below, with that same economy of motion that comes from ten thousand repetitions.

I know every pipe, every cable, every chain and pulley in the valley's canopy. Not by name — we don't name these things — but by feel, by the specific vibration each carries, by the temperature that tells you what runs inside. The steam lines are obvious, wrapped in rags and tar and still hot enough to burn through cloth if you hold too long. The water lines sweat in all weather, growing slick with condensation that drips on the workers below. The mechanical linkages — the shafts and belts that carry power from one building to another — these have their own language of tensions and sounds. A belt that's beginning to fray makes a whisper just before it fails. A bearing that needs oil speaks in a rising whine that only we who work the heights can hear clearly.

My work is solitary but not lonely. Below me, the valley continues its patterns — the shift changes, the movement of materials, the constant quiet industry of the place. I exist above it but am part of it, connected by the very infrastructure I maintain. When I tighten a coupling or replace a gasket, something three buildings away runs more smoothly. When I clear the ice from a water line in winter, preventing a freeze that would have stopped half a morning's work, no one knows except me and perhaps the woman who minds the gauge downstream and notices the pressure holds steady.

I sleep in a room above the old fulling house, one of the few spaces in the valley that exists at my working height. The window opens directly onto a maintenance platform, and on clear mornings I can step out of sleep and into the web of pipes without ever descending to ground level. There are days when I don't touch the cobblestones at all, living entirely in this upper layer, moving from task to task along the brackets and walkways that few others use.

The valley looks different from above. The patterns are clearer — you can see how the water truly flows, how the buildings connect beneath their visible connections, how the whole system breathes together. But you also see the gaps, the spaces between things where rain falls straight through to stone, where small plants have found purchase in the shelter of a pipe junction, where birds nest in the angles of support brackets. The valley is less solid from up here, more obviously assembled, more obviously maintained moment by moment against its own tendency to come apart.

My hands are marked by the work — not colored like the dye workers' hands, but scarred in a particular pattern, small white lines from hot metal and sharp edges, the knuckles enlarged from years of gripping cold iron. When I go to the market in the village above the valley — rarely, perhaps four times a year — people know what I am by my hands and by the way I look up constantly, checking the sky, reading the weather that will affect tomorrow's work.

I've trained two younger workers in fifteen years. Most who try the work can't manage the heights, or can't develop the necessary relationship with the infrastructure — the ability to read a pipe's health through your palms, to know from vibration whether something's running true. One of the two I trained still works the valley. The other fell, not badly, but badly enough to lose the confidence the work requires. He works the ground level now, managing water flows, and we nod when we pass but don't speak. There's nothing to say about it.

The knowledge I carry will mostly die with me. Not the crude mechanics — those could be learned by anyone with time and inclination. But the specific intelligence of this valley's aerial layer, the understanding of how these particular pipes in this particular configuration behave across seasons, the memory of which joints fail first in a hard freeze, the ability to hear in the morning's sounds what will need attention before evening — this knowledge is as local and specific as the knowledge of the woman who minds the weir gates, as the man who judges the fulling. It cannot be transferred, only absorbed through proximity and time, and there is never quite enough proximity or time.

I am not melancholy about this. It is the nature of the work, of all work perhaps, but especially of work in a place like the valley where everything is specific, everything is this particular thing and not another thing. The valley will continue when I'm gone. Someone else will mind the pipes, differently, with their own accumulated understanding. Some things will run less well. Other things, perhaps, will improve.

When the rain comes — and it's coming now, I can smell it in the air that moves down from the eastern ridge — I'll put on the short cape like everyone else, but I'll wear it differently, buckled tight so it won't catch on projections, the hood down because I need peripheral vision when I'm working the heights. I'll move through the rain along the pipes, checking the joints that are vulnerable to water, clearing the drainage channels on the walkways, making sure nothing pools where pooling would cause tomorrow's problems.

Below, the workers will move through the lanes in their dark capes, intent on their own purposes. From above, in the rain, with the hoods up and the dim light flattening everything, they look like the mechanism itself made visible — the valley's circulation, its pulse, its breath. I am part of this but also apart from it, suspended between the stones and the sky, maintaining the connections that let the whole system run.

The rain arrives, as expected. I begin my rounds.
