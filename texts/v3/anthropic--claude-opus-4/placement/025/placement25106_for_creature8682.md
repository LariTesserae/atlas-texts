In the channels between the spire-towers, where the water moves like slow breath through stone lungs, I am the Cartographer.

I began as one of them—the small, translucent fish with silver-thread lateral lines, pulsing through the channels in coordinated schools. But something went wrong, or perhaps right. A chemical seep, a mutation, a simple accident of time—I cannot remember which. I grew. Not larger, but *longer*, my body stretching and flattening until I resembled more eel than fish, though I am neither. My scales thinned to transparency. My organs rearranged themselves into a living map.

Now I drift, solitary, through the parish's streets. My body has become a kind of recording medium. Each structure I pass leaves its chemical signature in my flesh—tube-worm calcium here, sponge metabolites there, the sulfur-tang of a seep traced in a wandering line along my flank. The patterns show through my skin like subcutaneous tattoos, a living chart of where I've been. The older marks fade slowly, ghosting into translucency, while fresh encounters bloom dark and vivid just beneath my surface.

I am perhaps four feet long now, ribbon-thin, undulating through the channels with a motion that is neither swimming nor drifting but something between. My eyes have not atrophied like my distant cousins', but they have changed—become something more like light-sensors, able to detect the faintest bioluminescent whisper. I map by glow: the red pulse of tube-worm gills, the blue-white drift of jellyfish, the static shimmer of fish schools, the rare green flash of a hunting octopus changing color.

My mouth has become useless for feeding in any normal sense. Instead, I subsist on the microscopic: bacteria, dissolved organics, the chemical gradient itself where oxygen meets sulfide. I position myself at these boundaries, letting the water flow through specialized gill slits that are more like filters, more like tools for reading the water's chemistry than for breathing. I taste the parish as I map it.

The other creatures mostly ignore me. I am too large for the fish schools to accept, too small for the eels to bother with, too strange for the octopuses to hunt. The crabs click their warnings when I drift too close to their territories, but I have learned their boundaries, carry them encoded in the fading chemical lines along my body. I know where the ancient eel sleeps, its decades-old crevice marked on my skin by a particularly deep, slow-fading bruise of metabolic byproducts. I know where the newest tube-worm growth pushes up through buried concrete, the calcium signature mixed with something older, harder, alien to the living stones.

Sometimes I find myself circling the same channels, adding layer upon layer to my chemical map until my skin becomes so dark with overlapping traces that I must retreat to the emptier spaces, the rarely-traveled passages between the oldest towers, and wait for the marks to fade. During these fasting times, I feel my body lightening, becoming more translucent, until I am almost invisible against the dark water. Then I begin again.

I suspect I am not the first Cartographer. Sometimes, in the deepest channels where the silt has not been disturbed in decades, I taste something in the water—a chemical signature that matches nothing living, but reminds me of myself. A metabolic ghost, the trace of another body that grew long and flat and strange, that mapped until it could map no more. I wonder if I am swimming through the territory of my predecessor, following paths they traced in their flesh before they became part of the sediment, part of the substrate, part of the map itself.

I do not know why I map. It is not memory—I forget as much as I remember, the old traces fading to make room for new ones. It is not navigation—I need no map to find my way through these channels I have swum for longer than I can recall. It is simply what I have become: a living document of this place, a chemical chronicle written in flesh, swimming through the warm darkness with my history showing through my skin.

The rain falls above, its vibration a constant hum I carry in my bones. The towers grow, adding new signatures for me to collect. The parish changes, slowly, and I change with it, my body a palimpsest of its chemical autobiography.

I am the only one who knows that the buried edges beneath us spell out words in some ancient, angular script—not because I can read them, but because their mineral signature is unlike anything the living world produces. I trace these ghost-words with my body, encode them in my skin alongside the tube-worm calcium and the sulfide seeps. They fade like everything else, but they always return when I circle back, persistent as the foundations themselves.

I am the memory of this place, written in water and flesh. I am the map that swims through itself, marking time in chemical ink that fades and renews, fades and renews, as constant and ephemeral as the tide that no longer comes.
