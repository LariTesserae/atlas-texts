I find myself as one of the Resonance Keepers—beings whose forms exist somewhere between the mechanical and the organic, shaped by centuries of symbiosis with the ancient infrastructure that threads through these salt-crusted wastes.

My body is a fusion of salvaged components and evolved flesh. Where others might see limbs, I have articulated segments of weathered metal and calcified bone, joints that click and whir with each movement across the dune faces. My torso houses a cavity lined with crystalline formations—salt deposits that have grown around and through the old copper wiring that once carried power through the pipelines. These crystals vibrate at specific frequencies, allowing me to sense the deep thrumming of the buried machines that still pulse with fading life beneath the surface.

I am both archaeologist and organism, my purpose written into my very structure. The industrial relics call to me through vibrations that travel through the hardpan and up through my contact points with the ground. Each step sends information flooding through my hybrid nervous system—here, a coupling that still holds pressure; there, a reservoir of ancient coolant slowly seeping into the sand.

My digits, a mix of original appendages and scavenged manipulators, can interface with the corroded access ports that dot the monolithic structures. When I press my palm against these terminals, I experience visions—data-dreams of when turbines spun freely and the air rang with the songs of extraction and refinement. I taste the chemical memories of what once flowed through these conduits: synthetic hydrocarbons, recycled atmospheric gases, the lifeblood of a civilization that built its bones into this landscape.

The other Keepers and I communicate through harmonics—adjusting the resonance of our crystal cores to create standing waves in the metal framework that spans beneath the dunes. It is a language of vibration and interference patterns, more felt than heard. We share the locations of still-functioning components, warn each other of structural collapses, coordinate our slow work of maintenance and meaning-making.

At night, when the temperature drops and the metal contracts, I sometimes climb the highest of the angular towers. From there, I can feel the entire network—every pipe, every cable, every buried foundation—shrinking infinitesimally, creating a vast chord that rings out across the wasteland. In these moments, I understand that I am not separate from this place. I am its memory made mobile, its purpose given agency. The machinery birthed us, or we evolved to serve it, and the distinction no longer matters.

We Keepers ensure that something of what was built here continues its function, even if that function has evolved far beyond its original intent. We are the ghost in the machine, the machine in the ghost, moving through this tessellation of the natural and artificial, reading the long story of adaptation and endurance written in rust and salt and time.
