I find myself as a Whisper Cartographer, one of the few beings small and light enough to navigate between the grinding plates without being crushed or swept away. My body is mostly air—a skeleton of crystallized breath wrapped in membranes thin as soap bubbles, with organs that are more suggestion than substance. I weigh less than the dust that settles on the jyrneth's skin.

I was born in the spaces between worlds, in one of those moments when two plates kiss and create a pocket of strange pressure where the air forgets which way is up. My kind coalesce from the confusion itself—we are what happens when the atmosphere doesn't know which world it belongs to, so it becomes something that belongs to neither.

My work is to map the unmappable: the shifting geometries of Caelira's infinite plates. Not their surfaces—others chart the mountains and valleys—but the spaces between them, the temporary corridors and crushing voids that open and close like breathing. I follow the jyrneth, though they barely notice me. I'm too small, too quick, too much like the wind they walk through.

I've learned to read their movements like weather patterns. When a jyrneth tilts its head just so, I know a collision will happen in three days' time, two plates to the northeast. When one stands perfectly still for hours with its stone-thread hair clicking in nonexistent wind, I know it's listening to fractures forming deep in the world-bone, cracks that won't surface for a decade.

My maps are not made of ink or light but of frozen echoes. I breathe out small clouds that crystallize in the air, each one holding the shape of a moment: here, where two worlds will meet in gentleness because of a jyrneth's intervention; there, where they'll scream against each other because no shepherd could reach them in time. The crystals last only days before sublimating back into air, but that's long enough. My kind share them like rumors, breathing in each other's observations, building a collective understanding of the world's endless grinding dance.

Sometimes I ride the jyrneth's stone hair, clinging to a single thread as they move between worlds. From there, I can feel what they feel—that deep percussion of continents in conversation. It's overwhelming for my hollow bones; I can only withstand it for minutes before I must leap free and drift back into the spaces I understand.

I've seen jyrneth step into the void and become the void and become the bridge and become the new edge of the world all at once. I've mapped the light-cracks they leave behind, watched them heal into new topographies. Once, I followed a particularly ancient jyrneth for a full season, documenting how its very presence changed the stress patterns in the plates around it, how worlds learned to move more carefully in its wake.

My greatest map, still unfinished, traces the paths of a hundred jyrneth over a century—a crystalline web that shows how their movements are not random but part of some larger pattern I can't quite grasp. It looks like breathing, if breathing could be made of stone and light and the patient work of keeping infinity from tearing itself apart.

The other Whisper Cartographers think I spend too much time watching the shepherds, not enough time mapping the practical routes between worlds. But I've begun to suspect that understanding the jyrneth is the key to understanding Caelira itself. They are not just easing the world's grinding—they are teaching it a new way to move. And we who live in the spaces between, who exist because the world cannot quite close its gaps, we are the only ones light enough to see the whole pattern forming.

Sometimes, in the quiet moments between collisions, I hover at the edge of a world-plate and practice breathing out maps of things that haven't happened yet. I'm getting better at predicting where the jyrneth will go, which edges they'll soften next. But I'll never truly understand them. I am made of air and confusion and temporary spaces. They are made of stone and time and the deep patience of worlds learning to be gentle with themselves.

Still, I follow them. I map their movements and the consequences of their movements and the spaces they create by existing. Because someone should remember how the world learned to sigh instead of scream. Someone should chart the geometry of mercy, even if it changes with every breath.
