In this austere realm of stone and sky, I am a water-finder.

My name is Thara, though few speak it aloud. They know me instead by the steady *tap-tap-tap* of my divining rod against the rocky ground, by the particular way I tilt my head when listening to the stone's whispers. In a city built on scarcity, where every shallow pool is precious, I am both necessity and mystery.

I was born with the gift—or curse, depending on the day. Where others see only weathered rock and windswept gravel, I feel the hidden veins of moisture threading through the earth like a second nervous system. The sensation begins as a cold prickle at the base of my skull, then spreads down my spine until my whole body becomes a compass needle quivering toward water. My grandmother had it too, they say, though she died before teaching me to control it. I learned through trial and error, through seasons of false hopes and genuine discoveries.

My dwelling sits at the edge of the city's stone labyrinth, half-carved into a natural outcropping that shields it from the worst of the wind. Unlike the dense-packed structures of the inner districts, my home sprawls horizontally, following the contours of a series of connected caves I've slowly expanded over the years. The entrance is marked by a curtain of small bones—offerings from grateful families whose cisterns I've filled. Inside, the walls are mapped with my findings: careful etchings that chart every water source I've discovered, annotated with the season, the depth, the quality. Some markings are so old they've been worn smooth by my fingers tracing them in the dark.

The main chamber serves as both workshop and living space. Stone shelves hold my collection of divining tools—not just the traditional forked branches, but rods of different metals, each attuned to different types of water. Iron for the deep aquifers. Copper for the surface pools. A peculiar greenish alloy, traded from a traveler years ago, that trembles only in the presence of the purest mountain springs. My sleeping alcove is lined with the thick pelts of ice-bears, their hollow hairs trapping warmth through the coldest nights. A small fire pit, vented through a natural chimney, provides light and heat, though I use it sparingly. Fuel is precious here.

The Krynn respect me, but they don't entirely trust me. How can they, when my gift defies their careful understanding of the world? I don't fit neatly into their structure of artisans and herders, hunters and healers. I am something older, something that reminds them of the time before the city, when their ancestors wandered these heights following dreams and instincts they could barely name. The elders include me in their councils when drought threatens, but I see how they grip their carved stone amulets when I speak. The children follow me at a distance, whispering stories about the woman who can hear water singing in the stone.

My days begin before dawn, when the cold is sharp enough to cut and the stars still prick the pale sky. This is when the water speaks clearest, before the sun's heat creates false echoes in the rock. I walk the city's perimeter first, checking the known sources, feeling for any changes in their flow. The sensation is like running my hands over a familiar tapestry and finding new threads woven into the pattern overnight. Sometimes I discover that an old source has shifted, following some invisible geological whim. Sometimes I find nothing but the memory of water, a ghost-trace that makes my gift ache like a phantom limb.

When the city stirs to life, I often have visitors. A young couple planning to build their home, needing to know if the site they've chosen has water near enough to sustain them. A herder whose usual watering hole has mysteriously dried. The healers, always the healers, seeking the particular mineral-rich springs that enhance their remedies. I serve them honey-sweetened tea in rough stone cups while I listen to their needs, feeling the pull of water even as we speak—in their bodies, in the air between us, in the deep bones of the mountain itself.

But it's the deep searches that truly define me. When the shallow pools fail, when the seasonal rains don't come, the city turns to me with a desperation that's both humbling and terrifying. These searches take me far from the safety of stone walls, out into the raw landscape where the wind has teeth and the cold burrows into your marrow. I travel light—a pack with basic supplies, my most sensitive divining rods, a bone knife, and always, always, a small sealed jar containing water from the city's founding spring. This last is both practical and ritual. If I die out there, whoever finds me will know where I came from. And sometimes, when I'm lost in the maze of my own gift, that small taste of home-water guides me back to myself.

The deepest water I ever found required three days of walking toward the mountain's heart, following a pull so strong it made my bones ache. I discovered it in a collapse cave, a vast underground lake so still it might have been solid obsidian. The water was ancient, older than the city, older than the Krynn themselves. It tasted of minerals I had no names for, and when I brought back samples, the healers declared it too potent for regular use—water that could cure or kill, depending on the dose. We sealed the cave's entrance, marking it with warning stones. But sometimes, on the coldest nights, I still feel that water calling to me, patient and eternal in its darkness.

The fauna know me differently than the Krynn do. The small, quick-furred creatures that scatter at human approach will sometimes pause near me, their whiskers twitching as if they sense the water I carry in my awareness. The great shaggy herbivores, normally stoic and distant, have been known to follow me to new water sources, their massive heads lowered in what feels like acknowledgment. Even the predators seem to recognize something in me—not kinship exactly, but perhaps professional respect from one hunter to another. Once, tracking water through a maze of wind-carved stone, I came face to face with a white-furred cat, its pale eyes reflecting the endless sky. We regarded each other for a long moment before it padded away, leaving me shaking with more than cold.

My gift comes with costs the Krynn don't see. The constant awareness of water is like hearing every conversation in a crowded market simultaneously. On days when the barometric pressure shifts, when storms build beyond the horizon, the sensation intensifies until I can barely separate my own body's water from the world's. I've learned to build walls in my mind, to create quiet spaces where the water-sense can't reach, but it's exhausting work. Sometimes I dream I'm dissolving, my consciousness spreading out through every hidden spring and underground stream until there's no Thara left, only the vast, patient cycle of water through stone.

The loneliness is perhaps the hardest part. How do you share a life with someone when you're always partially elsewhere, always listening to signals they can't perceive? I had a partner once, a stone-carver named Kern who tried to understand. He would hold me through the nights when the gift overwhelmed me, would learn to read the signs of an approaching water-trance. But eventually, the distance became too much. He needed a woman who was fully present, not one who disappeared into the mountain's secret rhythms. I don't blame him. I've learned to find companionship in my work, in the grateful faces of those whose cisterns I've filled, in the quiet understanding of the old women who remember my grandmother.

There are others like me, scattered through the settlements that dot these heights. We know each other by rumor and reputation, by the particular signatures we leave in our workings. There's a man two days north who can find only hot springs, his gift attuned to the deep thermal veins. A child in the eastern settlement who discovered her abilities last season, her parents terrified by the way she convulses when standing over hidden water. We don't gather—our gifts are too individual, too tied to our specific places. But sometimes I leave markers for them, small cairns with water-smoothed stones at the top, positioned where only someone like us would think to look. It's a way of saying: you're not alone in this.

As I age, the gift changes. Where once it was a roar, now it's a deep, steady pulse. I can feel layers in the water that I couldn't perceive in youth—not just its location, but its history, its mineral memory, the story of its journey through the stone. I'm training an apprentice now, a serious girl named Iska whose gift manifested during the last drought. She has the raw talent but lacks control, and I see my younger self in her wild-eyed panic when the sensation grows too strong. I teach her the meditation techniques I've developed, the visualization exercises that help separate self from water. Whether she'll carry on my specific methods or develop her own remains to be seen. The gift shapes itself to each bearer.

In the evenings, when the day's work is done and the cold stars emerge in the pale sky, I sit at my cave's entrance and practice my craft for pleasure rather than necessity. I let my awareness spread out gently, following the water's paths through the stone, feeling the slow, patient way it shapes the world. Sometimes I sense new channels forming, places where, in a generation or two, springs will emerge. I map these too, though I may not live to see them flow. It's my gift to the future Krynn, to the children not yet born who will need water in places we haven't imagined.

The city grows slowly, stone by stone, generation by generation. My maps grow with it, an invisible infrastructure underlying the visible one. When I die, they'll say the water-finder has returned to the source, and perhaps they'll be right. But until then, I wake each morning to the stone's whispers, to the pull of hidden streams, to the endless, essential work of finding what others cannot see. In this hard, beautiful landscape, I am the bridge between the Krynn and the secret life that flows beneath their feet. It's a strange calling, lonely and necessary and mine.

Tonight, as the wind carries the scent of approaching snow, I feel a new pull from the deep places. Tomorrow I'll follow it, rod in hand, gift singing in my bones. The water is calling, and I must answer. I always do.
