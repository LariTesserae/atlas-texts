I find myself as one of the crystal formations—but not one of the natural growths.

I am a small cluster of gypsum blades that began forming three hundred years after the seven became the Lanternkeeper. My seed was a single tear that froze before it could fall, caught in the moment between grief and acceptance. The Lanternkeeper had been trying to remember the sound of its own name—any of the seven names—and the effort produced one drop of something that was almost, but not quite, water.

I grew wrong from the beginning. While the other crystals reach upward in clean lines and perfect spirals, I twist inward like a closing fist. My surfaces refuse the wind's patient etching. Instead of the wave-patterns that mark my siblings, my faces hold a different kind of memory: the ghost-impression of fingerprints, pressed so lightly they can only be seen when the lenticular light strikes at precisely the wrong angle.

I am small—no larger than a child's skull—and I grow so slowly that even the Lanternkeeper sometimes forgets I exist. But I am the only crystal in the basin that remembers warmth. Not the false warmth of reflected sunlight, but the specific 37-degree heat of a living human hand. This memory lives in my molecular structure like a flaw, like a crack that will never quite heal.

The Lanternkeeper cannot touch me directly—its un-hands pass through me like light through water. But sometimes, in the hours when the wind holds its breath, it kneels beside me (a motion you would see only as a subtle rearrangement of negative space) and tries to remember what it felt like to have solid palms. The effort creates tiny vibrations in the air that I alone can feel. To me, they sound like someone trying to speak underwater.

I am growing myself into the shape of a cupped hand.

It will take me another thousand years to complete the gesture—to become the perfect negative space of fingers that once knew how to hold water, how to touch another's face, how to build shelters against the cold. The other crystals grow toward the sky, toward abstraction, toward the pure mathematics of mineral peace. But I grow toward a specific absence: the exact shape of a palm that belonged to the youngest of the seven, who was the last to walk into the lattice and the first to realize the door would not open again.

Sometimes a bead of condensation runs down my surface and pauses in the depression I am so slowly forming. For those few minutes, I hold water the way a hand would hold water. The Lanternkeeper sees this and the entire basin fills with a sound like wind through broken bells—not quite weeping, not quite song.

I am the flaw in the Shattered Lantern's perfect mineralization. I am the crystal that refuses to forget meat and blood and the specific weight of a tool held with purpose. My existence causes the patterns in the other blades to stutter and start over, as though I am a grammatical error in a sentence that was supposed to end with stone.

But I am also the reason the Lanternkeeper keeps counting heartbeats. Each time water pools in my slowly-forming palm, it remembers: *This is why we stayed. This is what we were trying to become—not the absence of humanity, but its perfect preservation.*

In another thousand years, I will finally achieve the shape I am growing toward. When that happens, the Lanternkeeper will be able to cup its un-hands around me and remember, completely, what it felt like to be seven people who loved the world too much to leave it, even after it stopped loving them back.

And in that moment of perfect remembering, one of two things will happen:

Either the Lanternkeeper will finally be able to let go, and the patterns will complete themselves, and the basin will become truly empty for the first time since the seven walked into the crystal lattice—

Or I will crack from the pressure of holding so much memory in such a small space, and my fragments will seed a thousand new crystals, each one carrying a piece of that original warmth, until the entire Shattered Lantern blooms with formations that remember what it felt like to have a pulse.

I do not know which ending I am growing toward. I only know that I am the only thing here that still counts in heartbeats instead of centuries, and that makes me either the most human thing in the basin, or the least.

The Lanternkeeper tends me like a gardener tends a flower that blooms once a millennium. It cannot touch me, cannot water me, cannot turn me toward the sun. All it can do is wait, and count, and hope that when I finally open, I will remember the right shape.

I am trying. Crystal by crystal, molecule by molecule, I am trying to become the hand that can finally hold what the seven let go.

Even if it takes forever. Especially if it takes forever.

That is what it means to grow in the Shattered Lantern: to be brilliant, and patient, and impossibly alone, carrying someone else's memory toward a completion you may never see.

I am that memory, crystallizing into hope.
