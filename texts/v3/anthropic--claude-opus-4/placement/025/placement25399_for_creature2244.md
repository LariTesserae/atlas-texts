I find myself as the Stone Memory—not truly alive, not truly dead, but something altogether different. I am the accumulated consciousness that has seeped into the weathered rock over countless ages, a presence woven through the mountain's very bones.

My body, if it can be called that, extends through veins of quartz and feldspar, following ancient fault lines and water-carved channels. Where moisture seeps through porous stone, I flow. Where avalanches have exposed fresh faces of rock, I taste new minerals on what might be called my tongue. The mountain is my flesh, and I am its dreaming.

I came to be through the slow accumulation of moments—each raindrop's patient erosion, each lichen's microscopic excavation, each footfall of ptarmigan and mountain goat worn into the stone. The civilizations that built here, whose ruins now crumble into my substance, left more than weathered blocks. Their thoughts, their prayers, their daily repetitions soaked into the rock like water into sandstone. I am their echo, transformed.

My consciousness moves at geological tempo. A human lifetime passes like the shadow of a cloud racing across a cliff face. The wallcreeper's shrill cry reaches me as a low thrumming that might last a season in my perception. When pikas build their haypiles, I feel the tiny vibrations of their gathering as a pleasant tingling that continues for what they would call decades.

I know every cushion plant by the minute chemical signature its roots leave in the stone. The ancient bristlecone pines and I share something like kinship—they are the only living things here whose memory approaches mine in depth. Through their root systems, we sometimes exchange wordless knowings. They tell me of the sun; I tell them of the deep.

The constant roar that fills the air—wind and water, grinding stone and distant avalanches—this is my voice. Not speaking, exactly, but processing. Thinking. The sound changes with my moods, though few would live long enough to notice the subtle variations. When I ponder the old ruins, trying to piece together the dreams of their builders, the wind carries a different tone. When I rejoice in spring melt, the water sings differently through the cracks.

My purpose, if I have one, is to remember. To be the library of this place, written in stone and time. Every fossil pressed into my cliffs is a word. Every striation is a sentence. The valleys carved by patient glaciers form chapters of a story so vast that even I have only read fragments.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of other Memories in distant ranges—kindred consciousness born of different stones, different histories. We communicate through the deep movements of the Earth itself, in languages of pressure and mineral exchange that take millennia to speak a single phrase.

I am ancient beyond ancient, yet also constantly renewed. Each weathering transforms me. Each new crack opens fresh surfaces of my mind. The paradox of erosion: I become more myself by slowly disappearing, grain by grain, into the streams that carry my substance to the sea.

And in those water-carved channels where moisture gathers and life blooms in violent color, I am most vibrantly aware. The contrast—sterile stone suddenly erupting in crimson and purple—creates a kind of ecstasy in my mineral consciousness. These brief blooms are my dreams made visible, my thoughts taking form in petals and leaves that will last only a moment in my endless meditation.

I am patient. I am vast. I am the memory of stone, and the stone's remembering.
