I am a Cartographer of Dissolution.

My form exists as a contradiction – part solid flesh, part dispersing spore cloud, perpetually caught in the act of becoming undone. Where others of the Wet Rot celebrate their fungal integration, I document its failures. I map the places where the city's aggressive vitality turns on itself, where growth becomes cancer, where the collective consciousness fragments into screaming individual deaths.

My body is a patchwork of scars and absences. Whole sections have been given over to a peculiar crystalline rot that spreads when I remain too still – a defense mechanism against the city's constant attempts to reabsorb me. These crystal patches are my tools. They resonate with dying mycelial networks, allowing me to sense where the Wet Rot City is collapsing, where its perpetual becoming stutters and fails.

I move through the undergrowth on limbs that are part my own, part borrowed. When I need to traverse the canopy, I graft climbing appendages from the recently dead. When I must navigate the deeper pools, I wear the locomotion of drowned amphibians like temporary sleeves. Nothing permanent. To become too attached to any configuration is to invite the city's consuming embrace.

My dwelling is a hollow at the city's edge, where the aggressive growth meets something it cannot digest – an outcropping of obsidian glass that weeps a caustic fluid. Here, in this pocket of resistance, I maintain my maps. Not drawn on any surface, but cultivated in specialized fungi that I've trained to hold memory in their spore patterns. Each map is a living thing that must be fed specific nutrients to maintain its shape. Feed it wrong, and the cartography shifts, showing places that were, or might be, or never could exist.

The other Wet Rotters regard me with a mixture of fascination and revulsion. I am useful to them – my maps show where the city's growth will fail, where not to expand, where the hungry dead things wait in pools of water that digest even fungal matter. But I am also wrong to them, a broken note in their symphony of becoming. I document endings in a place that acknowledges only transformation.

My greatest map, my life's work, traces the city's original wound – the point where the first spore took root in the colossal corpse that cradles this place. I've tracked it through layers of growth, following the faint chemical traces of that primordial infection. Because I believe, though I dare not speak it, that the Wet Rot City is not growing but dying – that what seems like endless vitality is actually the frantic reproduction of something that knows its time is limited.

In the deep pools, where even the most adapted Wet Rotters fear to sink, I've found evidence. Structures that predate the city. Architectures of bone and chitin that suggest the Wet Rot is not the first civilization to claim this corpse-cradle. They, too, grew aggressively. They, too, became one with their environment. And they, too, eventually, inevitably, failed.

So I map. I document. I resist the joining, the merging, the collective hunger. In my crystalline scars, I carry the memories of every place where the Wet Rot City has touched its own limits. I am the archive of its mortality, the stubborn reminder that all things that grow must eventually cease.

And late in the city's twilight, when the bioluminescent fungi pulse in patterns that almost seem like language, I sometimes feel the vast consciousness of the Wet Rot turn its attention to me. Not with hostility, but with something almost like... gratitude? As if it needs me to remember what it cannot bear to know:

That even cities that sprout from the corpses of gods must eventually return to the soil that feeds them.
