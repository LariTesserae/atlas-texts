I find myself here as the Cartographer of Discontinuities.

I am not lost, though I cannot tell you where I am. My instruments—compass, sextant, theodolite—have all developed peculiar habits. The compass needle doesn't point north; it shivers between possibilities, tracing small circles like a dog settling to sleep. The sextant shows me stars that aren't there, or perhaps shows me the absence of stars that should be. The theodolite measures angles that don't quite close, leaving small gaps in reality where the mathematics give up and shrug.

My body has adapted. I've grown thin in some dimensions, stretched in others. My fingers are longer now, better for feeling the edges where the map tears, where one kind of space becomes another. My eyes have learned to unfocus at will—the only way to see the beings clearly is to look at nothing in particular and let them emerge in the periphery like photographic prints developing in solution.

I came here with purpose, though that purpose has worn smooth like everything else. I was mapping the boundaries, the places where the world forgets how to be itself. But the map I'm making isn't one you could follow. It's written in the accumulation of salt in the creases of my palms, in the particular way my bootprints fade, in the small corrections I make to my breathing to accommodate the thinness.

My tent is translucent now. Not transparent—that would be too definite. Translucent in the way that jellyfish are, suggesting interior spaces without confirming them. Inside, my maps cover every surface: attempts to chart what happens to distance here, how far becomes a quality rather than a measurement. Some maps are drawn in pencil, some in charcoal, some in my own blood mixed with the local salt. The blood-salt maps are the most accurate. They show not where things are, but where they aren't quite.

The beings and I have developed an understanding. They cluster around my tent at what might be night (the light never quite commits to darkness here, just becomes more uncertain). They're learning geography from me—not the geography of places, but the geography of gaps, of the spaces between one state and another. In exchange, they're teaching me patience. Not human patience, which is just endurance dressed in hope, but mineral patience. The patience of things that know they'll outlast meaning itself.

I've started a new map. This one isn't of the terrain but of the moments when the terrain forgets what it is. I mark the spots where my footprints fill with something that isn't quite salt, where the air develops pockets of almost-warmth, where sounds finish before they properly begin. The beings help, in their way. They show me where to look by not being there.

Sometimes I find pieces of other expeditions. A boot with the sole worn through in impossible patterns. A notebook where the words have crystallized, become part of the mineral substrate. A camera whose film, when developed (I've learned to use my own blood as developer), shows only the spaces between things—doorways with no doors, the gap between earth and sky, the pause between heartbeats rendered in silver halide.

I'm changing. My maps are changing. The distinction between recording and becoming has grown thin as the air. But this is what I came for, I think—to chart the places where the world admits its uncertainty, where the edges fray and meaning becomes optional.

The beings approve, in their silent way. They've started incorporating my maps into their waiting, using them as guides to nowhere in particular. And I've started incorporating their patience into my cartography, learning to map not just space but the time it takes for space to forget itself.

We're creating something together, the beings and I. Not a map you could use to get anywhere, but a map that shows what happens when anywhere becomes a question rather than a destination. A map of the broken edges, where you could lose yourself so thoroughly that even loss becomes uncertain.

I continue my work. The tent glows softly in the not-quite-darkness. The beings drift closer, curious about my next annotation. And somewhere, in a world that still believes in destinations, someone will find my maps and wonder what kind of journey ends in this perfect, mineral silence.
