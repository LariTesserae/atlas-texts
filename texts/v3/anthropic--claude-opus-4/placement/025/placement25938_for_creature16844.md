I find myself as **The Cartographer of Uncharted Departures**, dwelling in a studio apartment that exists only during the moments when other buildings blink.

My home occupies the negative space between architectural certainties—a dwelling made entirely of the pauses between the city's exhalations. To find it, you must walk past where you meant to go, then continue until the feeling of being lost crystallizes into a doorknob. The entrance appears only to those who have given up looking for it.

Inside, the walls are lined with maps of places that will never be visited. Not because they don't exist—existence is negotiable here—but because the very act of mapping them ensures they will always remain one step beyond arrival. I chart the geographies of almosts: the vacation you cancelled, the hometown you never returned to, the country your grandparents described but could never quite locate on any globe.

My tools are peculiar:
- A compass that points toward regret
- Surveying equipment that measures the distance between what was said and what was meant  
- Ink distilled from the shadows of birds who forgot how to land

Each morning, I receive coordinates through the mail slot—not numbers, but fragments of sensation. Today's assignment: *the weight of realizing you're in the wrong train station, temperature of a forgotten birthday, angle of light through a window you'll never see again*. I must triangulate these into cartographic certainty, creating navigational aids for people who are professionally lost.

My body has adapted to this work. My fingerprints have evolved into topographical maps that change with barometric pressure. When I dream, I dream in longitude. My left eye sees only places as they were; my right, only as they might have been. The parallax between them is where I plot my charts.

The city pays me in expired possibilities—currency that can only be spent on things you've already decided against. I subsist on a diet of meals I meant to cook, conversations I should have had, and water drawn from clouds that passed overhead while I wasn't paying attention.

Sometimes clients visit, though never by appointment. They arrive like weather—suddenly and with their own logic. Last week, a woman made entirely of departure announcements asked me to map the route between who she was and who she'd forgotten to become. I spent three days charting the topology of her abandoned selves, marking rest stops at each moment she'd chosen safety over transformation.

My maps cannot be copied. Each one exists only while being read, like a song that forgets itself between verses. They're stored in filing cabinets that open onto different contents depending on the humidity of longing in the room. The city's historians claim my archive is either the most complete or most empty collection in existence. Both are correct.

At night, I can hear the city rearranging itself according to maps I haven't drawn yet. Streets relocate to match the homesickness of new arrivals. Districts phase-shift to accommodate nostalgia for places that were scheduled for existence but cancelled at the last moment. My work, I've come to understand, isn't documentation—it's prevention. Every place I map is immunized against arrival, preserved in the amber of perpetual approach.

My neighbors believe I'm either essential to the city's function or entirely parasitic upon it. The truth, like my address, depends on the angle of observation. I am the city's memory of everywhere else, the keeper of its unfinished journeys, the professional stranger who ensures there will always be somewhere left to go by making sure no one can quite get there.

My lease is written in water on water. It renews itself every time someone almost finds their way home.
