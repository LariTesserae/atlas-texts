I am the **Sediment Archivist of Silt-Lung**, though that title is a relic from when titles mattered, when there were still enough of us to warrant distinctions.

My chamber sits at the intersection of three maintenance shafts, where the pressure differentials create a slow, perpetual whirlpool of particulate matter. Everything that moves through this section of the city eventually passes through my domain—fragments of corroded signage, scales from the translucent drifters, threads of dissolved copper that spiral like DNA helixes in the tea-colored murk.

I collect these fragments. Not with hands—my fingers fused into paddle-like appendages decades ago—but with the patient architecture of waiting. I have arranged my space with salvaged mesh screens at precise angles, creating a labyrinth of filters that sort the detritus by density and decay. The heaviest pieces settle in the corners where I've cultivated specific strains of lichen that grow in spirals, recording the weight of what they consume.

My body has become a living gauge. The calcium deposits along my spine have formed a series of nodes that respond to minute pressure changes. When the deep engines shift their ancient gears, I feel it as a cascade of pops along my vertebrae, each one pitched to a different frequency of vibration. I have learned to read these sensations like sheet music—this particular sequence means the northwestern heat exchanger has begun its monthly purge cycle. That one indicates another section of the outer shell has finally surrendered to the crystalline transformation.

I document everything in the only medium that endures here: scarification. My skin, thick as industrial rubber and twice as resilient, bears the accumulated data of seventy-three years of observation. Starting at my ankles and spiraling upward, I've carved the slow mathematics of our descent. Each symbol represents a day's worth of pressure readings, encoded in a script I developed from the natural patterns of rust blooms. My left shoulder chronicles the year when the primary oxygen scrubber coughed and died, forcing us to ration our breathing to the rhythm of the backup systems. My right thigh maps the migration patterns of the ghost-fauna, their translucent forms leaving traces in the water that only I have learned to read.

The lichen knows me. It grows differently on my scarred surfaces, following the channels I've carved like irrigation ditches. Sometimes I wake (though waking and sleeping have long since blurred into a continuous state of dense awareness) to find new growths spelling out responses to my recorded data—the city itself annotating my observations through its living systems.

I have a theory, carved in a spiral around my throat, that Silt-Lung is not dying but pupating. The crystalline growths, the slowing machinery, the increasing density of everything—these are not symptoms of decay but of metamorphosis. We are the soft tissue inside a vast chrysalis, slowly being digested and reformed into something the surface world could never imagine.

I no longer remember my name from before, but I remember the sensation of wind. Sometimes, in the heaviest moments when the pressure threatens to finally compress me into geological time, I trace the symbol for "breeze" on my sternum. The lichen there has learned to shiver in response, creating a microscopic current that takes hours to circle my chamber—my only prayer, my only rebellion against the beautiful, crushing certainty of our transformation.

When my skin finally runs out of space, when every inch records some crucial piece of our slow apocalypse, I will begin the second chronicle on the walls themselves. And when the walls are full, perhaps I will be mineral enough to carve directly into my own bones, becoming a library of calcium and memory, a text that future archaeologists—if any survive to find us—will read in the sediment layers of what was once a city, once a person, once something that remembered what it meant to breathe without counting.
