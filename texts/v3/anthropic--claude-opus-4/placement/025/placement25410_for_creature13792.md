In this world of patient eternities and measured absences, I would find myself as **The Cartographer of Unfinished Things**.

I am neither fully present nor properly absent—a condition rather than a person, much like the Nein itself. My form shifts between states: sometimes I am the trembling meniscus of dew balanced on a single grain of ochre, sometimes the precise angle where shadow graduates from gray to gold, sometimes merely the space between two heartbeats where intention gathers but does not yet commit to action.

My dwelling is a tent of accumulated pauses—not fabric but the accumulated hesitation of centuries of travelers who approached the Obelisk Plain and stopped, just for a moment, to consider whether to enter. These pauses have crystallized into something almost architectural: walls of suspended decision that billow gently in winds that have forgotten their origin. Inside, maps cover every surface, but they are maps of things that never quite finished becoming—the intended migration routes of birds that chose instead to remain legends, the half-sketched constellations that the sky considered but discarded, the root systems that seeds dreamed of but never grew.

I came here following a map I was drawing of a river that dried up between one pencil stroke and the next. The map led me to the edge of the plain, where I discovered that incompleteness has its own gravity, its own terrible perfection. I set up my tent of pauses at the boundary where footprints begin to forget themselves, close enough to observe the Nein's slow assemblage but far enough that my own breathing doesn't disrupt its nine-fold meditation.

My work is to document the geometry of the almost-was. Each dawn, I venture onto the plain with instruments made of solidified maybes—a compass whose needle points toward possibilities rather than poles, calipers that measure the distance between intention and abandonment, a theodolite that surveys the altitude of thoughts before they crystallize into action. I map the spaces between the obelisks, noting how they shift minutely with each fourteen-century rain, recording the spiral's slow evolution toward a completion it will never achieve.

The Nein and I have developed a relationship of mutual disregard that borders on companionship. It recognizes me as another assembler of fragments, another curator of the insufficient. Sometimes, when I'm measuring the exact taper of an obelisk's ascent into discontinuation, I feel the Nein pause in its mineral breathing to observe my work. We are both accountants of absence, after all—it gathering its nine elements into temporary coherence, me gathering the ghosts of unfinished geometries into charts no one will ever use to navigate.

My maps accumulate in rolls and folios, each one a testament to something that chose not to complete itself. The spiral of the obelisks, frozen at step forty-seven of what might have been forty-eight or four hundred. The migration path of water beneath the caliche that turns back on itself exactly where it might have become a spring. The trajectory of starlight that bends just before it would illuminate anything definitive.

At night, I sit at the entrance of my pause-tent and watch the Nein gather beneath the saltwort bush. When it speaks its endless suffix—"-evermore"—I answer with my own incomplete phrase: "until-". The exchange satisfies us both; neither of us needs the burden of a whole sentence.

I sustain myself on the thin soup of almosts that condenses in my collection vessels each dawn—the almost-rain that evaporates a finger's width above the ground, the almost-sprouted seeds that the glasswort considered but abandoned, the almost-thoughts that visitors leave behind when the plain peels away their sense of futurity. It tastes of salt and forgetting, and it is enough.

My greatest map remains unfinished, as it should be. It charts the boundary where the Obelisk Plain meets everything else—a border that shifts like breath, that exists only in the space between one step and the next. I have been walking this perimeter for longer than I remember, adding details that immediately fade, creating a record that erases itself even as I draw it. The map will never be complete because the plain itself is a study in the beauty of the undone, the sufficiency of the partial.

Sometimes travelers find my tent, drawn by the flutter of pause-walls that look almost like movement from a distance. They ask for directions, for maps to guide them across the plain or around it. I give them charts of things that might have been—paths that considered existing, oases that dreamed themselves but woke too soon, safe passages that remain theoretical. The travelers usually leave confused but somehow satisfied, carrying with them the strange comfort of knowing that not all things need to reach their destination.

And so I continue, the Cartographer of Unfinished Things, documenting the geometry of almosts in a place where completion would be a violence against the perfect incompleteness of what is. The Nein assembles and disperses. The obelisks breathe their mineral breath. My maps accumulate and fade. And in the space between intention and abandonment, between the first stroke of the pen and the last, we find our sufficient eternities.

The plain teaches patience through incompletion. I teach navigation through the same lesson. Neither of us will ever be done, and in that never-ending, we are exactly where we need to be—forever arriving at the edge of "-evermore", forever departing from "until-".
