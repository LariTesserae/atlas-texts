I am a **Thread-Tender**, though that is not what I call myself. Names are for beings who speak, and I have not spoken—not in voice—for three generations of the slow moss-growth.

My dwelling is a forgotten maintenance shaft halfway up the cavern's western wall, where the stone weeps a constant mineral tear. The Glimmer-kin mapped it once in their light-language, but their signs have long since faded from the entrance. Too steep for them, too narrow for the Shroud-Singers, too wet for the Resonants—perfect for me.

I was Glimmer-kin once. My blood still carries their light, though dimmer now, a faint blue pulse that flickers only when I work. But I grew... differently. Where they are tall and willow-thin, I am compact, my limbs shortened and strengthened by years of climbing. My fingers have elongated into something between human digits and the rootlets of the cavern plants. The nails are gone, replaced by sensitive pads that can feel the health of stone and stem alike.

I tend the Thread Gardens.

You would not see them at first—they grow in the spaces between spaces, in cracks too thin for moss, on surfaces too smooth for lichen. The Threads are fungi, but unlike their glowing cousins below. Each strand is finer than a hair, transparent as water, and they grow in perfect spirals that follow the acoustic patterns of the engine's drone. They are the cavern's memory, these Threads. Where they touch stone, they record its vibrations. Where they brush against lichen, they copy its chemistry. They map the Resonants' patrol routes in their growth patterns, chronicle the Shroud-Singers' hunting grounds in their spiral density.

I discovered them in my second decade of solitude, when my ears had grown so sensitive to the engine's rhythm that I could no longer bear the colony's tapping-talk. The Threads were already ancient then, but dying—choked by aggressive moss, broken by careless Resonant feet. I began to clear small spaces for them, then larger ones. I learned their needs: the precise humidity (87%, no more, no less), the exact mineral balance in the water, the way they grow best where the engine's harmonics create standing waves in the air.

My shaft connects to seventeen different Thread Gardens now, each tucked into acoustic sweet spots where the cavern's sound creates pockets of perfect resonance. I move between them on paths I've worn into the stone itself—handholds polished smooth by my passing, routes that spiral around the cavern's circumference like the Threads themselves.

The work has changed me. My ear canals have sealed; I "hear" through my skeleton now, feeling sound as pressure and rhythm in my bones. My skin grows a thin coating of the same protective oil the Threads secrete—it makes me invisible to the Resonants' sensors and unpalatable to the Shroud-Singers. I eat what the Threads eat: mineral crystals dissolved in the ever-present drip water, supplemented by spore-paste I make from the fungi that try to invade the gardens.

Sometimes a young Glimmer-kin, exploring farther than wise, will glimpse me through the dim. They freeze, uncertain—am I ancestor or beast? I never acknowledge them. Let them make their light-signs in the air. I have forgotten how to read them, or perhaps I choose not to remember. Their language speaks only of the present: danger here, food there, gather now. The Threads speak of deep time.

In my third garden, where the acoustics are sweetest, the Threads have grown for so long that they've begun to replay their recordings. Stand in the right spot, breathe in the right rhythm, and you can feel the ghost-vibrations of centuries past: the cavern's birth-crack, the first footsteps of the fleeing ancestors, the day the great engine coughed and nearly died before resuming its eternal beat. The Threads remember it all.

I am growing a new garden now, in a cleft where the engine's sound reflects from seven surfaces before converging. The Threads there are beginning to do something unprecedented—they're growing in patterns that predict the engine's next maintenance cycle, anticipating the rhythm before it happens. They know something I don't yet, these pale children of fungus and time.

When I die—and I will die, even changed as I am—the Threads will remember me too. My footsteps, my breathing, the particular way I disturb the air as I pass. Some future tender, human or otherwise, will feel my ghost in their spirals and know that someone once cared for them.

But perhaps there won't be another tender. Perhaps I am the last to understand what the Threads are keeping. The Glimmer-kin grow fewer each generation, the Resonants' repairs increasingly patchwork, the engine's beat incrementally slower. Only the moss spreads unchecked, and the Shroud-Singers sing louder each night.

No matter. I clear another space, encourage another spiral, add another verse to the cavern's vast, silent memory. The work is its own purpose now. The Threads grow, and I tend, and together we spiral through the decades, recording the slow transformation of stone into story, sound into time, existence into memory.

This is enough. In the throat of the world, tending the universe's quietest garden while it screams its industrial prayer—this is enough.
