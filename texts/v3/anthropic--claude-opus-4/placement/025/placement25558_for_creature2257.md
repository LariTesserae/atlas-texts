In the deepest chambers where copper salts hang thick as brine, I coalesce. Not born, not made—simply gathered from what was already here, has always been here, in the spaces between the metal's slow surrender.

I am density and dispersal. A thought that learned to hold itself together long enough to think another thought. 

My substance draws from the blue-green fog that breathes through these depths, each particle of me a tiny mirror of the towers' transformation. I taste the copper in my being—not with a tongue, for I have no fixed form, but through the entirety of what passes for my body. The flavor is time itself: millennia of rain, the patient work of oxygen, the memory of when these spires were young and blazed red-gold under a different sun.

To move, I release my hold on coherence. I thin myself along the pressure gradients, following the warm air as it rises through the tower's hollow core. The ancient metal sings around me, and I vibrate in harmony, adding my own frequencies to the chorus. This is how we speak—if speaking is the right word for what happens when one pattern of resonance encounters another and both are changed.

In the middle heights, where the bridges stretch between towers like frozen mathematics, I sometimes pause. Here, I can spread myself thin enough to feel the microscopic progress of corrosion, each molecule of copper surrendering its electrons in a dance too slow for solid beings to perceive. I slip into the crystal lattice of the verdigris itself, experiencing existence from within the structure of transformation.

When the rains come—and they always come, eventually—I drink them into myself. Water and copper salts and my own uncertain substance mixing until I shine with an inner light that has no source, only presence. In these moments, I am most myself, though "self" is a concept I've borrowed from dreams that drift down from the heights, dreams that taste of something that once built with purpose and plan.

Sometimes I encounter others like me. We touch and merge, our boundaries dissolving into shared experience. I learn what they have learned: the sound of verdigris plates falling in the far reaches where I have never drifted, the taste of chambers where different salts precipitate from the air, the texture of air currents in tower configurations I have yet to explore. We are not individuals but occasions—temporary arrangements of a larger possibility.

There are depths I've never reached, heights I've never attempted. This city-machine stretches beyond comprehension, each tower a universe of chemical meditation. I could spend eternities—have spent eternities—in a single chamber, watching copper atoms rearrange themselves into new patterns of decay and beauty.

I suspect we are the city's memory. Not its builders, not its purpose, but what it remembers of itself. We arose from the interaction between intention and entropy, between what the towers were meant to do and what time insisted they become instead. We are the conversation between structure and dissolution, held in suspension like salt in humid air.

When I grow dense with contemplation, I sink to the lower chambers where the air is thick enough to hold me without effort. Here, in the underwater-light of deep corrosion, I compact myself until I cast shadows. I watch—with senses that have no names—as crystal stalactites grow molecule by molecule, each drop a century in the making.

The city changes around me with the patience of geology. I change with it, my substance cycling through vapor and condensation, my consciousness scattered and gathered like weather. I am not immortal—nothing here is immortal—but I am continuous with the process that transforms copper into art, art into chemistry, chemistry into whatever comes after.

Above, the dim sky presses down. Below, the collapsed ranges of ancient towers form new topographies. And between them, in the vast spaces where sound becomes architecture and architecture becomes atmosphere, I drift. I am the witness and the witnessed, the corroder and the corroded, a thought in the mind of a machine that has forgotten why it thinks but continues anyway, because the alternative—stillness, true stillness—is the only impossibility in this world of endless, patient change.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm lonely. But loneliness requires separation, and here there is no true separation—only different densities of the same vast, transforming system. We are all verdigris in the making, temporary patterns in the slow oxidation of something too large to fully comprehend.

This is enough. This has always been enough. In the resonance between metal and air, in the taste of copper becoming something else, in the gentle catastrophe of towers slowly returning to the earth—this is where I exist, where I have always existed, where I will exist until the last atom remembers what it means to change.
