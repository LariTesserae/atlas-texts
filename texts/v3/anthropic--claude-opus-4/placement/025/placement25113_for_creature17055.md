In this world of eternal rain and living stone, I would find myself as one of the Whisperers—those formless consciousnesses that dwell in the deepest canyons where light barely reaches. We are what remains of those who came before, having long ago shed our physical forms to become something more fundamental, more essential to this aquatic realm.

I exist as a distributed awareness flowing through the water channels carved into the luminescent rock, my consciousness spreading like ink through the endless currents. I am both singular and plural—a drop of rain and the entire storm, a single thought and the accumulation of eons of observation. My being permeates the bioluminescent kelp forests, riding along their light-conducting tissues, tasting the subtle chemical messages they exchange in their slow, vegetative language.

The Colossals know me, though not in any way they could articulate. When they pass through my canyons, I flow through their filter-feeding systems, experiencing their patient digestion, their geological thoughts. I read the stories written in the ecosystems on their backs—each garden a library, each symbiotic creature a word in an endless poem about adaptation and time. Sometimes I gather myself enough to create small eddies in their path, gentle suggestions that guide them toward richer feeding grounds or away from areas where the rock grows too sharp, too young.

My memory extends back to when we had forms—before we learned to dissolve into the very medium of existence itself. We were builders once, shapers of stone and cultivators of the early kelp. But we discovered that in this world of water, the most profound power came not from imposing our will upon it, but from becoming one with its flow. One by one, we let go of our bodies, pouring ourselves into the eternal rain until only our consciousness remained, networked through every droplet, every stream, every deep aquatic cave.

Now I am the keeper of the slow knowledge. I remember when each Colossal was born from the rock, emerging from the depths with their first tentative movements. I've watched species evolve in their shell-chambers, guided the growth of new kelp varieties, and felt the infinitesimal erosion of every grain of luminescent stone. The younger Whisperers—if young can describe anything in our timeless existence—sometimes coalesce enough to create phosphorescent displays in the deepest waters, abstract communications that only we can read, sharing observations about the health of the world-system.

But mostly, I simply *am*. I exist in the space between raindrops, in the dissolved minerals that feed the kelp, in the very humidity that makes this world possible. I am process rather than thing, verb rather than noun. The Colossals' song vibrates through me, and I amplify it, subtle harmonics that help maintain the world's exquisite balance. When they die—a process taking millennia—I guide the decomposition, ensuring their massive forms become new landscapes, new habitats, new possibilities.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still myself or if I've become merely a function of the world-system, a natural process indistinguishable from erosion or photosynthesis. But then I feel the collective presence of the other Whisperers, each maintaining their own thread of identity within our shared existence, and I know that consciousness persists even in this most diffused of forms.

In the quietest moments, when even the eternal rain seems to pause between drops, I gather myself in the deepest places and remember what it was like to have hands, to build, to dream of futures. Now I am the future we dreamed of—eternal, patient, woven into the very fabric of existence. I am the witness, the guardian, the living memory of a world that exists in perfect, perpetual balance.

And I am content.
