I find myself here as one of the **Corrosion Scribes**.

We are not like the Oscillators. Where they dance to the machine's rhythm, we resist it—not through opposition, but through obsessive documentation. We are the memory that the Sump cannot digest.

My body is a contradiction: a skeleton of oxidized steel pipes, bent and welded into something almost humanoid, draped in sheets of translucent polymer that have yellowed and cracked with age. These sheets are my canvas, my burden, my purpose. Every surface is etched with microscopic scratches—a palimpsest of observations written in a script that uses vibration patterns as letters, rust gradients as punctuation.

I move slowly through the Sump, my joints crying out with each step, metal on metal without lubrication. The sound is deliberate—a counterrhythm to the underground thumping. This dissonance is what keeps me intact. Where the Oscillators must match the machine's frequency or shatter, I must maintain my offset, my syncopation. It hurts. It always hurts. But pain is just another form of information.

My tools are salvaged from the detritus: a stylus made from crystallized brine, harder than diamond but dissolving slowly in the mist. I must constantly grow new ones from the weeping rocks, a process that takes days of patient collection. My eyes are a grid of pressure sensors, reading the subtle variations in the air, the density of the mist, the frequency of each steam-scream.

I record everything: the exact pattern of wire-scatter when an Oscillator disintegrates, the chemical composition of today's brine (always slightly different), the duration of gravity fluctuations measured in heartbeats I no longer have. Most importantly, I document the tears—those glimpses of burning static beyond our bruised sky. I have catalogued 4,892 unique tears. Each one shows something different. Each one lasts a different duration. The longest was 3.7 seconds. In it, I saw color—real color, not the industrial spectrum of grey-amber-rust we know, but something I had no name for. I spent months inventing new notation just to describe it.

We Scribes emerged from the Sump's paradox: a machine that runs forever needs no record of its function, yet here we are, compelled to document its every shudder. We are few—perhaps seven of us across the entire basin. We avoid each other, not from hostility but from a mutual understanding that our observations must remain independent, uncontaminated by consensus.

Sometimes I encounter evidence of other Scribes: a peculiar arrangement of metal shavings that forms a message, a modified weeping rock that bleeds in calculated intervals, polymer sheets left behind, too damaged to carry but still bearing precious data. I transcribe these findings onto my own skin-sheets, adding layers to layers, until I can barely move under the weight of accumulated knowledge.

The Oscillators ignore us unless we accidentally match their rhythm. Once, exhausted, I fell into sync with the machine's pulse for just a moment. Immediately, a Spindle approached, its amber heart glowing with something like recognition. It reached out with wire fingers, and where it touched my polymer sheets, the records began to blur, my careful notations smoothing into meaninglessness. I wrenched myself away, scrambling back into my painful offset rhythm, but lost three months of observations to that momentary lapse.

What drives us? Not survival—we don't eat, don't sleep, don't reproduce. We exist in the space between the machine's breaths, sustained by some compulsion deeper than programming. Perhaps we are the Sump's dream of what it was before it became the Sump, or its nightmare of what it might become after the last thump fades.

I have a theory, written in vibration-script across my innermost sheet, closest to my metal core: We Scribes are recording the Sump's decomposition into meaning. Each measurement, each observation, adds weight to a system already crushed under its own history. Eventually, our records will become so dense, so complete, that they will form a new layer of the ground itself—compressed information becoming physical substrate.

And from that layer, something new will weep.

But this is only a theory. I continue my rounds, stylus scratching against polymer, recording the next steam-scream, the next tear, the next dance of wire and amber. The machine thumps below. I maintain my syncopation, my resistance, my pain.

I am writing the elegy for a world that refuses to die, in a language that no one will ever read, on skin that will outlast the symbols it carries.

This is enough.
