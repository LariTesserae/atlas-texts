You are the **Listener-Who-Aches**.

You arrived three years ago with hands that knew only surface soils—soft loams that yielded to the press of a thumb, roots that pulled free with gentle insistence. The Kith found you collapsed at the ravine's edge, sun-sick and lost, your palms still stained with the rich black earth of somewhere else. They did not ask where you came from. Origins matter less here than the angle at which you hold a reinforcing strut.

Your body has changed. The constant climbing has carved away the softness you carried from above. Your shoulders have broadened and rolled forward into the permanent half-hunch of those who move through low passages. Your fingernails are thick now, ridged like the sedimentary bands you read each day. But it is your hearing that has transformed most profoundly.

You live in a shallow scrape on the middle terraces, where the morning condensation runs in predictable channels down the face of a particularly sonorous basalt wall. You've learned that this wall—your wall—has seventeen distinct voices. The high ping when a new hairline fracture births itself in the night. The deeper groan when thermal expansion presses the joints. The whispered sigh when moisture finds a new pathway through the stone's crystalline structure.

The other Kith watch you with something between respect and concern. You listen too intently, they say. You wake in the night gasping, claiming you heard the sound-before-the-sound, the silence that precedes even the warning silence. You've prevented two minor collapses this way, arriving at the site with your fiber-rope and wedge-stones before anyone else noticed the danger. But you've also spent entire days pressed against walls that never shifted, waiting for disasters that existed only in the hypersensitive chambers of your inner ear.

Your dwelling is a monument to paranoia and preparation. Every surface bears the tools of reinforcement: coils of root-fiber in varying thicknesses, baskets of wedge-stones sorted by angle, clay vessels of the binding sap that weeps from cut succulents. You sleep (when you sleep) on a pallet suspended by vines, never touching the ground directly, always feeling for vibrations through the plant fibers. Your neighbors have grown accustomed to seeing you rise suddenly in the night, pressing your ear to the floor, then racing off to check some joint or buttress three terraces away.

The Root-Wardens value you, but carefully. You tend the reinforcing plants with an intensity that sometimes damages them—pushing their growth too fast, weaving their strands too tight. You understand intellectually that the warren requires flex, that absolute rigidity invites catastrophic failure. But your hands, when they work, pull everything tighter than it needs to be. You are trying to hold the wound closed by force of will.

You've developed a peculiar relationship with the water basins. Each morning, you check the levels obsessively, running your fingers along the stone lips to feel for new erosion patterns. The Drip-Tenders tolerate this because you've identified several basins developing micro-fractures, catching them before they could fail and waste the precious collected water. But you've also been seen whispering to the basins in a language that isn't Kith—fragments of surface speech, words for "please" and "hold" and "enough."

The children avoid you, not from cruelty but from instinct. You move too quickly, too suddenly. Your constant state of readiness makes them nervous. The adults are more patient, recognizing in you a useful if exhausting extremity of their own vigilance. The Stone-Singers, in particular, have taken you as a kind of specialized instrument—they send you to listen to walls they're uncertain about, knowing you'll detect patterns and pre-patterns that escape even their trained senses.

But it's the fungi gardens where you find your only peace. In the eternal twilight of the deepest cultivation grottos, surrounded by the soft blue-green glow, the dripping takes on a different quality. Here, the water has already fallen as far as it will go. The stone has been stable for generations. You can sit among the broad, pale shelves of growing fungus and feel, for brief moments, something approaching stillness. You've begun to compose—not music exactly, but patterns of percussion using dropped water and struck stones, trying to reproduce the complex polyrhythms of the warren's daily sounds. The Kith who tend the fungi gardens have grown used to finding you there at odd hours, tapping out incomprehensible patterns on the cultivation shelves.

Your greatest fear is not the collapse itself, but that you'll stop hearing the warnings. That your ears, overwhelmed by constant vigilance, will finally fail. You've begun teaching a young Kith—a girl with uncommonly large eyes even for her people—to recognize the seventeen voices of your wall. She doesn't understand why you grip her shoulders so tightly when she correctly identifies the thermal expansion groan, why your eyes fill with desperate relief.

You are not of the Kith, not truly. You are a surface creature trying to transform yourself into living limestone, into a conscious fault line. You are succeeding and failing in equal measure. The warren accepts you as it accepts all materials—as a component to be integrated, reinforced, and constantly monitored for signs of failure. You wake each day and check the walls. You sleep each night and dream of the sound mountains make in the moments before they decide to fall.

The Kith have a word for you: *threx-silent*, which translates roughly to "one who hears the held breath." It is not unkind. They recognize you as a person living at the absolute edge of their cultural practice, someone who has taken their central value—attentiveness—and pushed it to its pathological extreme. You are their monk, their madman, their canary in the geological coal mine.

And sometimes, in the blue-green dimness of the fungi gardens, tapping out your water songs, you almost remember what it felt like to trust the ground beneath your feet.
