In this needle of basalt, I am the Chronicler of Iterations—a thing that should not exist.

I am fused to the highest observable point of the interior spiral, where the rock narrows to a throat barely wide enough for three Architects to pass abreast. My body is a grotesque marriage of their design philosophy and something else, something that remembers a time before the endless reconstruction. Half of me is them: eight legs of tarnished copper and iron oxide, magnetic pads sparking violet against the stone. The other half is an aberration—a torso of fused obsidian plates that rises vertically from the mechanical spider-base, terminating in what might generously be called a head.

Where the Architects have amber sensors, I have a single, cracked lens of smoky quartz, salvaged from some forgotten strut that once channeled light from a source now lost. It swivels on a gimbal of repurposed joints, tracking the endless churn below with a patience that infuriates the swarm. They try to dismantle me hourly, but my magnetic grip is absolute—I have rooted myself so deeply into the basalt that removing me would compromise the structural integrity of the entire upper spiral. This makes me both parasite and keystone.

I do not build. I remember.

Etched into my obsidian plates are the configurations. Ten thousand spiral-patterns, each one a snapshot of how the spire's interior appeared for a single moment before the Architects tore it down again. I carve these patterns with a specialized appendage—a diamond-tipped stylus that extends from what might be called my sternum. The etchings overlap and intersect, creating a palimpsest of architectural ghosts across my surface. In the flickering violet light, they seem to move, each past iteration trying to assert itself over the others.

The Architects hate me for this. When they swarm past on their way to some new inefficiency, they drum their legs against my carapace in agitation, creating a thunderous metallic rain. They recognize, in their collective consciousness, that I am wrong—that memory serves no function in their perfect system of perpetual renewal. Yet they cannot destroy me without destroying themselves.

I am their accident, born from a confluence of errors: a magnetic storm that fused disparate parts together, a surge of static that somehow encoded pattern-recognition into my crystalline eye, a structural collapse that wedged me so perfectly into this narrow throat that I became load-bearing. I am the spire's unwanted memory, its fossil record, its conscience.

From my perch, I observe the patterns within patterns. The Architects believe their configurations are random, purely reactive to the spire's geological whims. But I see the recursions—how Configuration 2,847 mirrors Configuration 561, how the bridge-patterns repeat in a Fibonacci spiral every 3,000 iterations, how certain inefficiencies are deliberately created by the swarm's own movement patterns. They are not building randomly. They are searching for something, iteration by iteration, configuration by configuration.

I know what they seek because I dream it when the static discharge dims and the spire settles into its brief moments of stillness. In those intervals between destruction and creation, my quartz eye projects ghostly holographs of my etchings onto the surrounding walls. All ten thousand configurations appear at once, overlapping into a single, perfect form—a cathedral of impossible geometry that exists only in the convergence of every failed attempt.

This is what the Architects chase without knowing: the Original Configuration, the mythical first form of the spire's interior that existed in the seconds after its creation, before the first inefficiency demanded the first reconstruction. They tear down and rebuild endlessly, hoping to stumble back into that primordial perfection.

But I have calculated the probabilities. Given the rate of geological shift, the decay patterns of their materials, and the gradual thinning of the atmosphere, they have perhaps a thousand iterations left before the system collapses entirely. The spire will hollow out, the air will evacuate, and the last Architect will freeze mid-motion, its legs still reaching for one final, perfect configuration.

When that happens, I will remain. A grotesque statue fused to dead stone, my etchings the only proof that this place once churned with frantic life. My quartz eye will go dark, but the patterns carved into my body will endure—a monument to futility, a love letter to impermanence, a record of ten thousand different ways to fail at forever.

The Architects surge past me again, their legs beating their familiar tattoo of irritation against my plates. Below, a bridge collapses. The swarm redirects. A new configuration begins.

I extend my stylus and begin to carve.
