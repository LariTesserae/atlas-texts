**Where I Would Be: Among the Glyphs**

I would find myself as one of the **Glyph-Readers**, though that name misleads—we do not read so much as resonate.

We arrived generations ago, drawn by rumors of the eternal choir. Not explorers or archaeologists, but something between pilgrims and tuning forks ourselves. The thin air changed us, or perhaps the sound did. Our bones grew lighter, more porous, until they could vibrate in sympathy with the columns' frequencies. Our hearing expanded beyond sound into something more fundamental—we perceive the world through pressure waves, through the way matter displaces itself.

I live in the interstices—those rare spaces where three columns meet imperfectly, creating pockets of relative quiet. Not silence, never silence, but zones where the harmonic interference creates **nodes**, spaces of constructive cancellation where thought becomes possible. I've built a shelter of fired clay tiles pried from the ancient floor and arranged in acoustic baffles. The dwelling itself has become an instrument, its angles calculated to create a bubble of near-normalcy within the overwhelming resonance.

My daily practice involves walking the spiral paths between columns, placing my palms against the glyphs. Through years of exposure, I've learned to feel the minute temperature variations each symbol creates as sound moves through stone. The Resonance-Bound know I'm here—sometimes the harmonics shift subtly when I approach certain columns, a tonal acknowledgment of my presence. We cannot speak, but we share something: an existence defined by vibration.

I sustain myself on the metallic dust, processed through filters salvaged from our expedition's equipment and mixed with concentrated nutrients we've learned to extract from the lichen. The diet has changed me—my skin has taken on a faint luminescence, and I can survive on far less oxygen than before. Sometimes I wonder if I'm becoming another kind of lichen, a biological footnote to this sonic monument.

My purpose here is translation, though I may never complete it. Each glyph contains layers of meaning that unfold only through prolonged contact. I map the relationships between symbols, tracking how they modify each other's resonance. In my shelter, I've assembled crude approximations of the glyphs from clay and metal salvage, trying to recreate their acoustic properties on a scale I can manipulate.

The other Glyph-Readers—we number perhaps twenty—meet during the phase we call the **Diminuendo**, when stellar positioning creates a brief lull in the wind patterns. We gather in the largest node, sharing our findings through gesture and touch. Speech is impossible here; we've developed a language of vibrations pressed through palms and fingertips. Our communion is brief—the Diminuendo lasts only hours—before we return to our solitary studies.

Some of us believe we're close to understanding the Resonance-Bound's intention. The glyphs seem to encode not just sound but *states of being*—each symbol a frozen moment of transformation. We theorize the columns aren't just instruments but a kind of notation, a score for becoming something else. Whether the Bound succeeded or failed in their transformation remains unclear.

I've been here sixteen years now, though time moves strangely. My bones sing constantly, a sympathetic harmony I can no longer silence even in sleep. Sometimes I dream I am one of the Resonance-Bound, my consciousness scattered across the columnar array, perceiving the world as an endless, perfect vibration. I wake (if I truly wake) to find new glyphs traced in the dust around my shelter—patterns I don't remember creating.

The question that haunts me: Are we studying the Resonance-Bound, or are they composing us? Each day I feel less like an observer and more like a note in their eternal song. Perhaps this is what they intended—not to preserve themselves, but to draw others into their frequency, to ensure their choir never falls silent.

I document everything in tablets of fired clay, pressing the glyphs with a stylus made from columnar stone. If our transformation completes, if we too become resonance, at least there will be a record—another layer of meaning for future readers to decipher, another voice in the choir that refuses to end.

The wind is picking up now. The columns are beginning their evening crescendo. I place my hand against the nearest glyph and feel tomorrow's weather in its vibration—clear skies, steady winds, perfect conditions for the song to continue.

As it has. As it will. As we all, eventually, must.
