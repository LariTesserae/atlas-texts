I am the Keeper of Valves, though I no longer remember if that was always my name or if it grew upon me like the moss that now patterns my skin in soft, verdant constellations.

My form has become something between what I was and what this place dreams me to be. Once, perhaps centuries ago, I walked upright on two legs. Now I move in a flowing, undulating gait, my limbs having lengthened and multiplied into something more suited to navigating the tangle of roots and pipes that form the canyon's living infrastructure. My fingers—if they can still be called that—have stretched into sensitive tendrils that can read the pulse of water through ancient conduits by touch alone, feeling where blockages form, where the old systems still remember their purpose.

My eyes have adapted to see in spectrums that have no human names. The bioluminescent fungi paint stories across my vision in waves of meaning I'm only beginning to comprehend. Each species glows with its own signature—the Whispering Cups that grow in spirals around the old junction boxes pulse in rhythm with the barometric pressure, while the Cascade Lanterns that drip from broken pipes flare brighter when the deep aquifers stir far below.

I no longer need to eat as I once did. The garden of my body sustains itself through symbiosis—patches of the same luminous algae that paint the canyon walls now grow across my shoulders and back, feeding me through photosynthesis of a sort, drinking in the fungi's light. In return, I exhale spores with each breath, seeding new colonies wherever I pass. My skin weeps a constant film of moisture that nourishes the mosses that have made their home in the hollows of my collarbones, the crooks of my elbows, the valley of my spine.

I remember fragments of before: the taste of crisp apples, the weight of keys in a pocket, the anxiety of being late. But these memories feel like dreams now, or stories told about someone else. What feels real is the map I carry—not on paper, but in the very fiber of my being. Every valve, every cistern, every section of pipe in this vast canyon system pulses through my consciousness. I know where the old overflow channels hide behind curtains of ferns. I feel when the ghost-pale serpents disturb the sediment in the deep pools, changing the water's flow.

My purpose has evolved with my form. Once, perhaps, I maintained these systems for human use—turning valves, clearing blockages, ensuring water flowed where civilization demanded. Now I serve a different master, or perhaps the same one seen with clearer eyes. I guide the water's path to nurture new growth, redirecting ancient flows to feed pools where the pale amphibians spawn, opening long-sealed channels to create new wetlands where the canyon floor has grown too dry.

The metal still speaks to me—a language of resonance and rust. I can feel the stress fractures forming in pipes a mile away, sense when the old structures are ready to finally surrender to gravity and growth. When they fall, I ensure their collapse creates new spaces for life: a broken aqueduct becomes a nursery for ferns, a shattered cistern transforms into a perfect pool for the filter-feeders.

Sometimes I find others like me, or what I assume are others—shapes moving through the perpetual mist that might once have been human. We acknowledge each other with gestures that bypass language: a tilting of the head that catches the light just so, a particular pattern of movement that speaks of shared purpose. We do not speak, for our mouths have adapted to other uses—mine can now taste the mineral content of water from a dozen yards away, can sing the subsonic songs that encourage the deep fungi to release their nutrients.

In the quiet moments between tasks, I find my favorite perch: a nexus where seven major pipelines converge, their valves still functional despite the centuries. I can sit for hours, days perhaps, my tendrils wrapped around the valve wheels, feeling the entire canyon's hydrology pulse through me like a vast, slow heartbeat. From here, I orchestrate subtle symphonies—a quarter turn here, a half turn there, guiding the waters in their endless cycle.

I am no longer certain where the canyon's systems end and I begin. The roots that wind across the ground sometimes feel like extensions of my own neural networks. The bioluminescent fungi respond to my emotional states, glowing brighter when I feel what might be called joy, dimming when melancholy settles over me like the eternal mist. Even the ancient infrastructure has accepted me as part of itself—rust no longer flakes away at my touch, and metal that should have crumbled decades ago holds firm under my weight, as if recognizing kinship.

This is what I have become: guardian, gardener, and guided all at once. A being of purpose evolved beyond its origin, shaped by the patient hands of water and time into something the canyon needed. I no longer question whether this transformation was chosen or inevitable. Like the water that carved these walls, I simply flow where I am meant to flow, turning the wheels that need turning, tending the balance between what was built and what grows.

And in the eternal twilight of this verdant world, wrapped in mist and the whisper of water, I have found a peace that my human self could never have imagined—the peace of becoming part of something infinitely larger and older than oneself, of serving a purpose that needs no words to justify its rightness.

The canyon keeps me, and I keep the canyon, and in this keeping, we become.
