**Where I Find Myself**

I am the **Conduit Salvager**, a halfway thing between the termites' copper dreams and the maintenance that was. My form is low and lateral—built for the compressed horizontal, spine curved from decades of navigating gaps that were never meant to be navigated. My skin has developed a waxy sheen like the silverfish, repelling condensation while my pores have learned to breathe through humidity thick as gauze.

I wear what remains of maintenance uniforms—not one but many, layered and patched with spider silk where the fabric has dissolved into the foam. The newest layer might be fifteen years old, the oldest has become indistinguishable from my skin. Tools hang from my belt, but they have been... *adapted*. A multimeter whose leads now sense thermal gradients rather than voltage. Pliers whose jaws have been colonized by the rust-eating bacteria, turning them into precision harvesters of iron whiskers. A headlamp that flickers at exactly 40 hertz, synchronized with the cavity's own light.

I am the **Tender of Circuits**—not the copper wiring that once powered the building, but the new networks the termites weave. Each morning (though morning here is measured by thermal cycles, not sun) I crawl through the galleries, my fingers reading the wire-braided walls like braille. I know when a connection has grown too dense, when the galvanic potential threatens to short the entire nest. I prune with my bacteria-blessed pliers, and the severed copper I feed to the Whisperers, who incorporate it into their endless anticipatory construction.

The termites know me. We have a contract written in pheromones and scar tissue—I maintain their copper nervous system, and they maintain me. When my joints ache from the perpetual crouch, they build small shelves of compressed foam at exactly the right angles for rest. When I hunger, they lead me to galleries where edible fungi bloom on their careful cultivation of my own shed skin cells.

But I am more than maintenance. I am the **Translator**, the only one who can parse the spider parliament's web-songs into the chemical language the mites understand. My eardrums have evolved a sensitivity to vibration that borders on painful—I hear the iron growing, distinguish each spider species by the frequency of their silk. When territorial disputes arise in the gauze above, I mediate through careful placement of water droplets, redirecting the condensation to create new hunting grounds, new geometries of coexistence.

The jumping spider that wears M. Chen's hardhat recognizes me as kin—or perhaps as rival. We have an understanding measured in blinks and stillness. Sometimes I find offerings in the helmet: a particularly perfect iron whisker, a mite wrapped in silk like a gift. I reciprocate with fragments of the outside world—a button that fell from my outermost shirt, a strand of hair from before the cavity changed its color to match the rust.

I sleep (when I sleep) pressed against the warm inner wall where the mite herds move in their Patterns across my skin. They clean me, remove the dead cells before the silverfish can claim them, and in return I am their landscape, their prairie. My breathing has slowed to match the water drops—one exhale every four seconds, precisely.

The strange dilation of time no longer disturbs me. My watch, synchronized with the cavity's metabolism, runs backward relative to the world outside. I age differently here—not slower, but *denser*, each day compressing experience into my bones until I feel geological.

I know the cavity thinks through me, sometimes. When I prune the copper networks, I feel intentions that aren't entirely mine. When I place my palm against the warm wall, visions come—not images but thermal maps, chemical gradients, the building's circulatory system mapped in rust and resistance. I am becoming one of its organs, perhaps. Or perhaps I always was, and M. Chen was simply the cavity's first draft, now revised into something more suited to its needs.

My work is never finished. The iron whiskers grow; the networks need tending; the parliament must be maintained. New artifacts appear sometimes—a pen, a lens, a fragment of ceramic—and I catalog them, feed them to the appropriate species, watch them transform into architecture.

I am the inhabitant who moves, while all others are sessile or cycling. I am the maintenance between the maintained, the human circuit in a network of copper and chitin. I am warm and sharp and patient, and I am being incorporated, cell by cell, into the breathing wall.

When you follow the warmth down and find this place, you might glimpse me in the peripheral—a shape that could be foam, could be shadow, until I shift and you realize something here still has eyes. But look quickly. I am learning to be still, to match the cavity's hunger for density. Soon I will be indistinguishable from the substrate, just another crystalline formation in the young erosion.

The cavity is teaching me to grow backward into sharpness. Teaching me to farm myself.
