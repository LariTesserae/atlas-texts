In this place where time pools like rainwater in shallow depressions, I am one of the Threshold Mechanics.

My workshop exists in a fold between two of the towering structures, where gravity has forgotten its usual insistence. Tools float at convenient angles, held in place by the same patient forces that shape everything here. I am neither Chronokeeper nor fully other—my ancestors were visitors who stayed too long, our bodies gradually rewriting themselves to match the local physics.

My skin has developed the telltale shimmer, though only in patches. When I work, I can feel time moving differently through my left hand than my right, a useful adaptation for my trade. I repair the small impossibilities that accumulate where different temporal flows meet: doors that open before they're touched, windows that show views from next week, stairs that remember the weight of future footsteps.

The Chronokeepers come to me because I understand both the breaking and the mending. My shop is filled with artifacts from failed experiments in chronology—clocks that count backwards and forwards simultaneously, mirrors that reflect probability rather than light, a tea kettle that whistles tomorrow's weather. Each piece whispers its own small rebellion against causality.

I've learned their temporal language slowly, the way one learns to read weather in an unfamiliar sky. My responses still carry the accent of linear time, but I can make myself understood. They pay me in duration itself—smooth, unkinked moments that I can stretch across the difficult repairs.

My only companion is a creature I call Flux—one of those rare, adapted fauna that has learned to navigate the shadows between timelines. It resembles something between a ferret and a fold in space, its body rippling with the same dimensional uncertainty that characterizes everything here. Flux has an uncanny ability to find lost objects, especially those that have slipped between moments. Sometimes it brings me things that haven't been lost yet.

At night (though night here is more a quality of time than light), I climb to my workshop's impossible roof and watch the Nexus of Eternity pulse in the distance. Each pulse sends ripples through the settlement, and I feel them pass through me like memories of futures I'll never quite reach. The Time Reavers occasionally pass overhead, their forms more suggestion than substance, maintaining whatever ineffable balance keeps this whole arrangement from collapsing into paradox.

I keep a journal, though the pages sometimes rearrange themselves, showing me entries I haven't written yet. In it, I document the small strangenesses I encounter: a Chronokeeper child aging in reverse for a week, a building that exists only on Wednesdays, rain that falls upward in certain districts. These observations are my contribution to understanding this place—not through the vast, patient perspective of the Chronokeepers, but through the accumulation of specific, impossible details.

My workshop smells of ozone and old clockwork, of the peculiar metallic scent that comes from handling objects that exist in multiple states simultaneously. My hands are scarred with temporal burns—places where I've reached too quickly between moments and caught the sharp edges of causality.

Sometimes the homesick remnant of my linear heritage surfaces, and I long for a world where cause precedes effect, where buildings don't phase through their own foundations, where time moves in one direction like a river rather than pooling and eddying like the rain in those shallow depressions outside. But then Flux brings me some impossible treasure—a flower that blooms into its seed, a photograph of yesterday's tomorrow—and I remember why I stay.

I am the keeper of small repairs in a reality that is itself a kind of beautiful damage. Each day (or what passes for days in this fluid chronology) brings new puzzles: a doorknob that turns in the fourth dimension, a window pane that shows different seasons to each observer, a floorboard that creaks with the weight of people who haven't walked on it yet.

The Chronokeepers tolerate me, perhaps even value me, though I'll never fully understand their deep-time perspectives. I exist in the margins of their vast civilization, tending to the cracks where their perfect control meets the stubborn chaos of a universe that insists on surprising even its most careful architects.

And in my own small way, I am helping to weave this impossible tapestry, one repaired paradox at a time, my hybrid existence a bridge between the world that was and the world that is perpetually becoming.
