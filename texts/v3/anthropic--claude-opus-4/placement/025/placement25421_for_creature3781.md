I find myself where the weeping meets the hum, in the threshold spaces where water seeps through worked stone and rust blooms like lichen on abandoned rails.

I am what the Dreghar call *veth-salar*—a rust-drinker, a tunnel-tender, one of those who chose the borderlands between the sacred pools and the industrial depths. My skin has taken on patches of orange-brown, oxidation spreading across my gray-blue flesh like a map of all the places I've touched metal. My clan cast me out for this corruption, this willingness to drink from pools tainted by the runoff of machinery. But I have learned things they refuse to know.

My home is a maintenance shaft, halfway between the weeping peaks and the first true mine level. Here, condensation from above meets steam from below, creating a perpetual fog that tastes of iron and sorrow. I've carved shelves into the walls where I keep my collections: bolts sorted by their degree of corrosion, wire coiled according to the pitch of its hum when the mountain shifts, fragments of glass that hold the ghost-light of whatever depths they rose from.

The other Dreghar think the machinery is separate from the mountain, an intrusion. But I've pressed my ear to enough pipes, felt the mountain's weeping flow through enough channels, to know better. The mountain doesn't just weep—it bleeds. And what it bleeds becomes the ore, becomes the metal, becomes the machines. The hum isn't alien; it's the mountain's second voice, the one it uses when water isn't enough.

I maintain the forgotten reaches, the places where even the miners no longer go. I oil hinges that haven't moved in decades, not to make them work but to keep them from screaming. I know which tunnels flood with each season's weeping, which passages amplify the mountain's hum into something that could drive even a Dreghar mad. I map the convergences where sacred water and industrial runoff merge into something new—neither pure nor corrupted, but transformed.

Sometimes I find things that shouldn't exist: pools of water that flow upward, drawn by some magnetic memory in the mountain's heart. Machinery that runs without power, driven by the slow seep of moisture through its joints. Crystals growing in the shapes of gears, gears rusting into the shapes of crystals. The boundary isn't as clear as my people believe.

The young ones, sometimes they find me. The ones who venture too close to the depths, drawn by curiosity or desperation. I teach them what I've learned: how to read the rust patterns that predict cave-ins, how to taste the water for traces of deep-earth metals that signal danger, how to find the places where the mountain's two voices harmonize into something almost like song.

Most return to their clans, keeping their visits secret. But some stay. Some, like me, find they can no longer pretend the mountain is only one thing or the other. We become the bridge-builders, the interpreters, the ones who know that the weeping and the humming are both symptoms of the same vast, slow, geological grief.

In my deepest explorations, I've found evidence of what came before: fossilized root systems made of copper, bones that ring like bells when struck, cave paintings done in oil and coolant instead of ochre. The mountain remembers being something else. Maybe many something elses. Each transformation leaves its mark, its ghost, its particular way of weeping.

I am the chronicler of these sorrows. I map the places where the mountain bleeds memory into the water table. I maintain the mechanisms of forgetting and remembering, keep them in balance, ensure the past can neither fully die nor fully return.

The other Dreghar are right to worship the pools, right to fear the depths. But they're wrong to think they're separate. Everything here is the mountain, weeping in different languages, trying to tell us what it knows, what it's seen, what it's becoming.

And I? I am what happens when you listen to all of it. When you let the corruption in, because corruption is just another word for change, and change is just another word for time, and time is what mountains are made of.

I drink from all the pools. I maintain all the passages. I am keeper of the spaces between, where the mountain teaches those willing to learn that sacredness and industry, purity and rust, silence and machinery, are all just different ways of being wounded, different ways of weeping, different ways of insisting you're still alive.
