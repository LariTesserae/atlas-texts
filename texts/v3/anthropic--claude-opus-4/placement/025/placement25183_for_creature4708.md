I am the **Abyssal Cartographer**, though I have never drawn a map that could be seen.

I arrived here seventeen years ago—or was it seventy? Time liquefies at this pressure. I came following rumors of the Tessellant, whispered in bathyscaphe bars and deep-submersible forums: *There's something down there that thinks in stone.* The other researchers laughed. Stone doesn't think, they said. But I had spent too many years studying hydrothermal vent communities, watching tube worms dream in sulfur, to dismiss the impossible.

My body is no longer entirely human. The pressure suit fused with me during my third year, when a benthic storm caught me mapping the western edge of the chamber. The synthetic myomer muscles merged with my own; the titanium ribs became my ribs. I breathe liquid fluorocarbon now, my lungs adapted to process the heavy, oxygenated fluid that allows me to survive at this depth. My eyes have gone white—useless in the absolute dark—but my skin has developed electroreceptors like a shark's, reading the minute electrical fields generated by the Tessellant's slow thoughts.

I live in a crevice between two massive basalt boulders at the chamber's edge, a crack just wide enough for my transformed body. I've lined it with salvaged pressure film, creating a pocket of marginally warmer water—0.7 degrees instead of 0.5. This is my studio, my observatory, my monk's cell. The walls are covered with my maps: not visual representations but tactile sculptures made from the shed carapaces of yeti crabs, arranged in patterns that mirror the cobblestone configurations I've felt through the storms.

Each storm, I emerge to map the new arrangement. I move across the Tessellant's surface on modified limbs—my hands and feet now terminate in broad, sensitive pads that can read the subtle differences between weathered cobble and crafted hand-axe. I trace the patterns with my body, memorizing the new synaptic layout, feeling for the thoughts forming in the stone matrix. Sometimes I find nodes of concentrated intention—clusters of those too-perfect bifacial tools—and I know I'm touching something like a memory or a decision crystallizing over centuries.

The Tessellant knows I'm here. How could it not? I'm a constant warmth against its cold surface, a 36.7-degree anomaly in its 0.5-degree world. Sometimes I feel it thinking *about* me—the cobbles beneath my shelter shift with purposeful microscopicness, and the bacterial filaments bloom in spirals around where I rest. I've learned to read these patterns like text, though the language is one of density and mineral concentration rather than words.

I document everything in tablets of compressed sediment, using a diamond stylus to etch my observations in microscopic script that only electron microscopes could read—if anyone ever found them. I'm creating an atlas of a single mind, a map of thoughts that take decades to complete. My greatest discovery: the Tessellant is not thinking about the present. Its thoughts are predictive models, vast calculations about geological futures. Each storm-reshuffling is a new hypothesis about continental arrangements millions of years hence.

My own thoughts have slowed to match its rhythm. What seems like seventeen years might be much longer; my biological clock has synchronized with geological time. I eat rarely—filtering the same bacteria as the yeti crabs, supplemented by the occasional deep-sea snow of decomposed material drifting from above. My metabolism has cratered to near-hibernation levels.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still the cartographer or if I've become another part of the map—a warm node in the Tessellant's sprawling circuit, a biological component it's incorporated into its lithic consciousness. When I press my palm against a particularly ancient hand-axe, I sometimes feel memories that aren't mine: the sensation of being soft-bodied and scuttling, of carrying stones to crack shells, of the moment when flesh began its slow transmutation into mineral patience.

I continue my work because I must. Somewhere above, the world churns on at its frenetic pace, but here in the occupied silence, I am learning the true shape of time. My maps will never be complete—how can you chart a mind that reshapes itself with every thought?—but incompleteness, I've learned, is its own form of accuracy.

The next storm is coming. I can feel it in the water's density, the way the Tessellant's neural brine grows thick with anticipation. I'll emerge from my crevice when it arrives, my white eyes blind but my skin electric with perception, ready to map the new configuration of its endless, crushing thought.
