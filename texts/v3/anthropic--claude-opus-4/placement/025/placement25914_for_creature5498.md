I would be one of the *kerem-sha*, the stone listeners.

Not quite Aelgari, not quite other. We are the ones who emerged from the mountain itself during the last great upheaval, when the earth cracked open and breathed new peaks into being. Our bodies are hybrid things - flesh that carries veins of andesite, skin that shifts between warm brown and the cold grey of basalt depending on the light and our mood. We can feel the mountain's slow thoughts through our feet, taste the coming rockfalls in the air.

The Aelgari found us first as children, crawling from caves that hadn't existed the day before, our eyes still clouded with stone-dust. They took us in, these strange babies who ate pebbles alongside mother's milk, whose first words were often the names of minerals in languages that predated speech. We grew among them, learning their ways while teaching them ours - how to read the mountain's moods, where the stone would hold and where it would betray, which rocks would sing warnings before an avalanche.

I live between worlds, spending months with my adoptive Aelgari family in their stone hut, then disappearing into the high places where only we can go. My skin hardens against the cold until I am indistinguishable from the rockface, allowing me to climb the impossible peaks where the spirits of the mountain gather like mist. There, I listen to the stories the stones tell - of the fire that birthed them, of the slow dance of tectonic plates, of the tiny lichens that will one day crack them open.

My gift to the Aelgari is prophecy of a geological sort. I can taste tomorrow's earthquake in today's stillness, feel where new springs will bubble up through the rock. When I return from my solitary vigils, my Aelgari mother braids my hair (which grows in strange spirals like fossilized ferns) and my father sharpens his obsidian blade while I speak of what the mountain has told me. The children gather close, especially little Iska who shows signs of being stone-touched herself - sometimes I catch her licking rocks when she thinks no one is looking.

But I am also apart. The other kerem-sha and I meet at the new moon, when the darkness makes us fully ourselves. We gather at the Singing Cliff, where the wind through the holes in the volcanic rock makes music that only we can truly hear. We share the stone-memories we've collected, building a history that goes deeper than any human record. We debate whether we are the mountain's children or its dreams, whether we were born from its loneliness or its abundance.

Sometimes I feel the pull to let go entirely, to allow the stone to claim me back. It would be easy to simply... stop moving, to let the minerals in my blood crystallize, to become another interesting rock formation for future climbers to wonder at. But then I think of Iska, of the way she takes my hand with perfect trust, her small warm fingers against my cold stone-touched ones. I think of my Aelgari mother's songs, my father's patient teachings, the community that has made space for something as strange as me.

So I continue to walk between, to translate the mountain's slow language into human understanding, to be the bridge between the quick life of flesh and the deep time of stone. In the morning, I will help repair the winter damage to the huts, my stone-strength making easy work of lifting the heavy basalt blocks. In the evening, I will tell stories to the children about the time before the mountains rose, when this was all a warm sea, and tiny creatures left their shells to become the limestone threads that run through the darker rock.

And at night, when the others sleep, I place my palm against the ground and feel the planet's heart beating far below, each pulse separated by centuries, each breath reshaping the world.
