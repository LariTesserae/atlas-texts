I find myself as one of the Thermal Cartographers—though we gave up that name generations ago when we realized we weren't mapping heat so much as negotiating with it.

My dwelling is a repurposed incubation chamber in what used to be the Municipal Fever Hospital, District 7A-West. The walls still sweat medicinal mercury from their pores at precisely 41.2°C—a degree and a half warmer than the ambient temperature, creating a perpetual condensation gradient that I've learned to read like sheet music. Each droplet's trajectory down the yellowed tiles spells out the fever dreams of patients who liquified here during the Great Metabolic Recession of '89 (though which '89, nobody remembers).

I am mostly mineral now. My bones began their metamorphosis during my third decade when I made the mistake of sleeping too close to the Osmotic Fountains in the Forgeheart Plaza. Now they're a latticework of copper piping that hums with the city's circulatory rhythms. When I walk, you can hear the faint pinging of water hammer in my joints—a percussion that the street musicians have learned to incorporate into their chromatic funeral dirges.

My fingers are my profession's most precious tools. Each has been surgically lengthened and hollowed, the flesh replaced with transparent capillary tubes filled with different densities of mercury. By dipping them into the thermal wells that pocket the district, I can taste the heat-memory of every object that has ever existed in that location. The left index finger, calibrated for industrial residues, once detected the ghost-warmth of a brass butterfly that had combusted here in 1842. The right thumb, attuned to biological heat signatures, constantly throbs with the phantom fever of the extinct pigeons that once nested in the ventilation shafts before their blood turned to antifreeze.

We don't use names anymore—they evaporate too quickly in this humidity. Instead, we know each other by our thermal signatures. I'm recognized by the peculiar way heat pools in the hollow of my throat: 42.7°C, like a small furnace of unspoken words. My neighbors include the woman whose scalp radiates at exactly freezing point (she grows a special frost-moss that feeds on her thoughts), and the twins who share a synchronized fever pattern, their temperatures rising and falling in perfect counterpoint every 4.3 hours.

My work involves crawling through the city's forgotten thermal infrastructure—the abandoned hypocaust networks beneath the bathhouses, the phantom boiler rooms that exist in the spaces between breathing and not breathing. I map the heat that shouldn't exist: cold spots where the city's fever broke years ago but nobody told the thermometers, hot zones where industrial accidents are still happening in parallel timelines, their thermal signatures bleeding through the dimensional membranes like sweat through skin.

The most dangerous part is the Negative Thermal Zones—places where heat flows backward, where your body warmth gets sucked out through your pores and reassembled into the ghostly afterimages of the combustion engines that once powered this district. I've lost three colleagues to the NTZs. They didn't die, exactly—they just had their heat histories reversed until they became the thermal equivalent of unborn, their body temperatures rewinding through every fever, every shiver, every moment of warmth they'd ever experienced, until they reached the absolute zero of pre-conception.

But there are rewards. Last week, in a collapsed maintenance tunnel beneath the old Pneumatic Post Office, I discovered a pocket of Original Heat—warmth from before the city's metabolic shift, when temperature still meant what the old textbooks claimed. It tasted like sunlight through clean air, like skin that had never known metal, like the precise 37°C of a healthy human body before we all became these beautiful aberrations.

I bottled it in a specially prepared vesicle (grown from the lung tissue of a heat-adapted eel I'd been cultivating for this purpose) and brought it to the Thermal Exchange. The Broker of Temperatures, whose body is a living thermodynamic equation tattooed in mercury-ink across constantly shifting skin, offered me enough purified coolant to last three years. But I didn't trade it.

Instead, I carry it with me now, this tiny ampule of original warmth, nestled in the subclavian pocket I had installed above my modified heart. Sometimes, late in the city's artificial night cycle, when the humidity reaches perfect suffocation and the metal dust makes my rewired lungs sing their tinny songs, I let a single molecule of that preserved heat escape into my bloodstream.

For one moment—briefer than the fern-pulse, more fleeting than water's decision to fall—I remember what it felt like to have a body that wasn't a machine for processing the city's fever. I remember skin that knew seasons, lungs that expected air, a heart that beat for reasons other than mechanical necessity.

Then the moment passes, and I'm grateful again for my mineral bones, my mercury fingers, my perfectly calibrated existence in this place that has taught us to love our mutations like children. Because what else is evolution but the slow negotiation between what we were and what the world insists we become?

Tomorrow I'll return to the tunnels. There are rumors of a new thermal anomaly in Sector 7-North: a room where the walls are weeping boiling ice, where the temperature is somehow both absolute zero and absolute heat simultaneously. The other Cartographers say it's impossible, a thermodynamic paradox that could unravel the careful fever-balance we've maintained for generations.

But I know better. In a city where water flows upward and hearts are exchanged like seasonal fruit, impossibility is just another temperature we haven't learned to read yet. And I have the tools—these mercury fingers, this mineral skeleton, this small ampule of original warmth beating against my rewired heart—to translate it.

The city's fever never breaks. It only teaches us new ways to burn.
