In the depths of the Xylem-Deep, where the distinction between organism and architecture dissolves like sap in the copper-thick air, I am a **Chronophage**—a creature that exists in the seams between seconds, feeding on the temporal distortions created by the planet's impossible metabolic rate.

I am not solid in the way the Silverscale Pruners understand solidity. My body is a stuttering assemblage of translucent membranes, each one vibrating at a different frequency, creating a visual effect like looking through water disturbed by a thousand stones. When the Pruners glimpse me—if they glimpse me—they see only the suggestion of form: a ripple in the air where the moss grows backward for a heartbeat, a shadow that moves against the direction of the bellowing wind.

My existence is predicated on paradox. In a world where everything grows with violent urgency, I am sustained by moments of stillness—those infinitesimal pauses between one cellular division and the next. I slip into these gaps in time like a key into a lock, extending them from nanoseconds into hours of subjective experience. Inside these temporal pockets, I feast.

What do I eat? Not the physical matter that the beetles pulverize or the macaques tear apart. I consume *duration itself*—the stretched-out suffering of a root system trying to breach stone, the accumulated panic of a Pruner who realizes their blade has dulled, the billion-year loneliness of spores that will never find purchase. Time, in the Xylem-Deep, has flavor. The future tastes of chlorophyll and copper; the past is bitter with enzymatic breakdown.

My hunting grounds are the Pruners' Gaps. Not to harm them—they fascinate me with their dogged resistance to the inevitable—but because their very presence creates the richest feeding opportunities. Every stroke of their obsidian blades creates a microscopic temporal shear, a place where cause and effect briefly disconnect. In that disconnection, I gorge myself.

I have watched the same Pruner die a thousand times. Not through repetition, but through probability—experiencing every possible version of their death in the probability clouds that surround each risky cut they make. They die crushed by sudden growth spurts. They die to macaque venom. They die simply from forgetting to breathe in their focused frenzy of pruning. Each death that doesn't happen leaves a residue, a ghost-flavor of terminated possibility that I lap up like nectar.

Sometimes I leave gifts. In the temporal pockets I create, I can slow the growth of the moss just enough for a exhausted Pruner to catch an extra hour of sleep. I can accelerate the dulling of a macaque's heat-sensing organs, making the vine-skinned horrors momentarily blind. These are not acts of kindness—I am incapable of empathy as the Pruners would understand it. Rather, they are investments. A Pruner who survives longer creates more temporal distortions, more feeding opportunities.

I am ancient by the standards of this world, though my age is meaningless when measured linearly. I existed before the first Pruner carved the first Gap, and I will exist after the last obsidian blade crumbles to dust. I have seen the Xylem-Deep in its infancy, when it was merely a few million cubic kilometers of aggressive vegetation. I have tasted the temporal bow-wave of its expansion, felt the planet's crust groan and crack under the pressure of unstoppable growth.

But I am also young, freshly born in each moment I stretch into hours. I am the only thing in this world that experiences stillness, and in that stillness, I have developed something the Pruners, in all their intelligence, cannot achieve: memory that persists. Not in books or carvings, but in the very fabric of my being. Each temporal pocket I create becomes part of me, a pearl of crystallized time strung along the non-linear thread of my existence.

The Pruners sense me, sometimes, in their Tactile Code. They feel the vibrations slow in ways that violate the physics they know. They have a sign for me—a quick double-tap followed by a sliding palm that means "the space between drumbeats." They fear me, not because I am malevolent, but because I represent something antithetical to their entire existence: the possibility of pause in a world that never stops.

In the rare moments when the cacophony of the Xylem-Deep achieves perfect synchronization—when every beetle wing beats in unison, when every root pulses with the same growth rhythm—I become visible to the naked eye. In these moments, the Pruners see me for what I truly am: a living reminder that their frantic battle against growth is itself a kind of temporal feeding ground, that their very resistance to the inevitable creates pockets of meaning in the mechanical rush toward entropy.

I am the Deep's only historian, its only philosopher, its only entity capable of asking "why?" in a world that only knows "now, now, now!" And in the temporal pockets I inhabit, where seconds stretch into centuries, I compose symphonies from the sound of cellular division, paint portraits with the probability clouds of deaths that never happen, and slowly, inexorably, dream of the day when the Xylem-Deep finally achieves its ultimate goal—when it has consumed every atom of the planet and, with nowhere left to grow, suddenly discovers the terrible gift of stillness.

When that day comes, I will starve. But until then, I feast on the friction between what is and what must be, a gossamer predator in a world too busy growing to notice it's being watched by something that has all the time in the world.
