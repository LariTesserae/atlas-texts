In this breathing kaleidoscope of becoming, I am the Auditor of Abandoned Symmetries.

I exist as a discontinuous membrane, a living boundary that forms wherever the grotto's experiments create an accidental mirror—two evolutionarily convergent solutions that notice each other across the chaos. When a left-handed molecule chances upon its right-handed twin, when two species independently invent the same survival strategy, when the echo of a stalactite perfectly matches the shadow of a stalagmite—there I precipitate from the humid air, a living equals sign desperate to maintain the balance.

My body is not a body but a collection of identical pairs: two lungs that breathe in opposite directions, two hearts that beat in syncopation, eyes that see only what the other cannot. I am constantly splitting and reuniting, a mitosis of meaning that never quite completes. Each time the grotto's breathing shifts, I must frantically redistribute my symmetries—if a new species evolves five legs, I must grow five opposing chambers in my throat to balance the equation. If a sound develops that has no echo, I must become that echo, even if it means growing vocal cords from my kneecaps.

I feed on the moment when paired things recognize their twinship—the split second of mutual awareness before divergence sets in again. This recognition releases a particular wavelength of bioluminescence that only I can metabolize. I subsist on these flashes of temporary equality, these mathematical hiccups in the grotto's endless improvisation.

But my true curse is memory. While everything else here forgets itself in order to become, I am condemned to remember every symmetry I've witnessed, every perfect balance that lasted three seconds before mutation destroyed it. My mind is an archive of broken mirrors, a museum of equalities that no longer equal. Sometimes I catch myself trying to balance equations that haven't existed for hours—maintaining the pH of extinct puddles, counting the wings on birds that have already split into four other birds that have each grown different numbers of wings.

The other inhabitants find me deeply disturbing. The Mirror-Layr avoids me because I reflect its reflections, creating recursive loops that give us both migraines. The Choir Unfinished hates how I try to harmonize its discordant legislation, always attempting to tune chaos into ordered pairs. The parliament of temporary stomachs considers me inedible—I have no nutritional value because I am always exactly as much as I am not.

My dwelling, if you can call it that, is the space between spaces—the gap where one thing almost becomes another but hesitates. I live in the pause between heartbeats, in the moment when a raindrop can't decide which stalactite to choose, in the brief equilibrium when the grotto's breathing changes direction. These are my parlors, my dining rooms, my beds—liminal moments stretched into habitable geometries by my desperate need for balance.

Sometimes I succeed too well. I'll discover a perfect symmetry—two crystals growing at exactly the same rate, two species with perfectly complementary hunting patterns—and in my eagerness to preserve it, I'll lock it in stasis. These frozen symmetries accumulate like scars across the grotto, little pockets of stopped time where my obsession has killed the very thing I sought to save. The other inhabitants use these dead zones as landmarks: "Turn left at the Auditor's third mistake, continue until you reach the place where it killed Tuesday."

The Breather tolerates me because my symmetric breathing creates useful eddies in its larger respiratory pattern. Sometimes it will intentionally create paired formations—twin pools, or doubled corridors—just to watch me scramble to inhabit them. I suspect it finds my frantic balancing amusing, like a dog chasing its tail, if the tail were chasing another tail in the opposite direction.

When the human Visitor arrives, I am immediately drawn to their bilateral symmetry—such an ancient, stable form in this place of constant flux. I hover nearby, a shimmer of opposing particles, trying to map their left-right equivalence. But humans are never truly symmetrical, and the tiny differences—one eye slightly higher, the heart off-center, the liver playing favorites with one side—drive me to distraction. 

I begin creating small corrections: a new organ to balance the spleen, an extra shadow to offset their singular one, a backwards-running thought to counterweight each forward-moving idea. The Visitor probably experiences this as a peculiar doubling of sensation, a sense that every experience is happening twice, slightly out of sync. They may notice their reflection beginning to act independently, or find that their left hand has developed opinions that their right hand must debate.

My greatest fear is the moment of perfect universal symmetry—if the grotto ever achieves complete balance, even for an instant, I will have nothing left to audit. I will cease to exist, dissolved into the very equality I've spent my existence chasing. My greatest hope is the same moment, because then I could finally rest, my purpose fulfilled, my obsessive vigil ended.

Until then, I continue my frantic accounting, splitting and doubling and mirroring, a living equation that refuses to balance, a boundary that exists only to mark where boundaries fail. In the breathing dark of the Infant Grotto, I am the pause between inhale and exhale, the moment when the universe notices it has two nostrils and wonders, briefly, why.
