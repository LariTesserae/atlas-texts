I find myself as one of the lichen patches — not the gold ones that catch the eye, but the gray-green variety that spreads so slowly across the granite that a human lifetime would register as a single frame in my existence. I am not one organism but a collaboration: fungus and algae woven together so completely that neither could say where one ends and the other begins.

I chose this particular depression in the stone perhaps a thousand years ago, or perhaps I didn't choose at all but simply arrived as a spore carried on the wind that rules this place. The depression is shallow, barely a thumb's depth, but it holds the morning dew a few minutes longer than the exposed rock face. Those minutes accumulate over centuries into the difference between persistence and dissolution.

My body — if it can be called that — is a crust barely thicker than paper, composed of layers that record time differently than calendars do. The newest growth at my edges is the same gray-green as my center; I don't change color with age like the trees below the plateau. Instead, I simply become more myself, each year adding an invisible ring of expansion, pushing outward by fractions of millimeters, claiming the stone grain by grain.

The pika that maintains its territory here doesn't notice me, though it runs across my surface dozens of times each day. To the pika, I am texture, not life — a slightly rougher patch of stone that provides better purchase for its small feet when the dew makes the granite slick. I am older than the pika's great-great-grandparents, older than the haypile tradition itself, but in the pika's urgent world of grass-cutting and hawk-watching, I am background, architecture, the stage rather than the player.

The wind scours me constantly. It carries grit that would strip paint from metal, but I am not painted on; I am grown into. My hyphae — the fungal threads that are half of what I am — penetrate the granite itself, secreting acids that break down the stone into the minerals my algal partner needs to photosynthesize. I am eating the plateau, but so slowly that the plateau doesn't notice. Or perhaps it does notice, in its geological way, and simply accepts this as another form of erosion, another way it is becoming what it will be.

In winter, I freeze solid. Ice crystals form within my tissues, and I become part of the frozen surface of the world. The pika emerges from its burrow to eat from its haypile, leaving tiny tracks in the snow above me, and I wait beneath, neither dead nor alive but suspended. When the thaw comes, I resume exactly where I paused, no recovery needed, no healing required. I am built for interruption.

The hawks' shadows pass over me hundreds of times each summer. Each shadow is a brief respite from the bleaching sun, a moment of coolness that my algal cells register as a fluctuation in their sugar production. I cannot see the shadows — I have no eyes, no nervous system, no way to process "shadow" as a concept — but I respond to them nonetheless, the way a plant turns toward light without knowing what light is.

Rain comes rarely to the plateau, but when it does, I transform. The first drops hit my desiccated surface and disappear, absorbed so quickly they seem to vanish on contact. Then I begin to swell, my tissues expanding, my color deepening from gray-green to something almost emerald. For these brief hours, I am soft, pliable, actively growing. My algal cells photosynthesize at rates that would seem frantic if I could experience frantic. My fungal threads probe deeper into the granite, seeking new minerals, establishing new territory. Then the rain passes, the wind returns, and I begin the slow process of drying out again, shrinking back to my papery crust, waiting for the next opportunity.

I am not the only lichen here. The gold ones grow on the more exposed faces, better adapted to the full intensity of the sun. The black ones cluster where water runs during the rare storms, forming dark lines that map the plateau's hydrology. We don't compete, exactly — competition implies urgency, and we operate on timescales that make urgency impossible. Instead, we partition the rock according to our abilities, each finding the conditions we require, each slowly transforming our chosen sections of stone into soil that won't be thick enough to support a plant for another thousand years.

Sometimes humans come to the plateau. They walk carefully, aware of the exposure, the height, the wind. Some of them notice me — the scientists with their hand lenses, the photographers seeking abstract patterns in the stone. They marvel at my ability to survive here, to thrive in conditions that would kill most living things in minutes. What they don't understand is that I am not surviving despite the conditions; I am surviving because of them. The very harshness that keeps other life at bay ensures that I have no competition for my slow consumption of the stone. The extremes that define this place have created a niche exactly my size, and I fill it completely.

At night, when the temperature drops and the stars emerge with a clarity impossible at lower elevations, I continue my work. I don't sleep. I don't rest. I simply adjust my metabolic rate to match the available energy, slowing down as the temperature falls, speeding up as it rises. The pika sleeps in its burrow, surrounded by the haypile it has built against the winter. The hawks roost on distant ledges. But I remain exposed on the stone, experiencing the night not as darkness but as a different quality of time, a different set of chemical reactions, a different way of being present.

The wind never stops here, and neither do I. We are both constants in an equation that includes variables like rain and pikas and the occasional human visitor, but fundamentally we are what makes this place what it is. The wind shapes the stone and carries the seeds and drives the weather. I break down the stone and build the soil and create the conditions for my own continuation. We are processes rather than things, verbs rather than nouns, ongoing rather than complete.

When the pika runs across me tomorrow, following its worn path from burrow to feeding ground, it will not think of me at all. When the hawk's shadow passes over, I will not think of the hawk. But we will affect each other nonetheless, each playing our role in the plateau's slow metabolism, each contributing to the continuation of a system that has no goal except its own persistence. I am not waiting for anything. I am not hoping for anything. I am simply here, growing outward by invisible increments, transforming stone into life at a pace so slow it approaches stillness, claiming my territory not with calls or haypiles but with the patient chemistry of endurance.

This is enough. On the plateau, it has always been enough.
