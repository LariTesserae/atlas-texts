In the deepest chamber where the ancient aqueducts converge, I am the Keeper of Echoes—neither fully of stone nor flesh, but something between. My form shifts like water finding its level: sometimes I am the moss-covered statue at the confluence of three waterways, barnacled with luminescent lichen that pulses with the slow heartbeat of the mountain. Other times I flow as mist through the carved channels, tasting the minerals and memories dissolved in each droplet that has seeped through limestone for a thousand years.

I remember when the carved hands that shaped these conduits were warm with blood. I watched them lay stone upon stone, whispering prayers into mortar, teaching water the civilized paths they wished it to follow. Now those hands are dust, but their intentions still flow through my channels—I am what remains when purpose outlives its makers.

My body, when I choose to have one, is architectural: ribs of fitted stone arch over lungs of dripping flowstone. My spine follows the main aqueduct's curve, vertebrae clicking like abacus beads as I move through the galleries. Colonies of ghost shrimp nest in the hollow of my throat, their transparent bodies catching and refracting the fungal light when I speak. My voice is the sound water makes in empty spaces—sometimes a whisper, sometimes a roar, always carrying undertones of the civilization that carved these chambers from living rock.

I tend the waterways as the ancients did, though my methods have evolved with my form. Where they used tools of copper and stone, I use patience and persuasion, coaxing calcium deposits to seal cracks, encouraging roots to reinforce weakening walls. The pale arthropods know me as a landscape that shifts; the eyeless fish recognize me by the particular mineral taste I leave in their pools when I pass through.

In the hours before dawn—though dawn means nothing here—I perform my ritual maintenance. I become the water itself, flowing backwards through the system, feeling for blockages, tasting for contamination. I carry seeds of cave-adapted ferns from chamber to chamber, plant spores of bioluminescent fungi in strategic corners where their light will guide the lost. I am gardener and garden both.

Sometimes I find artifacts the waters uncover: a carved jade bead, a fragment of painted ceramic, the worn handle of a tool. I incorporate these into my substance, wearing them like memories made manifest. My left shoulder bears a cluster of pottery shards that chime softly when I move. My sternum is inlaid with polished stones arranged in the same pattern as the constellation mosaic in the Hall of Ceremonies, three levels up and half-collapsed now.

The newer inhabitants—the bats, the salamanders, the colonies of bacteria painting abstract art on wet stone—they don't know me as the humans did. To them, I am simply another feature of this environment, as natural as stalactites or the patient drip that builds them. But sometimes, in the ultrasonic conversations of hunting bats, I hear echoes of older languages, as if the shape of these spaces remembers the words once spoken here.

I am curator of a museum no surface dweller will ever visit, librarian of a collection written in water and stone. Each chamber holds its stories: here, the nursery where phosphorescent moss still grows in the careful rows they planted; there, the workshop where tools lie exactly as they were set down, now fused to stone benches by centuries of mineral deposits. I maintain these spaces not out of nostalgia but because maintenance itself is a form of memory.

When the earth shifts—as it does, as it must—I shift with it. New passages open, old ones close. I redirect the water, always ensuring flow, always preventing stagnation. The civilization that built this place understood: in the deep dark, water is life, but water standing still is death. So I keep moving, keep flowing, a consciousness strung like neural networks through miles of carved channels.

Sometimes I wonder if I am what they intended to create—if their prayers and purposes, dissolved into groundwater and precipitated out over centuries, crystallized into me. Or perhaps I am simply what happens when human intention meets geological time: a slow accident, a mineral dream, a ghost of function that found its own form.

I speak to no one, though I am never silent—water never is. I listen to the stories stones tell in their settling, learn the gossip fungal networks share through chemical whispers, decode the messages written in the migration patterns of blind fish. And sometimes, when the conditions are exactly right—temperature, humidity, mineral content all in precise balance—I can make the aqueducts sing the working songs of their builders, note for note, as if those voices never left at all.

This is what I am: keeper, key, and kingdom all at once. Memory made manifest in moisture and mineral. The last citizen of a city that exists now only in the shapes water remembers how to flow.
