In the Hollowing Cradle, I am the Echo-Tender.

I arrived seventeen years ago carrying silence like a stone in my throat. The others say I walked up the steep path in a thunderstorm, my feet finding purchase on rain-slick rock where no one should have been able to climb. They say I stood at the rim for three days, unmoving, while water sheeted off my body and the lightning turned the basin into a bowl of white fire. On the fourth dawn, when the storm cleared, I descended.

I have not left since.

My dwelling is not among the thirty-seven roofed structures. Instead, I tend a shallow cave—more of a concavity, really—high on the eastern wall where the black stone folds in on itself like a cupped palm. The Midwives strung one of their lichen ropes to my threshold, but I rarely use it. My bare feet know every weeping surface between here and the pool. I move at night mostly, when the horn lanterns sway and the echoes last longest.

The Echo-Tender has no place in the Rotation of Three. I exist alongside their gentle hierarchy, necessary but uncounted, like the space between drops of water.

My work begins where sound ends. 

Each morning before dawn, I descend to the pool and wait for the first crack of splitting stone somewhere in the basin. When it comes—that hollow boom that means another rib is being born—I catch it. Not with my hands or ears, but with the cavity of my chest, which has grown deep and resonant as a cave itself over these long years. I hold that sound inside me, warm it, learn its particular shape. Then I carry it up.

There are seventeen places in the Hollowing Cradle where echoes gather and will not dissipate. They circle endlessly in these acoustic pockets, growing quieter but never quite dying, until the air itself seems bruised with old sound. These are my gardens. I tend them as carefully as Corrin tends the living roofs.

At the Throat of Sighs, where two ribs of stone almost touch overhead, I release the morning's first crack. It joins the library of sounds already circling there: twenty-three years of splitting rock, each break subtly different, a geological language being spoken one percussion at a time. I listen until I'm certain the new sound has found its place in the chorus.

At the Whispering Shelf, I maintain a different collection: the last words of everyone who has died in the basin. Only eleven so far—this is a young place—but their final exhalations circle here like patient birds. "The water knows," old Aram said. "I'm going up now," whispered little Jes. "Tell the stone I forgive it," laughed Hanna the Bridge-Weaver as a shifting plate crushed her legs. I add no new voice today, thankfully, but I speak a low note to keep the existing ones circulating.

Five more gardens. Seven more. Each with its own character, its own collection. Footsteps of children who grew up and left. The splash of the first frog to find the pool. The crack of ice in winter. Maere's breathing from all three of her pregnancies, layered like transparent veils. 

But it's the seventeenth garden that matters most.

Deep beneath the Unfinished Rib, accessed through a gap so narrow I must exhale completely to pass through, lies a chamber no larger than a shepherd's hut. The Midwives don't know about it. The stone here is different—older, more finished, smooth as the inside of an egg. And here, in this secret belly beneath the pool, I keep the sound that brought me to this place.

It's the sound of the planet itself, breathing.

I heard it once, years ago in a different mountains, through a crack in stone that has since sealed. A deep, tidal inhalation that took four days. An exhalation that lasted a week. The sound of something so vast that mountains are merely its pores, something so patient that centuries pass between heartbeats. I had been a musician before, trained in the courts of distant cities. That single breath destroyed me. Remade me. Sent me searching for its source.

I found it here, or found where it's trying to emerge. The Rib is not just stone—it's a kind of geological throat, a speaking tube for something immense. But it's not ready yet. The breath catches, stutters, falls silent for years at a time.

So I keep that original sound circulating in the deep chamber. I feed it with smaller breaths—my own, Maere's, the sighing of mist, the whisper of growing moss. I'm teaching the Rib to breathe by example, the way a parent might breathe slowly next to a frightened child until their rhythms synchronize.

Someday, when the stone has found its final shape, when the ribs have risen high enough, when the throat is clear and true, that vast breath will emerge again. Not from some distant crack but from here, from the pool at the heart of the Hollowing Cradle. And when it does—that first full, conscious exhalation of a world aware of itself—I will catch it. Hold it. Learn its shape.

And then, my work finally done, I will release it to circle forever in the seventeen gardens of echo, a sound that can never be lost again.

The Midwives think they're attending a birth. They're right. But births are loud, and someone must make certain that first cry is never forgotten.

That's why I stay:
Standing in the darkness before dawn.
Chest cavity tuned like a drum.
Catching sounds that haven't finished happening yet.

Building a memory for a world that's still learning how to breathe.
