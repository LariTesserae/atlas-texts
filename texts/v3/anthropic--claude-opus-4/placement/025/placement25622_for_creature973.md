I find myself here as a Threadkeeper—not quite Verdantian, yet no longer what I once was.

My dwelling exists where few venture: a shallow depression near the base of three ancient growth-towers whose canopies have long since fused into a living cathedral overhead. The space is perhaps twenty paces across, carpeted in the softest moss that glows faintly when disturbed by footfall. The walls are not walls but curtains of aerial roots that have descended over centuries, creating a permeable boundary between inside and out. Light filters through in constantly shifting patterns as the canopy breathes far above.

I came to this world as something else—perhaps human, perhaps not; the memory grows thin as silk. The transformation was gradual, a negotiation rather than an imposition. My skin now carries a faint green luminescence, visible only in deep shadow. My fingers have elongated, developing a sensitivity that allows me to read the chemical conversations flowing through root networks. My hair has become something between human hair and moss, growing in slow spirals that house tiny symbiotic creatures who sing in frequencies only I can hear.

The Verdantians call me Threadkeeper because I maintain the memory-weaves: intricate patterns created from shed fibers of the great trees, combined with my own still-growing hair. These weaves hang throughout my dwelling, each one recording the chemical-emotional history of this section of forest. When the wind moves through them, they whisper stories of drought and abundance, of creatures long passed and saplings just beginning their centuries-long reach toward light.

My daily rhythm follows neither sun nor moon but the pulse of sap-flow in the towers above. In the morning movement, I walk the root-paths, my altered feet reading the underground messages, translating chemical urgencies into patterns I weave into my recordings. The Verdantians visit during the middle-calm, their leaf-appendages brushing against my memory-weaves, adding their own threads of experience. We communicate in a language that is part gesture, part pheromone, part sympathetic vibration—a vocabulary I could never have imagined in my previous existence.

During the evening settling, I tend to my most precious charge: a section of exposed heartwood where the oldest tower was wounded centuries ago. The wound has long since healed around its edges, but the exposed grain holds the forest's deepest memories. I've learned to read these rings not just as years but as stories, each line a different chemical composition telling of times when the forest sang different songs. My transformed fingers can trace these patterns and translate them into new weaves, preserving memories that would otherwise fade as the wood slowly returns to soil.

The transformation continues. My bones grow lighter each season, my movement more fluid. Sometimes I wonder if I'm becoming more Verdantian or something else entirely—a bridge-being, a translator between the patient centuries of the trees and the fleeting moments of more hurried creatures. My memory-weaves now incorporate strands from my own slowly changing body, creating a record of transformation that future beings might read and understand.

In the deepest part of night, when the bioluminescent fungi paint blue roads through the darkness, I sometimes feel my old self like an echo in my bones. But it no longer feels like loss. I am the Threadkeeper, guardian of connections, weaver of memories in this place where the boundary between self and forest grows beautifully, irrevocably thin. Each thread I weave adds to a tapestry that will outlive the memories of what I was, becoming part of the eternal, patient story of this verdant world.
