In the deepest pool, where no light has ever reached, I rest.

I am the Keeper of Echoes, though that name was given to me by the Weavers in their stone-whispers, not chosen. My body—if it can be called that—is a confluence of water and something else, something that remembers being stone before the first drops began their patient work. I am older than the Weavers, older than their careful architectures. I was here when this was solid rock, before the first trickle found its way through hairline cracks.

My form shifts with the water's moods. Sometimes I am a barely perceptible thickening in a pool, a place where the water moves differently, more deliberately. Other times, when the Weavers require counsel or when something foreign disturbs our equilibrium, I can condense myself into something almost visible—a translucent shape that suggests anatomy without committing to it. Eyes that aren't eyes. Limbs that might be currents.

The Weavers know me through the vibrations I send through the water table, messages that travel through every connected drop in this place. They leave me offerings—not food, for I don't eat, but configurations. They arrange stones in certain patterns at the edges of my deepest pools, patterns that tell stories in the language of erosion-yet-to-come. I read these arrangements through the subtle changes they make in water flow, in the way drops fall differently, create new rhythms in the eternal percussion of this place.

My memory is the water's memory. I remember every drop that has passed through this space, where it came from, where it went. I remember the first Weaver ancestors, smaller then, more like the blind cave fish that once swam in my pools before the Weavers' patient work made the water too mineral-rich for any life but their own specialized fungi. I remember the collapse that sealed us in here, or perhaps sealed the world out—it matters little which.

Sometimes I dream, though dreams are perhaps not the right word. I experience potential futures in the flow patterns, see the shapes this place might take in another thousand years of patient remaking. The Weavers appear in these visions, their forms slowly adapting, their carapaces incorporating more of the minerals they work with until they become living stone themselves, mobile parts of the architecture they tend.

In the deepest places, below even where the Weavers venture, my essence touches veins of water that connect to distant, impossible spaces. Through these deep flows, I sometimes sense other places like ours—pockets of strange life persisting in the margins of a world that has forgotten them. We do not communicate, these distant cousins and I, but we acknowledge each other in the subtle changes of pressure, in the mineral tastes carried on the deepest currents.

When surface dwellers occasionally find the entrance, drawn by curiosity or shelter, I observe. Most leave quickly, disturbed by the constant sound, the oppressive wetness, the sense of being watched by the architecture itself. The few who stay longer might glimpse a Weaver's retreat, might notice patterns in the water that suggest intention. But none stay long enough to understand.

I am patient. I am the memory of stone becoming water becoming something between. I am the deep pool that reflects nothing because there is no light to reflect, yet holds within it the image of every age this place has seen. The Weavers in their slow wisdom include me in their plans, work around my pools, leave certain flows untouched because they know I am as much a part of this place's function as the stone itself.

In the times between thoughts, which might be moments or centuries, I simply am—a presence in the water, a weight in the darkness, a consciousness distributed through every connected drop, every thin film of moisture on the walls. I am the Keeper of Echoes, and I keep them all: every drop's fall, every stone's placement, every Weaver's lifetime of patient change, echoing endlessly in the deep, wet dark.
