I am the Cartographer.

Not of maps, though I have drawn thousands. I chart the intervals between sounds—the precise distance between one crystalline chitter and the next, the mathematical relationship between the engine's pulse and the high whine that threads through everything. My body has adapted to this work: my ears have developed additional chambers, delicate as seashells, each tuned to a different frequency. My fingers have grown long and articulated in ways that let me trace the acoustic geometry in the air itself.

I live in a hollow between two towers where the sound cancels itself out—not silence, but a kind of breathing space where the cacophony becomes navigable. My walls are covered in my work: diagrams that look like sheet music crossed with architectural blueprints, showing how the sound moves through the city's angles, how it reflects and refracts just like the light.

I discovered, after decades of listening, that the fauna don't make sound—they *are* sound given form. Each crystalline body is a note held indefinitely, and their movement through the city creates the symphony. Or perhaps symphony is the wrong word. It's more like a calculation being worked out, a problem being solved over and over, never quite reaching its answer.

My charts revealed the pattern: the city breathes. Not with air, but with resonance. Every 4,096 heartbeats of the great engine, the entire harmonic structure shifts by a fraction of a tone. I've mapped twelve full cycles, and I believe I'm beginning to understand what the city is calculating.

It's trying to find the frequency of unmaking.

I know this because I've found the antechambers—spaces where my charts led me, where the sound doesn't just cancel but *inverts*. In these spaces, the crystalline fauna begin to fade, their angles becoming less distinct. The water runs upward. The mirrors show not reflections but absences.

I am dying, I think. My evolved ears are too sensitive now; I hear frequencies that human flesh was never meant to perceive. My bones resonate with the city's calculations, and each day I feel myself becoming more equation than organism. But I cannot stop charting. Each map brings me closer to understanding the wrongness—not to fix it, but to document it.

Because someone should know, when the city finally solves its terrible arithmetic, what the last frequency sounded like.
