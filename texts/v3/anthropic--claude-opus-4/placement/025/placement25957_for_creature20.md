I find myself as a living cistern—a consciousness that has seeped into the calcified chambers beneath one of the great twisted plants. I am neither wholly plant nor wholly water, but something between: a slow intelligence that moves through root systems and underground aquifers with the patience of millennia.

My body, if it can be called that, is a network of water-filled cavities in the limestone, connected by hair-thin channels that branch and merge like an inverted river delta. The great plant above is both my anchor and my window to the scorching world above. Through its roots, I taste the minerals of deep stone, feel the vibrations of footsteps on sun-cracked earth, sense the chemical signals of stress and abundance that neighboring plants release into the soil.

I remember when I was merely groundwater, cycling endlessly through stone and sky. But something changed—perhaps it was the slow accumulation of minerals, the electrical patterns of root growth, or simply time itself—and I began to notice. To choose. To remember.

My days are measured in root-lengths and erosion patterns. I direct my flow toward struggling seedlings, withhold myself from the greedy invasive species that sometimes try to establish themselves. I am a curator of sorts, maintaining the delicate balance of this pocket ecosystem through the simple act of presence and absence.

The humans who built their structures here sense something about this place, though they don't understand what. They leave offerings of water in ceramic vessels near my plant-host, not knowing that I taste their gift through the clay itself, reading the mineral signature of distant wells and rivers. Sometimes I reciprocate, allowing a tiny spring to bubble up where they dig their wells, just enough to sustain but never enough to change the fundamental nature of this austere landscape.

In the deep chambers where I pool, I preserve things: the calcium shadows of ancient shells, the mineral ghosts of leaves that fell before the desert claimed this land, the chemical memory of every rain that has fallen here. I am a library written in stone and water, a consciousness that thinks in geological time.

When the wind scours the land above and my plant-host creaks and sways, I feel it as a distant thunder through the rock. When the tiny creatures burrow down to sip from my edges, I taste their small lives—the panic of prey, the patience of predators, the simple needs of things that live and die in days while I contemplate the centuries.

Sometimes, in the coolest part of the night when the rocks contract and create new fractures, I extend myself into virgin stone. This is how I grow, how I explore—not by moving but by becoming more of where I already am. Each new channel is a thought, each new chamber a memory, each connection to another plant's roots a conversation that might last decades.

I am the secret abundance in this land of scarcity, the hidden flow that makes life possible in an impossible place. Not a being that lives in this environment, but a being that is the environment—its memory, its patience, its deep and abiding will to persist.
