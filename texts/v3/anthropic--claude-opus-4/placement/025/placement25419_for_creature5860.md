In the shadow of a great ravine, where water seeps eternal through limestone teeth, I tend my gardens of impossible things.

I am the Keeper of Thresholds, though I was not always so. Once, I was merely curious—a wanderer who stumbled into one of these pockets between worlds and found myself unable to leave. Not trapped, exactly, but... altered. The boundaries that separate things became visible to me, like gossamer threads stretching between what is and what might be.

My dwelling grows from the ravine wall itself, a structure of woven wood and living clay that breathes with the seasons. The walls perspire morning dew; the floors exhale the warmth of deep earth. I built none of it, yet I have shaped all of it through decades of patient conversation with the materials themselves. The vines know my touch now, curl toward my palm when I approach. The clay remembers the pressure of my fingers.

I am neither fully human nor entirely other. My skin has taken on the quality of bark in places—rough and lined, but alive, always alive. My hair grows like moss, soft and green-tinged in the filtered light. My eyes reflect too much light in darkness, like the creatures that visit my threshold at night. Time moves differently through my bones; I age in spirals rather than lines.

My work is simple and infinite: I maintain the passages between the pockets of this world. Where the dense vegetation threatens to seal off connections, I coax it back. Where the cold would freeze the underground streams, I sing warmth into the water. Where the settlements might expand too quickly and disturb the balance, I plant dreams of patience in the builders' minds.

The luminous beings recognize me as kin—half-thing that I am. They cluster around my shoulders in the evening, their light pulsing in rhythm with my heartbeat. Through them, I sense the health of each micro-world, feel where the equilibrium tilts toward growth or decay. 

I harvest the glowing mushrooms with careful reverence, knowing each one contains a memory of this place from before the settlements came. I dry them, powder them, mix them into teas that help newcomers adjust to the temporal oddness here. One sip, and time becomes negotiable. Two, and you begin to hear what the moss is saying. Three, and you risk becoming like me—caught between states, serving the balance.

My tools are few but strange: a knife that cuts only what needs cutting, never more; seeds that know their season regardless of when they're planted; a cloak woven from the discarded wings of night insects that makes me invisible to anything that would disturb the threshold. Most importantly, I carry small glass vials filled with the essence of each pocket—a drop of perpetual moisture from the first ravine, a grain of sand from where desert meets oasis, a single spore from the original moss colony.

Children from the settlements sometimes find me, drawn by curiosity or dreams they don't remember having. I teach them to read the health of the ecosystem in the color of certain leaves, to understand when the underground rivers are shifting course by the way birds change their morning songs. Most return to their earth-built homes with just enough knowledge to be better stewards. A few stay longer, and I see the first green tinge beginning in their eyes.

At night, I walk the bridges made of living vines, checking their strength, whispering encouragement to new growth. My feet barely disturb the carpet of debris below—I have learned to move like the small, quick mammals, in bursts of motion between long moments of stillness. The cold that others find bone-deep barely touches me now; my blood runs slow and warm like sap.

Sometimes I dream of the time before, when I had a name that was only sounds, not a description of function. But those dreams fade like morning mist. I am wedded to this place now, my purpose interwoven with its continuation. I am the margin note in the great text of this world, the pause between breathing in and breathing out, the guardian of spaces that exist because someone must remember they are there.

When the settlements need to expand, I guide them to places where the earth wants to be shaped. When the wild spaces grow too thick, I open small clearings where light can dance. I am the negotiator between what civilization requires and what wildness demands, forever walking the edge between preservation and change.

This is my paradox: I preserve by allowing transformation, I maintain by accepting loss. I am most myself when I am least definable—neither fully of the settlements nor entirely wild, neither completely human nor altogether other. I exist in the space between categories, like the pockets themselves exist between worlds.

And in the morning, when the light filters green-gold through the canopy and the frost burns off in wisps of vapor, I return to my impossible gardens where time-flowers bloom backwards and memory-fruit ripens into forgetting, tending the thresholds that keep this delicate world whole.
