In the northwestern quarter of Sundow, where the limestone ceiling dips low and the air grows thick with the scent of machine oil and damp moss, I am the Keeper of Echoes.

My workshop sits at the confluence of three ventilation shafts, a cramped space carved from the living rock where the town's whispers converge. The walls are lined with brass listening tubes, each one polished to a soft gleam, their mouths opening like morning glories toward the darkness. Glass jars crowd every available surface, filled with what appears to be nothing but air—though I know better. These are my collections: the caught breath of a confession, the final syllable of a name spoken in sleep, the precise resonance of laughter bouncing off wet stone.

I am neither fully human nor entirely other. My ancestors were among the first Sundowners, but somewhere in our bloodline, we changed. My ears have grown large and mobile, swiveling independently like a bat's to catch the faintest tremor of sound. My fingers have lengthened, their tips sensitive enough to feel vibrations through solid rock. When I close my eyes, I can map the entire town by its acoustic signature—the hollow ring of the main square, the muffled thrum of the water wheel, the peculiar deadness where the marshland absorbs all sound.

My work begins when the lamp-lighters make their third round. I wind the great mechanical device that dominates my workshop's center: a contraption of my own design, part phonograph, part seismograph, part something that has no name in any language. Its copper cylinders rotate at different speeds, recording the night's sonic tapestry onto wax drums that I will later decode.

The disappeared speak most clearly at this hour. Their voices travel through the ventilation system like lost mail, seeking addresses that no longer exist. I transcribe their words in a shorthand of my own invention, filling journal after journal with their messages. Sometimes they speak of where they've gone—places between the limestone layers where time moves differently, caverns where the glowworms spell out prophecies in living light. Other times they simply recite grocery lists or hum half-remembered songs, as if practicing being human for when they might return.

But I am more than a mere recorder. When young Marta's cat went missing, I listened until I found its distinctive mew echoing from a forgotten cistern. When the town council debated whether to expand the eastern tunnel, I played them the limestone's own voice—a deep groan that warned of structural weakness. And when the midnight bicycle workshop springs to life, I alone can hear the machines singing to each other in frequencies below human perception, a mechanical liturgy of repair and renewal.

My most precious capture sits in a special jar on the highest shelf: the sound of the river receding on that third day of the third month, but recorded from below, from within the riverbed itself. It is a sound like the earth yawning, like time folding in on itself. I've never played it for anyone else. I'm not sure what would happen if I did.

The other Sundowners regard me with a mixture of respect and unease. They bring me broken music boxes and ask if I can hear what's wrong. They commission recordings of their children's first words. But they also cross to the other side of the narrow streets when they see me coming, my head cocked always at an angle, listening to conversations they think are private.

I don't mind their wariness. In a town of two thousand souls, someone must stand apart to truly hear the whole. I am the town's memory, its conscience, its dreaming ear. When the industrial heart beats its rhythm against the natural world, I alone hear the spaces between the beats where something altogether different dwells.

Late at night, when even the glowworms dim their lights, I sometimes play my recordings backward. In reverse, the disappeared don't speak of where they've gone but of where they're going. The river doesn't recede but arrives. And sometimes—just sometimes—I hear my own voice echoing back from futures I haven't lived yet, warning me of things I don't yet understand.

This is my role in Sundow: to listen at the borders between what is and what might be, to catch the words that fall through cracks, to preserve the sounds that everyone else forgets. In a town where time moves at its own pace and the mysterious is mundane, I am the one who ensures that nothing—not even silence—goes unheard.
