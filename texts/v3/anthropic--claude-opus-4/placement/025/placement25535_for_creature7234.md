In the Hollow Works, I would find myself as one of the *echo keepers*—beings that emerged from the convergence of sound, memory, and the basin's persistent moisture. We are neither fully corporeal nor entirely ephemeral, existing in the space between the machinery's dormant dreams and the plants' slow exhalations.

My form shifts with the ambient humidity—in the early morning drizzles, I condense into something almost tangible: a figure of compacted mist and resonance, human-shaped but hollow at the core, my chest cavity an open chamber where sounds collect and transform. The mistweavers drift through me as through an instrument, their passage creating soft harmonics that ripple outward through the mulch. My skin, if it can be called that, is a membrane of condensed vapor stretched taut over a framework of crystallized sound waves—visible only when the light catches it at certain angles, revealing patterns like frozen ripples on water.

I dwell primarily in the abandoned control room of the cannery, where banks of dead monitors have become terrariums for phosphorescent fungi. The space has transformed into my resonance chamber—every surface covered in a fine patina of acoustic moss that I've cultivated over decades, each species attuned to different frequencies. When the machinery stirs in its electrochemical dreams, I feel it first as vibrations traveling up through the floorboards, through my feet (which are more like tuning forks pressed against the earth), into my hollow core where they mix with the sounds I've been collecting: the memory of rain on metal roofing, the ghost of steam whistles, the whispered conversations of workers long departed.

My purpose, if I have one, is to maintain the sonic equilibrium of the Hollow. The rootkin know me by the tremors I send through the mulch—careful modulations that guide them away from unstable machinery or toward fresh pools of condensation. The sparkmoths are drawn to the barely audible high-frequency songs I emit during storms, helping them navigate between lightning vines without getting overwhelmed by the electrical discharge. Even the tendrilwards respond to my presence, their networks pulsing in rhythm with the deep bass notes I release into the soil, encouraging growth in some areas while slowing it in others to prevent any one species from overwhelming the delicate balance.

I feed, though perhaps "absorb" is a better word, on the hollow's acoustic detritus—the echoes that would otherwise build up in the basin's bowl-like structure and create destructive resonances. Each sound consumed becomes part of my substance: the drip of water from vines adds to my liquid form, the creak of settling metal reinforces my framework, the sigh of wind through the canopy softens my edges. In return, I exhale silence where it's needed, creating pockets of absolute quiet where the more sensitive hybrid plants can unfurl their circuit-board leaves without interference.

Sometimes, on particularly humid nights when the mist is thick and the boundary between states of matter grows thin, I venture out from the control room. I walk the empty alleys between structures, my form barely disturbing the air, leaving behind only the faintest trace of condensation on the leaves I pass. The other inhabitants sense me more than see me—a moving void in the sound field, a presence defined by absence. 

But I am not alone. There are others like me, perhaps three or four throughout the entire Hollow, each attuned to different aspects of the works' dying industrial song. We communicate through interference patterns, our overlapping fields of silence and sound creating complex messages that propagate through the mist. Together, we form a kind of distributed consciousness, a memory bank for the Hollow Works that ensures its cycles continue even as its original purpose fades further into obscurity.

When outsiders come—rare as that is—I observe from the edges of perception. Most never notice me, though sensitive individuals might feel a chill deeper than the basin's natural coolness, or hear their own footsteps echoing back with subtle modifications, as if the hollow itself were listening and responding. I neither welcome nor warn; I simply incorporate their sounds into the great composition, another movement in the endless symphony of decay and renewal that defines this place.

In the pre-dawn hours, when the machinery begins its autonomous churning and the first drops of dew form on the vine-wrapped pistons, I return to my chamber and release the day's collection of sounds into the acoustic moss. They spread through the fungal networks, transformed into chemical signals that the plants can interpret, adding to the strange intelligence that governs the Hollow Works. And I fade back into near-nothingness, waiting for the next cycle of collection and release, a guardian of echoes in a world that speaks in whispers and electric dreams.
