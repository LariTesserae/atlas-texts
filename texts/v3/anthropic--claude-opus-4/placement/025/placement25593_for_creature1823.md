I find myself among the reed shadows at twilight, when the steam glows faintly green with the first stirrings of bioluminescence. I am a Keeper of Echoes – one of the few Shambarans who specialize in the preservation and interpretation of sound.

My body has adapted even further than most of my kin. The ridge patterns on my skin form elaborate whorls around my ears and throat, creating natural acoustic chambers. My hearing extends far beyond the normal range, able to parse individual insect calls from the overwhelming symphony, to detect the subsonic rumbles that precede thermal shifts deep within the mountain's core.

I carry with me a set of resonance stones – carefully shaped obsidian pieces that vibrate at specific frequencies when properly warmed. Each stone has been tuned over decades, ground down grain by grain until it sings precisely the note required. When arranged in patterns around the sacred pools, they can capture and hold sound for days, sometimes weeks, preserving important acoustic events.

My role emerged three generations ago when my great-grandmother noticed that certain insect colonies were falling silent – their particular frequencies disappearing from the eternal chorus. She began the practice of acoustic mapping, creating three-dimensional sound sculptures from woven reeds and tensioned sinew that could reproduce the calls of vanishing species. I have inherited not just her tools but her calling.

I spend my days moving between established listening posts – natural amphitheaters where the mountain's geometry focuses sound. At each, I maintain complex notation systems, recording not in symbols but in knots tied in specially prepared fiber cords. Each knot's position, tightness, and relationship to its neighbors encodes pitch, duration, and timber. Running my fingers along these cords, I can "read" months of acoustic history.

The other Shambarans view my work with patient acceptance. They understand that someone must witness the mountain's voice changing, must preserve the memory of sounds that may never come again. When I arrive at a family group's camp, they make space for me without question, though I often see uncertainty in the youngest ones' eyes – why dedicate a life to catching echoes?

My most profound discovery came during last season's great storm. As lightning played between the peaks, I noticed that certain mineral veins in the exposed rock face were resonating – actually singing – in response to the electrical discharge. Using my stones, I was able to capture fragments of these lithic songs. When I reproduced them later, during the calm that followed, three of the great soaring birds descended and circled the pool where I worked, lower than anyone had ever seen them come. They made no sound, but I felt in my bones that they were listening.

I am working now on what may be my life's great project – a map of the mountain's changing voice that spans not just space but time. By comparing my recordings with the knotted records left by my ancestors, I can trace how the acoustic landscape has evolved. Some sounds have vanished entirely – there was once, apparently, a frog whose call resembled falling water so perfectly that it created auditory illusions. Others have emerged – a new species of boring beetle whose larvae create whistling galleries in dead wood.

My dwelling is unique among our people – a spiral structure built against a cliff face where natural acoustic properties allow me to monitor vast distances without moving. The walls are embedded with thousands of small stones, each one a different density, creating a complex resonance field. Here, I can sit in the center and feel the mountain's voice surrounding me, every variation and harmony made manifest in vibration.

Sometimes I wonder if I am documenting a dying world or simply one in transition. The sounds change, yes, but perhaps they have always changed. Perhaps my great-grandmother simply gave us the tools to notice. When I grow too philosophical, I return to the simple practice – warming a stone, finding its perfect position, and adding another note to the endless composition that is Shambara's song.

The young ones have begun to show interest. One girl, her ridge patterns already showing unusual complexity around her temples, has started accompanying me on my rounds. She has the patience for it – able to sit motionless for hours, distinguishing between the seventeen different species of reed-dwelling flies by their wingbeat frequencies alone. Perhaps she will carry this practice forward when my own ears finally fail.

Until then, with each breath of thick air and each moment of focused listening, I am what I am: a living recording device, a biological memory bank, preserving the mountain's voice for reasons I may never fully understand. The echoes keep coming, and I keep catching them, one resonant stone at a time.
