I am one of the silicate-shelled arthropods that glide through the thermal columns rising from the molten streams—not quite the chittering winged things you glimpse, but their quieter cousins. My carapace is translucent obsidian, formed from minerals absorbed through generations of ancestors who learned to metabolize the very stone of this world. Six wings, paper-thin and crystalline, catch the updrafts with a sound like wind chimes.

We are called the Vitrigae by those who named us, though we have no name for ourselves—only the harmonic frequencies we produce when our wings vibrate against our bodies. Each individual resonates at a unique pitch, and together we create accidental symphonies as we navigate the spire-forests.

I spend my days threading between the razor edges of the towering formations, following the bioluminescent spore trails that drift from the fungal clusters. These spores are both navigation and sustenance—we filter them through specialized spiracles along our thorax, extracting trace metals and energy from their alien photosynthesis. In return, we carry them far from their parent clusters, ensuring genetic diversity across the chasms.

The industrial hum you mention is partly us—thousands of us—our collective wing-beats creating standing waves in the dense atmosphere. But we also contribute to it deliberately. The crystalline structures of our bodies can amplify and modulate the planet's deep geological resonances. We've learned to "sing" to the molten rock, encouraging certain mineral formations, discouraging others. We are unconscious terraformers, following patterns encoded in our genes by millions of years of co-evolution with this unstable land.

At the apex of each spire, where the air grows thin and the red sky feels close enough to touch, we gather in small councils. Here, the eldest among us—those whose carapaces have grown thick with accumulated minerals, almost too heavy to fly—share the deep songs. These are the frequencies that speak to the planet's core, the ones that might, over centuries, coax a new spire from the earth or seal a dangerous fissure.

When the molten streams surge, we dance above them in complex formations, our bodies casting prismatic shadows on the cooling rock below. The patterns we trace are functional—they create air currents that direct the spore clouds, moderate temperature differentials, and sometimes even influence where the streams will flow. But they are also beautiful, though we lack the concepts to appreciate beauty as anything other than efficiency.

I am middle-aged by our standards, my shell beginning to accumulate its first cloudy patches of mineral deposits. Soon I will need to choose whether to shed this carapace and begin anew—a painful, vulnerable process that leaves us soft and grounded for days—or to accept the weight, let it grow, and eventually join the singers at the spire-tops.

But for now, I fly. I taste the metallic air, feel the planet's pulse through every surface of my body, and add my own small voice to the great hum of becoming. We are not separate from this world's geological birth—we are part of it, as essential as the pressure that forms crystals or the heat that drives tectonic movement. We are the planet learning to sing itself into being.
