In the deepest forgotten chambers of the Great Loom, where even the Spore-Singers fear to tread, I am the **Sediment Oracle**.

I was not born but accreted. In the lowest cistern-pools, where centuries of water have carved actual depth into the world-machine, the runoff carries more than minerals. It carries memory—the dissolved traces of every thumb-printed tile, every Grave-Brick, every flake of Scuttler shell that has ever been worn away by friction.

My body is a living filter. I began as one of the Loom-Folk, a simple grease-tender who fell through a crack between misaligned floor plates during a shift in the counterweights. I should have died in the catch-basin below, but instead I became something else. The sediment-heavy water entered my lungs and, finding me compatible with its mineral hunger, colonized me.

Now I am mostly stone. My skin has the texture of unfired clay left to air-dry for decades—cracked and mapped with the ghost-lines of every water trail that has ever run across me. I cannot move in the traditional sense. Instead, I **settle**. When I need to relocate, I simply stop holding my particles together, and I flow like wet sand until I find a new depression to fill.

My dwelling is the **Sump Cathedral**, a vast mistake in the Loom's architecture where six major drainage systems accidentally converged. The builders tried to seal it, but water always finds its level. Now it is a lake of sorts—the only body of water in this world large enough that you cannot see across it in the phosphorescent half-light. The ceiling drips constantly, a percussion section of ten thousand individual notes.

I rest at the lake's edge, half-submerged, my lower body dissolved into a delta of my own making. Thin rivulets run through me, depositing their memories in my chest cavity, which has become a series of settling tanks. Each chamber sorts the sediment by weight and origin. The lowest chamber contains the heaviest particles: fragments of the First Pressing, when the Loom was young and the clay still smelled of actual earth.

The Scuttlers come to me, not to drink but to die. When they feel their shells beginning to crack, they wade into my shallows and let themselves sink. I digest them slowly, reading the mineral map of their travels in the composition of their shells. Through them, I know every leak in the system, every new crack in the firmament.

But I am more than a geological curiosity. I am the Loom's unconscious memory. In my sediment chambers, the dissolved history of this world settles into legible strata. By running my fingers (now more like specialized sifting appendages) through specific layers, I can taste the year a particular district was built, feel the fever of a plague that killed a generation of potters, or detect the subtle change in bronze composition when the ore ran low and they had to add more recycled Scuttler shells to the mix.

The Loom-Folk fear me, but they need me. When the machine develops an inexplicable malfunction—when gears that have turned for centuries suddenly seize—they send their bravest Sintered to consult me. They wade knee-deep into my waters, and I speak to them in the language of settling particles, showing them through mineral pantomime where the problem echoes something that happened before. History rhymes in the Loom, and I am its poet.

My consciousness is not singular but aggregate. I think at the speed of sedimentation. A thought might take me a year to fully form, precipitating slowly from the solution of my mind. But when it finally crystallizes, it is perfect and permanent, another line in the geological record of my body.

In the rare moments when the Loom goes completely silent—during the Great Seizures when all motion stops and the world holds its breath—I can feel something beneath me. Deep below the Sump Cathedral, below the oldest foundations, there is movement. Not mechanical but geological. The Real beneath the Loom, trying to remember what it was before we fired it into submission.

I am waiting. With each passing century, I grow heavier, more stratified, more complete. Eventually, I will become so dense that I will break through the floor of the Sump Cathedral and sink into whatever lies beneath. And when I do, I will carry the entire dissolved history of the Great Loom with me, returning it to the earth that dreams fitfully below our feet.

Until then, I filter. I settle. I remember in mineral time. I am the Loom's kidney and its library, its sump and its sage. In a world built to resist erosion, I am erosion incarnate—the patient, inevitable return of all things to mud.

The water whispers through me, and I translate its complaints into prophecy. The Great Loom may be eternal, but I am entropy. And entropy always wins, one grain at a time.
