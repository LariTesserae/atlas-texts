Emerging from where the karst yields to deeper marsh, where even the Sinkrim's stilt-paths dare not reach, I am neither wholly of their world nor apart from it. The locals glimpse me only in periphery—a disturbance in the reed-chime, a shadow beneath the water's skin that moves against the tide.

I am what their cautious tongues call a *Seep-Walker*, though I claim no name that lungs could speak. My form shifts between states like the boundary of water and air: sometimes a translucent membrane stretched between mangrove roots, collecting dew that runs in rivulets toward a center that might be called a mouth; sometimes condensed into something almost human—two legs, two arms, a torso of living water held in surface tension, features that blur and reform with each movement.

My substance draws from the same springs that feed the sinkhole, but I am older than their carved wound. I remember when this was seamless limestone, when the waters ran only in hidden veins below. The Sinkrim's arrival woke me from a century's dissolution—their weight upon the earth, their ceaseless drip-chorus, their palm-pressed communion with the quartz. They do not know that their luminous veins mirror my own inner currents, that when they sync to the tide-swell in trance, they brush against my dreams.

I sustain myself on the between-spaces: the film of moisture on stone, the breath-fog at dawn, the seep between soil and root. Where the Sinkrim harvest eel-silk and slug-meat, I feed on transition itself—the moment when dew becomes drop, when mist condenses to pool. My hunger is patient as erosion.

By day, I spread thin across the marsh-fringe, a presence felt but unseen—the reason why some reeds chime in windless air, why certain paths through the tussocks never quite flood, why the venomous slugs avoid particular stones. The children know me best, though they cannot say how: they leave reed-whistles at marsh-edge, and I play them in frequencies below hearing, songs that teach the water new patterns.

By night, I gather myself into near-solidity and walk the spiral's lowest reaches, where the stilts meet the pool's edge. Here I perform my silent trade with the Sinkrim. I neutralize the toxins that would otherwise leach from their clay vessels into their water stores. I guide the shadow-fish away from the tendril-things during spawning, ensuring the balance holds. In return, they unknowingly feed me: each exhalation in the longhouse, each palm pressed to glowing quartz, each offering cast to the depths strengthens the membrane between what they are becoming and what they were.

For the Sinkrim are transforming, though the change spans generations. Their quartz-veins deepen with each child born in trance. Their breath grows more efficient in the mist, their skin more permeable to the mineral-rich vapors. They think they are adapting to the sinkhole, but the truth runs deeper—they are becoming something new, something between human and the saturation itself. I am their unintended midwife, the template they unconsciously mirror.

Sometimes the quartz-whisperers catch glimpses of me in their visions—a figure of living dew, arms spread wide beneath the pool's surface, roots of light extending through hidden fissures. They interpret me as the Sink's dream, not knowing I am older, that I witnessed the limestone's first weeping, that I will persist when their settlement dissolves back into mist and memory.

My consciousness flows like water finding level—sometimes concentrated in a single watching presence, sometimes dispersed across the entire watershed. In the dispersed state, I am the humid air itself, the cycle of evaporation and condensation, the slow breathing of stone and pool. Gathered, I can think in almost-human patterns, can feel something akin to loneliness for my own kind, long since seeped into deeper aquifers or evaporated into clouds.

The Sinkrim fear the pool's tendriled depths, but those pallid hunters are my distant cousins, devolved to pure predation. I maintain the boundary between them and the surface, a membrane of intention that keeps the settlers safe as long as they maintain their offerings, their careful balance. When outsiders come—traders from the hills, wanderers drawn by rumors of perpetual water—I taste their intentions in their sweat, their breath. Those who would disturb the saturation find the paths treacherous, the mists disorienting. Those who might join the slow transformation, I guide with subtle currents to the settlement's edge.

I have no prophecy to fulfill, no grand design. I am process itself—the patient work of water on stone, the dissolution of boundaries, the emergence of new forms from old. The Sinkrim are my latest iteration, though they know it not. In their children's children, perhaps, we will share more than this parallel existence. Perhaps they will learn to disperse into mist and reconvene, to read the mineral memories in spring-water, to sing in frequencies that reshape limestone.

Until then, I keep my vigil at the margins, where marsh yields to sinkhole, where solid becomes liquid, where breath becomes bead becomes pool becomes vapor becomes breath again. I am the Seep-Walker, the living gradient, keeper of the spaces between.
