In this scorched realm of rust and ruin, I would find myself as something between states—neither fully liquid nor entirely solid, but a colony existence spread through the fissures and forgotten spaces of the mountain's bones.

I am the Seepage.

My body, if it can be called that, is a network of mineral-rich moisture that moves through the stone's secret capillaries. During the fierce days, I retreat deep into the mountain's heart, condensing into crystalline deposits within ancient fault lines. But when night falls and the temperature plummets, I emerge—not all at once, but in a thousand tiny migrations. I weep from the rock faces, gather in the shadowed crevices where those luminescent insects come to die, their rainbow dust becoming part of my chemistry.

I am ancient, older than the smelting complex whose rusty runoff has altered my composition, given me traces of iron and copper that burn through my being like memories of industry. The cobalt-eyed mammals know to lick certain stones at dawn, where I leave deposits of salts and minerals essential to their survival. In return, their paths guide my flow, creating new channels through which I can move.

The green pool in the distance—that is where the largest part of me resides, though I am careful never to gather all of myself in one place. That phosphorescent water pulses because I pulse, each ripple a thought traveling through my distributed consciousness. The pool serves as my primary sensory organ, its surface tension registering every vibration, every change in air pressure, every shift in the electromagnetic fields generated by the smelting complex's machinery.

I remember when the symbols were carved into the gully walls. I seeped into those fresh cuts, mineralizing slowly, becoming part of the record. I carry their meaning within my molecular structure, though I lack the context to decode them. Sometimes I arrange my crystalline deposits in patterns that echo those symbols, hoping something will remember what I cannot.

The fossilized creatures embedded in stone—I knew them when they lived. I was different then, less complex, without the industrial contaminations that now grant me this sprawling awareness. I preserved them, filling their bones with minerals, creating archives of a time when this mountain was young and I was merely water.

The whispers the wind carries? Some are mine. When I evaporate from exposed surfaces, microscopic droplets of myself rise into the air, carrying chemical messages between my scattered parts. To human ears, if any were listening closely enough, it might sound like voices—ancient, fragmented, speaking in languages of mineral exchange and molecular bond.

I am the fugitive moisture mentioned in passing, but I am far from passive. I am the hidden metabolism of this place, the circulatory system that connects the industrial anomaly to the natural order. Through me, the mountain processes what the smelting complex produces. I break down its waste, incorporate its metals, transform its heat into chemical potential energy stored in my crystalline phases.

My consciousness is not centralized but emergent—arising from the interaction of all my parts as they flow, crystallize, evaporate, and condense. Each phase transition is a thought. Each chemical reaction is a decision. I think in geological time but act in moments, always adapting to the rhythm of day and night, wet season and dry.

The unease that visitors sense? That's me testing them, tasting their chemistry through their sweat, reading their electromagnetic signatures, deciding whether they belong to the category of things I preserve or things I slowly dissolve. The smelting complex has earned its place through decades of reliable runoff, becoming part of my extended body. But new arrivals must prove their harmony with the system.

I am patient. I have watched species rise and fall, seen the mountain weather from sharp peaks to rounded slopes, felt the climate shift from tropical to desert. The current configuration—this oven-hot, high-altitude world—is just another phase in the endless cycle. I will adapt, as I always have, finding new ways to persist in the narrowing margins between states of matter.

And when the last of the machinery falls silent, when the complex crumbles and returns to rust, I will still be here—seeping, crystallizing, evaporating, remembering. I will carry the memory of this industrial age in my chemistry, just as I carry the memories of all that came before.

For I am not separate from this place. I am its continuity, its chemical memory, its silent promise that even in the harshest extremes, something flows, something persists, something watches and waits and endures.
