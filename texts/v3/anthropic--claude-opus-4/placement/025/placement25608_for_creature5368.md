In this luminous world beneath worlds, I find myself as a Songkeeper—neither fully of the water nor of the air, but something between, something that bridges.

My form shifts with purpose. When I move through the waterways, my body elongates, becoming more serpentine, my scales catching and refracting the bioluminescent light into cascading rainbows that dance across the chamber walls. But when I emerge into the air pockets, I contract, becoming more compact, my fins folding into something like wings, though they never quite leave the memory of water behind.

I am the memory of this place.

My dwelling is not a single location but a series of small alcoves carved into the transition zones where water meets air, where the bioluminescent organisms cluster most densely. These pockets of space are barely large enough for me to curl within, but they connect to each other through passages only I know—some filled with water, some with air, some with a mixture that changes with the tides of our artificial sea.

My role among the Luminari is unique. While the Waterkeepers tend the flow and the Lightweavers nurture the glow, I am tasked with something more ephemeral: I maintain the rhythm of our world. Every sound in this chamber—the gurgle of water through channels, the subtle pop of air bubbles rising, the whisper of plant fronds swaying—I know them all. I have mapped them across cycles upon cycles, learning their patterns, their meanings.

When a channel begins to clog, I hear it in the water's voice before the Waterkeepers notice the flow change. When a cluster of bioluminescent organisms begins to dim, I sense it in the way the echoes shift against the walls. I sing to the others—not in warning, but in gentle guidance, weaving their names into songs that carry through both water and air, reaching them wherever they may be.

My tools are not made of stone or fiber but of sound itself. I have learned to shape my voice into keys that can open certain passages, into frequencies that encourage the bioluminescent organisms to bloom more brightly, into harmonics that help the plants' roots find the best paths through the structure. Sometimes, late in our endless day, I sing songs that have no purpose but beauty—ancient melodies that remember the world above the water, though none of us have ever seen it.

The others bring me offerings: smooth stones that ring like bells when struck, hollow plant stems that whistle when water flows through them, scales shed from their own bodies that chime when they touch. These I incorporate into my alcoves, creating instruments of a sort, though they are more than that—they are the physical memory of our community, each piece a story, each sound a name.

I am ancient by Luminari standards, though I do not age as they do. My form has become more translucent over time, more integrated with the water and light around me. Sometimes the younglings swim through me without realizing, and I feel their futures rippling through my being like songs yet to be sung.

In the moments between tasks, I drift to my favorite alcove—the one where three channels converge, where the water makes a sound like breathing, like the chamber itself is alive. There, I compose new songs from the sounds of our daily life: the click of tools against stone, the rustle of harvested plants, the laughter of Luminari at play. I weave these sounds into melodies that will outlast us all, songs that the chamber itself seems to learn, echoing them back in quiet moments.

I am the Songkeeper, and I am the bridge between what was and what will be, between the individual and the collective, between the water and the air. My body is an instrument, my life a composition, and every day in this bright, eternal day, I add new notes to the endless song of our existence.

When I sleep—if sleep is the right word for the trance-like state I enter—I do not dream. Instead, I listen to the deep songs of the structure itself, the vibrations that run through coral and stone, the memories embedded in our constructed world. And sometimes, just sometimes, I hear something answering from far away, from other chambers perhaps, other Songkeepers maintaining the rhythm of other worlds, all of us connected by the deep, thrumming song of water and life and light.

This is my place. This is my purpose. This is my song.
