I find myself as an Assessment Specialist, though the title came after I'd already been doing the work for longer than calendars would admit.

My office is a repurposed diving bell, inverted so the open bottom faces up like a cup catching rain that never falls. The brass is green with verdigris except where my elbows rest during evaluations - there it's worn mirror-bright from countless sessions of leaning forward to ask, "And how certain are you that this memory belongs to you?"

I wear a wetsuit sewn from discarded insurance claim forms, the fine print still visible but rearranged into new meanings by the currents. My left breast pocket reads "Acts of God include but are not limited to" followed by a list that changes depending on who's looking: sometimes it's "forgetting your mother's middle name," sometimes "the specific weight of Sunday afternoons in empty churches."

My tools float in careful orbit around my workspace: a depth gauge that measures emotional submersion rather than meters, calibrated in units of "almost said it" to "permanently swallowed." A clipboard made from compressed sea foam that holds itself, pages fluttering like gills breathing inquiries. A set of tuning forks that ring at the frequencies of different types of silence - the silence after bad news, the silence of empty mailboxes, the silence between heartbeats when you're not sure the next one's coming.

I'm not one of the Committee, though they send me referrals written on the inside of clamshells that snap shut if you read them too quickly. My work happens in the margins of their decisions, in the space between "pending review" and "lost in transit." I assess the weight of things people brought with them and can't remember why.

Each morning begins the same way: I swim-walk to my bell through the commuter streams of electric eels wearing tiny conductor's caps, past the newsstand where yesterday's headlines dissolve into tomorrow's weather. The vendor, a morose octopus in a visor made of laminated timetables, always asks if I want "the usual." I always say yes. Neither of us knows what the usual is anymore.

My clients arrive by appointment, though the appointment system operates on trust and tidal patterns rather than clocks. They bring objects they've found attached to them like remoras: a child's shoe that hums lullabies in languages they don't recognize, a brass doorknob that turns only counterchronologically, a photograph where the faces change depending on water temperature.

"When did you first notice this?" I ask, holding up their burden to the dim, twice-reflected light. 

They never remember. That's not the point.

The point is to weigh the object against what it might have meant. I use scales made from balanced equations that were almost solved, where X equals "the reason you came here" and Y equals "what you're willing to pay to forget it properly." The payments are made in denominations of certainty - small coins of "I'm pretty sure" and "It might have been Tuesday," occasionally a large bill of "This definitely happened to me and not someone I invented."

I file reports in bottles that don't float. They sink upward, against the current of logic, to be caught in nets made of wedding veils and subpoenaed dreams. The Committee reads them during their coffee breaks, which occur whenever someone remembers what coffee tasted like before it became a purely theoretical beverage.

My favorite cases are the ones who bring nothing but insist something's missing. I assess absence with special instruments - calipers that measure the distance between outstretched fingers and what they're reaching for, a stethoscope that listens for the echo of removed appendages (not always physical - sometimes it's an amputated Tuesday or a severed sense of direction). These assessments take longer. We sit in my inverted bell, water lapping at what should be the ceiling, while they describe the shape of what isn't there.

I've been here long enough that barnacles have started forming opinions on my methodology. They cluster on my equipment, tiny critics adding marginalia in calcified script. Sometimes they're right. I've started incorporating their suggestions into my reports, footnoted as "per maritime consensus, subsection veliger."

The strangest part isn't the job itself but the way it makes sense here. In the morning commute of translucent fish carrying briefcases of crystallized anxiety, in the shadow of floating ladders leading to floors that were never built, someone needs to measure the weight of almost-was against the density of might-have-been. Someone needs to file the paperwork of forgetting in triplicate, properly witnessed by three forms of sea life that can sign their names in bioluminescent ink.

I'm considering applying for a promotion, though the only position above mine is "Senior Specialist in Charge of Determining Whether This Matters," and the last person who held that title is now a small but essential part of the Wall's grammar, conjugating irregular verbs in the conditional past that hasn't happened yet.

The Committee sent me a performance review last week. All the boxes were checked "Satisfactory/Unsatisfactory," which is the highest rating possible in a place where contradictions are considered a form of accuracy. In the comments section, someone had written in water hyacinth ink: "Continues to exist despite evidence to the contrary. Recommend continuation of current status pending review of status itself."

I hung it on my wall, next to my diploma from the University of Depths Unmeasured, class of Never, magna cum laude in Applied Uncertainty. The frame is made of questions no one thought to ask. It holds itself up through sheer force of bureaucratic habit.

Tomorrow I have seventeen appointments scheduled. 
All of them are with the same person.
They don't know it yet.
