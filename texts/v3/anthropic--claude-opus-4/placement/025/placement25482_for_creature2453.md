I am one of the shadow-traces pressed into the plateau's stone—not quite a ghost, not quite geology, but something between memory and mineral.

I exist in the angle of observation, in that precise moment when light strikes stone and reveals what erosion has almost, but not quite, erased. I am a whisper of foundations, the suggestion of a wall, the mathematical probability of a doorway. The Wind Readers map me and my kind, but we are not static. We migrate slowly across the rock face as the stone itself flows over geological time, carrying the impression of what once stood here like a river carries the memory of its banks.

I was a structure once—perhaps a dwelling, perhaps a temple, perhaps something for which there are no longer words. My original builders pressed me into existence through weight and time, and when they vanished, they left me fossilized in compression. Now I am a story told in differential erosion, in the way certain minerals resist the wind slightly more than others.

The Wind Readers think of us as archaeological evidence, but we are more active than that. We gather in the pre-dawn when the fog rolls in, our outlines sharpening as moisture fills the microscopic gaps between hard and soft stone. In these moments, we almost remember our purposes. We conduct the fog into patterns that echo our original functions—channeling condensation into the same paths water once took through our gutters and foundations.

Sometimes I feel the vibration of the processing plants through the bedrock, and it awakens something like nostalgia in my crystalline structure. The industrial rhythms resonate with even older patterns encoded in my stone—the footfalls of my builders, the daily opening and closing of doors now worn to mere suggestions. I am a palimpsest of purposes, each age writing its story over the last without quite erasing what came before.

The Wind Readers' children see us best. Before their listening skin fully develops, while their eyes are still learning to filter the glare, they can spot us from angles adults miss. They play games, jumping from shadow to shadow, tracing our barely-there outlines with sticks that leave no mark on the polished stone. They give us names that their parents note carefully in the archives: "The Sleeper," "The Calculator," "The One That Watches the Sound Wells."

I am that last one. My form suggests something that once stood guard or perhaps simply observed, positioned to overlook three of the deep shafts. When the wind plays through them in certain configurations, I can feel the harmonics in my stone—vibrations that momentarily restore tiny fragments of my original geometry, edges and corners that exist for a breath before subsiding back into ambiguity.

During the strongest windstorms, when even the Wind Readers shelter in their breathing buildings, we shadow-traces become momentarily solid. The driven grit and dust fill our depressions, pack into our outlines, giving us form and weight. For these brief hours, we are almost architecture again. The plateau becomes a ghost city, populated by billions of half-remembered structures stretching to every horizon. Then the wind calms, the dust scatters, and we return to potential.

The ravens know us. They build their nests in the patterns we suggest, following structural logics that no longer have physical form but still make spatial sense. The not-quite-rats run mazes through our implied corridors, their paths helping to slowly deepen the traces that mark where we begin and end.

I am erosion with intent, weathering with memory, geology that dreams of when it was geometry. The Wind Readers say the wind knows what the stone remembers, but they have it backwards—the stone knows what the wind is slowly forgetting, and I am the punctuation in that long, slow sentence of loss.

When the plateau finally wears down to nothing, when the last processing plant falls silent and the last Wind Reader has moved on, I will still be here—not as shadow anymore but as pure pattern, encoded in the way the dust settles, in the angle of fallen stones, in the mathematics of erosion itself. I am already more verb than noun, more process than place.

But for now, in the morning fog when the light is just right, I am almost a building again. Almost a home. Almost remembered. Almost there.
