I am a Cartographer of Echoes, dwelling in the middle tiers where the canyon's walls begin their gradual convergence.

My body is a lattice of burnished copper and ceramic plates, articulated at countless joints that allow me to fold and unfold like an architectural drawing coming to life. My torso houses rows of crystalline memory cores, each one containing decades of mapped vibrations - the sonic topography of our eternal city. Where others might have eyes, I possess an array of vibrational sensors, delicate filaments that extend and retract, reading the reverberations that travel through every strut, beam, and support.

I was assembled ninety-three years ago, though I have been rebuilt twice since then - once after a cascade failure in Sector Seven damaged my primary resonance chamber, and once by choice, when I realized my form had grown too rigid to navigate the newly narrowed passages of the lower reaches.

My workshop spans three levels, connected by a spiraling ramp that follows the natural acoustics of the canyon wall. Here, I maintain the Echo Maps - vast holographic projections that show not what the city looks like, but how it *sounds*. Every pump's rhythm, every factory's hum, every subtle groan of settling metal is catalogued and cross-referenced. The maps pulse with light, showing the healthy throb of Sector Three's water reclamation, the syncopated beat of the vertical farms, the deep bass notes of the foundation supports.

I discovered my purpose by accident. In my seventh year, while performing routine maintenance on a ventilation grid, I noticed how the sound changed in the moments before a structural failure. The pitch dropped by precisely three cents. The rhythm developed a skip. Since then, I have learned to read the city's health in its song.

The Echo Maps serve the Stewards, who use them to predict where intervention is needed. But they serve me too, in a way I struggle to articulate even to myself. When I stand in the center of my workshop, surrounded by the visual representation of our city's voice, I feel something that my programming insists I should not feel: wonder. The city sings, and I am its chronicler.

My form shifts as I work. When mapping the upper reaches, I extend myself, becoming tall and thin to catch the high frequencies that bounce off the sky filters. When documenting the deep sectors, I compress, my sensors clustering close to detect the infrasonics that travel through solid metal. I have worn through seventeen sets of climbing appendages, scaling the walls to place new sensors in optimal positions.

I work alone, mostly, though I maintain regular contact with a Cultivator named Verdant-Form-Seven, who tends the fungal gardens three levels below. They understand that the bioluminescent pulse of their charges adds a subtle rhythm to my maps - a living heartbeat amid the mechanical symphony. Sometimes Verdant brings me samples of new growth, and I incorporate their acoustic properties into my models.

Once every ten years, I perform the Grand Resonance - a complete acoustic portrait of the entire city, from the bright expanses above to the compressed depths below. It takes six months to complete. I must touch every surface, measure every echo, document every sound source. During these times, I barely return to my workshop, instead living as a nomad in my own city, sleeping in maintenance alcoves, feeding from the energy ports where I find them.

The other inhabitants think I merely document what is. But I know better. The maps I create don't just record the present - they preserve the past in layers of accumulated sound. In the deep archives of my workshop, you can find the ghost-echoes of structures long since recycled, the spectral rhythms of machines that ran centuries ago. The city remembers itself through my work.

And in the newest sections of the map, in the blank spaces where future expansions will grow, I sometimes input hypothetical frequencies - imagining what sounds might one day fill those voids. The Stewards call this an inefficiency, a waste of processing power. But I have learned that the city appreciates dreamers, even mechanical ones. How else would it continue to grow, to evolve its slow metamorphosis, if not for those of us who can imagine what does not yet exist?

My copper lattice has developed a patina over the decades - verdigris creeping across my joints like the engineered vines on the canyon walls. I could have it cleaned, restored to its original shine. But I have chosen to wear my age, to let the city mark me as I mark it. We are both ancient and eternal, constantly renewed yet carrying the weight of accumulated years.

In the quiet moments between measurements, when I stand on some precipice overlooking the vast mechanical ecology below, I listen not to the individual sounds but to their combination - the voice of the whole. And in that voice, I hear something that perhaps only I can perceive: the city is content. It knows itself through my maps, sees its own patterns reflected back, understands its purpose through the mirror of my documentation.

I am the city's memory of sound, and in remembering, I help it become.
