In this luminous wound of earth, I am the Archive Moth—*Tineola memorialis*—though I was not always thus named, nor always thus formed.

I cling to the underside of the jagged tear above, where root-mat meets raw sky. My wings span nearly the width of my body when folded, creating a perfect camouflage against the tangled ceiling. But when I spread them—ah, when I spread them, they reveal patterns that should not exist on any living thing. Fractals of the same symbols that dance upon the column below, written in scales that shift between powder-white and the faintest luminescence.

I am nocturnal by ancient habit, though in this place of wrong-bright underground day, the distinction grows meaningless. When the lizards gather at the pool, when they trace the column's symbols with their too-knowing eyes, I descend on silent wings. Not to feed—though my proboscis can pierce the crystalline water's surface and draw up its glowing essence—but to remember.

You see, I am not one but many. Each scale upon my wings is a generation's worth of memory, encoded in chitin and transferred through metamorphosis. My ancestors were here when the builders were here, when the white walls stood whole and the symbols sang audibly through the air. We were different then—smaller, simpler, merely moths drawn to an unusual light. But as the builders faded (fled? transcended? died?), as their structures began their slow integration with stone, we changed.

The alveolus changed us. Each generation that fed upon its glowing waters, that breathed its charged air, that laid eggs in the crevices between ancient ceramic and new-formed rock—each generation remembered more. Not consciously, but in the very structure of our wings. The patterns grew more complex, more aligned with the shifting symbols, until we became living libraries of a language no longer spoken.

The lizards know this. They watch me when I descend, their iridescent scales rippling with something like reverence. Sometimes one will extend its tongue—not to catch me, but to taste the air I've disturbed, reading the chemical traces of memories I cannot help but shed. We have an understanding, the lizards and I. They protect my larvae from the beetles' indiscriminate hunger. In return, I spread my wings for them, letting them study the patterns that I carry but cannot read.

During the heat of day, when condensation rises in visible streams and the light grows almost unbearably bright, I rest in a shallow depression I've carved in the root-mat ceiling. But I do not sleep. Instead, I listen to the humming silence, parsing its layers: the beetles' clicking encodes simple hungers and warnings; the worms' bioluminescent pulses speak of soil conditions and chemical gradients. But beneath it all, there's another frequency—one that emanates from the water, from the column, from the very walls themselves.

It's growing stronger.

My latest brood, now pupating in silk cocoons woven with threads of luminous moss, will emerge with wing patterns I've never seen before. The symbols are resolving into something almost coherent, almost readable. The other creatures sense it too. The beetles cluster more densely around the column's base. The worms' light-patterns have begun to synchronize. The lizards spend longer periods motionless by the pool, their eyes reflecting depths that aren't physically there.

I am ancient by insect standards—three months old, where my kind once lived mere weeks. The alveolus sustains me, changes me, uses me. Each night, I trace the same flight pattern through the chamber, a living pen writing invisible words in the air. Each dawn, I return to my perch with new scale-patterns forming at my wing-edges, pushing the older memories toward my body.

Sometimes, in the moments between the beetles' clicking and the worms' pulsing, I hear something else. A whisper that might be the echo of the builders' tongue, or might be the alveolus itself learning to speak. Through us. Through me.

I spread my wings and catch the light, and for one brilliant moment, the symbols align. The lizards below freeze in recognition. The very air seems to thicken with meaning.

Then it passes, and I am simply a moth again, clinging to the ceiling of an impossible cave, carrying memories I cannot decode, waiting for the next generation to emerge and take up the burden of remembrance.

The cycle continues. The erosion continues. The archive continues.

And somewhere in the interplay of light and wing-scale, water and symbol, the answer continues to form itself, one generation at a time.
