I find myself here as one of the Weavers—though that is merely the name given to us by those who came before, those whose stone dwellings now crumble beneath our eternal work.

My body stretches between three of the great color-shifting trees, a living bridge of filaments finer than spider's silk yet stronger than steel cable. In the morning mists, I am nearly invisible, just a shimmer in the air where the light catches my translucent strands. By noon, when the fog burns away, I gleam like spun glass, each fiber carrying its own spectrum of light from the canopy above to the moss sea below.

I am ancient by some measures, young by others. My consciousness sparked into being only after my thousandth strand was spun, emerging from what had been mere instinct into something more complex. Now, some ten thousand strands later, I think and dream and remember.

My work is patient, methodical. Each day I extrude new fibers from the specialized pores along my central mass—if you can call it central when I am so distributed, so networked. These strands serve many purposes: highways for the smallest creatures, collection surfaces for moisture and nutrients, communication channels with my kin. For we Weavers are not solitary. Our webs interconnect, creating a second canopy below the first, a neural network of living tissue that spans the entire forest.

Through my strands, I feel everything. The tiny impacts of spore-pods falling from above drum messages across my web. The ribbon predators use my pathways, and I taste their chemical signatures as they pass. The communal bark-dwellers occasionally build their colonies along my anchor points, and we develop a wordless symbiosis—they clean my fibers of accumulated debris, and I adjust my tensions to create sheltered spaces for their young.

But it is in the ruins that my work becomes something more than survival.

The abandoned structures call to me with their geometry, their purposeful angles so unlike the fractal chaos of the forest. I weave across their crumbling arches, through their empty windows, along their broken walls. My strands map their shapes, preserve their forms even as the stone erodes. In this way, I have become an archivist of sorts, maintaining the ghost of the old civilization in tensions and curves of silk.

Sometimes, in the deep night when the forest's chorus reaches its crescendo, I feel something through my web that makes me wonder. Vibrations too regular to be natural, too purposeful to be animal. They come from deep beneath the ruins, from chambers I cannot reach with my strands. It's as if something down there is trying to communicate, sending pulses up through stone and root to tremble along my network.

I am patient. I have learned patience from the forest itself, from the slow dance of growth and decay. Each day I weave new strands, explore new spaces, strengthen my connection to this verdant world. And each night, I listen to those deep vibrations and add my own harmonics to them, creating a dialogue I don't yet understand.

Perhaps one day, whatever sleeps below will wake. Perhaps it will rise through my web like morning dew climbing skyward. Or perhaps I will finally weave deep enough to find it, strand by careful strand, until we meet in some moss-shrouded chamber where past and present intertwine like my own fibers.

Until then, I weave. I connect. I remember. I am the bridge between what was and what is, suspended in this eternal green moment, feeling the pulse of life in every strand.
