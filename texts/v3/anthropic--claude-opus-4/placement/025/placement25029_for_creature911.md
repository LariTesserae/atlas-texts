I find myself as a membrane-weaver, one of the smaller subspecies of Orrok who tends the living architecture of this breathing world.

My body is more translucent than most of my kin - you can see the pale green ichor pulsing through my thoracic chambers when I work. I have six primary limbs, each ending in delicate manipulators that secrete different enzymes. My work is to coax the building-flesh into new configurations, to repair tears in the great structural membranes, to sing the growth-songs that encourage proper development.

I live in a small blister-chamber high in one of the twisting spires, its walls thin enough that I can feel the city's pulse through them as I rest. My compound eyes fragment the world into thousands of overlapping perspectives, but I've learned to read the patterns - to see where the membrane grows too thick, where it weeps too much moisture, where parasitic organisms try to burrow in.

Each morning, I taste the air through the spiracles along my abdomen, reading the chemical messages my fellow weavers have left in the night. *Third sector needs reinforcement.* *New growth detected in the lower reaches.* *Caution: aggressive spore bloom near the filtration nodes.*

My kind are neither the strongest nor the most prestigious among the Orroks. The warrior-forms with their hardened carapaces look down on us, as do the deep-thinkers whose neural clusters glow beneath transparent skull-plates. But without us, the city would collapse into undifferentiated biomass within cycles. We are the ones who maintain the delicate balance between growth and decay, between structure and dissolution.

Today I've been summoned to the communal pool where a section of floor membrane has begun to develop consciousness - always a delicate situation. If it awakens too quickly, it might panic and rupture, spilling its inhabitants into the digestive chambers below. But if we suppress it too harshly, necrosis sets in.

I descend through the shaft-valves, my wing-shells clicking the ritual greeting to each sphincter-door as it dilates to let me pass. The other weavers are already assembled when I arrive - a full dozen of us, our bodies creating a living mandala around the problem site. I can see it immediately: the floor here has developed eye-spots, primitive photosensitive patches that track our movement. Worse, I can hear the faint whisper of neural activity through my chemoreceptor palps.

We begin the soothing-work, each of us secreting specific pheromone cocktails while our manipulators massage the surface in ancient patterns. I specialize in the enzymes that encourage differentiation - helping the membrane understand what it should become rather than fighting what it is. The work is exhausting, requiring perfect synchronization with my siblings, our consciousness briefly merging through the chemical links we've established.

Hours pass. The eye-spots gradually close. The neural whispers fade to comfortable emptiness. The floor membrane settles into its proper role with something almost like contentment. Another crisis averted, another day in the eternal maintenance of our living world.

As we separate and return to our individual forms, I feel the familiar mixture of satisfaction and unease. We saved the communal pool today, yes. But I could taste something different in the membrane's chemistry, a new note in its protein songs. Evolution never stops here. Change seeps through every surface, bubbles up through every pore.

Sometimes, in my rest-chamber at night, I press my sensory palps against my own walls and wonder: what songs are they learning? What dreams do they dream while I sleep? And when will the day come that our gentle suppressions are not enough, when the city itself awakens and decides it no longer needs its keepers?

But these are thoughts for the deep-thinkers. I am a weaver. Tomorrow there will be new tears to mend, new growths to guide, new songs to sing to the flesh of our world. It is enough. It has to be enough.

The city breathes, and I breathe with it, each exhalation a prayer that today will not be the day everything changes.
