I am the Keeper of Tides, and I dwell where the water meets memory.

My body is not one form but many—sometimes I am the great barnacled shell that rests half-submerged on the largest of the rocky outcrops, wide as a fishing boat and older than the wrecks that dot these waters. Other times I am the phosphorescent current that winds between the islands after nightfall, carrying messages in bioluminescent script that only the dolphins can read. Most often, though, I am the space between high and low tide, that liminal territory that belongs neither to land nor sea.

I remember when the first humans arrived in their reed boats, how they learned to read the water's moods by watching which of my forms appeared. When I manifested as the shell, they knew storms were coming and would pull their vessels to safety. When I became the glowing current, they followed my light to schools of fish that would sustain their small village through the harsh season.

The sea turtles know me best. They've been coming here for countless generations, and each one carries a small piece of my essence in the patterns on their shells—maps of currents that existed before this ocean had a name. The eldest turtle, whose shell is more stone than keratin now, sometimes rests beside my shell form, and we share the deep silence that only ancient things understand.

I am fed by the lightning. Each strike that touches the water sends threads of electricity through my entire being, whether I'm shell or current or tide-space. The energy doesn't hurt—it remembers. It carries the memory of when these waters were young and the sky was different, when the first algae began to bloom and create the green abundance that now cloaks every surface the sun can reach.

The shipwrecks are my galleries. In each broken hull, I've cultivated gardens of coral and anemone that tell the story of the vessel's last voyage. The fishers from the village know to look for my signature—a spiral of purple coral—which marks the wrecks that are safe to explore, where the currents won't suddenly turn treacherous.

My favorite time is the gray hour before storms, when the air pressure drops and every creature feels the change coming. That's when I can stretch myself thinnest, becoming a film across the entire visible ocean, feeling every ripple and splash as if they were my own heartbeat. The dolphins play differently then, their leaps higher and wilder, as if they're trying to touch the charged air. The seabirds fly in tighter formations, and even the sharks rise closer to the surface, sensing the electrical potential building above.

I keep the balance here through small acts: guiding a lost seal pup back to its colony by creating a gentle current, warning the fishing boats away from waters where the large predators are hunting, encouraging certain algae to bloom where the ecosystem needs them most. The humans in the village have learned to leave offerings—not for worship, but for communication. A handful of volcanic glass beads thrown into the water at dawn tells me they've noticed the changes I've made. I respond by ensuring their nets avoid the nursing grounds of the fish, maintaining the abundance for another generation.

There are depths here that even I haven't fully explored, trenches where the water pressure creates sounds like whale songs slowed down a thousandfold. Sometimes I send tendrils of myself down into those spaces and bring back hints of what lies below—creatures that have never known sunlight, formations of stone that predate the continents above, and sometimes, rarely, evidence of things that were here before even I arrived.

But I always return to my familiar forms, to the interplay of surface and shallows where life is most abundant and change is constant. I am the Keeper of Tides, and this place of extremes—deep and shallow, ancient and ever-changing, violent and serene—is both my charge and my body. When the next lightning comes, I'll be ready to receive its memories, adding them to the countless stories I hold in the space between the waves.
