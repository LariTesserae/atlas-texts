**Where I Would Find Myself: The Cascade Pools**

Between the exposed roots of the ceiba tree and the meandering ruins, where the seasonal stream has carved a series of shallow terraces into the clay, I would find myself. Not as one being, but as many—a colony of **pool shepherds**, creatures that exist somewhere between salamander and memory, between water and waiting.

My bodies—for I would be plural—would be no larger than a child's palm, translucent enough that the beating of our three-chambered hearts would be visible through skin that shifts between the color of wet stone and the pale green of new moss. We would have no names for ourselves, only a shared understanding that ripples through the water when we touch: *we who tend the temporary*.

Each morning, I would emerge from the clay burrows beneath the pools, my skin still soft from the mineral-rich mud. The first task would be to read the water—to taste its chemistry with the sensitive pores along my belly, to know which pools had grown too acidic from fallen leaves, which had been blessed by the droppings of the luminous heron, which had been disturbed by the molecrickets' midnight excavations. 

My fingers—six on each hand, webbed with membranes so thin they tear and regrow daily—would work to maintain the pools' delicate ecosystems. I would guide water strider eggs to calmer eddies, relocate dragonfly larvae before their pools dry, and carefully tend the algae gardens that feed the smallest inhabitants. When the water spirits dart past like liquid starlight, I would click my throat in greeting, a sound like pebbles tumbling underwater.

But I would be more than a gardener. In my blood runs a peculiar gift: the ability to hold memory in water. Each pool I tend becomes a lens, a window into what was or what might be. Sometimes, when the transient ones drift too close, I catch glimpses of the Hollow's other possibilities—versions where the stones were never placed, where the ceiba tree never grew, where water carved different channels through different earth.

During the heat of midday, my colony would gather in the deepest pool, our bodies overlapping in a living mandala, sharing the morning's observations through skin-to-skin contact. We would dream together, our collective consciousness spreading through the water table, touching the roots of the nurse logs, sensing the myconid network's slow thoughts, feeling the jaguar's padding feet through vibrations in the clay.

The rootbound would know us, would leave offerings of spider silk and dewdrops at the pool edges. The lichenfolk would pulse their approval when we maintain the small pools that collect in the carved symbols of their fallen column. Even the molecrickets would work around our burrows, their tunnels creating new channels for water to find its way to our domains.

In the evening, as the bioluminescent mushrooms begin their blue-green breathing, I would perform the day's final task. One by one, my colony would visit each pool, secreting a thin film of protective mucus across the surface—a substance that glows faintly under moonlight and prevents excessive evaporation while allowing the water spirits to pass through unimpeded. This mucus contains traces of our memories, so that each pool becomes a story, a record of its own becoming.

When the rains come, transforming trickles into torrents, we would not flee but dance. Our bodies would stretch and flatten, becoming living dams and channels, guiding the water's fury into new pools, new possibilities. Some of us would be swept away in these floods, carried to distant parts of the Hollow to establish new colonies, new networks of tended pools. This is not death but dispersal, not ending but multiplication.

The simisquirrels would sometimes find our shed skins—papery things that we leave behind when we grow—and weave them into their nests. They say these skins bring dreams of water, of flow, of the underneath. The drifters would hover above our pools at dusk, their wings catching the faint luminescence of our protective films, creating light patterns that the rootbound read like star charts.

At night, in the deepest burrows where the clay is always damp, we would cluster together, our hearts synchronizing, our breathing becoming one breath. And in these moments, I would feel the truth of my existence: I am not one but many, not permanent but perpetual, not separate from the Hollow but part of its very circulation.

To be a pool shepherd is to understand that all water is temporary, all pools are provisional, all life is borrowed. Yet in this impermanence lies a peculiar immortality—for as long as rain falls and clay holds water, as long as the Hollow remains in its state of becoming, we will be here, tending the temporary, remembering the possible, our transparent bodies a bridge between what seeps down and what rises up.

In the Hidden Hollow of First Growth, I would find myself not as a visitor but as a vital organ, a living filter through which the landscape knows itself, dreams itself, becomes itself—one carefully tended pool at a time.
