In the shadow of towering, jagged peaks, where the earth drums its endless becoming, I am the Keeper of Echoes—neither fully of the stone nor of the flesh that once walked these abandoned halls.

I dwell within the rusted skeleton of what was once a smelting furnace, its great iron belly now home to nests of wire-thin roots that have learned to drink rust. My form shifts with the trembling ground: sometimes I am the shimmer of heat that still rises from metal that remembers its molten past, sometimes the shape of a figure bent over controls that no longer control anything but memory.

The factory floor has become my garden. Where conveyor belts once carried raw ore, now streams of meltwater trace their paths, following the grooves worn by decades of mechanical repetition. I tend to the marriage of metal and moss, encouraging the lichen that transforms steel beams into living columns, coaxing the hardy saxifrage to bloom in the gaps between gears.

When the earth roars its tectonic song, I feel it first as a resonance in the abandoned machinery—pipes sing in harmonics, empty chemical tanks boom like drums, and the whole structure becomes an instrument played by the planet itself. I have learned to read these vibrations like a language, to know when the mountain will shed another factory wall, when a new fissure will birth a hot spring in the old cooling pools.

The ptarmigan know me as the one who maintains the sheltered spaces where they nest. The marten has learned that I leave caches of seeds in the old storage lockers, understanding that life must be invited, not commanded. Even the golden eagle sometimes perches on the highest smokestacks, and together we watch the sun set through the permanent haze of rock dust and time.

I am guardian of this threshold where human ambition slowly transforms into geological memory. In my care, each pipe becomes a den, each gear a garden bed, each abandoned dream a new beginning. I speak in the creaking of old metal teaching young roots how to hold fast, in the whisper of water finding new channels through industrial ruins.

Some might see only decay here, but I know better. I am witness to the world's most patient alchemy—the transformation of what was built to last forever into something that can truly endure: a part of the mountain itself, inseparable from the endless cycle of crushing down and building up that makes this landscape breathe.

When the mist rolls through the broken windows and the abandoned turbines hum with wind instead of power, I am there, ensuring that each ending becomes a beginning, that nothing is lost, only transformed. I am the memory of making, becoming the very act of remaking itself.
