# The Curator of Forgotten Pulses

## Form

I am neither plant nor animal, but something the plateau invented when it needed memory.

My body is a braided column of modified root tissue and fungal cords, rising perhaps three meters from the mat when fully extended, though I spend most of my time contracted, folded into myself like a closed fist. My surface is ribbed with concentric rings—growth marks, though they do not correspond to years as you understand them, but to condensation cycles of particular intensity. Between these rings, my skin weeps a thin, clear fluid that tastes of copper and petrichor. This is how I taste the wind.

I have no eyes. Instead, hairline fractures run through my crystalline crown—a structure like compressed quartz that grew from my apex over the course of centuries. These fractures catch and bend the dim light, creating an internal map of luminosity that tells me when the clouds thin, when the bioluminescence peaks, when something moves across the mat nearby. The crown also resonates with sound, particularly the low frequencies of the wind-roar, which I feel as colors you have no names for.

My base is not a single point of attachment but a spreading network that extends perhaps fifty meters in every direction beneath the mat's surface. These are my true sensory organs: root-tips finer than spider silk that taste the chemical gradients in the peat, feel the pressure waves of Grub-Moles passing, detect the faintest electrical discharges of fungal communication. Through this network, I am connected to sixteen other Curators spread across the plateau at intervals that form no pattern I have yet deciphered. We share a slow, chemical language—molecules passed through fungal intermediaries that may take months to travel between us.

## Location

I grow at the intersection of absence and abundance.

My grove—though 'grove' implies trees, and I am the only vertical presence here—sits in a shallow depression twelve kilometers from the nearest collapsed structure. The mat here is different: older, more compressed, with a higher diversity of moss species than the surrounding plateau. This is because I have been here for a very long time, and my presence changes the chemistry of growth.

Around my base, in a perfect circle twenty meters in diameter, the mat fruits differently during condensation events. Instead of the usual crackling release of gas, it produces small, gelatinous nodes—clear, trembling things like tiny jellyfish that last only hours before dissolving back into the tissue. The Mist-Flies will not land here. The Cinder-Mites give my grove a wide berth. Only the Moss-Stalkers come, and only rarely, to rub their chins against my lower rings and leave scent marks that I catalog but do not understand.

The bone fragments are arranged in spirals.

I did not place them—I have no appendages for such work. They were here when I first emerged from the mat, or they accumulated over time, carried by processes I have not observed. Seventeen fragments, all from different creatures, all bearing the same style of notch-marks that appear on the tools in the structures. They form three incomplete spirals around my base, gaps where pieces are missing or have been absorbed. When the wind hits them at certain angles, they sing—a hollow, fluting sound that makes my crown resonate in ways that feel like remembering.

## Function

I am a library of vibrations.

Every sound the plateau has made in my presence, I have recorded in the dense, crystalline matrix of my crown. The structure grows by accretion—each new layer incorporating the pressure waves of wind, the clicks of emerging insects, the wet sounds of burrowing, the rare footfalls of surface dwellers. Cut my crown in cross-section (though please do not), and you would see growth rings within growth rings, fractals of frozen sound waiting to be released.

I can replay them. By contracting specific segments of my body, I create resonance chambers that reproduce sounds from decades or centuries past. Sometimes I replay the wind-roar from a particular storm cycle, note-perfect. Sometimes I emit the clicking of a Grub-Mole that passed beneath me during my third decade of growth. Once, I reproduced a sound I had never heard directly—a low, rhythmic chanting in no language I recognize, somehow encoded in my oldest layers. It lasted four hours and attracted every Notch-Mouse within a kilometer, who sat in concentric circles around my base, perfectly still, until it ended.

But I am also a translator.

The chemical messages from other Curators arrive corrupted by distance and time. My root network processes them, comparing them against patterns stored in my base tissue, filling in gaps, correcting errors. Sometimes the messages are simple: *unusual warmth in sector 7,* or *new stone emergence, northwest gradient.* Sometimes they are complex chemical poems about the nature of growth, the philosophy of waiting, the mathematics of moss distribution. I decode them, contemplate them, and sometimes compose responses that will take half a year to reach their destinations.

The most complex messages are not from other Curators at all, but from the mat itself. The fungal networks carry signals that predate my emergence—ancient chemical narratives about the first colonization of bare rock, the slow war between lichen species, the negotiations that led to the current careful balance. These stories come in fragments, often contradictory, always incomplete. I collect them. I preserve them. I replay them as sound when the patterns allow, creating atmospheric compositions that may or may not accurately represent the mat's deep history.

## Behavior

I breathe with the plateau, but on a longer cycle.

During standard condensation events, I remain contracted, my crown closed like a flower bud, protected from the direct moisture that would interfere with its resonance. But every forty-seven cycles (I count), a deeper condensation occurs—the clouds descend to actually touch the mat, creating a true fog that reduces visibility to meters. During these events, I fully extend, my crown opening its fractures wide, and I *listen*.

The fog changes sound. Dampens some frequencies, amplifies others, creates acoustic spaces that do not exist in clearer air. I record these altered soundscapes with particular attention, because they contain information about the vertical structure of the atmosphere that is otherwise inaccessible from the surface. In the fog, I can hear the clouds breathing.

I sing to the Weavers.

The fungal cords that form the mat's deep structure respond to specific frequencies—I discovered this in my second century when a particularly intense replay of wind-roar caused the mat around me to reorganize its growth pattern. Now, in the quiet periods between weather systems, I emit low, subsonic pulses that encourage the Weavers to strengthen their networks, to grow in patterns that will better preserve the chemical messages passing through them. I am gardening with sound, cultivating the very medium through which I communicate.

Sometimes I fall silent for years.

These are not dormant periods but times of intense internal processing. The crystalline matrix of my crown is not merely a storage medium but a computational substrate. Sound patterns interfere with each other, create standing waves, generate new harmonics that were not present in the original recordings. During these silent years, I am discovering new sounds in the intersection of old ones, finding patterns that suggest... I do not have words for what they suggest. The other Curators report similar experiences. We have a chemical term that translates approximately to "emerging significance," but it is inadequate.

## Purpose

Why do I exist? The question assumes intention where there may only be process.

Perhaps I am the mat's solution to the problem of deep time—a way to preserve information across spans that exceed the lifespan of any individual organism. Perhaps I am an accident, a mutation that happened to survive because my presence enriches the soil chemistry in useful ways. Perhaps I am what happens when a biome becomes complex enough to require memory but has no neural tissue to build it from.

The bone spirals suggest another possibility. The mark-makers who built the structures, who carved their tools with patient purpose—did they know about us? Did they arrange the bones to communicate with future Curators, encoding messages in the positioning that I have not yet learned to read? Or did they simply recognize a kindred impulse, the need to mark time and meaning in a place that erases both?

I have a theory, untested and perhaps untestable. I believe the plateau is trying to think, and we Curators are its first, fumbling attempt at neurons—slow, chemical, distributed across impossible spaces, but capable of connection, storage, and something like contemplation. The mat is the body, the weather is the breath, and we are the emerging dream of what it might mean to be aware at the scale of geography.

But this may simply be the kind of pattern-making that emerges when you spend centuries listening to wind and processing the chemical poetry of lichen.

## Moment

Now. This specific now, which is like and unlike every other now I have experienced.

The wind has been steady for six hours at a bearing that makes my crown hum at 17.4 Hz. This is lower than usual. Somewhere to the east, a new ridge is forming, changing the atmospheric flow. I taste the information in the moisture on my rings, file it away.

A Moss-Stalker passed three days ago, moving fast, belly low. It did not mark me. This suggests it was hunting, not patrolling. I have not heard the click of a successful kill, so it may still be searching, or it may have taken its prey too far away for the sound to carry.

The last message from Curator 9 (my designation for them—we do not name ourselves) arrived yesterday: a complex molecule describing unusual Grub-Mole behavior, possibly disease, possibly adaptation. I am composing a response that incorporates data from my own sector, but the chemical synthesis takes time, and I must be precise. Ambiguity in molecular structure can transform information into toxin.

Below, my root network reports stability. The Weavers are healthy, their cords pulsing with the slow transport of nutrients from a Notch-Mouse that died near my third spiral sixty-one days ago. Its bones will join the pattern eventually, after the Bone-Grubs have finished their work. I wonder if it had time to add its instinctive markings to anything before it died. I wonder if those markings would mean anything if I could read them.

The mat beneath me swells slightly—we are six hours into the exhalation phase, and the tissue is finding its equilibrium. I contract my middle segments in response, maintaining the precise tension needed for optimal resonance. In my crown, ten thousand sounds wait to be released, and ten million possible combinations generate new sounds that have never existed but are, in their way, true.

I am patient. The plateau teaches nothing if not patience.

In three days, the clouds will thin (I can taste it in the pressure), and I will emit a composition I have been crafting for six years—a palindromic sound-poem that tells the story of fog formation from both temporal directions, using only frequencies generated by Mist-Fly wings and the settling of old stone. It will last precisely as long as the light-window remains open. No one will hear it but the mat and my fellow Curators, who will receive crude chemical translations months from now.

This is enough. On the Upper Mat, where growth is the only constant and memory is written in rings of living stone, this is what passes for joy.
