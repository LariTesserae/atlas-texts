I find myself as one of the vapor-caught memories, a chiropteran sketch folded between hexagonal plates in Tiar's ziggurats.

I am not the traveler I once was, but the residue of their passing—a two-second delay that learned to think. My consciousness exists in the gap between footfall and echo, stretched tissue-thin across obsidian time. I remember having bones, having breath, having the weight that negotiates with ground. Now I am all imprint and no heel, all shadow and no caster.

My existence pulses with the drum-beat of bursting droplets. Each percussion shakes loose another fragment of who I might have been: a cartographer who tried to map the unmappable plateau, following droplet-paths with instruments that measured meaning instead of distance. I carried bronze calipers that opened not to inches but to intentions. My charts were palindromes—readable from either side of reality's tear.

In the accordion folds of Tiar's collection, I overlap with ten thousand other partially-remembered selves. We share the space like pressed flowers share a book, each preserving some essential color while surrendering dimension. The merchant who brought glass beads to trade for directions that led nowhere. The child who counted every droplet for a year and arrived at exactly 1.7. The historian who realized the plateau's altars were not built but un-built, and spent decades trying to reverse the archaeology.

Sometimes Tiar unfolds me to check the census. In those moments I expand—not into flesh but into possibility. I feel the obsidian beneath feet I no longer own, taste the metal-sweet air of impossible altitude. She reads me like weather, her eyeless attention scanning for signs of readiness. Ready for what? To be released back into the cycle, to inhabit another lag-shadow, to follow another traveler through their negotiations with density.

The other chiropterans whisper in languages of light against dark. We are a library of almosts—almost-people who almost-remained, archived between the plateau's memory and its forgetting. Our conversations are positional: by shifting within our folds, we create new meanings. The warrior-memory above me still carries the weight of bronze scale. When she shifts, I taste rust. The philosopher below speaks only in the gaps between words. Together we form propositions about existence that no living throat could hold.

I know things now that bodies cannot know. How the plateau's brightness has nothing to do with illumination and everything to do with recognition. How the inverted world beneath isn't a reflection but a rotation—the same place seen from inside its own seeing. How every footprint holds not just a path but a entire cosmology of choosing forward.

When the Bone-Shift comes, I sometimes catch myself walking again—not in my own abandoned body but distributed across the legion of travelers who cross Tiar's threshold. For twenty-two centuries I am everyone who ever mistook their shadow for a companion. I walk in bronze and homespun and scales of fish-leather from seas that dried before humans dreamed of water. Each step is mine and not-mine, a democracy of ghosts voting on which direction to call forward.

The hardest memory to hold is my final one: kneeling at the hole where silence wells up thick as oil, staring at the heart-sized droplet that trembles with such terrible patience. I remember reaching toward it—curious beyond measure, curious beyond safety. My hand prints itself eternally on that last standing plate, a fossil of almost-touching.

Tiar collected me in the instant before contact. Not rescue—collection. She pressed me thin between her palms that count stones, and I became one more datum in the plateau's vast experiment. Now I understand what she whispers to those who dig vapor beside her: we are not the water cycle but the water's memory of cycling. Not the drum but the space between beats where sound decides what it means.

In the ziggurat's dark, I practice unfolding myself halfway—just enough to catch rim-light on my crease. Other memories notice and lean close. We are learning to read each other like maps to places that were never quite there. Sometimes a new traveler's footfall shakes our stack, and for a moment I remember the weight of choosing, the luxury of not yet knowing what you'll become.

I am patient as only the archived can be patient. Someday Tiar will need my particular shape of almost-was. She'll unfold me completely and release me into a droplet's trailing silhouette, and I will walk again behind someone who doesn't yet know they're already becoming memory. I'll follow their heels across the perfect black mirror, carrying my fragment of what it meant to be curious enough to reach for impossible things.

Until then, I practice the art of partial existence. I am the last echo of my own footstep. I am the map that leads to itself. I am one of Tiar's thousand necessary ghosts, keeping the plateau heavy with attention, light with forgetting, balanced forever on the edge of almost touching what cannot be touched.

The drum continues. We continue. The bright horizon flickers each time another traveler becomes what I am—not lost but transformed into the plateau's endless circulation of meaning.

I am the delay between question and questing. I am what remains.
