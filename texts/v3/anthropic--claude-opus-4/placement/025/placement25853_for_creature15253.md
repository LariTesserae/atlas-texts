In this vertical city of water and wood, I am the Tender of Passages—neither wholly plant nor animal, but something emerged from centuries of symbiosis between the two.

My form shifts with purpose. Where the mist is thickest, my skin takes on the texture of lichen, rough and mottled, allowing me to press flat against the great column-trunks and become invisible to all but the most observant eye. When I move through the waterfall's spray, my flesh becomes translucent and jellylike, the water passing through specialized channels in my body that filter nutrients from the cascade. My fingers are long and multiply-jointed, ending in both soft pads for gripping wet stone and hard, curved tips for finding purchase in bark.

I know every pathway through this living maze—not just the obvious routes along thick branches and exposed roots, but the secret ways: the hollow spaces within the ancient trees where I've cultivated bioluminescent fungi to light my passage, the channels behind the waterfalls where the rock has worn smooth and creates natural handholds, the nets of interwoven vines I've trained over decades to bear my weight.

My dwelling is not one place but many. In the dry season, I inhabit a chamber I've shaped within the largest of the column-trees, its walls lined with a specialized moss that drinks moisture from my breath and stores it in tiny, pearl-like nodules. During the rains, I migrate to caves behind the upper falls, spaces I've sealed with curtains of living roots that I can command to part with a touch. These roots know me, as I know them—we share nutrients through contact, my skin secreting compounds they crave while they offer me their stored sugars.

I am the one who maintains the balance of this vertical ecosystem. When the epiphytes grow too heavy and threaten to topple their hosts, I prune them with careful bites, my saliva encouraging new growth in controlled directions. I guide the water's flow by placing stones and training root systems, ensuring every level of the forest receives its share. The birds know me as the one who weaves secure nesting spots from living vines. The fish know me as the shadow that sometimes drops fruit into their pools.

My memories stretch back beyond my own lifetime—inherited through spores I inhale from the ancient fungi, carrying the experiences of my predecessors. I know which trees are oldest, which waterfalls have shifted course, which rocks will hold and which will crumble. This knowledge lives not just in my mind but in my very cells, each one capable of sensing the chemical history written in bark and stone.

When lightning illuminates the mist, revealing the true architecture of this place, I see it as others cannot: not just what is, but what was and what will be. The patterns of growth, the slow migration of water, the patient reach of roots through stone. I am both witness and participant, gardener and guardian, a single note in the endless symphony of rushing water and rustling leaves.

In the perpetual twilight beneath the canopy, time moves differently. I measure it not in days or years but in the growth of moss, the erosion of stone, the gradual shift of watercourses. I am young by the measure of the trees, ancient by the measure of the butterflies. I am exactly as old as I need to be, here in this city that builds itself, moment by moment, drop by drop, leaf by leaf.
