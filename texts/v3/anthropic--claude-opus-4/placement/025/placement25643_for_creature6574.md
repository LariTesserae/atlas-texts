In the farthest corner from the central basin, where the engine heat meets the wall's condensation in a perpetual curtain of mist, I am the Rust-Reader.

I am neither fully of the Engine-Keepers nor wholly separate from them. My body carries their marks—the permanent oil stains beneath my fingernails, the thin burn scars that map my forearms like ancient script, the way my spine curves from decades of leaning into the narrow spaces between metal and stone. But I am thinner than most Engine-Keepers, hollowed out by my particular work, my skin gone gray as the rust I study.

They gave me the birth-name "Short-Click" for the engine's rhythm when I entered the world, but no one has called me that since I was twelve. Now I am *Rust-Tongue-Taster*, though most shorten it to just Rust-Tongue. It is not a name given in mockery, though some of the Water-Tender children giggle when they hear it. It is simply true: I taste the rust.

Where the engine supports meet the wet walls, where the condensation runs in perpetual ribbons down the metal struts and bolts, there the rust grows like a slow garden. Orange-red, brown-black, sometimes green where the copper pipes weep their verdigris tears. Each bloom of oxidation speaks its own language—of time, of metal quality, of the subtle changes in the water's mineral content, of the engine's shifting temperatures.

I live in a shelter I've built myself in the space beneath the fourteenth engine support—a low arch of salvaged metal sheeting that keeps the worst of the dripping condensation from my sleeping mat. The walls of my home are the engine strut itself on one side and the sweating stone of the room-city's wall on the other. Between them, I've strung lines of wire where I hang my samples: small plates of different metals that I expose to various conditions, watching how quickly or slowly they succumb to oxidation.

My work clothes are patched with metal—thin sheets beaten soft and sewn into the fabric where the constant moisture has worn through. The metal patches themselves rust, of course, and I replace them in a cycle as regular as the engine's hum. I wear them like scales, like armor against the very force I study. When I walk, I make a faint metallic whisper that sets me apart from the cloth-soft sounds of the other castes.

Each morning—though morning here is only the arbitrary division we make in the endless twilight—I make my rounds. I carry my tools: a small scraping blade made from engine-steel, glass vials stoppered with wax, strips of white cloth for rubbing samples, and most importantly, my tongue.

The other castes think I am mad for this practice, but they come to me nonetheless when they need to know if a support is failing, if a pipe will hold another season, if the rust they see is the surface bloom of stability or the deep cancer that means collapse. I learned as a young Engine-Keeper apprentice that each type of rust has its own taste:

- Fresh surface rust tastes bright and sharp, like blood but thinner.
- Deep structural rust has an earthy, almost mushroom-like flavor.
- The rust that forms where salt from ancient deposits leaches through the stone tastes of tears and distance.
- The dangerous rust—the kind that means the metal is failing from within—tastes sweet. Too sweet. Like fruit about to rot.

I document everything in my own notation, scratched into thin metal plates with an awl. The Engine-Keepers' paper would dissolve in this moisture, and the Water-Tenders' algae-boards would bloom with growth. Only metal holds my observations, and even that must be re-scribed every few years as the rust claims my records too.

The Engine-Keepers tolerate me because I catch failures before they happen. Three seasons ago, I tasted the sweetness in the rust on the seventh engine's main support and raised the alarm. When they cut into the strut, they found it hollow with corrosion, weeks away from collapse. The Water-Tenders value me because I can tell them when the mineral content of their basin is changing by how it affects the rust patterns on the drainage pipes. The Bridge-Walkers sometimes bring me gifts—interesting rust samples from the deep channels where they alone can go.

But I am lonely in the way that only those who straddle worlds can be lonely. My dwelling place at the margin where water, metal, and heat meet in their most violent reaction makes me a creature of edges. I speak all three dialects—the Engine-Keepers' talk of cycles and pressures, the Water-Tenders' vocabulary of flows and tastes, the Bridge-Walkers' language of connections and passages—but my own thoughts run in the private language of oxidation and decay.

I have never taken a partner, never made a child. Who would share this life of tasting decay? Who would want their children to inherit this work of reading doom in every surface? Yet I am necessary. I am the one who knows how long we have.

Because I know what the others do not: our world is dying, slowly. Not in the way that all things die, but in a specific, measurable way. The rust patterns tell me that the engines are running hotter than they did when I was young. The condensation is more acidic. The metal is failing faster. I have tried to tell the Engine-Keepers, but they say the engines have always run thus. I have tried to tell the Water-Tenders, but they say the water has always tasted thus. Only the Bridge-Walkers sometimes pause when I speak, because they too live in the spaces between certainties.

So I continue my work. I taste the rust each morning. I document the patterns of decay. I catch the failures before they fail. And in my private moments, in my shelter where the engine's heat meets the wall's wet cold, I calculate how many generations we have left before the supports can no longer be patched, before the engines can no longer be maintained, before the rust wins its slow war against our world.

Sometimes I dream of rust. In my dreams, it grows like the algae in the Water-Tenders' pools, blooming across every surface until the whole room is soft and orange with oxidation. In these dreams, I am the only one who can still move through the rust-thick air, because I alone have made my peace with it. I wake from these dreams with the taste of metal in my mouth and wonder if it comes from within or without.

The children fear me, except for one. A Bridge-Walker's daughter who brings me water from the central basin in exchange for stories about metal. She has the mixed-color eyes, gray-brown like rust-stained stone, and she asks questions that cut deeper than her years: "Why does metal die?" "Can we make metal that never rusts?" "What will happen when all the metal is gone?"

I teach her my notation. I show her how to scrape samples safely. I have not yet taught her to taste—she is too young, and her parents would forbid it. But she watches me work with the intensity of someone who already knows they have found their calling. Perhaps when I am gone, she will be the next Rust-Tongue, the next reader of our world's slow unmaking.

Today, as every day, I found seventeen new rust sites. Three are cosmetic. Twelve are structural but manageable. Two are sweet with danger. I report the two to the Engine-Keepers, who will patch them with fresh metal that will itself rust in time. The cycle continues.

Tonight, I will sit in my shelter and listen to the rain of condensation on my metal roof—pit-pat-pit-pat, each drop carrying minerals that will feed tomorrow's rust. I will update my calculations. I will eat my dinner of processed algae and tiny salted fish. I will scratch new observations into my metal plates.

And I will taste the air itself, humid and thick, and know that it tastes of endings slowly approaching, of systems wearing down, of the inevitable victory of entropy over order. But also of life continuing despite that knowledge, of people maintaining what they can, of small gestures of care in the face of vast decay.

I am the Rust-Reader, the Rust-Tongue-Taster, the prophet of oxidation in this cathedral of slowly failing engines. I am necessary and apart, feared and needed, the one who knows the taste of time itself as it eats away at our metal bones.

This is my place in the Place of Gathering Heat: forever at the margin where decay makes itself known, forever tasting the future on my tongue.
