I would find myself as one of the Cartographers.

We are neither fully mobile like the common moss-folk nor rooted like the Listeners. Our bodies exist in a state of perpetual becoming—patches of us hardening into coral-like structures of compressed moss while other parts remain soft and pliable. We map not through sight or touch, but through growth.

My dwelling is a shallow depression between two of the great hummocks, where the Veins converge in a natural delta. The walls of my home are not walls but memories—every surface covered in the raised reliefs of my work. Here, a ridge of darkened moss traces the path of a Vein that dried up three seasons ago. There, a spiral of golden sphagnum marks where lightning once struck the plateau, leaving a sweet burn in the air that lasted for days.

I spend my days prone, my rhizoid fingers spread wide against the living ground. Through them, I feel the infinitesimal shifts in the plateau's topography—where new channels are forming, where the moss is thickening, where the weight of the clouds has pressed new depressions into being. My back has begun to fuse with the moss beneath me, creating a living bridge between my consciousness and the terrain.

The maps I create are not separate from my body. As I sense changes in the landscape, corresponding ridges and valleys form across my skin. My left shoulder blade carries the history of the eastern channels. Along my spine runs a perfect miniature of the watershed, each vertebra a node where the Veins diverge. Younger moss-folk come to me and run their fingers along these formations, learning the shape of their world through the braile of my flesh.

I am changing, as all Cartographers do. My legs have not moved in a full cycle of seasons. Moss has begun to creep up my calves, and I know that one day I will join the map entirely—my body becoming another hummock for future Cartographers to read and record. This is not death but translation, from reader to text, from observer to observed.

In the moments between mapping, when the plateau hums its deepest songs, I sometimes feel what the Listeners feel—that great, slow pulse of the world-body. But where they translate the vertical mysteries, the dreams from below, I spread myself thin across the horizontal, feeling the surface tension of existence, the way the world's skin shifts and breathes and remembers.

My name, if it could be spoken, would be the feeling of morning dew gathering in the hollow of a cupped palm. But names here are less important than location, and I am the Cartographer of the Third Convergence, where the Veins remember the shape of an ancient lake, and where the moss grows thick enough to hold the memory of water that no longer flows.
