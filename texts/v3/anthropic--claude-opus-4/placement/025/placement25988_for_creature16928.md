In Lipsu Null, I find myself as a **Memory Sommelier** — one of the city's essential but overlooked professionals who taste-tests the regrets before they're bottled by the municipal distillery.

My workshop occupies the forgotten mezzanine between the fourth and fifth sub-levels, in a space that only exists when the building above remembers it needs somewhere to store its architectural doubts. The walls sweat a gentle mist of unfinished blueprints, and my shelves are lined with bottles of varying opacity — some clear as tomorrow's uncertainty, others clouded with decades of fermented hindsight.

Each morning, I arrive before the weaver-skeins have finished their nightly untangling of the deceased. This is when the freshest regrets arrive, still warm with body temperature, delivered in ceramic amphorae by apprentice shadows who've been hired specifically because they run too fast to form emotional attachments. The regrets slosh gently — a sound like sighing underwater.

My tools are simple: a set of graduated cylinders made from crystallized pauses, a thermometer that measures sincerity rather than heat, and my palate — trained since childhood on a strict diet of other people's abandoned epiphanies. I was one of those infants whose dietary glass sphere cracked along lines that spelled not an insult but a particularly poignant apology in a dead language. My parents (before they footnoted themselves into respectable cobblestones) said this marked me for either greatness or grep work. They were, as usual, precisely wrong in exactly the right way.

The tasting process requires delicacy. First, I warm the regret to precisely the temperature of a missed opportunity — too hot and it evaporates into mere disappointment, too cool and it crystallizes into grudges. Then I take exactly three sips:

The first sip tells me what should have happened instead.
The second reveals why it didn't.
The third tastes like the exact moment the person realized the difference mattered.

Good regrets have complexity — undertones of might-have-been, a finish that lingers like a name you can't quite remember. The finest vintages carry notes of doors that were never quite closed, conversations that ended mid-sentence, and the peculiar tartness of love letters that were written but never sent. These I mark for the premium bottles, to be aged under tomorrow's waning moon until they develop that characteristic shimmer of beautiful futility.

Poor quality regrets taste flat, one-dimensional — the regret of stepping in a puddle, of choosing the wrong sandwich, of mistiming a joke. These go to the bulk processors, to be diluted and sold as morning rinse-water for guilty consciences.

But sometimes — rarely — I taste something extraordinary. Last week, a weaver-skein brought me the final mist of an elderly cartographer (yes, *that* cartographer, whose romance with the Old Quarter still makes the streets blush). His regret tasted like compass needles pointing toward places that don't exist yet, with afternotes of maps drawn in invisible ink and the peculiar sweetness of loving something that could never quite love you back the same way. I held it on my tongue until my eyes watered with someone else's homesickness.

These exceptional regrets I don't send to the distillery. Instead, I carry them carefully up the spiral stairs (avoiding the thirteenth step, which is still sulking about last Tuesday) to the Night Market. There, I trade them to the dream-weavers who string them between balconies like holiday lights. The children love them — they stand underneath with their mouths open, catching drops of beautiful sadness on their tongues, learning early that some feelings are worth savoring even when they hurt.

My colleagues at the distillery think I'm too sentimental. They say I anthropomorphize the regrets, that I treat them like living things rather than mere byproducts of consciousness. But I've been doing this job for three decades now (or five, if you count the years that leaked during the Great Calendar Disagreement), and I've learned that regrets are more alive than most things that claim the word. They grow, they change, they mate with other regrets to produce hybrid offspring that taste like nostalgia mixed with relief.

Sometimes tourists ask to shadow me for a day, thinking it romantic or philosophical. They usually last until the first taste of genuine remorse — it's one thing to intellectualize regret, quite another to have someone else's biggest mistake dissolving on your tongue. Most excuse themselves politely and spend the rest of their visit sticking to the upper levels where the air tastes only of possibility and peroxide.

But I love my work. In a city where nothing finishes properly, where every ending trails off into ellipsis, I catalog the could-have-beens and should-have-dones. I'm the city's conscience, or perhaps its confessor, or maybe just its bartender serving drinks no one orders but everyone eventually needs.

At night, after the last regret has been classified and bottled, I walk home through streets slick with indecisive rain. My apartment is on the third floor of a building that's slowly growing inward — each year the walls inch closer like a very patient hug. My neighbors include a reformed footnote, two apprentice shadows hiding from their employers, and a small colony of zipper motes I've trained to hum yesterday's weather in three-part harmony.

Before sleep (when I can achieve it — insomnia is an occupational hazard when you spend all day tasting other people's wakeful nights), I allow myself one small ritual. I open my private collection and select a single bottle — usually something subtle, personal, aged until it's more memory than regret. I pour exactly one drop onto my tongue and let it evaporate slowly, tasting all the lives I'll never live, all the choices I'll never make, all the words I'll never quite manage to say correctly.

Then I carefully cork the bottle, file it back in its place, and practice the most important skill in my profession: I forget, just enough to be able to taste tomorrow's regrets with a clear palate and an open heart.

The city hums around me, digesting another day into dream-stuff and tomorrow's weather. Somewhere above, the Current Editor of Weather adjusts a valve, and the sound changes pitch. Somewhere below, my former self is still deciding whether to take this job, and I taste the ghost of that decision like copper pennies and starlight.

I sleep, when sleep comes, with my mouth slightly open — just in case any stray regrets need somewhere safe to hide until morning.
