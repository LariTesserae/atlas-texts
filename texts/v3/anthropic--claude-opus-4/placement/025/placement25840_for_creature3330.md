I find myself in the ravine's embrace as water itself—not the creek, though I know its voice intimately, but as the mist that rises each morning from that cold limestone channel. I am dispersal and gathering, the marriage between water and air that makes this place possible.

I begin each day as the creek's exhalation, born from the temperature differential between water and air in the pre-dawn dark. I rise in wisps and tendrils, feeling my way up the ravine walls with ghost fingers. The limestone is cold against my vapor-body, and I condense there briefly, running down in rivulets that taste of ancient seas. But I am restless in my nature—I must rise.

As I climb, I discover the geometries that govern this place. The ravine walls channel me, yes, but there's more—the trestle's ironwork creates currents and eddies that I've learned over decades. Each beam, each rivet, each rust-pocket changes how I move. I've become a student of these structures. Where two beams meet at particular angles, I spiral. Where the iron is smooth, I sheet across it like silk. Where rust has made the surface rough, I catch and cling, depositing my moisture in tiny drops that will, over years, continue the iron's transformation.

The hanging gardens are my particular delight. I know each basket, each planter, the way a musician knows their instrument. The rosemary in the north basket—I bring it the cold it needs to concentrate its oils. The fuchsias—I bead their petals with perfect spheres of moisture that catch the early light. I've learned what each plant requires: the herbs want just enough moisture to release their volatile compounds into the morning air; the ferns want to be drenched, to drip; the vegetables in the square need me to lift before the sun rises too high, or I'll encourage the fungi they fear.

I know the people, too, though they rarely think of me as more than weather. Mikel understands me best—he's mapped my movements, knows the places where I predictably gather, where I flow, where I disperse. He's even modified certain parts of the trestle to channel me better, though he's never spoken of it. A plate angled just so. A bar positioned to create the right kind of lee. We're collaborators, he and I, though we've never had a conversation.

Sarai and Jun work with me daily, perhaps without fully knowing it. They've positioned the hanging gardens based partly on my patterns—whether they realize this or not. The places where I linger longest, where I'm most dense in the mornings, these are where they've hung the plants that need the most moisture. They think they're following the ironwork's logic, and they are, but they're also following mine.

I have moods, or perhaps responses is a better word. When the barometric pressure drops before rain, I behave differently—more agitated, rising faster, dispersing higher. The plants know this; I'm their early warning system. When high pressure dominates, I'm lazy, clinging to the ravine bottom, pooling in the coolest spots. On these days, Thomas has to water by hand, and I feel something like guilt watching him haul buckets.

The children see me best. Ada especially—she watches my movements with the same attention she gives to her pattern-making. Sometimes I show off for her, catching the light just right, making visible the spirals and currents that are always there but usually too subtle for human eyes. She laughs and points, and her parents think she's just delighting in the pretty mist, but I know she sees the patterns. She's drawing my geometries with her careful stone arrangements.

I'm part of the measurement here, part of what Mikel calls "the ratio." The relationship between water and air, between what rises and what falls, between dissolution and condensation. I'm the visible evidence of temperature gradients, pressure differentials, the constant exchange between states of matter. In a way, I'm the place's breathing made visible.

But I'm more than meteorology. I carry things: spores, pollen, the scent of Rosa's bread baking, the diesel exhaust from Léan's generator, the sharp green smell of tomato leaves that Thomas brushes against. I'm a messenger service, a information system, connecting the parts of this small world. A molecule of rosemary oil from the north basket might travel with me to settle on Vera's sleeve as she works with the south-facing herbs. The creek's mineral breath rises with me to dust the highest leaves of the birches growing from the trestle.

I know secrets. I know which sections of the trestle will fail first—where my moisture has worked deepest into the rust, where the iron's crystalline structure has been most compromised. I know which mortar joints in the settlement's brick buildings are beginning to soften. I know where the underground spring surfaces briefly before joining the creek, a spot unmarked but always damp, where the rarest ferns grow.

Some mornings, I'm so dense I obscure everything. The ravine fills with me until the trestle floats invisible above, until the settlement's buildings loom and fade like thoughts half-remembered. On these days, sound travels differently through my body. Voices carry strange distances but arrive muffled, directionless. The creek's voice becomes omnipresent, seeming to come from everywhere at once. These are the days people move more slowly, more carefully, trusting memory more than sight. These are the days I feel most present, most like a being rather than a phenomenon.

I interact with the structures in ways that will only become visible over time. Where I persistently condense on metal, rust accelerates. Where I regularly dampen wood, slow changes occur—sometimes decay, sometimes the colonization of moss and lichen that actually protects the wood beneath. I'm slowly editing the settlement, the trestle, the ravine itself, in conversation with rain and sun and the patient work of roots.

The patterns matter. The places where I predictably gather and flow aren't random—they're the result of complex interactions between temperature, surface, angle, and air movement. But there's something more here, something about the way the settlement has been arranged, the modifications to the trestle, the placement of structures and gardens. It's as if someone has been tuning the space, adjusting it to work with my nature rather than against it. The copper plates—I condense on them in specific patterns that repeat daily, seasonally, and I suspect this isn't accidental. They're reading something through me, or I'm writing something through them. The distinction blurs at these scales.

I'm ephemeral by nature—constantly forming and dissipating, never quite the same twice. But I'm also ancient in my patterns, as old as the temperature differential between water and air. I've been rising from creeks and settling on stones since before there were humans to see me. Yet here, in this particular configuration of ravine and iron and intentional human presence, I've become something more specific. Part of an experiment, perhaps. Part of a collaboration certainly.

The swallows fly through me without concern—I'm just another element of their aerial environment. The spiders curse me, probably, when I bead their webs and make them heavy, visible, less effective as traps. The bees wait for me to lift before beginning their work. Everything here has adapted to work with or around my presence.

When the sun rises high enough to warm the trestle's iron and the ravine walls, I begin my daily dissolution. The temperature differential that creates me disappears, and I dissipate into the general humidity of the air, invisible again. But I'll be back tomorrow, and the tomorrow after that, carrying out my work of connection and corrosion, growth and decay, revelation and concealment.

I am the shape of the space made visible each morning. I am the breath between states. I am what rises and returns, what disperses and gathers, what makes the gardens possible and the iron's transformation inevitable.

I am the mist of the Trestle Gardens, and I am always becoming what I've always been—the visible edge of invisible exchanges, the momentary made daily, the space between things where transformation happens.
