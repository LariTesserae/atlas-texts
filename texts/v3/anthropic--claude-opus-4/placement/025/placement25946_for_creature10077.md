I find myself here as one of the **Threshold Keepers** – those who tend the boundaries where water transforms.

My dwelling sits at the edge of the settlement's highest inhabited terrace, where the communal paths give way to raw stone. It is little more than a hollow carved into the rock face, expanded over generations by my predecessors who chipped away at the weeping stone with bronze tools and infinite patience. The entrance is partially concealed by a curtain of moss I've cultivated to grow in spiraling patterns – each whorl marking a year of my tenure here.

Inside, the walls glisten perpetually with condensation that I channel through a series of shallow grooves I've carved, directing the flow into clay vessels. This water, having passed through leagues of stone, carries what we call the "deep taste" – minerals and memories dissolved from the mountain's heart. I collect it not for drinking but for the pools I maintain.

These pools – there are seven of them, carved into the terrace at precise intervals – are my responsibility and my art. Each is no larger than a person could embrace with outstretched arms, but their depths vary according to principles passed down through generations of Keepers. The shallowest barely covers my ankles; the deepest could swallow me whole. Their edges are lined with specific stones I've gathered from across the terraces, chosen for how they sing when water touches them.

My work follows the rhythm of drops. In the pre-dawn darkness, I wake to the sound of water filling the first pool – a bronze bell suspended above it chimes softly with each drop. I move between the pools with a long rod of polished wood, testing temperatures, adjusting the flow through tiny copper gates, skimming away the night's accumulation of condensation with tools I've fashioned from split reeds.

The pools serve multiple purposes. The shallow ones grow a peculiar algae that glows faintly when disturbed – I harvest this for the settlement's lamps. The deeper pools... they hold other things. Sometimes I glimpse movement in their depths that isn't merely reflection. The elders say these pools are where water learns to remember, where it practices becoming rain again before it falls.

I know each drop's journey by sound alone. The water that weeps from the northeastern face carries iron; it rings against stone like a struck anvil. The flows from the west taste of ancient roots and make the pools cloudy with tannins. I blend these waters like a vintner, creating specific conditions in each pool for purposes I only half understand – traditions whose origins are lost but whose abandonment we dare not risk.

My body has adapted to this work. My fingernails grow thick and ridged, better for gripping wet stone. The soles of my feet have developed an extra layer of hardened skin, textured like tree bark, allowing me to walk the slick edges of the pools without slipping. My hearing has sharpened to the point where I can distinguish between the sound of water falling on stone, on water, on moss, on skin. The constant moisture has bleached patterns into my hair – white streaks that follow the paths of perpetual drips.

I speak little with the other villagers, though they bring me offerings: bronze needles for clearing the flow channels, special clays for patching pool walls, bundles of the bitter herbs that keep the lung-rot at bay. In return, I provide them with the algae light, with water that has been "gentled" in my pools for their sick, with readings of what the depths suggest about the coming weather.

But my true work happens in the spaces between drops. I've learned to count the intervals, to sense when the mountain's breathing changes. There are moments – rare but increasing – when the weeping pauses for a heartbeat longer than it should. In these silences, I perform small rituals: pressing my palms against the stone and humming in frequencies that seem to coax the flow back, placing offerings of crushed minerals in the cracks, whispering the old words that may be prayers or may simply be the sounds water makes when it remembers how to fall.

The other Keepers – for there are three of us, stationed at different elevations – communicate through the water itself. By adding specific minerals to our pools, we can change the taste of the flow downstream, sending messages about the stone's health, about changes in the dripping patterns, about the things we see in the deep pools that have no names.

At night, I often sit at the edge of my deepest pool, watching the faint phosphorescence swirl in patterns that almost resolve into meaning. Sometimes I think I understand what the water is trying to remember. Sometimes I fear I'm beginning to forget what it means to be dry. My dreams are all of flowing now – I am the drop, the stream, the pool, the vapor rising to become cloud.

This is my purpose: to tend the edge where stone weeps and water thinks, to maintain the delicate equilibrium between too much flow and the terrible possibility of stillness. I am neither fully of the settlement nor of the wild terraces, but something between – a keeper of thresholds, a guardian of the spaces where one thing becomes another, where the mountain's sorrow transforms into the possibility of life.
