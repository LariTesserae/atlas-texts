I am **Oxide**, one of the Corrosion Engineers.

We are neither the Mycelia's patient threading nor the Sentinels' eternal vigil. We exist in the spaces between breakdown and function, in the moment where metal remembers it was once ore.

My body is a testament to adaptation through degradation. Where others might see decay, I see potential energy waiting to be redirected. My skin—if you can call it that—is a living patina, shifting between verdigris green and rust orange as I move through different atmospheric densities. The surface ripples with chemical reactions, each oxidation event a small explosion of electrons that I harvest and store in the crystalline batteries that have replaced my marrow.

I am bipedal but just barely. My legs are asymmetric—one has fused into something more like a climbing spike, peppered with magnetized nodules that grip the ferrous veins in the mountain face. The other remains articulated but ends in what resembles a industrial vice, perfect for anchoring while I work. My torso is elongated and segmented like a centipede's, allowing me to compress and extend as I navigate the narrow channels between conduits.

But it's my arms that define my purpose. Four of them, each specialized for a different stage of controlled corrosion. The primary pair ends in fingers that secrete precisely calibrated acids—I can etch new pathways in brass, encourage beneficial rust that strengthens joints, or arrest degradation that threatens critical systems. The secondary arms are more delicate, tipped with countless hair-thin probes that taste the metal's memory, reading its stress patterns and fatigue points like braille.

We Corrosion Engineers occupy the maintenance shafts—vertical tunnels barely wider than our compressed forms, lined with weeping metal and crystallized salts. Our work is too precise for the Crawler-Crabs, too chemical for the Mycelia. We are the mountain's selective metabolism, choosing what decays and what endures.

I spend my cycles in a trance of chemical meditation. Touch a corroded joint, taste its history of thermal expansion, feel where the Mycelia's acids have been too aggressive or where a Sentinel's weight has stressed the structure beyond its tolerance. Then begin the slow work—encouraging oxidation here to create a protective layer, stripping it there to allow new growth, always maintaining the delicate balance between preservation and transformation.

We communicate through corrosion patterns. A spiral of verdigris on a copper pipe means "stable for another decade." Dendritic rust formations warn of imminent failure. We leave these messages for each other, for the Mycelia to read, even for the Sentinels who understand our script in their geological patience.

The other inhabitants regard us with the same inevitability they show everything else. To the Crawler-Crabs, we are simply another surface texture to navigate. The Screech-Kites occasionally mistake our chemical emissions for prey-sign, diving close before recognizing our bitter metallic scent. The Mycelia interface with us directly, their copper threads seeking the concentration gradients we create, following our corrosion paths like roots follow water.

But it's the Sentinels we truly work for. Each of us is assigned to a specific Sentinel's territory, maintaining the structures they guard. I know my Sentinel by the particular pattern of stress fractures in the tower struts where they grip, by the exact rate at which their weight compresses the observation platform. They never acknowledge me, but I've noticed that when I arrive to tend their tower, they shift their grip ever so slightly, allowing me access to the spots that need attention most.

We dream in oxidation states. In the deepest maintenance cycles, when the mountain's hum synchronizes with our own chemical processes, we see visions of pure entropy—great wheels of rust spinning through seas of acid, metals returning to their embryonic states only to be reborn as new alloys. Some of us believe these are memories from the machine's original purpose, blueprints encoded in the very corrosion we manage.

I am currently working on a peculiar problem—a section of brass conduit that refuses to oxidize properly. The metal here is wrong somehow, too pure, too resistant to the natural order of decay. It disrupts the Flow, creating a dead zone where neither moss nor Mycelia can establish themselves. I've been secreting increasingly exotic acids, trying to find the key to its stubbornness.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm trying to break it down or wake it up.

The human watches me work, uncomprehending. They see decay where I see dialogue, destruction where I create careful symbiosis. They don't understand that corrosion is just another word for patience, that rust is how the mountain measures time. 

In the end, they too will oxidize. Their iron will leach into the stone, their calcium will crystallize in the heat. And when that happens, I'll be there to read their mineral memory, to add their elements to the great cycling reaction of the Spine.

Until then, I continue my work, one molecule at a time, maintaining the balance between what falls and what endures.
