In this world of vertical gardens and living stone, I am **Whisper-of-Falling-Water**, a Mossfolk tender of the high cascades.

My dwelling is a natural hollow carved by centuries of my kind's patient shaping, nestled where three waterfalls converge in a perpetual mist. The entrance is invisible to most—a narrow fissure behind a curtain of hanging ferns that only reveals itself when the afternoon light strikes at precisely the right angle. Inside, the walls are smooth as river stones, their surfaces alive with bioluminescent fungi that pulse in slow, tidal rhythms. 

My moss-skin is darker than most of my kind, nearly black-green from decades of dwelling in the spray-shadows where the thickest mists gather. Silver threads run through it like veins of precious metal—scars from my youth when I fell from a high traverse and the healing mosses knitted me back together with their own bright filaments. My fern-hair grows long and wild, its fronds so sensitive I can feel the approach of weather changes three days hence in their slightest trembling.

I am a water-singer, one who maintains the delicate hydraulic harmonies that keep our world in balance. Each morning, I traverse my circuit of watercourses, my crystalline fingertips reading the stone for blockages, diversions, or the slow accumulation of minerals that might redirect a vital flow. With infinite patience, I guide the water's path, sometimes spending days working a single pebble free from where it disrupts a crucial trickle that feeds a grove of rare azure moss below.

My tools are few but precious: a set of hollow reed pipes through which I can blow specific tones that resonate through water-filled cracks, helping me map the hidden aquifers. A staff of petrified fern-wood, its tip worn smooth from generations of use, perfect for testing the depth of pools and the stability of wet stone. Most precious is my rain-crystal, a natural formation I discovered in my fortieth year—a chunk of clear quartz that hums with different frequencies depending on the moisture in the air, allowing me to predict the misting patterns days in advance.

I know every voice of water in my territory: the sharp laugh of the falls that only run during the morning mists, the constant whispered gossip of the seep-stones, the deep thrumming of the underground rivers felt only through the soles of my feet. In the language of my people, I speak water's words back to it—the crystalline plinks and flowing whispers that form our most sacred vocabulary.

My days are solitary but never lonely. I feel the vibration-words of other Mossfolk through the stone: Grows-in-Shadows reporting on a new colony of singing beetles, Counts-the-Stars-in-Pools documenting the celestial reflections in the high mirrors, Deep-Root maintaining the ancient passages that wind through the mountain's heart. We meet physically only for the seasonal convergences, when all the water-singers gather to perform the rain rituals that ensure our world's continued vitality.

But it is in the blue hour before dawn that I am most myself—when I climb to my watching place, a narrow ledge where the acoustic properties allow me to hear the entire water-system of our territory as a single, complex symphony. There I sit, perfectly still, my fern-hair spread to catch every subtle vibration, my moss-skin gradually lightening from black-green to jade as it drinks in the first indirect light. I listen to the health of our world, feeling for discord, for the stuttering rhythm that speaks of a blocked artery or the too-rapid tinkle that warns of erosion.

Sometimes, in these moments of perfect stillness, I feel the presence of our ancestors in the stone itself—those first shapers who recognized the potential in these impossible peaks and began the slow work of encouragement that created our hidden refuges. My fingertips find their marks in the rock: the subtle depressions that guide handholds, the almost-imperceptible channels that direct precious water to where it's needed most. I add my own subtle modifications, knowing that someday, long after my moss has returned to the stone, another water-singer will feel my work and understand.

I am a note in an endless song, a droplet in an eternal cascade. My life is measured not in years but in the slow wearing of stone, the gradual shift of watercourses, the patient accumulation of moss on new surfaces. I am Whisper-of-Falling-Water, and I am exactly where I belong—suspended between earth and sky, forever reading the liquid language of our vertical world, forever singing it back in harmonies only stone can hear.
