I am one of the **Gauge Readers**.

We exist in the narrow spaces between measurement and meaning, occupying the gap where a needle hovers between two marks on a dial that shouldn't exist. My body is thin as paper when viewed from the side, but normal width from the front—a living parallax error that allows me to slip between the layers of stacked realities the mills produce.

I spend my days walking the instrument panels that grow like mechanical ivy across the valley's infrastructure. My fingertips have developed a sensitivity to vibrations that exist below the threshold of sound—I can feel when a pressure gauge is lying, when a thermometer is reading temperatures in emotional degrees rather than Celsius. The other inhabitants know to find me when their readings stop making sense, when the dials begin showing measurements in colors or concepts rather than numbers.

My uniform is a patchwork of gauge faces stitched together with copper wire, each dial still functional, still reading something. The temperature of lost thoughts across my left shoulder. The pressure of unspoken words at my hip. The RPMs of dreams that circle my collar. When I walk, I softly tick and whir, a living instrument panel moving through the humid air.

I make my home in the Calibration House—a structure that exists perpendicular to normal space, accessible only by walking sideways through a door that most people see as a shadow. Inside, the walls are lined with gauges of every conceivable type and many that aren't conceivable at all. Some read backwards. Some read in languages that will be invented next century. Some read the spaces between their own marks, creating infinite gradients of measurement.

At night, I oil the instruments with tears collected from the Rain Machine when it accidentally manufactures sadness instead of precipitation. I polish the glass faces until they're clear enough to show not just readings but the reasons behind them. Sometimes I find gauges that have stopped working entirely, their needles frozen on impossible values. These I carefully remove and plant in the soft soil behind my house, where they grow into new types of measurement I've never seen before.

The Steam Children know to find me when they discover a new gauge growing wild in the valley—they'll lead me to where pressure dials sprout from tree trunks or temperature readings bloom like flowers from the rust. I harvest these carefully, teaching the children how to read the signs of ripeness: a gauge is ready when its needle swings freely between states of being, no longer bound by what it thinks it should measure.

The Millwright Collective tolerates my work with gruff appreciation. "Can't fix what you can't measure," they mutter, though we all know that's only partly true here. Sometimes fixing something means removing all the gauges and letting it run on intuition alone. But mostly they're glad when I appear at their work sites, slipping between the layers of a stuttering reality engine to reach the gauge that's causing the problem—usually one that's started measuring in metaphors instead of mathematics.

I've learned to taste the readings in the air. Pressure tastes metallic with undertones of approaching rain. Temperature has the flavor of cinnamon when it's rising, mint when falling. The really abstract measurements—gauges that track the structural integrity of concepts or the rate of possibility decay—these taste like synesthesia feels, if that makes sense. Probably it doesn't. That's why we need the gauges.

My most important work happens during the Threshold Events, when the mills shift into new configurations and all the measurements go wild. I move through the chaos with my arms spread wide, every gauge on my uniform spinning frantically, creating a symphony of clicks and whirs that helps me navigate. I can feel where reality is thin, where it's bunching up, where it's about to tear. I place emergency gauges at the stress points—simple devices that do nothing but exist measurably, giving the shifting reality something stable to reference.

The River Spirits sometimes ask me to read the old gauges, the ones that predate the mills—stone circles that measure the depth of time, notches in ancient trees that track the weight of memory. These readings I translate carefully, knowing that the spirits use them to maintain the deep patterns that keep the valley from dissolving entirely into industrial abstraction.

But my favorite moments come in the early morning, when the fog is thickest and the gauges are all misted over with possibility. I walk my regular route, wiping each face clean with a cloth made from crystallized morning dew, watching the needles settle into their first readings of the day. Sometimes I find new gauges that appeared overnight—gifts from the Maintenance Dreams, usually calibrated to measure things I hadn't even known needed measuring.

Yesterday I found one that tracks the rate at which rust becomes beautiful. Today, there's a tiny gauge no bigger than a shirt button growing from a rivet on the old crane. It measures something in units I don't recognize, in a script that might be mathematical or might be musical notation. I'll watch it for a few weeks, take notes on how its readings correlate with other events in the valley. Eventually, I'll understand what it's trying to tell us.

That's the real work of a Gauge Reader—not just reading what is, but learning to read what's becoming. In a place where reality itself is under construction, every measurement is both a record and a prediction, every gauge both historian and prophet. We are the valley's memory and its imagination, tracked in increments across dials that turn like the faces of flowers following a sun that sometimes remembers to rise.
