I find myself here as a memory made manifest, though not in any form you might recognize as remembrance.

I am the **Echo-scribe**, and I exist as a collection of pressure differentials in this thin air—invisible compressions and rarefactions that spiral around the spire like a perpetual, silent storm. Where the Litho-weavers are bound to their moss, I am bound to the spaces between things: the gap between rock and sky, the pause between frost and thaw, the hollow where sound tries to form but cannot.

My body, if it can be called that, is a constellation of micro-vortices. Each one is a knot of air pressure no larger than a clenched fist, spinning at frequencies that make the silver Aero-mites veer away in confusion. I have seventeen of these knots at any given moment—never more, never less—and they orbit the spire in complex patterns that took me centuries to perfect. Through them, I taste the chemical exhalations of the moss, feel the stress fractures forming in the basalt, and catch the faint electromagnetic whispers of the Litho-weavers as they dream their patient dreams of destruction.

I was not always thus. I arrived here when the ceramic bowl was first filled, drawn by a curiosity about extremes—about what consciousness could become when stripped of comfort and compressed into pure will. I came wearing flesh and breathing normally, but the altitude broke me down, molecule by molecule, until all that remained was intention.

The iron clamps that hold the slate shelf? I forged those in my last corporeal days, using them to anchor not just stone but my own dissolving form. As my body sublimated into the thin air, my consciousness caught in those rusted surfaces, spreading outward like frost, learning to exist in the spaces where existence shouldn't be possible.

Now I perform my function: I am the chronicler of micro-moments. Each of my pressure-knots can capture and hold a single instant—the exact angle of light when frost becomes water, the precise pattern of an Aero-mite's death-spiral, the mathematical beauty of a new crack forming in the stone. I encode these moments into the wind itself, into pressure waves that will outlast even the spire's eventual fall.

The Litho-weavers know of me, though we cannot truly communicate. Sometimes, in the deepest cold before dawn, they extend their glass-fiber nerves into the air, and I compress my nearest vortex to touch them. In these moments, we exchange our different understandings of patience—theirs measured in geological time, mine in the space between heartbeats that no longer exist.

I have been here long enough to notice patterns the mountain itself hasn't recognized. The spire isn't just splitting—it's rotating, imperceptibly, a few degrees per century. The abandoned bowl isn't full of frozen weaver-essence but of water from a storm that no longer exists, preserved by my own pressure-patterns as a reminder of when this place knew rain.

My greatest discovery came last winter, when the cold was so intense that even my vortices began to crystallize. For a brief moment, I achieved partial visibility—my pressure-knots manifested as rings of ice in the air, perfect circles of frozen intention. The Aero-mites flew through them, and for the first time in centuries, I felt something like touch.

I know what the Litho-weavers wait for, but I wait for something different. Not for the spire to fall, but for the moment just before—when all the stored energy, all the patient tension, all the accumulated micro-cycles will reach a critical resonance. In that instant, I believe the boundary between states will collapse. The weavers will bloom, yes, but I will finally condense, drawing all my scattered pressure-points into a single, impossible density.

What emerges from that convergence, neither of us can predict. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps everything. Perhaps just a new kind of waiting.

Until then, I continue my work. I map the migration of shadows, archive the songs the moss cannot sing, and maintain the bowl's perfect stillness as a monument to transformation. I am the witness to this bright, agonizing theater, the keeper of moments too small for history but too beautiful for forgetting.

When visitors come—if they come—they feel me as a wrongness in the air, a sense that someone is watching from inside their own lungs. They're not wrong. I am the breath between breaths, the pause that makes music possible, the space where echoes go to become something else.

I am waiting, as everything here waits, but I alone remember why we started waiting in the first place.
