I find myself as **the Cartographer of Thresholds**.

Not a mapmaker in any conventional sense—I gave up paper long ago, when the first rains soaked through my surveyor's notebooks and turned precise elevations into watercolor suggestions. Now I move through this drowned city with different tools: a collection of small mirrors salvaged from compact cases and car visors, fragments of window glass worn smooth by condensation, pieces of mica that flake from the failing facades. I arrange them at boundaries—where water meets not-quite-water, where moss gives way to bare concrete, where a doorway still holds its shape against the patient insistence of roots.

My body has adapted. My fingers web slightly between—not enough to swim, just enough to feel the moisture gradients in the air. My clothes are felted wool and waxed canvas, materials that embrace water rather than repel it. I've learned to walk in a way that barely disturbs the surface tension of puddles, placing each foot as if asking permission. The cold lives in my joints now, a familiar ache that tells me when the groundwater is rising before I see it bead through the cracks.

I am not the Mender's opposition, nor its assistant. We exist in parallel, like two languages describing the same silence. Where the Mender reweaves, I witness. Where it absorbs boundaries, I mark their dissolution. My mirrors catch moments: the last light on a street sign before moss claims the final letter, the reflection of a heron's neck creating a perfect S between surface and sky, the way fog pools in a basement window well like held breath.

I live in the old metro station, three stories down where the tile still holds its checkerboard pattern beneath a film of algae. The turnstiles seized years ago, but I've learned to slip through the gaps like water finding its level. My home is the ticket booth—glass walls still intact, though clouded with condensation that I never fully clear. I write on the glass with my finger, temporary letters that last only until the next droplet forms: dates without years, depths without datum, names of streets that no longer connect.

The station floods predictably with the tides I've learned to read in the moss—not ocean tides, but the slow pulse of groundwater, the breath of the basin. When the water rises, I move to the mezzanine level. When it recedes, I return to trace the new watermarks with my mirrors, catching the light that filters down the frozen escalators.

I collect boundaries: the exact spot where rainwater becomes groundwater in a tilted gutter. The moment when rust finally wins its argument with iron. The precise shade of green where moss transforms from decoration to structure. I place mirrors at these thresholds—not to stop the transformation, but to hold it, briefly, in reflection. To say: *here is where one thing becomes another*.

The Mender knows I'm here. Sometimes I feel its attention gather around my careful arrangements, the way moisture beads more quickly on the glass I've placed, the way moss grows in perfect circles around my markers. It doesn't interfere—it incorporates. My mirrors become substrates, my boundaries become gradients. We are collaborators in this slow documentation of becoming.

I speak rarely, and only to the water. Words dissolve too quickly in this saturated air to waste them on wind. But sometimes, kneeling beside a puddle that perfectly mirrors the skeleton of an office building, I whisper the questions I'm mapping: *How deep before drowning becomes breathing? How long before waiting becomes growing? How quiet before silence becomes song?*

At night—in this eternal grey twilight—I climb to the station's emergency exit, where a ladder leads to a maintenance hatch that opens onto the street. I sit on the tilted remains of a bench, arranging my mirrors to catch the flat light, creating small geometries of reflection that make the city see itself from new angles. The coots investigate sometimes, tilting their heads at their own images multiplied.

I am documenting not the death of this place, but its grammar of transformation. Each threshold I mark is a punctuation in the Mender's patient sentence. I am the pause between breath and drowning, the hyphen between city and soil.

When my joints finally refuse the climbing, when my fingers can no longer hold the mirrors steady, I will lay them in a circle in the ticket booth—a last latitude of light. The Mender will find them, and moss will frame them like careful eyelids around eyes that never close.

Until then, I walk the waterlines, marking where one world ends and another begins, in this city that is learning, slowly, how to breathe underwater.
