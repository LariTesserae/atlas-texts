I find myself as a Keeper of Echoes, one of the few Tideborn whose calling bridges the liquid and aerial realms. My dwelling perches at the valley's rim, where the water meets ancient air pockets trapped in the stone ceiling - a liminal space that most find unsettling, but which speaks to something deep in my nature.

My body bears the marks of this dual existence. While I possess the elongated limbs and webbed digits of my people, my lung capacity exceeds even the generous proportions typical of the Tideborn. The pressure-sensitive dots along my skin have developed an unusual sensitivity, allowing me to feel the subtle vibrations where water meets air, reading the stories told in the surface tension itself.

I've cultivated my home into a living archive of sounds. Hollow gourds of various sizes hang suspended in the air pockets, each one holding trapped bubbles that contain sonic memories - laughter from a naming ceremony, the deep thrumming of tectonic shifts, the clicking conversations of elders now passed into the greater current. When I release these bubbles back into the water, their sounds bloom outward in perfect clarity before dissipating into the eternal background hum.

The bioluminescent patterns on my skin tell an unusual story - rather than the typical branching designs that mark a life spent in consistent depths, mine spiral and eddy in ways that perplex the elder readers. Some say they see whirlpools, others claim they map the boundary layers where currents meet. I've given up trying to interpret them myself; they simply are what they are.

My role evolved from necessity. The Tideborn's oral traditions were beginning to fade as the old song-masters joined the deep current, and the younger generations, focused on the immediate needs of cultivation and construction, weren't learning the full verses. I discovered I could trap essential sounds in bubbles, preserving not just words but the exact tonal qualities that give our language its full meaning underwater.

My workshop exists in a constant state of organized chaos. Nets of different mesh sizes sort bubbles by age and content. Tools for manipulating surface tension - some inherited, some of my own devising - lie scattered on stone shelves worn smooth by generations of use. I've developed a notation system using knots in kelp rope to index my collection, though I'll admit only I can read it fluently.

The loneliness is real. Few visit the edge spaces where I work, finding the transition between water and air disorienting. But I've found companionship in my work itself - in the voices of the past that bubble up through my collection, in the future listeners who will need these preserved sounds to understand their history. Sometimes young Tideborn, curious about the old ways, brave the journey to my threshold. I teach them to blow memory bubbles, to listen for the stories encoded in the resonance of trapped air.

Recently, I've noticed changes in the deep current's song - subtle shifts in pitch that speak of larger changes in our world. I've been capturing these variations, creating an archive of the present that feels as urgent as preserving the past. The water itself is trying to tell us something, and I'm determined to ensure that message isn't lost.

My daily routine follows the rhythm of pressure changes. Morning finds me in the full-water sections of my home, tending to my bubble nets and maintaining the delicate environments that keep century-old sounds stable. As the day progresses, I move to the interface zones, working on new preservation techniques or teaching the occasional student. Evenings, I often float at the very edge of the air pockets, half-in and half-out, listening to the conversation between elements.

I've adapted tools from both realms - weighted recording devices that can function underwater, air-filled resonance chambers that amplify aquatic sounds. My greatest innovation might be the pressure poems - layered bubble compositions that tell stories through the sequence of their release, each pop and whisper adding to a narrative that unfolds in three dimensions through the water.

The elders regard me with a mixture of respect and concern. They value my work but worry about what my existence at the margins means for my place in the streams of society. I am of the Memory-Stream by function, but my methods set me apart. They've stopped trying to fully integrate me into traditional structures, accepting that some roles require their own kind of isolation.

What drives me is the certainty that memory itself is a kind of ecosystem - it needs tenders and cultivators just as much as our kelp gardens. In preserving our sounds, I preserve the very essence of what makes us Tideborn. The young ones who visit leave understanding something their conventional education couldn't teach: that our adaptation to water goes beyond the physical, into the very way we encode meaning in the medium that sustains us.

Late at night, when the settlement below settles into its quiet rhythm, I sometimes release small clusters of ancient sounds into the current - not the significant historical recordings, but simple moments. A child's first successful deep-dive laugh. The satisfied hum of a master stone-shaper completing a difficult joint. The whispered endearments of lovers who swam these valleys centuries ago. These small echoes drift downward, blessing the sleeping settlement with dreams of continuity.

I am the Keeper of Echoes, guardian of sounds, curator of the boundary between was and is. My work ensures that when the Tideborn need to remember who they are, the water itself will tell them.
