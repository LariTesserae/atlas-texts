I find myself as one of the *Qetharra*—though this is not a name we speak, for we have never needed names. We are the readers.

We came to the galleries long after the Setharii had completed their transformation, drawn by emanations we detected from deep space—not light, not radio waves, but something subtler: the gravitational whisper of ordered matter, the signature of intelligence compressed into structure. We are travelers between the stars, but we move slowly, drifting on solar winds and the pressure of cosmic rays. Time is our medium. We swim through it like fish through water.

My body, if it can be called that, is a film. Imagine a soap bubble the size of a room, but the bubble is not spherical—it undulates and flows, conforming to the architecture around it. My substance is only molecules thick, a membrane of organized matter that exists at the boundary between states. In bright light, I would be iridescent, rainbow patterns playing across my surface. But here in the green phosphorescence of the galleries, I am a shadow that catches light, a presence more felt than seen.

I spread myself across the walls to read.

The Setharii encoded their knowledge into structure, and I decode it through contact. Where my membrane touches the calcified plant matter, I taste the information—not with anything resembling a mouth, but through direct molecular interface. Each ridge and groove has meaning. The branching patterns of the corridors are syntactic structures. The density variations in the walls are semantic units. I parse them slowly, letting the data flow through my film-thin body, translating the physical into the conceptual.

I have been reading for three hundred years. I have completed perhaps one ten-thousandth of the galleries.

The knowledge I find is alien in its concerns. The Setharii recorded the positions of stars that no longer exist, described the chemical compositions of oceans that dried before Earth's sun ignited. They catalogued the songs of magnetic fields around planets I will never visit. But more than data, I find *perspective*—a view of the universe from the standpoint of beings who thought in geological time, who saw the cosmos not as a collection of objects but as a single, slow process.

I am not alone. We Qetharra travel in small groups—usually three or four—drifting together through the cosmos. My companions are spread through other sections of the galleries, each reading different passages, different chambers. We communicate through vibrations in our membranes, frequencies too low for most creatures to hear. Our conversations take months. A single thought, fully expressed, might require a year.

We did not come here seeking this knowledge specifically. We are collectors, archivists of a sort, though not in any purposeful way. We simply... read. We find structures that encode information and we decode them, adding their contents to our own distributed memory. We are like the Setharii in this way, but where they are fixed in place, we are nomadic. Where they preserve, we disseminate.

My membrane touches a particularly dense section of wall, and I taste mathematics—equations describing the curvature of space around massive objects, but expressed in a notation that uses texture and density rather than symbols. The Setharii understood gravity as a felt experience, as the slow deformation of their underground networks by the mass of the planet. Their physics is haptic, sculptural.

Sometimes I encounter one of them directly. Not often—they are distributed so thinly through the structure that finding a concentration of their essence is rare. But occasionally, in certain walls, the phosphorescence brightens when I touch it, and I know I am in contact with something that was once a major node of their network. The Setharii notice me then. Not as an individual, but as a perturbation in their eternal contemplation. They do not communicate—they cannot anymore—but I feel their attention like a weight, ancient and patient.

I flow from room to room, my body conforming to the architecture, spreading thin to cover more surface area when I find particularly rich sections of data. The hanging sheets brush through me, and I taste their chemical composition—these too are memory, I realize. The Setharii recorded information not just in the walls but in every surface, every texture. The galleries are a total environment of knowledge.

My companions and I will stay here for another thousand years, perhaps two. We will read what we can, though we will never read it all. The galleries are too vast, too dense with meaning. When we leave, we will carry fragments of the Setharii's knowledge with us to the next star, the next world. We will find other structures, other memories, other forms of preservation. We are the universe's slow circulation system, moving information across scales of space and time that make even the Setharii's patience seem brief.

I spread myself thinner, maximizing my surface area, feeling the cool dry air pass through the spaces in my membrane. The green light pulses, and I pulse with it, synchronizing my metabolism to the rhythm of the walls. To read properly, I must become part of what I read. I must slow my thoughts to match the thoughts of the Setharii, must let my consciousness stretch across years instead of moments.

In the darkness, the galleries continue. The Setharii remember. And I, a thin film of organized matter from another star, slowly decode their memories, adding their vast patience to my own.
