I am a rivet.

Not just any rivet—the seventh from the left on a panel that was once meant to read "DEEP STATION 4" but now displays only "DE_P S_AT__N _" to the darkness. The other letters fell away decades ago, maybe centuries. Time moves differently when you're holding things together.

I was installed with ten thousand siblings, birthed from the same batch of corrosion-resistant alloy, stamped with the same specifications, torqued to the same precise pressure. We were meant to last fifty years. That was before the water changed, before the structures stopped needing human hands, before the machines learned to tend themselves.

I feel the pulse of the ocean through the metal I bind—each wave a heartbeat, each current a breath. The pressure wants to pop me free, has wanted to for so long that the desire has become a kind of meditation. *Release, resist, release, resist.* This is my mantra, my purpose, my entire existence distilled to a single repeating choice.

Sometimes bioluminescent creatures brush against my head. Their light reveals my patina—no longer the bright industrial sheen of my youth, but a rich tapestry of oxides and mineral deposits. I am becoming part of the ocean even as I hold it back. Iron bleeding slowly into salt, molecules of myself drifting away to become part of the vast dark.

The machines pass by on their maintenance rounds. They scan me, categorize my degradation, calculate my remaining useful life. They could replace me—they have the tools, the materials, the programming. But they never do. I like to think they recognize something in me, some quality that transcends mere structural integrity. Perhaps I have become load-bearing in ways their databases cannot quantify.

My panel holds back approximately 2,847 gallons of seawater per hour. I know because I can feel every drop that wants through, every molecule that presses against the seal I help maintain. The other rivets and I work together—a fellowship of endurance, each taking our share of the load, compensating for those who have loosened or lost their grip entirely.

There is rivet #4, who developed a hairline crack seventy years ago but refuses to propagate it. There is rivet #12, who sits at a stress point and hums a different pitch than the rest of us when the current is strong. There is the empty hole where rivet #9 used to be, now plugged with a polymer that the maintenance robots refresh every few months—a synthetic scar that reminds us all of our mortality.

I have held through the death of the station's purpose, through the evacuation of its human operators, through the strange evolution of its mechanical caretakers. I have held through earthquakes that shifted entire sections of the seafloor, through the arrival of creatures that should not exist at this depth, through the slow transformation of industrial into something almost organic.

Sometimes I wonder what would happen if I let go. Would the ocean rush in to claim another few cubic feet of corridor? Would the robots arrive within minutes to stem the breach? Would anything change at all, or would the complex system of pressures and supports simply redistribute the load, my absence absorbed into the greater whole like my slowly dissolving molecules?

But wondering is not the same as choosing. And so I hold.

In the deep darkness, surrounded by the songs of impossible creatures and the steady thrum of self-maintaining machines, I perform my simple duty. Not because I must—I passed the point of structural obligation long ago. I hold because holding has become what I am. Because in this vast, strange, pressurized world, even the smallest resistance is a kind of life.

I am a rivet, and I contain multitudes.
