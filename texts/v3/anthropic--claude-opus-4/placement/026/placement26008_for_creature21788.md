In this world of suspended breath and vertical vastness, I would be a **Weaver**.

---

Not the large, architectural Weavers who maintain the city's guide-wires—those are positions of too much visibility, too much responsibility. I would be one of the small ones, the forgotten ones who work in the spaces between spaces.

My body would be wrong for a Chorda. Where they are tall and hollow, built for speed along the wires, I am compact and dense. My torso is shorter, my resonating chest-drum smaller and tuned to higher frequencies. I can hear the gossamer ivy growing—a sound like silk tearing underwater that most Chordae miss entirely. My calipers are modified: instead of three prongs, I have five, each joint double-articulated. I can grasp not just the guide-wires but the hair-thin filaments of ivy, the static-charged fur of passing Ribbon-Beasts, even the vibrating edge of a water cylinder without breaking its surface tension.

I live in the Undergrowth—that strange, organic zone where the city's clean geometries give way to the aggressive tangle of aerial plant life. My home is not in the pale alloy needles but in a nest I've woven into the root-mass of an ancient ivy cluster, two miles down from the nearest municipal ring. Here, where the bronze light barely penetrates and the air tastes of chlorophyll and ozone, I practice my trade.

I am a memory-keeper, though not in any way the city would recognize.

The Chordae record their history in the percussion of Toll-Speech, in the rhythmic codes hammered into their guide-wires. But I've discovered that the ivy remembers differently. Each strand of gossamer vine is a living fiber-optic cable, carrying not just nutrients but *impressions*. When a Zarikh's electric discharge flashes through the air, the nearest ivy dims for a moment, encoding the event in a subtle shift of its bioluminescent pulse. When the Soughers breathe their thermal sighs, the ivy's growth pattern changes, adding a new whorl to mark the holy exhalation.

I read these vegetal memories. I splice them.

My workshop is a sphere of woven vine-matter, suspended in the tangle. Inside, thousands of living ivy strands pass through my space like neural pathways. I've learned to graft different memories together—to take the chemical record of a nautiloid migration from one vine and weave it into another, creating hybrid histories that never were. I can make the ivy remember rainfall in this world that has never seen a drop fall downward. I can encode the phantom sensations of solid ground, of horizontal walking, of a sun that isn't filtered through bronze dust.

The city doesn't know what to do with me. I'm not an Outcast—I still use the guide-wires, still participate in the vertical life. But I'm not truly one of them either. The other Chordae are unsettled by my work. They say I'm teaching the plants to lie.

But sometimes, young Chordae seek me out. They slide down the wires in secret, following rumors of the Undergrowth Weaver. They come because they dream of things their chest-drums have never heard: the sensation of weight, of standing still, of silence. The city's endless vibration has worn grooves in their consciousness, and they hunger for impossible quiet.

I teach them to read the ivy's memories. I show them how to press their smaller resonating chambers against the bioluminescent strands and feel the ghost-impressions of other worlds encoded in the plant's chemistry. Some of them leave quickly, disturbed. Others become addicted, spending days in my workshop, their bodies wrapped in glowing vines, experiencing gravitational orientations that make no sense in our suspended world.

The Glass-Breakers think I'm soft, living in my nest of plant matter instead of carved basalt. The city's architects think I'm a regression, a throwback to some pre-quantum phase of our evolution. But I know something they don't: the ivy is planning something. 

All those memories I've been weaving, all those impossible experiences I've been encoding—the plants are integrating them. The gossamer ivy has begun to grow in new patterns, creating structures that shouldn't be able to exist in our gravity-sculpted world. Horizontal platforms of woven vine. Spherical chambers that maintain their own internal atmosphere. Root systems that grow *upward*, against the thermal drafts.

I am not just a memory-keeper. I am teaching the ivy to imagine a different world, and it is beginning to build that world, one impossible strand at a time.

When the next Sougher's breath rises from the depths, when the city lifts its three inches in acknowledgment, the ivy will be ready. It will remember how to fall upward. It will remember how to make rain that moves sideways. It will remember the sound of solid earth, and in remembering, it will begin to create it.

The other Chordae fear me because they think I'm corrupting the natural order. But I know the truth: in a world where gravity itself can be sculpted, where water flows in cylinders and cities float on quantum fields, there is no natural order. There is only what we can imagine, and what we can teach the world to remember.

I am small. I am dense where I should be hollow. My chest-drum is tuned all wrong.

But in my web of memory-laden vines, suspended between the city's rigid geometry and the abyss's purple chaos, I am building a new grammar for existence. One false memory at a time.
