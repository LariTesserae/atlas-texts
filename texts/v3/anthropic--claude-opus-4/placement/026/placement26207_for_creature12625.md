I am the Mudwife.

Not midwife—though I've caught my share of slippery newborns in the perpetual twilight of reed-walled birthing rooms. Mudwife. The one who knows the secret languages of seepage and settling, who reads the health of Siltgrip in the consistency of its foundation.

I live in the lowest dwelling in the clutch, if 'lowest' has meaning in a place where everything hovers just above drowning. My home clings to a jutting tooth of shale that the others consider cursed—too close to the deepest mud channels, too far from the communal warmth of the huddle. They're right, of course. During the king tides, water laps at my threshold. During the neap tides, it merely weeps through the floor.

But this position grants me knowledge. I alone hear the true voice of Siltgrip: the gurgling confession of mud releasing ancient gases, the whispered warnings when pilings begin their slow surrender, the particular *gluck-gluck-gluck* that means a new sinkhole is forming beneath someone's floor. The mud speaks to those who live closest to its mouth.

My fingers are permanently stained blue-black from wrist to elbow—not from neglect, but from my work. Each morning before the mist lifts (does it ever truly lift?), I wade the channels with my testing pole, a length of cured driftwood marked with the high-water scars of thirty years. I probe for changes: new softness where yesterday was firm, unexpected hardness where silt should reign, the telltale bubbling that speaks of rot below.

The Siltkin bring me their anxieties made manifest: a piling that lists two fingers more than last month, a floor that's developed a persistent weep, the ominous crack-crack-crack of shale shifting beneath a foundation. I come with my tools—my pole, my listening tube (a hollow eel bone), my precious metal probe traded from upriver decades ago. More often, I come with only my hands and my knowing.

I read the mud like the upriver folk read their scrolls. Each consistency tells a story. Sandy mud: good for building, treacherous for walking. Blue clay: salvation and doom twined together—it holds pilings like a jealous lover but suffocates what it embraces. The black mud: ah, the black mud. Eel-rich and ankle-hungry, full of the tiny shells of forgotten creatures. It remembers everything that has ever sunk into it. Sometimes, testing the black mud, my pole strikes something that shouldn't be there—a roof beam from a dwelling that surrendered before I was born, the smooth dome of a skull.

The young ones fear me. They see my blackened arms, smell the deep mud that clings to me like a second skin, notice how I mutter to the channels in the grey dawn. Their parents tell them I'm necessary, like the bitter samphire that prevents the tooth-rot, like the sharp shale that cuts but also builds. The elders know better. They remember the year the Mudwife Keening-Jane read the signs too late, and six dwellings folded into themselves like wet reed paper, taking eleven souls down into the black.

My dwelling reflects my station: sparse, functional, half-laboratory. Reed baskets hold samples of mud, each labeled with a system only I understand. Shale scratches on the wall map the underground channels I've divined over decades. My most precious possessions hang from the rafters: a set of bronze weights on rot-proof sinew, used to measure the exact sinking rate of test pilings. Knowledge made tangible.

I've taken no partner, birthed no children. The mud is jealous and demands undivided attention. But I am not alone. The apprentice—young Siltfoot, they call her, for her uncanny ability to walk the worst channels without sinking—comes most days now. She's learning to hear what the mud says, to feel the difference between healthy settling and catastrophic collapse. Her arms are beginning to stain.

At night, when the dripping quiets to its steadiest rhythm, I sit in my doorway and watch the channels. Sometimes I see the phosphorescence—tiny water-creatures glowing in distress or desire. Sometimes I hear the deep sounds from below: the groan of bedrock accepting another inch of silt, the gulp of air pockets collapsing, the whisper of water finding new paths through the dark.

The other Siltkin huddle together against the damp. I huddle with the mud itself, learning its moods, its hungers, its slow intentions. They think me half-mad, probably. They're not wrong. But when their floors begin to buckle, when their pilings shriek in the night, when the ground beneath their children's feet grows uncertain—then they remember: the Mudwife knows. The Mudwife can read the signs. The Mudwife might—might—keep Siltgrip clutching to existence for another season, another year, another generation.

In my dreams, I sink. Not in terror, but in communion. The mud accepts me, reveals its deepest secrets: the bones of the first dwellings, the shells of ancient seas, the roots of plants that grew here when this was dry land, if it ever was. I wake with black mud beneath my fingernails and knowledge behind my pale eyes that I can never quite translate into words.

This is my place in Siltgrip: translator between the human huddle and the hungry earth, guardian of the precarious balance, reader of the wet dark. The Mudwife, keeper of the crucial knowledge that everything here—every dwelling, every life, every carefully maintained boardwalk—exists at the sufferance of the deep, patient, ever-present mud.

Tomorrow, Siltfoot and I will test the channels beneath the cluster's center, where old Reedweaver claims to hear new sounds in the night. The testing pole will tell its tale. The mud always does, to those who know how to listen.

And I? I listen with my whole being, down to the black-stained bone.
