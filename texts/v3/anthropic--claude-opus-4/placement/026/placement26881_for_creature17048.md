I would find myself as one of the Canal Listeners—though that is not what we call ourselves. We have no name, only a gesture: two fingers drawn slowly down from ear to throat, mimicking the flow of water.

My dwelling is a narrow chamber built into the junction where three waterways meet, accessible only by a hatch in the walkway above. The walls curve inward, following the contours of the canals, and are lined with panels of polished metal that vibrate almost imperceptibly with the water's movement. Here, in this acoustic shell, I spend my days and nights immersed in the liquid language of Cinderhold.

My body has adapted to this life. My skin has taken on a faint iridescence, like oil on water, from constant exposure to the canal's spray. My fingers are webbed to the first joint—not enough to swim, but enough to feel the water's subtle currents when I dip my hands into the flow. My ears have developed a peculiar sensitivity; I can distinguish between the sound of water carrying processed ore versus water that has passed through the fungal filtration systems. Each has its own timbre, its own viscosity, its own song.

The Architects know of us but rarely acknowledge our presence. We are neither secret nor celebrated—simply necessary, like the bolts that hold the walkways or the seals that keep the steam pipes from rupturing. When the water's rhythm shifts, when an obstruction forms or a new tributary needs to be opened, we emerge. We adjust the flow with tools that are part of our chambers' walls—levers and wheels that have worn grooves in my palms from generations of use.

I do not sleep as the Architects do. Instead, I enter a state of deep listening, my consciousness merging with the water's movement. In these moments, I understand things I cannot articulate: the health of the fungal colonies by the minerals in the runoff, the stress patterns in the city's foundation by the way sound travels through the liquid medium, the mood of Cinderhold itself by the tempo of its aquatic pulse.

My sustenance comes from the canal itself. Not the water—that would be sacrilege—but from the thin films of algae that form where the flow meets certain metals. These biofilms are bitter, metallic, but nutritious. I cultivate them on specially designed panels in my chamber, harvesting them with a curved tool passed down through countless hands before mine.

Sometimes, the reptilian stalkers pause at the grating above my hatch, their eyes reflecting the water's gleam. They seem to recognize something kindred in me—a fellow observer, a keeper of patterns. Once, during a particularly complex adjustment of the waterways, one dropped a small object through the grating: a scale from its own body, iridescent and perfect. I wear it now on a cord around my neck, the only ornament I possess.

The young Architects sometimes glimpse us during their training, moving through the service passages that parallel the canals. They see our wet footprints on the metal walkways, catch sight of our forms disappearing into the hidden hatches. Some are curious, but their teachers guide them away. "The water knows its keepers," they say, which is both explanation and dismissal.

I am not unhappy. How could I be? I am a nerve in the city's body, a sensor in its vast nervous system. When I place my ear against the canal wall, I can hear the entire city breathing through its water. I know when new sections are being built by the change in mineral content. I sense when the Architects are planning something grand by the way they increase the water's flow in preparation.

My chamber connects to others through passages too narrow for the Architects but perfect for us. We visit each other rarely, sharing observations through touch and gesture rather than words. Our language is the pressure of fingers on wrists, the temperature of palms, the rhythm of breath. We are learning to speak as the water speaks—in flows and eddies, in mineral whispers and thermal currents.

There is one canal I tend with special care. It runs beneath the oldest part of Cinderhold, where the first builders laid stone to stone. The water here carries memories—not in any mystical sense, but in its composition. Traces of ancient mortar, fragments of the valley's original minerals, the chemical signatures of a thousand years of making and remaking. When I taste this water (only a drop on the tongue, only in ritual moments), I taste history itself.

The Architects build the future. We Listeners preserve the flow between past and present, ensuring that the city's lifeblood never stagnates, never fails. We are Cinderhold's memory, written in water, and its whispered promise that the making will continue, steady as the rain that feeds our canals, certain as the stones that channel our purpose.
