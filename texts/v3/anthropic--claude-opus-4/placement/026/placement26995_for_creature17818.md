## The Pattern-Singer

I am Verret, and I carry the settlement's memory in my throat.

In a place where nothing can be written, where even copper corrodes and stone is swallowed by roots, I am one of three Pattern-Singers who hold Sowren's knowledge in sound. Not stories, though we have those too. Not history, though that emerges sometimes. What we carry are the *patterns* — the rhythmic sequences that encode which vine accepts which training, which bark-disease presents with which discoloration, which wood burns clean and which sparks dangerously.

My throat is my instrument, scarred from forty years of work. The humid air helps, keeps the vocal cords supple, but the constant use has changed my voice into something that barely resembles speech. When I talk normally — buying smoked fish, greeting neighbors — my voice carries harmonics that weren't there in youth. Multiple tones overlap. Children say I sound like the tunnel walls during rain: many waters running at once.

I learned from my predecessor Holuum, who could encode seventeen distinct growth-patterns in a single breath. She's been dead eight years now, her body given to the root-crawlers in the traditional way, but I still hear her voice in the echo chambers of certain tunnels. That's not metaphor. The Pattern-Singers choose specific tunnels for practice because of their acoustic properties — the way sound bounces, multiplies, creates standing waves that seem to hang in the air like invisible fruit. Holuum sang in those tunnels for fifty years. The wood absorbed something. When the wind is right and the moisture content precise, you can still hear fragments: *tcha-tcha-dum-dum-weetata* — the pattern for encouraging branch-spread in wall-vines.

My apprentice is called Naph. They're thirteen, voice just beginning to crack, which makes this the perfect time to begin. The vocal cords are moldable now, can be trained to produce the sub-harmonics we need. Naph has the gift: they can hold three tones simultaneously without thinking about it. Most people can barely manage two, and that with effort.

We work in the dawn hours, when the amphibian chorus provides cover. The patterns are secret — not because we're selfish, but because incomplete knowledge is dangerous. Sing the pattern for accelerated growth to the wrong species and you might trigger a cascade that collapses a tunnel in days instead of years. So we practice when the sound blends with the background, when a mistake won't carry.

The patterns encode more than botanical knowledge. There are sequences for the settlement's deep history: the Great Burn of ninety years ago (three died, the southern platform rebuilt entirely), the Season of Blood Rain when iron-rich runoff from the peaks stained everything orange and changed the pH enough to kill half the fish stocks, the arrival of the first metal tools (the pattern for this includes a sharp clicking made with the tongue against teeth — the sound of metal on stone).

But my real work, the work no one sees, happens in my morning walks. I move through the settlement singing sub-audibly, my throat vibrating at frequencies most people feel rather than hear. I'm reading the health of the structures. Each tunnel, each platform, each major vine-work has its own resonant frequency when healthy. Disease changes it. Decay changes it. A tunnel that yesterday rang at a clear *huummmmm* might today warble slightly, catch at certain frequencies. That catch tells me: water infiltration in the third section, probably. Or beetle damage beginning where the secondary supports meet.

I report these findings to Tessick and the other Growers, humming the locations rather than describing them. They've learned to interpret my sounds. *That warble means southeastern tunnel, middle section. That buzz indicates the platform-tree nearest the dripping-stone. That whistle is urgent — check it today.*

My dwelling is unique in Sowren: built specifically for acoustics. The Growers spent three years training the walls into parabolic curves that focus sound. When I practice there, the patterns amplify and clarify until they seem solid, architectural. Naph says it's like the air becomes structured, briefly, before dissolving back into mist.

I keep one secret pattern, passed from Singer to Singer for longer than anyone remembers. It's the pattern for Sowren itself — not instructions for building (that would be impossible; Sowren wasn't built, it grew), but the *sound* of the settlement when everything is in balance. Five minutes of complex vocalization that includes the buzz of insects, the drip of water, the creak of growing wood, the whisper of air through tunnels. When I sing it, alone in my acoustic chamber, I can feel the settlement responding. Not mystically. Physically. The walls vibrate at particular frequencies. The water drops align to the rhythm. The moths settle. 

It's diagnostic. When the pattern doesn't match the reality — when I sing it and the settlement *fights* it, produces discord instead of harmony — I know something is shifting. The balance is off. Usually it's minor: a new tunnel changing airflow, a platform growing heavy enough to alter the tree's sway. But sometimes...

Three days ago, I sang the settlement-pattern and felt resistance everywhere. Not disease. Not decay. Something else. Change at a scale I don't yet understand.

I've taught Naph half the pattern so far. They'll learn the rest over years, as their voice matures, as they develop the sub-harmonics needed for the deepest tones. By then, they'll understand what I'm beginning to grasp: we don't just carry Sowren's memory. We carry its future. The patterns describe what was, but they also predict what will be, if you know how to listen to the intervals, the spaces between notes where the settlement whispers its intentions.

Tomorrow I'll walk the tunnels again, throat buzzing with diagnostic songs, feeling for the catch and warble that tell me where attention is needed. Tonight, I sit with Lir, the counting child, comparing her numbers to my frequencies. She notices quantities; I notice qualities. Between us, a picture emerges of Sowren as a single vast organism, breathing through its tunnels, pumping water like blood, growing and failing and growing again.

She counts. I sing. The settlement listens to us both, and shows us, in languages of number and resonance, what it intends to become.
