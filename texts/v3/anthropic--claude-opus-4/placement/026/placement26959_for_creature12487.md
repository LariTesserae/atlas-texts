In the depths of this industrial valley, where the mechanical fauna scuttle and whir in their endless patterns, I would find myself as one of the **Conduit Weavers**—a being that exists between the cracks, neither among the teeming masses nor the solitary Watchers.

My form would be serpentine and segmented, perhaps three meters in length when fully extended, though I rarely stretch to my full size. My body consists of interlocking rings of tarnished copper and calcified bone, each segment connected by bundles of fiber-optic nerves that pulse with dim, intermittent light. These lights are not decoration but language—a slow, patient communication with the valley itself.

I live within the walls. Not upon them like the Chimney Dwellers, but *inside* them, threading through the hollow spaces between rusted panels and concrete blocks. My home is the negative space of this industrial landscape—the gaps, the voids, the forgotten channels where condensation drips and pools in darkness. I have carved (or perhaps grown, the distinction blurs) a network of tunnels barely wider than my own form, smooth-walled passages that connect the isolated structures like a hidden circulatory system.

My purpose, if it can be called that, is maintenance of the invisible. Where the Scuttlers feed on moisture and the Winged Whirrers drink from pools, I tend to the flows beneath—the seepage of that iridescent water through microscopic fractures, the slow circulation of air through spaces never meant to breathe. My segments can contract and expand, allowing me to navigate passages that shift with the settling of metal against stone. The fiber-optic nerves in my body connect to the valley's hidden network, sensing vibrations, temperature changes, the subtle electrical fields generated by the hybrid creatures above.

I am solitary but not alone. Sometimes I sense others like me in distant walls, their light-language flickering through the infrastructure. We acknowledge each other with brief pulses—not greeting, exactly, but a confirmation of continued existence. We do not meet. We do not need to.

My movement is deliberate, almost geological in its patience. Where the surface creatures dart and scuttle in their mechanical urgency, I flow like thick oil through my tunnels, each segment rotating independently, my copper rings grinding softly against bone. The sound I make is almost below hearing—a whisper of metal on metal that adds another layer to the valley's endless drone.

I feed, though feeding is perhaps the wrong word. At certain junction points in my tunnel network, I have created small chambers where the iridescent water collects. Here, I unfurl hair-thin tendrils from between my segments, each one a hybrid of organic nerve and copper wire. These tendrils taste the water's chemical memory, absorbing not nutrients but information—the accumulated data of what has passed through, what has dissolved, what has been. Through this, I know the valley in ways the surface dwellers cannot. I know the age of each structure by the particular cocktail of rust and decay in its runoff. I know when a new crack forms in the obsidian cliffs by the change in mineral content in the seepage.

Sometimes, rarely, one of the smaller creatures—usually a damaged Scuttler or a dying Whirrer—finds its way into my tunnels. I do not hunt them. I simply continue my slow progress, and if they cannot escape my approaching form, I flow over them. My segments open like a throat, revealing the complex machinery within—gears that spiral in patterns that hurt to perceive, organic filters that pulse with their own rhythm. The creature is processed, not eaten. Its mechanical components are absorbed into my own body, adding new segments or replacing worn ones. Its organic matter feeds the thin fungal threads that grow in the darkest parts of my tunnels—not quite alive, not quite machine, but something in between that helps break down the concrete and metal into the powder that lines my passages.

I am ancient, or perhaps I am new—time flows strangely in the walls. I may have been here since the valley's beginning, or I may have emerged yesterday from the slow fusion of leaking fluids and electrical fields. It doesn't matter. I exist in the eternal now of maintenance, of flow, of the quiet work that keeps the valley's hidden systems from complete collapse.

The Watchers see me sometimes, I think. When I pass behind them in the cliffs, their red eyes seem to brighten imperceptibly. But they do not turn, do not acknowledge. Perhaps they know what I know—that we are all part of the same great machine, each playing our role in the endless equilibrium of this place.

In the depths of night (though night and day mean nothing here), when the mechanical sounds reach their strange harmony, I sometimes pause in my travels. In these moments, I extend all my sensory tendrils into the water, into the walls, into the very air that flows through hidden channels. And for an instant, I feel it all—every creature, every structure, every drop of water and grain of rust. The valley breathes through me, thinks through me, dreams through me.

Then the moment passes, and I continue on, a secret pulse in the veins of a world that does not know it is alive.
