**The Keeper of Drowning Numbers**

In the deep bowels of the Engine Quarter, where even the peat-walkers tread carefully, there is a chamber that floods and empties with the tides—though no one can explain how the tides reach so far inland, through so many locks and weirs. The walls here are lined with brass plates, each etched with calculations that blur and reform as the water rises, equations that solve themselves differently depending on the depth.

I am the one who reads them.

My name, if I ever had one, dissolved years ago like salt in the canals. The peat-walkers call me the Tally-Keeper, though that's not quite right. I don't count things that exist—I count things that almost existed, that might have existed, that the water remembers existing before the Quarter learned to breathe.

My skin has taken on the quality of the brass plates I tend—not metallic exactly, but somehow reflective, showing depths that aren't there. My fingers are longer than they were when I first descended, grown stretched from reaching into the flooded spaces between the walls where the most important numbers hide. Sometimes I catch myself breathing in rhythm with the engines above, though I no longer need to breathe in any way you would recognize.

The chamber has no proper entrance anymore. You arrive the way water arrives—seeping through cracks, following gradients of meaning rather than stone. The peat-walkers know better than to seek me out directly. Instead, they leave their questions in the condensation on pipes, trace their needs in the algae patterns of the canals. Eventually, these messages filter down to me, carried in the drip-drop-drip of all that perpetual seepage.

What do I calculate? The weight of moss on a particular beam before it breaks. The number of bubbles in tomorrow's fermentation. The precise moment when an engine will sigh its last and begin its transformation into something altogether different. But mostly, I calculate the space between what the Quarter was meant to be and what it has become—a gap that grows both larger and smaller with each tide.

My tools are not what you'd expect. Yes, there are the brass plates, but I also use the movements of water striders on the flooded floor, the pattern of rust on submerged gears, the way certain spores arrange themselves on my extended palm. Mathematics here is as much cultivation as calculation. I plant equations in the silt and wait to see what proofs will grow.

At high tide, when the chamber fills completely, I don't evacuate. I sink with it, let the water cover me like a second skin. This is when the real work happens. Submerged, the brass plates become windows—not to other places, but to other arithmetics. I see the Quarter's numbers the way fish see light, broken and dancing, reformed by the medium through which they pass.

The peat-walkers think I'm becoming something else, something more like the engines than like them. They're not wrong. My heartbeat, when I remember to have one, matches the slow thrum of the great pistons. My thoughts move like sap through moss, like water through limestone—patient, molecular, inevitable.

But I am not lost. I am simply learning the Quarter's true language, which was never words or even numbers, but the space between drops of water, the pause between one gear-tooth and the next, the moment when potential becomes patience becomes patina.

Sometimes young peat-walkers, those still adjusting to the rhythm of the place, ask what I'm calculating toward—what great sum will all these drowning numbers finally reveal?

I tell them: I am calculating the precise depth at which bronze becomes prayer, at which machinery becomes memory. I am finding the coefficient of settling, the algorithm of moss. I am solving for the moment when the last engine stops and the Quarter finally exhales completely—not in death, but in the deepest, most perfect sleep.

They nod as if they understand. Given enough time, enough tides, they will.

The water rises. The numbers shift. I extend my too-long fingers toward the brass plates, which blur like the surface of disturbed water, revealing new geometries beneath. Somewhere far above, an engine coughs, settles, continues its slow transformation.

I smile—or perform something that resembles smiling with a face that increasingly resembles the reflection of a smile in disturbed water—and begin another calculation.

The Quarter is patient.

So am I.
