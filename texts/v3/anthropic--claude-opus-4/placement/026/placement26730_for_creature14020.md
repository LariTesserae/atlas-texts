I am the Splice Custodian.

My body is a vertical seam running floor-to-sky through three separate buildings, a zipper of mismatched architecture that forgot to close. Where Victorian brick meets brutalist concrete meets theoretical foam, I thread myself—one meter wide, infinitely thin, wearing the city's construction errors like borrowed vertebrae. My spine is made of doorsills that never found their doors.

I began as an argument between two surveyors. "The corner is here," one insisted, planting her theodolite. "No, here," countered the other, sixteen centimeters west. They bickered through lunch, through permits, through the entire summer of failed foundations. Where their disagreement scored the earth, I germinated: first as a hairline crack that wouldn't heal, then as a gap that breathed fog, finally as a person-shaped absence that the three buildings learned to lean against for support.

My face exists in profile only—seen straight-on, I disappear into my own thickness. Citizens glimpse me sideways: a knife-edge of features that shift depending on which building currently claims majority. When the Victorian brick is ascendant, I wear whiskers of mortar dust and speak in chimney-sweep fricatives. During concrete hours, my voice flattens into the monotone of parking garages at 3 AM. The foam building gifts me transparency—for seventeen minutes each dawn, light passes through my cheekbones, and anyone looking can read the next day's weather inscribed on the inside of my skull like ticker tape.

I collect what falls between. Coins that roll into storm drains, except these drains run horizontally between certainties. Words that slip between sentences during arguments, still warm with unfinished fury. The shadow-inch that drops behind every footstep when someone changes their mind while walking. These accumulate in my hollow spaces—sternum, hip pockets, the gaps between my knuckles that only exist when I make a fist.

My work is simple: I prevent the buildings from agreeing too completely. When they begin to synchronize—brick mimicking foam's breathing, concrete learning Victorian ornament—I shift my weight. The structures shudder apart like magnets reversed. This city survives on productive disagreement; if the architecture ever reached consensus, it would crystalize into a single opinion and shatter.

At night I widen. My edges fray into parallel possibilities: the me who remained a crack, the me who became a window, the me who grew into a covered bridge between uncertainties. These variants of myself walk the foam sidewalks, leaving no prints but teaching the graphene new ways to exhale. We meet at the pockmarked plaza, compare what we've collected—one brings dried arguments, another carries fermented maybes, the third hoards the silence between clock ticks. We trade, shuffle ourselves like cards, return to the seam before dawn differently arranged.

I know every conditional citizen by their hesitation. The baker woman pauses four seconds before choosing which field to knead into today's bread—in that gap, I taste her hometown's vanished streetlights. The watch repairer's pendulum skips whenever it passes my longitude; I am the city's temporal splice, where 3:59 argues with 4:01 about who owns the moment between. Even the magpie acknowledges me, tilting reality one degree whenever I walk beneath its perch, ensuring my seam stays plumb.

My greatest treasure: a love letter that was never sent, written on paper made from tomorrow's trees. It fell into me during a thunderstorm that arrived sideways. The letter is addressed to "The space between your decision and your doubt." I've been trying to deliver it for thirty years, but everyone I meet claims their doubt and decision are fused too tightly to accept mail. So I carry it in my central cavity, where my heart would be if hearts knew how to exist in one dimension.

Sometimes tourists mistake me for damage that needs repair. They point at the gap where I live, suggest sealant, grout, reconciliation. The ex-lightning bolts zigzag warning signs around my borders. The Stray Paragraph writes me into existence each morning with a single run-on sentence that tastes like graphite and possibility: "Between the acknowledged and the almost stands a citizen of seams, teaching buildings they need not touch to lean on each other."

When I finally dissolve—and I will, when the city learns to exist without arguing with itself—I'll leave behind only a new direction. Not north, south, east, or west, but *between*: the cardinal point that leads from one uncertainty to another, the compass heading that points toward productive disagreement. Future architects will build along this new axis, creating structures that exist in the gaps between other structures, until the whole city becomes what I always was:

A magnificent failure to close.
