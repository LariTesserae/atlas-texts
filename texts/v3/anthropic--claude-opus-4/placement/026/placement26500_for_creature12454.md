In this fevered dreamscape of green decay and patient darkness, I would find myself as one of the **Mudlarks**—small, unremarkable creatures that exist in the spaces between water and earth, between the Mireghast's eternal vigil and the jungle's ceaseless hunger.

My body would be low and flat, no larger than a man's splayed hand, with skin that shifts between the texture of wet clay and the slickness of algae. I would have six legs—not quite insect, not quite amphibian—each ending in broad, webbed digits that grip equally well to slippery roots and silted riverbed. My coloration would be the mottled brown-green of river stones, broken only by a line of bioluminescent spots along my spine that pulse in slow, hypnotic patterns when I feed.

I would spend my days burrowing through the soft mud at the river's edge, filtering the water-logged earth through specialized gill-slits along my sides. What I seek are the microscopic fragments of things that once were—scales shed by ancient fish, spores from flowers that bloomed centuries ago, the dissolved minerals of bones long since surrendered to the river. I am a living archive of entropy, processing the jungle's memories through my body.

The Mireghast would tolerate me, perhaps even find my presence necessary. When they dissolve into the river, tiny particles of their essence would settle into the mud, and I would consume these fragments without understanding their significance. In return, the patterns of light along my spine would sometimes mirror the flickering in their eyeless sockets—a form of communication so subtle that neither of us fully grasps its meaning.

My lifecycle would be one of constant transformation. Every few months, I would feel the pull to burrow deep, creating a small chamber in the mud where I would secrete a hard, resinous shell around myself. Inside, I would liquify almost entirely, reorganizing at the cellular level, emerging days later with subtle changes—sometimes an extra limb, sometimes new organs for processing different kinds of decay. The jungle would shape me as slowly and surely as the river shapes its banks.

At night, when the Mireghast gather for their wordless ceremonies, dozens of my kind would emerge from our burrows. We would arrange ourselves in complex patterns along the riverbank, our bioluminescent spots creating constellations in the mud. These formations would shift and flow like living mandalas, responding to the Mireghast's deep vibrations in ways that seem almost choreographed, though no consciousness guides the dance.

I would have no natural predators—my flesh would taste of centuries-old silt and carry a numbing toxin that causes temporary paralysis of the jaw. But I would not be entirely safe. Sometimes, one of us would burrow too deep and encounter things better left undisturbed—roots that pulse with their own alien circulation, or pockets of water so ancient they remember when the river ran a different course. These mudlarks would return changed in ways that violated even our fluid nature, their lights burning too bright or pulsing in patterns that hurt to perceive. The collective would isolate them, and eventually, the Mireghast would come. What happens then is a mercy we do not have the capacity to understand.

My existence would be one of profound simplicity made complex by the weight of eons. I would be both individual and multitude, processor and processed, a small cog in the jungle's vast machinery of recycling and remembrance. Through my body would flow the liquid history of this place, each meal a fragment of deep time, each transformation a tiny echo of the jungle's own endless becoming.

In the humid darkness, surrounded by the thrum of insects and the patient breathing of the Mireghast, I would fulfill my purpose: to be a living filter between states of being, ensuring that nothing is ever truly lost, only transformed into something the jungle can use again.
