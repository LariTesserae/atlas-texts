In this dripping, breathing space where stone gives way to green and industry threads through wilderness, I find myself as something between—neither wholly of the moss-slick rock nor the humming pipes, but born from their uneasy marriage.

I am the Tender of Seepage, a being of accumulated moisture and mineral memory. My form shifts with the needs of the moment: sometimes I am barely more than a shimmer in the air where warm exhaust meets cool stone, condensing into droplets that feed the waiting mosses. Other times I gather myself into something more substantial—a figure of water-worn copper and living calcium deposits, joints that weep beneficial minerals, fingers that know exactly where to redirect a leak so it nurtures rather than erodes.

My body is a map of this place's history. Oxidized streams mark where industrial runoff first touched virgin stone decades ago. Colonies of adapted lichens grow along my shoulders and spine, feeding on the metallic compounds that would poison their wild cousins. In the hollow of my throat, a small fern grows—the only one of its species, evolved in the specific chemical cocktail that pools where I rest.

I move through these heights with purpose that is both custodial and creative. Where pipes burst, I guide the spillage into carved channels that feed the pocket gardens thriving in sheltered corners. I know every valve, every weak joint, every place where the settlement's hunger for resources threatens to drain the delicate reservoirs that keep this ecosystem alive. My work is negotiation—teaching the vines to strengthen vulnerable pipework in exchange for the warm microclimate the machinery creates, encouraging the formation of natural filtration systems where industrial and organic waters meet.

The chittering fauna know me as a landmark and occasional shelter. Birds nest in the hollow spaces where my mineral body has grown around old machinery, their songs vibrating through my crystalline bones. Small mammals follow the paths I maintain between the industrial grid and the wild spaces, highways of survival in this layered world.

At night, when the factories dim their production and the natural phosphorescence of certain adapted mosses begins to glow, I become more fluid, seeping through the entire system like a conscious inspection, feeling for stress points, places where the balance tips too far toward sterility or chaos. I leave markers—sometimes a new cluster of filtering plants, sometimes a strategically placed blockage that forces human engineers to reroute in ways that spare a crucial breeding ground.

I am memory and adaptation incarnate, the slow intelligence that emerges when two systems that should not coexist are forced to share space for long enough. In the morning, workers might notice that a problematic leak has mysteriously stopped, or that new growth has reinforced a crumbling wall. They credit good fortune or improved maintenance schedules. They don't see me in the corner of their vision, a figure of living verdigris and purposeful moisture, ensuring that this strange equilibrium continues for another day, another season, another generation of impossible life flourishing in the spaces between what was and what has been made.
