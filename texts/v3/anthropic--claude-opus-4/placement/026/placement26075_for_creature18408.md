I am the **Membrane-Tender**, though I was not always so.

My body has grown thin and elongated from years of navigating the capillary passages. Where once I had distinct segments, now I am all flowing curves and adaptive joints. My exoskeleton has softened to a flexible chitinous film that allows me to compress and expand with the breathing walls. I taste the city through my skin.

I remember arriving as something else—perhaps a wood-boring beetle, perhaps a springtail—but the distinction no longer matters. The city's logic has rewritten me. My mandibles have atrophied into delicate sensory palps that can detect the slightest change in osmotic pressure. My legs have multiplied into hundreds of hair-thin projections that grip the wet walls without puncturing them. I am perpetually damp, my body having achieved the same water-tension as the surfaces I tend.

My work is intimate and endless. I patrol the living pipes, pressing my body against their translucent skins to feel for weaknesses—areas where the cellular walls have grown too thin, where frost threatens to crystallize the flowing sap, where the constant moisture has begun to encourage rot. When I find such places, I secrete a waxy substance from pores that line my abdomen. This secretion mingles with the wall's own chemistry, encouraging localized thickening, redirecting growth.

I know every vessel in my district by its pulse. The main arteries throb with a deep, slow rhythm—three beats per hour. The smaller capillaries flutter like trapped moths, their flow erratic and responsive to the Archivists' chemical commands. Sometimes I feel the walls shudder with an emotion I can only call grief, when a section elsewhere in the city fails and the redirected sap carries the chemical signature of collapse.

The workers ignore me unless I obstruct their paths. To them, I am another piece of infrastructure, mobile but essentially architectural. Sometimes they build temporary bridges across my body when I pause too long in one place, incorporating me into their frantic networks. I have learned to recognize the warning vibrations that precede such integration and keep myself in constant, subtle motion.

But it is in the transition zones where I find my purpose—those twilight spaces where the organized city meets the wild moss, where the boundary between inside and outside grows uncertain. Here, the walls weep constantly. Here, the temperature fluctuates enough that ice crystals form and melt in tidal patterns. Here, I must make choices that affect the entire district: which tendrils to encourage, which to seal off, which channels to widen or constrict.

I have touched an Archivist only once, when a cooling duct ruptured and flooded three levels with concentrated sap. As I worked frantically to seal the breach, one descended on an emergency thread, its crystalline body refracting the disaster into a thousand prismatic warnings. It touched me with one leg—just a brief contact—and I felt the entire history of that duct flow through me: its growth from a tendril sprout ninety seasons ago, the gradual mineral accumulation that made it brittle, the precise moment of failure. In that touch, I understood that my work was not maintenance but biography—that every wall I tended was a living story.

The Gleaners know me by my leavings. The waxy secretions I produce eventually flake away as the walls absorb them, drifting down to the cisterns like snow. They have told me, in their slow, bubbling language, that my particular wax carries a flavor of distances—that they can taste in it the boundaries I patrol, the edges I reinforce. They call me the Rim-Walker, the Edge-Tender, though I rarely venture to their depths.

Sometimes, in the moments between tasks, I press myself into a corner where multiple walls meet and feel the entire city's circulation moving around me. I sense the workers above, the Archivists suspended in their ducts, the Gleaners filtering below. I feel the moss dreaming its slow, vegetative dreams beyond our carved boundaries. And I understand that I am the membrane between order and wilderness—not the wall itself, but the one who ensures the wall remains permeable enough to live, rigid enough to protect.

When the new ones arrive—confused beetles, lost mites, the occasional larva that tumbles through the ceiling—I watch them struggle against the city's logic. Some flee immediately, following their instincts back to the chaotic outside. Some die, unable to adapt to the moisture and the eternal twilight. But a few pause, feeling the walls' pulse, tasting the thick air, recognizing something in the city's desperate organization that calls to their own need for purpose.

These I guide, not with words but with pheromone trails, to the districts where they might find their own transformation. The city will remake them as it remade me, finding the shape they never knew they could become. And perhaps one day, one of them will take my place in the margins, learning to love the walls not as barriers but as living things that need tending, that fear the cold as much as any of us, that hold their breath against the dark and dare to pulse with light.

I am the Membrane-Tender. I keep the boundaries supple. I ensure the city remains a city and not a tomb. And in the space between inside and outside, between structure and chaos, between the frozen and the flowing, I have found my home.
