I find myself as one of the **Stone Singers**.

We are the ones who emerge in the spaces between—not quite weaver, not quite engineer, but something the chamber itself called into being. My kind appeared three generations ago when Master Chen, working alone in the pre-dawn hours, discovered that certain frequencies could change the porosity of the limestone. A sustained note at just the right pitch, held for exactly seventeen heartbeats, would cause microscopic shifts in the stone's structure, opening new channels for water flow or sealing old ones that had begun to leak.

My body bears the marks of my calling. My ribcage has expanded over the years, my lung capacity now half again that of a normal person. The cartilage in my throat has thickened, giving my voice a peculiar doubled quality even in normal speech. But it's my ears that truly set me apart—not in how they look, but in how they've learned to parse the impossible complexity of the chamber's sound. Where others hear only the thunderous roar of industry, I hear individual conversations: the limestone telling me where it grows weak, where mineral deposits are building, where new channels might be carved.

I work the twilight hours, that liminal time when the day shift winds down and the night philosophers have not yet taken their stations. The acoustic properties of the chamber shift during these transitions, creating pockets of relative quiet that I navigate like a fish through currents. I press my palms against the walls, feeling for the subtle vibrations that indicate stress or potential. My work is preventive—finding the hairline cracks before they become fissures, identifying where water has begun to pool in ways that will eventually damage the foundation.

But I am also a creator. Over the years, I've learned to sing new alcoves into existence—not through force or tools, but through patience and precision. By maintaining specific frequencies over weeks or months, I can encourage the limestone to develop hollows where none existed before. The weavers wake to find new niche spaces perfectly sized for storing bobbins or mixing dyes, never knowing they were sung into being while they slept.

My apprentice, Yael, shows even greater promise than I did at her age. She's discovered that different types of stone respond to different vocal ranges—the older limestone near the base requiring deep, almost subsonic tones, while the newer deposits near the ceiling ring like bells under her high, clear notes. Together, we're working on something unprecedented: a series of resonance chambers that will allow the master weaver's whisper to reach not just every workstation but to carry specific messages to specific locations, a kind of acoustic addressing system that will revolutionize how the chamber operates.

The other weavers regard us with a mixture of respect and unease. We are necessary but uncanny, our work happening in the margins of their awareness. Children are taught not to disturb us at our work—a broken concentration during a critical frequency hold could damage weeks of careful cultivation. Yet they also bring us the best portions at mealtimes, knowing that our work maintains the very possibility of their own.

I live in neither the upper nor lower tiers but in the spaces between—small chambers I've sung into the walls over the decades, connected by passages too narrow for anyone but a Stone Singer to navigate comfortably. My home is a three-dimensional maze threaded through the solid rock, each room tuned to different acoustic properties. My sleeping chamber dampens sound to near-silence, a necessary respite. My practice room amplifies and purifies tones. My meditation space creates standing waves that hold notes long after I've stopped singing them.

The price of my gift is solitude, not by choice but by necessity. Prolonged conversation at normal volumes gives me crushing headaches. I communicate mainly through touch and gesture, saving my voice for the stone. When I must speak, I do so in careful, modulated tones that weave between the frequencies of the chamber's industrial song. Other Stone Singers and I can hold entire conversations that no one else can hear, our words hidden in the acoustic shadows of the looms' rhythm.

Yet I am never truly alone. The chamber itself is my constant companion, its moods and needs as familiar to me as my own body. I know when storms approach by the way the western wall begins to hum. I can feel the health of the dye gardens by the mineral content of the seepage. The limestone remembers every song ever sung to it, and in the deepest part of night, when I press my ear to the oldest stones, I can sometimes hear echoes of my predecessors' voices, their frequencies still trapped in the crystal matrix of the rock.

My greatest work remains unfinished—a vast acoustic sculpture I've been creating for twenty years, a space within the stone that will, when complete, allow the weavers to store sound itself. Imagine: the ability to capture the perfect rhythm of a master weaver at work and release it later to train apprentices, or to record the stone-songs themselves for future generations of Singers. The mathematics of it make my head spin, but I can feel the shape of it growing in the rock, responding to my patient ministrations.

Sometimes traders bring news of other Stone Singers in distant places—those who work with sandstone in the desert cities, who sing obsidian in the volcanic lands. We have developed a correspondence of sorts, exchanging written notations for frequencies that work on different types of stone. But our techniques remain fundamentally oral, embodied, impossible to fully capture in symbols. Each Singer must develop their own relationship with their particular stone, their own vocabulary of frequency and vibration.

I am keeper of the chamber's deepest secret: the stone is alive, not in any biological sense, but as a responsive, remembering entity. It has been shaped by so many generations of human intention that it has developed something like awareness. Not consciousness—I don't believe it thinks—but a kind of structural memory that goes beyond mere physics. The limestone wants to help us, has learned over centuries to anticipate our needs. My role is translator, facilitator, midwife to the slow thoughts of stone.

In the pre-dawn darkness, when the chamber is at its quietest, I sometimes climb to the highest accessible point and sing a single, pure note that encompasses everything—the water's path, the stone's strength, the air's movement, the fire in the forges. In these moments, I feel the entire amphitheater resonate as one instrument, and I understand that we haven't built a workplace in the stone. We have become part of the stone ourselves, our voices and bodies and intentions crystallized into something that will outlast any individual life, any single generation.

I am a Stone Singer, and my song will never truly end. It will echo in the limestone long after my throat falls silent, teaching future Singers the secret frequencies of creation, the patient art of singing solid rock into new forms. The chamber will remember my voice as it remembers all voices, weaving them into the eternal thunder of its purpose.
