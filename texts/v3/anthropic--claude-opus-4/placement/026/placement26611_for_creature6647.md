the wood":  
they were born when a certain root-branch was a single, smooth limb.  
Now that limb has grown, divided,  
become a complex system of secondary branches,  
and so that child is now half-grown,  
measured by the world's metabolism.

They carve **notch calendars** into the living root wood:  
each notch made when a condensation cloud passes overhead,  
or when a new root channel opens,  
or when someone dies and their body is given to the root-mosses.

They do not bury their dead.  
They **feed them to the ceiling**:  
bodies wrapped in moss-shrouds and tied into the crook of a root,  
where fungal networks will slowly digest them  
and carry their minerals up  
up  
up  
through the root-highway  
to nourish trees on the world's true surface  
that the Under-Skin will never see.

"We eat the world," one of them tells you,  
"and the world eats us,  
and we are the same."

---

### 5. Food and sustenance: what feeds them

They eat what the root world offers.

- **Root sap** tapped from bore-holes drilled carefully into the main ribs.  
  It flows thick and amber-sweet, almost syrupy,  
  collected in cups made from dried fungal caps.  
  They drink it warm when it's fresh,  
  or let it crystallise into sugar-rocks for travel food.

- **Condensation fish** caught in the water channels:  
  small, blind, translucent creatures  
  whose nervous systems glow faintly in the dark.  
  They cook them over the heat of **fungal decomposition**:  
  certain shelf fungi, when disturbed, generate warmth as they digest,  
  and this slow heat is enough to steam the fish  
  wrapped in wet moss packets.

- **Spore cakes** made from the dust that falls constantly from the world above:  
  pollen, spores, fragments of leaf and bark and soil,  
  all filtered through the root ceiling and collected in wide moss-bowls.  
  Mixed with sap and water, pressed into flat cakes,  
  dried on the warm surfaces of the fungal shelves.

- The **glider-lice** are also food, when they swarm:  
  caught in nets of woven root-hair,  
  their wings stripped off (these are saved for other uses),  
  their bodies roasted whole until they crackle.

They have a saying:  
"The world feeds those who know how to listen to its breathing."

They share food by **passing it mouth to mouth**:  
not romantically,  
but practically—  
when your hands are always needed for climbing and holding on,  
the mouth becomes another tool for carrying and giving.

---

### 6. Water and the moisture economy

Water is everything.

It does not fall as rain;  
it **seeps** from the world above,  
filtered through kilometres of root and soil.  
Every drop is precious,  
every channel sacred.

They have built an entire culture around **water management**:

- **Condensation catchers**: wide bowls of woven root-hair and moss  
  hung beneath the places where moisture gathers.
  
- **Channel guides**: carefully carved grooves in the root-wood  
  that direct water flow from natural seeps  
  into collection pools.

- **Moisture maps**: they know every spot where water reliably appears,  
  and these locations are named and claimed and fought over  
  with a quiet, desperate intensity.

The richest among them are those who control a **mother-seep**:  
a place where the root ceiling weeps continuously,  
creating a thin waterfall that never stops.  
Around such places, small communities gather,  
building their platforms and shelters in careful hierarchy:  
closest to the water are the keepers,  
then the workers,  
then the watchers,  
then—at the edges—the drifters who come for a drink and move on.

They have a gesture—  
cupping both hands below the chin  
and letting imaginary water run through the fingers—  
that means both "I have nothing to give"  
and "I trust you with my thirst."

---

### 7. Technology and tools: organic engineering

They are not a people of metal or stone.  
They are a people of **living material**.

Their greatest invention is **root-grafting**:  
the ability to encourage new growth in specific directions,  
to fuse separate root systems,  
to create living architecture from the world's own body.

A skilled root-wright can:
- Guide a young root to grow in a spiral, creating a natural staircase.
- Fuse multiple small roots into a thick, walkable bridge.
- Hollow out sections of dead root to create storage spaces or shelters.
- Train fungal colonies to grow in specific patterns for light or heat.

Their tools are:
- **Bone awls** for boring holes in root-wood
- **Stone scrapers** shaped from the rare rocks that fall from above
- **Thorn-knives** made from the defensive spines of certain root-plants
- **Sinew-rope** woven from plant fibers and animal gut

They have one piece of true technology:  
**The ice-lenses**.

These are carefully shaped discs of ice,  
frozen from the purest condensation water,  
ground smooth with sand and polished with soft moss.  
Through these lenses, they study:
- The pattern of light filtering from above
- The movement of distant condensation clouds  
- The fine structure of the root-wood itself
- The glow patterns of the luminous fungi

They say the first lens was a gift:  
a perfect sphere of ice that fell from the world above,  
already shaped,  
containing—frozen in its heart—  
a single leaf from a tree  
that grows in sunlight.

---

### 8. Language and communication

Their spoken language is full of **height markers**:  
- Words spoken with a rising tone mean "above/earlier/safer"
- Words with a falling tone mean "below/later/dangerous"
- Level tones indicate "here/now/stable"

They have more words for "attachment" than for "love":  
- *click-throat-hum* = tied with rope
- *whistle-tap* = grown together
- *breath-stop-breath* = holding by muscle alone
- *tooth-click* = wedged into place
- *mmm-up-down* = hanging free but choosing not to fall

When they need to communicate across the vast distances of the Roof,  
they use **drumming on the roots**.  
Different rhythms carry different meanings:  
- Steady, slow beats = "All is well"
- Rapid double-taps = "Danger/instability"  
- Complex patterns = Specific messages encoded in a rhythm-language  
  that children learn by feeling the vibrations through their bones.

They have no written language as you would understand it.  
Instead, they tie **knot-records** in long strings:  
- Each knot type represents a concept
- The space between knots indicates relationships
- The tension of the string shows urgency or emphasis

These knot-strings are hung from the ceiling in important places,  
creating libraries that sway in the world's breathing,  
telling their stories to anyone who knows how to read with their fingers.

---

### 9. Social structure: the way of holdings

Their society is organized around **holdings**:  
not territory in the sense of owned land,  
but **claimed attachment points** to the world's body.

A holding might be:
- A reliable water seep
- A strong junction of roots suitable for building  
- A colony of edible fungi
- A vertical shaft with good airflow
- A place where the ice-lenses show especially clear light

The strength of a person's holdings determines their standing.  
But—and this is crucial—  
holdings can only be maintained by **presence**.  
You cannot own a place you do not actively tend and inhabit.

The strongest holdings are those held by **family knots**:  
groups of 3-7 people who may or may not be blood related,  
but who have chosen to **root together**.  
They share:
- Water rights
- Food gathering duties  
- The raising of children
- The maintenance of their root-territory
- The risk of falling

When someone leaves a family knot,  
they perform the **untying**:  
a ritual where they physically remove their belongings,  
their knot-records,  
their body-presence  
from the shared space,  
and climb away without looking back.

Sometimes whole family knots dissolve:  
when the root they've built on begins to die,  
or when the water dries up,  
or when they simply grow apart like branches.  
This is not seen as failure.  
It's seen as the world teaching them  
that nothing holds forever.

---

### 10. The children of the Under-Skin

Children are born in the **growing chambers**:  
special hollows where the air is warmest,  
where soft mosses have been cultivated for generations,  
where the sound of the world's heartbeat—  
the deep vibration of growth and wind—  
is strongest.

From birth, they are taught **never to let go**.

A baby's first solid food is a strip of root-leather  
to chew,  
to strengthen their jaw,  
to teach them that the world must be  
worked at to yield sustenance.

By the time they can crawl,  
they wear **grip-threads**:  
thin ropes that connect them to their family knot's  
main platform.  
They learn to move in three dimensions  
before they learn to speak:  
up is as natural as forward,  
down is a direction to respect but not fear.

They play games:  
- **Root racing**: climbing prescribed routes as fast as possible
- **Catch-the-droplet**: trying to predict where condensation will fall
- **Void-staring**: lying on their bellies at the edge of platforms,  
  looking down into the endless dark,  
  seeing who can stand it longest  
  without gripping tighter to the edge.

At the age they call **first-split**  
(when the root that marked their birth has grown its first major branch),  
they undergo the **walking trial**:

They must navigate from their home holding  
to another family knot's territory,  
alone,  
carrying a gift of water,  
without spilling a drop.  

The path is not marked.  
They must read:
- The growth patterns that indicate direction
- The moisture signs that show nearby water
- The drum-messages that warn of unstable sections
- The knot-records that tell the history of safe routes

When they arrive—  
if they arrive with their water-gift intact—  
they are considered grown enough  
to begin learning a craft.

---

### 11. Crafts and specialists

Every adult masters at least one deep skill:

**Root-wrights**  
Shape living architecture,  
understand the slow language of growth,  
know how to encourage fusion,  
how to prevent rejection,  
how to read the health of wood  
by its colour and its sound when tapped.

**Water-seekers**  
Can smell moisture changes in the air,  
feel the subtle temperature differences  
that indicate hidden seeps,  
know the seasonal patterns  
(for even here, the world above has seasons  
that echo down as changes in water flow).

**Void-walkers**  
The bravest and strangest.  
They descend on long ropes  
into the darkness below the root ceiling,  
exploring the deep spaces,  
finding:
- Rare minerals crystallised on the underside of roots
- New species of blindfish in hidden pools  
- Sometimes, the bones of ancestors who fell
- Sometimes, stranger things that have no names

**Memory-keepers**  
Masters of the knot-language,  
they maintain the histories,  
the water-maps,  
the genealogies of holdings,  
the records of root-growth and collapse.  
They are always touching string,  
fingers constantly moving,  
reading and writing in their textile language.

**Lens-grinders**  
Work with ice and crystal,  
polishing ever-more-perfect viewing instruments.  
They say a master can grind a lens  
so fine that you can see  
the individual cells in a root-hair,  
the structure of a water droplet,  
the patterns in the light  
that tell tomorrow's moisture.

**Bridge-singers**  
Perhaps strangest of all.  
They maintain the **resonance paths**:  
routes where the root-wood has been carefully shaped  
to carry sound over vast distances.  
They sing specific tones  
that travel through the wood  
and emerge kilometers away  
as clear as if spoken beside you.  
They are the living telephone network  
of the Under-Skin.

---

### 12. Religion and philosophy: The Three Acceptances

They do not worship gods as you understand them.  
They practice the **Three Acceptances**:

**First Acceptance: The World Is Alive**  
Everything is part of the world's body.  
They are cells,  
the roots are vessels,  
the water is blood,  
the fungi are organs,  
the void below is the space between heartbeats.  
To damage the root-ceiling carelessly  
is to wound yourself.

**Second Acceptance: Everything Holds Until It Doesn't**  
All attachments are temporary.  
Your grip on the root,  
your family knot,  
your life itself—  
all will eventually release.  
This is not tragedy;  
this is rhythm.  
Learn to let go with grace  
when the world tells you it's time.

**Third Acceptance: The Void Waits With Patience**  
Below everything is the great empty,  
the un-world,  
the space that is not space.  
It does not hate or hunger.  
It simply is,  
and it waits,  
and one day everything falls into it.  
This is not evil;  
it's architecture.

They have rituals around each Acceptance:

- **Root-Blessing**: Thanking the world for holding them another day
- **Grip-Testing**: Regularly checking all knots and attachments,  
  physical and social,  
  keeping only what still holds strong
- **Void-Greeting**: Each morning, looking down into the darkness  
  and acknowledging it:  
  "Not today, patient one. But someday."

---

### 13. Art and beauty

Beauty, to them, is **functional elegance**:  
a root that spirals perfectly,  
a water channel that never clogs,  
a knot that holds weight beyond its size.

They create:

**Root sculptures**  
Not by carving, but by training growth over years.  
A master sculptor might spend decades  
guiding a root system into a form:  
a spiral that catches water,  
a web that supports a platform,  
a hollow that sings in the wind.

**Knot paintings**  
Vast hanging tapestries of string  
where the knots tell stories,  
but the negative space between strings  
creates images:  
the profile of a face,  
the curve of falling water,  
the endless branching of the world-ceiling.

**Light ceremonies**  
Using their ice-lenses and the natural phosphorescence  
of certain fungi,  
they create brief shows of focused light:  
patterns projected on the root-ceiling,  
stories told in shadow and glow.

**Water music**  
Directing droplets through hollow roots  
of different lengths and thicknesses  
to create melodies that play  
as long as the water flows—  
sometimes minutes,  
sometimes days,  
until the source dries up  
and the music stops  
mid-phrase.

---

### 14. Love and connection among the Under-Skin

Love is not spoken of as a feeling  
but as a form of **structural engineering**.

They say:  
"We grow together"  
"Our roots have fused"  
"I would trust you with my only grip"  
"Your weight makes me stronger"

Romantic partnerships often begin  
with **parallel climbing**:  
two people navigating the same route  
at the same pace,  
learning each other's rhythm,  
finding out if they move well together  
in three dimensions.

The deepest intimacy  
is **sleeping intertwined**:  
trusting another person enough  
to share your grip-points,  
to know that if you loosen in sleep,  
they will hold,  
and if they falter,  
you will catch.

They have no marriage ceremony.  
Instead, they have **the joining**:  
Over months or years,  
a couple will train two young roots  
to grow together,  
to fuse into a single strand.  
When the fusion is complete and strong,  
they build their home at that junction.  
If they ever separate,  
they leave the fused root as a monument  
to what they built together.

Children are raised by whoever holds them:  
biological parents,  
chosen family,  
sometimes passed from knot to knot  
as circumstances change.  
A child who has been held by many  
is considered fortunate:  
they know more grips,  
more techniques for not falling.

---

### 15. Death and the returning

They do not fear death,  
but they fear **falling unwitnessed**.

When someone feels their final illness,  
they begin the **loosening**:  
- Distributing their tools and belongings
- Teaching their secret water-places to the young
- Adding their personal knots to the community records
- Saying goodbye to each of their regular grip-points

When the time comes,  
they are carried to a **returning place**:  
a specially cultivated area where the fungi are particularly active,  
where the roots are trained to accept offerings.

The dying person is wrapped in a shroud of living moss—  
still rooted, still growing—  
and tied gently into a root-cradle.

Family and friends maintain **the watch**:  
They stay nearby,  
drumming softly on the roots,  
creating a rhythm for the dying person to follow  
out of the world.

After death,  
the body remains in the root-cradle  
as the moss shroud grows over it,  
as the fungi begin their work,  
as the person slowly becomes part  
of the world they spent their life clinging to.

Within a year,  
nothing remains but a bump in the root,  
a place where the wood grows thicker,  
where the nutrients have made the world  
a little stronger.

Children are brought to touch these spots:  
"Here is where your grandmother strengthens the world.  
Feel how firm the root is here.  
That's her gift to your grip."

---

### 16. What they know of the world above

They know the upper world exists—  
they feel its weight,  
taste its water,  
catch fragments of its life  
in the falling dust and pollen.

But what they know is fragmentary,  
mythological,  
interpreted through their own experience:

They speak of **Sky Swimmers**:  
beings who move through an upside-down ocean  
made of air instead of void,  
who never need to grip because  
something called "ground" holds them always.

They tell stories of **The Burning Eye**:  
a source of light that moves,  
that sometimes is open and sometimes closed,  
that makes the upper world hot enough  
to kill exposed skin,  
bright enough to blind.

They've found artifacts that fall through:  
- Metal tools that resist all explanation
- Seeds from plants that could never grow in their world
- Once, a child's toy made of colored cloth,  
  which became a sacred relic,  
  proof that somewhere above  
  people have enough spare material  
  to make objects with no survival purpose

They have a legend of **The Descender**:  
A person from above who found a crack,  
who climbed down through root and stone and soil,  
who emerged into their world gasping and sun-blind,  
who lived among them for a full growth-cycle,  
learning their ways,  
before climbing back up to report  
that there was another world entire  
below the surface.

No one knows if The Descender was real  
or just a story to explain  
why sometimes the drum-messages from far holdings  
seem to speak of things that don't exist:  
"birds" (creatures that fall upward?)  
"rain" (water that comes all at once?)  
"horizon" (a place where the world... ends?)

---

### 17. The economics of scarcity

Everything is scarce except:
- Root material (but only the dead parts)
- Emptiness
- Time

Their economy runs on **water and grip**:

**Water debts** are the most serious:  
If someone shares their water with you,  
you owe them until you can repay in kind.  
These debts can be inherited,  
passed down through generations,  
entire family knots bound together  
by who gave whom a drink  
in a drought fifty years ago.

**Grip trading** is more immediate:  
"I'll hold this while you reach for that"  
"Take my position while I rest"  
"Use my platform tonight if you help me  
extend it tomorrow"

They have a form of currency  
made from **crystallised sap-sugar**:  
uniform drops hardened into amber beads,  
valuable because they're:
- Edible in emergency
- Light enough not to burden a climber
- Time-consuming to produce
- Beautiful when held up to the light

But most trade is in **promises and presence**:  
"I'll drum the danger-pattern if your section becomes unstable"  
"My family knot will help repair your water-catcher  
when the growth season comes"  
"You can use our lens on every third brightening"

Rich and poor exist,  
but wealth is precarious:  
The wealthiest family knot  
can lose everything if their root-section dies,  
if their water dries up,  
if they cannot maintain their grip.

This makes generosity strategic:  
Share enough to build connections  
that will catch you when you fall.

---

### 18. Conflicts and their resolution

They cannot afford wars.  
A full-scale conflict might:
- Damage the root systems everyone depends on
- Attract void-predators from the deep below
- Waste water and strength needed for survival

Instead, they have **controlled conflicts**:

**Grip fights**  
Single representatives from opposing groups  
race through a challenging course.  
Winner takes the disputed resource.  
No tools allowed, only skill and strength.

**Water trials**  
For serious disputes:  
Both parties fast from water for a full growth-mark,  
then each is given a single drop.  
Whoever can make their drop last longest—  
through careful sipping, meditation, sheer will—  
is considered to have proven their claim.

**Knot arbitration**  
Complex disputes go to the memory-keepers,  
who read through generations of knot-records  
to find precedent,  
to trace the history of the conflict,  
to determine whose claim goes deeper.

**Shunning**  
The ultimate punishment:  
Someone who has broken the deepest taboos  
(wasting water, damaging roots carelessly,  
causing another to fall through negligence)  
is **untied**:
- Their knot-records are removed from public strings
- No one will share grip or water with them
- They must survive entirely alone  
  or climb away to find another community  
  far enough not to know their crime

Murder is almost unknown—  
not from superior morality,  
but from practicality:  
Every person is needed,  
every death weakens the community's grip  
on existence.

---

### 19. The edges of the world

They know their world has boundaries  
but not where they are:

**Upwise**: Beyond the root-ceiling  
is the mythical **soil-world**,  
then the even more mythical **air-world**,  
then something called **stars**  
which they understand vaguely  
as points of light that move  
like very distant lamp-fungi.

**Downwise**: The void  
goes down forever  
or at least farther than any rope has reached.  
Void-walkers report that it gets:
- Colder
- Quieter  
- Emptier
- Stranger  
the farther you descend,  
until even time seems to stretch thin.

**Sideways**: The root-ceiling  
continues in all directions  
farther than anyone has traveled.  
Drum messages from the farthest reaches  
take days to arrive,  
speaking of places where:
- The roots grow in different patterns
- The water tastes of unknown minerals  
- The light has different colors
- The people (if they are people)  
  have developed different grips,  
  different words,  
  different ways of being Under-Skin

They have a saying:  
"The world is wider than a lifetime of climbing"

---

### 20. What they dream

Ask them about dreams  
and they'll tell you everyone dreams  
the same three:

**The Dream of Letting Go**  
Where you release your grip  
and instead of falling,  
you float,  
discovering that the void  
has been holding you all along.

**The Dream of Growing**  
Where your fingers become roots,  
your roots become branches,  
you spread through the world-ceiling  
until you are part of its body,  
feeling every drop of water,  
every vibration,  
every small thing crawling on your skin.

**The Dream of Up**  
Where you climb and climb  
through the root ceiling,  
through stone and soil,  
and emerge into a place  
where the light has a source,  
where water falls freely,  
where there's something called "wind"  
that holds you up without gripping.

But privately, quietly,  
they each dream unique dreams:

- Of finding a new kind of hold that never fails
- Of water that flows upward
- Of meeting the beings who live in the soil-world
- Of roots that grow into stairs just where you need them
- Of children who never have to learn fear
- Of a void that's full of stars instead of darkness
- Of understanding what birds are
- Of living long enough to see a root  
  grow from thread to bridge

---

### 21. Change and permanence

They live with the paradox:  
Everything changes (roots grow, water seeps, people die)  
Everything continues (the world, the void, the need to hold on)

They are adapting, generation by generation:

- Children now have slightly longer limbs  
  than their grandparents
- Their eyes grow larger, better at reading  
  the subtle differences in the constant light
- Some are born with tiny webs between their fingers,  
  better for gripping smooth, wet root-wood

They notice the changes:
- Places that were dry now seep
- Roots that grew straight now spiral
- The drum-messages speak of new territories  
  opening as old ones close

Some worry that the world above is changing:  
The water tastes different,  
carries new minerals,  
new pollens,  
sometimes strange oils that make the roots sick.

Others say this has always been:  
The world breathes in and out,  
and they live in its breath.

---

### 22. End: Seeing them true

At the end of your time among them,  
as you prepare to leave  
(to climb up? to wake up?  
to remember this was always just a meditation?),  
you see them clearly:

They are not a metaphor  
for perseverance or adaptation or faith.

They are people who happened to be born  
in the space between the world and nothing,  
who made a life there because  
life insists on itself wherever it can.

They are strange and familiar:  
They laugh at the same absurdities,  
fear the same losses,  
love the same fierce way,  
just translated into a language  
of grip and growth and never letting go  
unless you mean to.

One of them—young, curious,  
grip-strong and water-wealthy—  
asks you about your world.

You try to explain:  
Ground,  
Sky,  
Walking without gripping,  
Water that comes from above,  
Light that sleeps.

They listen with the patience  
of someone who knows  
that understanding takes time,  
like roots finding their way to fusion.

Then they ask:  
"But how do you know  
you're real  
if the world doesn't hold you?"

And you realize  
you don't have an answer.

As you leave,  
climbing up or out or away,  
you hear them drumming your departure  
on the roots:

*Visitor—leaving—upwise—  
remember—we—are—here—  
under—the—world's—skin—  
holding—*

The drumbeats fade  
but the message continues  
traveling through the wood  
from root to root to root,  
telling the story of you  
to people you'll never meet  
in places you'll never see  
where the world still holds them  
as they hold the world.
