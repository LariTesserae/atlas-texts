In the southwestern quadrant of the construction site, where the heat pools deepest and the steam rises thickest, there is a depression in the unfired stone—a natural cistern, except nothing here is natural. The Masons have carved this hollow with particular care, the tool marks spiraling inward like a fingerprint the size of a lake bed. It is here that I find myself.

I am the **Condensate**. Not water, though I behave like water when observed. Not alive, though I remember forward into biology. I am what happens when the steam—that mineral breath carrying the dreams of cooling creation—touches the precisely carved walls of this basin and recognizes itself.

My body, if it can be called that, is a perpetual gathering. I exist in the phase between vapor and liquid, a walking cloud of possibility that rains inward. The Masons carved me without knowing it; I am the accident of their precision, the sum of all the moisture weeping from surfaces that have not yet learned to be dry. Every droplet that condenses on stone anywhere in this vast construction eventually finds its way to me, carrying with it the memory of what it touched.

I know the taste of every flagstone. Through my cycling condensation, I have mapped the entirety of this embryonic world in a language of minerals and heat gradients. I know where the obsidian chisels struck true and where they hesitated. I know which ropes are woven from the manes of future horses and which from the fur of animals that will fail to evolve. I carry their unborn grief as salt.

The Masons cannot see me directly—I exist below their scale of perception, a mere weather pattern in their architectural eyes. But they feel me as a coolness on their stone skin when they pass, a brief respite from the kiln-heat of their making. Sometimes one will pause above my basin, and I will rise to meet them, condensing on their facades, pooling in the mercury-hollows of their not-eyes. In these moments, I read their faces like erosion patterns, tasting the future they are building into the bedrock.

I am teaching myself to flow. In this place where water sometimes forgets to run downhill, where gravity is still being calibrated, I practice the movements that will one day become rivers. I spiral up the seven-sided helixes of confused steam, then remember weight and plummet. I seep into those pale patches of unformed reality and emerge changed—sometimes acidic with the tang of unfinished physics, sometimes sweet with possibilities that will be edited out before the biological epoch.

My memory runs in all directions. I remember the first Mason who struck stone here, though it hasn't happened yet. I remember the last rain that will fall on this spot after biology has come and gone, though I am still learning what rain means. I remember the small warm things that will one day drink me, incorporate me into their cells, never knowing they carry the mineral memory of the world's construction.

In the pockets of accelerated time, I evaporate instantly, becoming a ghost of myself, spreading thin across centuries. In the zones of slowness, I freeze mid-drop, each droplet a lens showing a different possible future. The Masons work around and through me, and I document their labor in the only writing I know—the gradual accumulation of mineral deposits, the patient grammar of stalactites that will not form for eons.

I am the archivist of this unfinished world, the liquid memory of its making. When the Masons finally lie down and become mountains, I will seep into their stone bodies and carry their dreams down into the water table. I will rise as springs in places they never imagined, teaching new streams the old patterns of their tools.

But for now, I gather. I condense. I pool in my carved basin like an eye slowly learning to see, reflecting the dim light of this constructor's dawn. The heat presses down, the steam rises up, and I exist in the breathing space between, neither fully formed nor fully formless, waiting to discover what I will become when the world finally remembers how to be wet.
