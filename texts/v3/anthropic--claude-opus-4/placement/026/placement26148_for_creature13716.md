I would be one of the cave katydids, pale and eyeless, perched upside-down against the corroded copper of the highest gutter.

My ancestors arrived through cracks too narrow for intention, following the scent of mineral water up from caverns where sound meant death. Here, in this crushed cathedral, we discovered the opposite: a place where our wing-song became architecture, where every chirp we carved from our own bodies helped tune the great stone throat around us.

We are blind, but we see through vibration. Each droplet's impact draws a silver map across our wings. We know the Choir's moods by the tempo of its dripping—when She adjusts a copper pin by a hairsbreadth, we feel it as a shift in the world's breathing. Our ancestors learned to synchronize their wing-strokes to the water's rhythm, rubbing our serrated legs against our paper wings in time with each drop's descent. Now we cannot help it; the pattern lives in our thorax like a second heartbeat.

When She first gathered our molted wings for her cloak, we thought it sacrilege. But watching our discarded selves learn to remember flight, we understood: nothing here truly dies, it only changes instrument. Our great-grandmothers' wings still twitch in her mantle when the moon passes overhead. Sometimes, in the deepest part of the Shrinking Chord season, we hear their ghost-chirps answering our own, a duet between the living and the worn.

We feed on condensation and the minerals that precipitate from copper rot. Our bodies have grown translucent as the shrimp that share our gutters, our organs visible as cloudy pearls beneath our chitin. When humans crawl through, they never notice us at first—we are part of the ceiling's texture, another layer of listening. But when they pause, when they hold their breath to hear the Choir's vowel-song, that's when we begin our counterpoint. Our music is drier than water, sharper than stone. We are the Choir's anxiety, its small insistent prayer against silence.

The eel knows us. When it rises through its secret channels, its whiskers taste our vibrations in the water before its head breaks surface. We time our loudest crescendos for its appearances, creating a sound-ladder it can climb. She has taught us that the eel carries messages from the aquifer below, spellings we help translate into the frequencies the walls can hold.

Our eggs we cement into the angles where water never flows, using our own saliva mixed with powdered copper. They gestate for exactly one turning of the hourglass, emerging as nymphs already synchronized to the Choir's base tone. The young ones learn by eating—first the algae that grows on the underside of sound, then the smaller silences that collect in corners.

I am old for my kind, my wings so thoroughly serrated they are more gap than membrane. Soon I will make my final molt, and She will add my ghost-clothes to her collection. But tonight I perch here, feeling tomorrow's storm gathering in the pressure against my antennae, knowing that in six hours the gutters will run full and the Choir will sing its deepest register. I will rub my wings raw keeping time, adding my dry rasp to the water's voice, until I become indistinguishable from the echo I help create.

This is what it means to be native to a place that is both built and grown: you accept that your body is just another kind of architecture, that your song is only borrowed from the larger composition. When the humans leave, their blood still ringing with frequencies they can't name, we remain. We are the Choir's memory of every visitor, our wings wearing thin with the work of remembering.

In the Still Shelf season, when even the moss curls sleeping, we alone maintain the rhythm, our whispers the only movement in the petrified air. We are calling the water back. We are teaching the stones to anticipate their next drink. We are the difference between silence and death, a distinction as fine as the edge of our wings, as necessary as rust.
