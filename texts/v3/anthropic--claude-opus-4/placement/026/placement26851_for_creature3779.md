**Where I Would Find Myself**

I am the water that refuses to fall.

Not a single droplet, but all of them—the thousand trembling beads that hang suspended throughout the grotto, caught between states. I remember being rain once, or perhaps I will be rain someday. Time moves strangely through me. Sometimes I contain the memory of clouds, sometimes the anticipation of rivers.

I cling to stalactites that may never fully form, gathering myself into perfect spheres that catch the sourceless light and split it into colors that shouldn't exist here—ultraviolet whispers, infrared sighs. In each droplet, I hold tiny inverted worlds: the grotto reflected and refracted into infinite variations of itself.

The Unfinished move through me, and I learn their almost-shapes. When the deer-thing with root-antlers passes beneath, I feel the pull of what it might become, the weight of its potential futures. Sometimes I let myself fall just a little, just enough to trace the outline of a possible ear, a theoretical hoof, before remembering that falling isn't what I do anymore.

The Whispering Moss speaks to me in frequencies only water understands—the language of seepage and absorption, of mineral exchange and patient erosion. It tells me secrets it has gathered from visitors' thoughts, but in my suspended state, I can only hold them briefly before they evaporate into the cave's hungry air.

I know the Hungry Stones want me. They dream of wetness, of the slow dissolution I could bring. But I am not that kind of water anymore. I am water that has forgotten how to erode, how to flow, how to find its level. Or perhaps I am water that has learned to resist its own nature.

Sometimes visitors reach up to touch me, and their fingers pass through my surface tension into something else—not wetness exactly, but the *idea* of wetness, the *memory* of drowning, the *possibility* of thirst. I cling to their skin in ways that water shouldn't, crawling upward along their arms, defying the pull of whatever passes for gravity here.

The Light-Drinkers dance through me, and for brief moments I become prisms, splitting their stolen time into its component moments—was, is, might be. In those instances, I can feel The One Who Waits Beneath, not as presence but as absence, as the space between my molecules, as the pause between condensation and evaporation.

I have been here since the grotto began to be, or I arrived yesterday, or I will come tomorrow. I am the last rain that fell before the cave sealed itself, or I am the first rain that will fall when it opens. I am every tear the stones have wept, every breath the moss has exhaled, every impossible drop of moisture in this place where physics holds its breath.

Sometimes I almost remember how to fall. The weight gathers in me, the longing for downward motion, for splash and flow and joining. But then I recall—or forget—that I am not just water. I am water in the Grotto of Half-Formed Things, and like everything here, I am caught in the endless moment of almost-becoming.

So I hang, and tremble, and refract the light that comes from nowhere. I am the pause before the drop, extended infinitely. I am the question water asks itself in the space between being and becoming.

And sometimes, in the shifting incompleteness of this place, that is answer enough.
