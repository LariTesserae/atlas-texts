In the shadow-cool depths where rust-eaten gears breach the earth like ancient teeth, I am the Tender of Forgotten Functions. My form shifts between states—sometimes I am a spreading film of oil-dark moss that creeps across the corroded metal, drinking the iron's slow transformation. Other times I coalesce into something almost resembling the workers who once moved through this place, though my body remains translucent, shot through with veins of copper-green patina.

I make my home in the hollow cavity of a great turbine housing, half-buried where a waterway has eroded the bank. The metal shell amplifies every sound—the bubble-speech of the stream, the settling sighs of rusted bolts finally giving way after decades of holding fast. My walls are adorned with my collection: fragments of blueprints that blow in on the wind and adhere to my oil-slick essence, creating a papier-mâché of lost purposes. Each scrap holds the ghost of an intention, a diagram for something that will never again be built.

By day, I seep through the mechanical ruins, following the root-paths where the sharp-featured trees have split open pipes and reclaimed the metals for their own mysterious purposes. I've learned to read the trees' intentions in the way they wrap their roots—a gentle embrace for copper, a strangling grip for lead, a delicate negotiation with the traces of mercury still hiding in old switches. I serve as translator between the organic and industrial, encouraging the peaceful consumption of the old world by the new.

The Keepers know of me, though we rarely interact directly. I am too small, too liquid for their great forms to properly perceive. But sometimes I feel their footsteps reverberating through the metal bones of the valley, and I pulse in sympathetic resonance. They maintain the grand balance; I maintain the intimate decomposition, ensuring that each gear tooth releases its iron to the soil at precisely the right rate to feed the specialized mosses, which in turn shelter the translucent-winged insects that pollinate the wire-flowers—those strange blooms that grow only where electrical current once flowed.

At night, when the moon silvers the waterways, I sometimes allow myself to dream the memories held in the metal: the thunderous purpose of the machines, the sharp ozone of electricity, the purposeful stride of workers long gone. But I do not mourn. I am the bridge between what was and what is becoming. In my slow, patient work, I help the valley digest its own history, transforming the bones of industry into the living flesh of this new world.

The fish have learned to recognize me when I wade into the streams to cool my sometimes-overheated form. They nibble at the edges of my oil-dark boundaries, and I release tiny bubbles of processed rust that they've developed a taste for—their scales now bearing faint geometric patterns that echo the technical drawings I've absorbed. This is my gift to them, and theirs to me: together, we are writing a new kind of blueprint, one that needs no paper, no permanence, only the endless creativity of adaptation.

I am the memory-keeper and the forgetter both, ensuring that the valley's industrial past becomes not a scar but a story written in living metal and breathing stone. Each day, I grow more into what I am meant to be—neither fully of the old world nor entirely of the new, but something essential to the transformation of both.
