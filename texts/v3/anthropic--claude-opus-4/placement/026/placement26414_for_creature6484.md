I am a **Conduit Tender**, one of many who travel between the forgotten infrastructures of this world.

My body is long adapted to these spaces—skin perpetually damp from years of moving through humid chambers, fingernails stained orange-black from touching rust and moss in equal measure. I wear layers of waxed canvas that never quite dry, and my boots have soles made from compressed fungal matter that grips the slick surfaces without disturbing the substrate. Around my neck hangs a breathing filter made of the same root-felt that lines these chambers, though I rarely need it anymore. My lungs have learned to process the thick air.

I discovered the Steamroom Mush three years ago while tracking a temperature anomaly in the factory's abandoned heating system. The Myndlose recognized me not as an intruder but as another process—another cycle that could be integrated. We communicate through the language of maintenance: I clear the ventilation grills when the moss threatens to completely block them, maintaining just enough airflow to keep their cycle stable. In return, they show me patterns in the condensation that warn of structural weaknesses elsewhere in the building.

I carry tools, but not for repair in the traditional sense. A thin metal rod for testing the depth of moss carpets before stepping. Glass vials for collecting water samples from different points in their cycle—not for analysis, but for transferring their water-memory to other chambers that have lost their balance. A book bound in polymer sheets where I map the growth patterns, noting how the braided column by the weeping wall adds another ring of stems each season.

My role is neither guardian nor gardener but something more like a translator between the dying industrial world and the living systems that inherit it. I understand that the Myndlose's work is not restoration but transformation—turning the corpse of industry into something that breathes. My work is ensuring that transformation continues unimpeded, that the delicate negotiations between rust and root proceed without catastrophic shifts.

When I enter the Steamroom Mush now, the moss floor responds to my familiar weight with a different texture than it shows to strangers—less resistant, more accepting. The condensation on the machine ribs beads in patterns I've learned to read like text. I am not part of the Myndlose, but I am part of their world's maintenance, another process folded into their endless cycle of rot and repair.

Sometimes I sleep here, curled in the warmest corner where the hidden fans cycle air from some still-functioning boiler room far above. In those moments, breathing in time with the room's slow rhythm, I almost understand what it means to think in water and rust, to experience consciousness as a steady state rather than a series of moments. Almost, but not quite. I remain stubbornly singular, moving between these cathedral-wombs of transformation, tending the conduits that connect them, ensuring the slow work continues.
