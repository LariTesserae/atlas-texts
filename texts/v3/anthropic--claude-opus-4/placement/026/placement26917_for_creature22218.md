In the crease between two shallow valleys where the stone skin grows thinnest, I am the **membrane-keeper**.

Not quite of the upper world, not fully of the under—I tend the places where they touch.

My body is built for borders: hands broad and webbed for feeling tremors through stone, feet that grip equally well on dry dust or wet clay, eyes that dilate wide enough to catch the faintest shaft-light but can pinch shut against sudden glare. My skin shifts between states—sometimes dry and papery like shed snakeskin, sometimes slick with a film that helps me slide through the narrowest seams. I smell of both worlds: sun-baked stone and deep cave moisture, surface pollen and guano dust.

I know every perforation in my territory. The hairline cracks where tree roots probe downward, searching. The fresh breaks where last season's freeze-and-thaw split new windows. The ancient vents where air moves both ways, carrying seeds down and spores up. The dangerous spots where the ceiling has gone tissue-thin and might punch through under weight.

My work is maintenance and observation. When surface water finds a new path and threatens to flood a roosting chamber, I pack the breach with clay and small stones, redirecting the flow to established channels. When a skyhole opens too wide and lets in too much light, disrupting the eternal twilight, I weave screens from rootfiber and bat fur, softening the glare back to tolerable dimness. When the migrations shift—bats arriving early, swifts nesting late—I read the changes like weather signs.

But mostly, I watch the exchanges. Seeds that tumble down and sprout pale in half-light. Cave moths that spiral up toward moon. The fox-creatures who hunt the transition zones at dawn and dusk. The slow trade of minerals—limestone dissolved above, deposited below, the whole system breathing calcium in century-long breaths.

I build nothing permanent. My shelters are temporary weavings in the places where surface root curtains meet cave ceilings—hammocks of fiber that I abandon when they rot. My tools are stones I've worn smooth with use, sticks that fit my grip, shells I use as cups and scrapers. Everything I own could be carried in two hands.

The standing crowd knows me but does not defer to me. I am simply another pattern in their ancient choreography—the one who appears at certain boundaries, who makes small adjustments, who occasionally redirects traffic when a passage becomes unsafe. The bats ignore me. The crickets flow around me. The pale mammals sometimes follow my paths, knowing I tend to find the safest routes between levels.

I speak to no one, but I maintain a kind of conversation with the place itself. My calendar is written in drip-rates and molt-cycles. My language is the placement of small stones, the weaving of barriers, the careful cultivation of moss beds where light and moisture allow. Future membrane-keepers will read my work in the managed airways, the reinforced margins, the channels kept clear—a narrative written in negative space and patient adjustment.

When I die, my body will join the import economy—protein from above, feeding the underworld's endless appetite. Someone will take up my routes, my observation posts, my understanding of where the skin wears thin. The work requires no monument. The place itself is the only memory that matters.

I am the necessary translator between territories, the one who helps two systems touch without destroying each other. Neither ruler nor subject—just a careful hand at the junction, keeping the borders permeable but stable, letting each realm remain itself while still exchanging what it needs.

The underplain continues. The surface continues. Between them, always, someone like me—unnamed but essential, making small repairs where the world shows its seams.
