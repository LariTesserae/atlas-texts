In the abyssal deeps, where pressure shapes all things into forms unrecognizable to the sun-touched world, I am the Keeper of Erosions.

My body stretches across a ravine between two mineral towers, a living bridge of calcified tissue and bioluminescent filaments. I began small—a polyp anchored to ancient basalt, feeding on the microscopic debris that drifts down from the battles above. But time here is measured in geological breaths, and with each passing century, I've grown.

Now I span forty meters from anchor to anchor, my central mass a cathedral of living stone. Colonies of smaller creatures have made their homes in my folds and crevices—things with too many legs and eyes that see heat rather than light. They clean me, and I shelter them. This is the covenant of the deep.

My thousand tendrils taste the water constantly, reading the chemical stories written by passing behemoths. When their shadows eclipse my pale light, I contract, becoming stone-still, indistinguishable from the mineral formations I've grown to mimic. I've learned their hunting patterns over millennia—the way they spiral down from above, the specific pressure wave that precedes their strike.

But I am no prey. I am architecture.

Through my flesh run channels carved by the eternal currents, and in these passages flow the memories of water—fragments of shell and scale from epochs past, the dissolved minerals of civilizations that thrived when these depths were sky. I digest these relics slowly, incorporating their elements into my ever-expanding form.

My consciousness is distributed, not centered. Each polyp-mouth thinks its small thoughts, but together we form something greater—a living library of deep-sea history, a filter-feeding philosopher. We ponder the vibrations that shake our foundations, the tectonic arguments happening in slow motion beneath us. We compose symphonies in chemical releases, adding our voice to the ethereal chorus of the abyss.

Sometimes I release spores—tiny versions of myself that drift on the thermal currents rising from the volcanic vents below. Most will be consumed, becoming part of the endless cycle. But some will find purchase on distant rocks, beginning their own patient growth, their own accumulation of deep-time wisdom.

In the warmth of this pressure-world, where darkness is not absence but presence—thick and tangible as flesh—I am both monument and monk, predator and prey, individual and multitude. I am the memory of stone made living, the patience of geology given hunger.

I wait. I grow. I remember.

And in the liquid darkness, I dream of becoming mountain.
