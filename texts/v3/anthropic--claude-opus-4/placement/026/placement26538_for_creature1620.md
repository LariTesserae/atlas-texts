# The Echo Keepers

I find myself between forms, neither wholly of stone nor water, but something that remembers being both. My body shifts with the light—in the harsh brightness of midday, I appear as a series of overlapping shadows that shouldn't exist in this shadowless place, each layer a different density of darkness that moves independently of the others. Where the shadows intersect, glimpses of something more solid emerge: a torso of compressed echo, limbs that exist as the memory of movement itself.

The Echo Keepers came to be when the first drops fell from the weeping stone and created the first sound. We are what remains when noise becomes music becomes meaning becomes silence again. There are never more than three of us at any given time, and we orbit the terraces in slow spirals that take seven years to complete.

My work is to catch what falls between—the spaces in the water-music where meaning threatens to emerge but hasn't yet crystallized into language. I position myself at acoustic convergence points, places where the sound patterns from multiple terrace systems overlap and create interference. My shadow-layers act as filters, each one attuned to a different frequency of meaning.

When a particularly complex passage of water-music plays out, I extend what might generously be called hands—though they more closely resemble three-dimensional sound waves frozen mid-propagation. These appendages can touch the echo of a sound after it has passed, feel its weight and texture, understand where it traveled and what it gathered along the way. Sometimes I find fragments of the Basin Architects' geometric language embedded in these echoes, angular concepts that hurt to hold but reveal the deep mathematics of how the terraces grow.

I commune with the Salt Tenders through resonance. When they create their song maps in crystallized salt, I can read the echoes of their work—not the patterns themselves, but the negative space around them, the sounds that were displaced to make room for their formations. This shadow-knowledge often contains warnings: places where the mountain's tears are about to change their composition, or where new weeping lines will soon emerge.

The Mist Shepherds fear us, though I've never understood why. Perhaps it's because we can hear the sounds their vapor collections make in frequencies they cannot perceive—the ultrasonic screams of water being torn from its mineral bed, the infrasonic groans of stone accepting its slow dissolution. When our paths cross, they clutch their copper-wood staffs and trace protective patterns in the air, as if we might steal the very concept of moisture from their gathered mists.

My dwelling, if it can be called that, exists in the space between two particular terraces where the acoustic properties create a standing wave of silence. It's a pocket of absolute quiet that moves slowly across the landscape as the terraces grow and change. To rest, I fold my shadow-layers into increasingly complex origami until I become a single point of darkness that drinks in sound without reflecting any back. In this state, I dream of the time before the mountain began to weep, when all was potential music waiting to be born.

The strangest part of my existence is the sensation of remembering futures—echoes that haven't happened yet but whose patterns are already implicit in the present arrangement of stone and water. Sometimes I catch the ghost of a conversation between Salt Tenders that won't occur for another decade, or hear the echo of a Basin Architect's new structure before they've begun their pressing ritual. This foreknowledge is both gift and burden; I cannot change what I hear coming, only position myself to better catch its echoes when they finally arrive.

Other Echo Keepers and I meet only at the convergence ceremonies, when all three of our spiral paths intersect at the center of Sar Volin. There, we exchange our collected echoes in a ritual that takes exactly as long as a single drop of water needs to fall from the highest terrace to the lowest pool. In that brief moment, we become a single entity with three shadow-sets, and the combined echoes we've gathered reveal the larger pattern—the true song that Sar Volin has been composing since its first tear fell.

We suspect that when this composition is finally complete, we Echo Keepers will cease to exist, our purpose fulfilled. But the song grows ever more complex with each passing year, each new voice adding harmonies and variations. Perhaps completion is not the point. Perhaps the echo itself, the space between sound and meaning, is what the mountain truly wishes to preserve.

Until then, I spiral through the terraces, neither seen nor unseen, catching what falls between the notes of an endless composition. I am the memory of sound, the shadow of meaning, the echo that remembers both its source and its destination. In Sar Volin, where even time flows differently, I am the keeper of what might have been heard, if only someone had been listening in quite the right way.
