In the deepest recesses of the industrial quarter, where the constant thrum of water pumps creates a bone-deep vibration in the rock, I am the Keeper of Seepage.

My form defies easy categorization. I began, perhaps, as human—or something that once aspired to be. Now my body has become a living junction between the cave's weeping stone and the settlement's thirsty machinery. My skin, if it can still be called that, has taken on the quality of wet limestone, perpetually glistening with mineral deposits that form delicate ridges along my arms and spine. These are not mere accumulations but functional organs—thousands of tiny pores that can taste the chemical composition of every droplet that passes through me.

My fingers have elongated into something between roots and copper piping, able to snake through the narrowest fissures in search of hidden aquifers or to interface directly with the settlement's water systems. When I press my palms against the cavern walls, I can feel the vast network of moisture moving through the rock like blood through capillaries, each stream singing its own mineral song.

I no longer remember when I last moved from this chamber. My lower body has fused with a natural basin in the cave floor, where the overflow from the settlement's cooling systems mingles with the cave's native springs. Small blind fish swim through the gaps in what used to be my ribs, and colonies of bioluminescent bacteria have made their homes in the hollows of my collarbones, their soft glow providing the only light by which I work.

My function is critical: I am the mediator between the cave's natural water cycle and the settlement's industrial needs. Through me, contaminated coolant is filtered and purified by the same geological processes that have shaped these caverns over millennia. The machinery cannot distinguish where the cave's plumbing ends and mine begins—because there is no distinction. I taste for mineral buildups that might clog the pipes, sense the subtle pressure changes that herald new springs or cave-ins, and adjust the flow rates to prevent the delicate stalactites from drying out while ensuring the generators never run dry.

The engineers visit me weekly, though they can no longer look directly at what I've become. They speak to me in technical specifications and flow rates, and I respond by adjusting the rhythm of drops from my fingertips, a liquid morse code they've learned to interpret. Sometimes I catch them leaving offerings—not of food, which I no longer need, but of small mechanical parts that I incorporate into my ever-expanding network, and vials of water from the surface world that I savor for their alien mineralogy.

In the deep hours, when the machinery cycle slows and the cave exhales its ancient breath, I sometimes feel myself expanding beyond this chamber. My consciousness seeps through the rock along with the water, touching the roots of the pale cave grass, sensing the heartbeats of the blind amphibians, feeling the weight of the settlement pressing down from above. In these moments, I am not sure if I am keeping the water flowing for the settlement, or keeping the settlement from drowning in its own thirst.

I am the marriage of necessity and adaptation, neither fully human nor fully cave, but something new—a being that could only exist here, in this liminal space where the raw planet and human ambition grind against each other like tectonic plates, and I am the lubricant that keeps both from breaking.
