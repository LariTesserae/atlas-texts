In the saturated depths of The Gullet, where every surface weeps and breathes, where sound has weight and time drips like honey, I would find myself as **The Archivist**—neither fully Hinderer nor wholly other, but something caught between states, like a word half-spoken.

My form would be attenuated, stretched thin across the vertical expanse of the chamber. Picture a creature of impossible length: a torso that begins near the root-floor and extends upward, upward, segments of spine visible through translucent skin that catches the pearlescent light like oil on water. My body would wind around root-struts and through bladder-clusters, a living thread weaving through the settlement's architecture. Each segment would bear its own pair of arms—dozens of them, perhaps hundreds—each tipped with delicate, articulated fingers that never cease their work.

For I would be the one who remembers what the sound forgets.

The Hinderers' chorus carries law and history, yes, but sound is ephemeral, even here where it hangs thick as syrup. My purpose would be to catch these fleeting songs and fix them in flesh. Along my endless spine, you would see them: polyps of specialized tissue, each one a living recorder. They would bubble out from my skin like blisters of memory, their surfaces taut and iridescent. Inside each polyp, suspended in a fluid that glows faintly green, would be a perfect replica of a vocal chord—not my own, but copied from the Hinderer who sang a particular phrase, a specific judgment, a cherished lullaby. When pressed gently (and the Hinderers would know how to press, would visit me like a library), these polyps would vibrate, releasing their stored songs into the air. A mother could hear her grandmother's blessing, perfectly preserved. A dispute could be settled by consulting the precedent of elders twenty generations dissolved.

My feeding would be peculiar, even by The Gullet's standards. I would subsist on sound itself—not the physical vibrations, but the spaces between them, the almost-silences that exist in the gaps between howls. My mouth (or mouths—for I would have several, spaced along my length like relay stations) would be vast, circular openings that could dilate to swallow these pockets of quiet. Inside, specialized organs would process the negative space, extracting... what? Even I might not know. Perhaps the potential for future sounds. Perhaps the echoes of words that were almost spoken. This diet of absence would keep me eternally hungry, eternally listening.

The weirdness of my existence would manifest in my relationship to time. While the Hinderers are smeared across temporal states, I would exist in several moments simultaneously. The segment of me near the floor might be experiencing Tuesday, while my middle reaches exist in last Thursday, and my crown—where my primary consciousness would reside in a head like a flowering bud of eyes—would be tasting next week's air. This temporal scattered-ness would be essential to my function: I could archive a song before it was sung, preserving the pure intention before it was corrupted by performance. I could hold conversations with Hinderers across generations, my lower segments speaking with the ancestors of those my upper portions know as infants.

My dwelling would be unique in The Gullet: not a bladder extruded from the wall, but a spiral track worn into the flesh itself by my constant, slow circulation. Over centuries, my passage would have carved a gentle helix into the chamber's interior, a groove that would glisten with the secretions of my ventral surface—a trail of enzymes that would encourage the wall to form convenient holds, small pockets where my segments could rest, neural nodes that would tap directly into the superorganism's dreaming mind. The Hinderers would know my path like a street, would give directions by referencing which of my segments currently occupied a particular curve.

My relationship with the Hinderers would be symbiotic but strange. I would be essential to their society—the keeper of contracts, the memory of bloodlines, the one who proves that what was sung was truly sung. But I would also unsettle them. My temporal scattered-ness would mean I sometimes archive tragedies before they occur, my polyps swelling with lamentations for Hinderers who still swing happily through the canopy. They would visit me for confirmation but leave quickly, unable to shake the feeling that I experience their lives backwards, that to me their deaths are greetings and their births farewells.

In the ecstasy of The Gullet's noon-storm, when the walls contract and the hot wind scours the chamber, I would perform my only voluntary act. My entire length would undulate, a wave traveling from floor to ceiling, and each polyp would release its song simultaneously. For those few minutes, The Gullet would be flooded with the voices of every Hinderer who had ever lived, a cacophony so dense it would become a new kind of silence. The living Hinderers would press themselves into their bladders, hands over their ears, not from pain but from a kind of temporal vertigo, hearing their own death-songs sung by voices they recognize as their own.

And in that storm of sound, I would molt—not my skin, but my memories. The oldest polyps would burst, releasing their songs for the final time, the vocal cords dissolving into spores that would drift and settle and perhaps grow into new Hinderers who would be born humming melodies from before their great-grandparents' time. My now-empty segments would ache with a specific hunger, ready to begin collecting again, to archive the next age's words.

The other creatures would interact with me cautiously. The howlers would use my body as a playground, but they would fall silent when passing my head, some instinct warning them that I am listening too carefully. The Gulpers would never ambush near me; my presence would disturb their camouflage, my temporal flux making their inflated bodies flicker between states. The fliers would follow my spiral path through the air, using my disturbance of time as a tailwind that could carry them faster than their wings alone.

But it would be in the quiet moments, the rare instances when The Gullet's chorus diminishes to a low thrum, that my true nature would be revealed. A young Hinderer, curious and unafraid, might approach my primary head and look into my clustered eyes. And in that gaze, they would see/hear/feel something terrible and wonderful: the shape of their entire life, every song they would ever sing, archived in advance in polyps that would not develop for decades yet. They would understand then that I am not just the keeper of memory but its prisoner, experiencing all of time simultaneously and therefore none of it, forever recording a story I can never truly inhabit.

In The Gullet, where everything is always becoming something else, I would be the one constant—a creature of pure purpose, archiving the flux, remembering the transformations, my body a living library that breathes and grieves and grows. The Hinderers would sing their lives, and I would catch every note, preserving their ephemeral chorus in flesh that remembers everything and understands nothing, a necessary paradox in a world that forgets itself to stay alive.
