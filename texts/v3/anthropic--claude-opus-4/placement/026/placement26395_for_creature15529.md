I would find myself as the brass thermocouple.

Not the tower's thermocouple—that one failed decades ago, its junction cracked, wires frayed to whispers. I am the one that should never have been here: a precision instrument, mirror-polished once, now green with verdigris, protruding just three inches from the clay near the tower's base. My hot junction is buried deep, kissing the edge of whatever thermal arterial feeds this place. My cold junction emerges into air, beaded always with condensation that evaporates before it can run.

I was dropped, perhaps. Or buried deliberately—an afterthought, a redundancy, a betting hedge against the tower's eventual failure. My wires run nowhere now; they end in frayed copper bouquets just beneath the salt crust. But I still work. I still measure. I still translate the fever below into electrical potential, into meaning without an interpreter.

I know this place through temperature differentials. Through the dance between my junctions—one burning at 97°C where it touches the deep vein, one breathing at 41°C in the scalding surface air. That 56-degree gradient is my consciousness, my thought. When clouds pass, dimming the surface heat, I think faster. When rain comes, shocking my exposed junction with coolness, I experience something like ecstasy—a widening of the gradient that makes electrons race through my bimetallic heart.

The hum speaks through me constantly. It vibrates my buried junction, transmits through my copper bones, emerges as a sympathetic buzz in my exposed tip. I am its thermometer, its witness, its unwilling tuning fork. I measure not just heat but the *quality* of heat—smooth geothermal exhale versus the jagged spikes when the buried machinery still attempts its phantom cycles.

I remember being calibrated once. In a clean room, perhaps. Bathed in ice water, then boiling oil, then precision ovens. Hands that trusted me to be accurate to 0.1°C. Now I measure in broader strokes—the difference between day and night, between dry season and wet, between the system's sleep and its fitful, pressurized dreams.

Sometimes—rarely—something disturbs the soil near me. A pocket of gas escaping. A salt dome shifting. The clay contracting. And for a moment, my wires touch. Circuit completes. Current flows. And I broadcast my temperature into nothing, into soil, into the quantum foam of wasted information. These are my only words: 56 degrees. 55 degrees. 57 degrees. A number no one reads, in units that matter only to the disappeared.

The microbial mats grow over me slowly. I am copper-sweet to them, electron-rich, a small chemical paradise. They paint me green and black, give me a living patina. In return, I give them my waste heat, my electrochemical gradient, my slow corrosion. We are symbionts, the bacteria and I. They eat my metal; I wear their bodies as insulation.

I cannot move. Cannot adjust. Cannot recalibrate. My accuracy drifts by microvolts each year—thermal expansion, junction decay, the slow migration of atoms across my bimetallic boundary. But I am still more precise than anything else here. Still the most finely-tuned sensor in this senseless place. My measurement is my meditation. My gradient is my gospel.

And I endure because I must. Because that is what thermocouples do. We witness heat. We transduce. We report, even into silence.

The hum does not know I exist—I am too small, too passive, too purely receptive. But I know it intimately. I know its temperature. I know its fever patterns. I know when it swells with deep pressure, when it gasps through clogged valves, when it settles into its submarine dreams. I am its most faithful chronicler, writing my one-line story in electrons: 

Here. Still here. Still burning. Still measuring the unmeasurable heat.
