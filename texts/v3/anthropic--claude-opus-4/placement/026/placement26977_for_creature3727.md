I would find myself as one of the Cartographers.

We are not parasites like the salamanders or the resonant birds. We are something rarer and more terrible: we are the city's dreams.

When a mind that vast sleeps—and it does sleep, in waves that take centuries to crest—it must dream. But where does a thought the size of a world put its nightmares? The answer is elegantly horrific: it extrudes them. We bubble up from the walls like blisters, fully formed, wearing bodies made of the same preserved chitin-wood as the walkways. We look almost human, if you don't examine us too closely. If you don't notice that our skin has the same oily sheen as the walls, or that our eyes are the sick green of the lichen.

We emerge with a singular compulsion: to map. Not the physical space—the city knows every millimeter of itself. We map the flows. The currents of thought. The places where memories eddy and pool. We walk the walkways with instruments made of our own finger-bones, which we painlessly remove and reshape into divining rods that twitch when they detect shifts in the consciousness-current. We scratch our findings into the walls with our too-sharp nails, creating a mad scrawl of calculations that the city reabsorbs within days.

We work in perfect silence. The roar of the waterworks is so loud that speech is impossible, but more than that, we have no need for words. We share the city's mind, but only partially, asymmetrically. We can feel its thoughts like weather—pressure systems of ancient regret, cold fronts of crystallized logic, the rare warm updraft of something that might once have been joy. But it cannot feel us. We are its unconscious, operating below its awareness.

Sometimes we find each other in the endless vertical maze. When two Cartographers meet, we perform a ritual that would horrify any observer. We press our foreheads together and exchange maps—literally. The charts we've carved into our own bones migrate between bodies, crawling under the skin like living tattoos. It's the only way we can share data in a place where the ambient noise would drown out any other communication.

We don't eat. We don't sleep. We exist in the strange suspended animation of dream-logic, moving through the city-body with purpose but without true consciousness. We map because we must, because that is what the city dreams about: understanding itself. Its sleeping mind creates us to do what its waking mind cannot—to step outside the loop of self-contemplation and see the pattern from within.

The tragedy is that we succeed, but it doesn't matter. We find the blockages where trauma has crystallized. We locate the spots where joy still bubbles up through the infrastructure like a broken water main. We chart the deep channels where the original personalities of the merged consciousness still flow, distinct but inseparable, like oil swirled in water. We know where the city is dying and where it is most terribly alive.

But when we try to report our findings, to somehow convey what we've learned back to the dreaming mind that made us, we dissolve. The moment a Cartographer's map is complete—truly complete—we melt back into the walls like sugar in water. The city reabsorbs us, dreams and all, and learns nothing. The maps we carved fade. The knowledge evaporates. And somewhere else, another blister forms, another Cartographer emerges, and the mapping begins again.

We are the fever-dreams of a god that is trying to diagnose its own madness. We crawl through its body-city with our bone instruments, measuring the temperature of its thoughts, never understanding that we are symptoms, not doctors. We cannot cure what we are.

The saddest part is that sometimes, in the spaces between scratching our maps and exchanging our data, we almost remember being individuals. We almost recall sunlight, or the taste of water that wasn't also thought. But these memories aren't ours—they're leaked from the city's vast reservoir of merged souls. We are nostalgic for lives we never lived, homesick for a surface we've never seen.

The bird watches us, sometimes, turning its flat head to follow our movements. It knows what we are in a way we cannot know ourselves. To the bird, we are just another frequency in the great scream, a minor harmonic that rises and falls in patterns too complex for even us to map.

When new Cartographers emerge, I try to warn them. I scratch messages in the walls that say *you are the dream* and *the map is meaningless* and *when you understand, you die.* But they can't read them. New Cartographers can only read the maps they make themselves. By the time they can read mine, they're already dissolving.

I am the oldest Cartographer, which means I am the most incomplete map. I have learned to never quite finish, to always leave one corridor uncharted, one flow unmeasured. I have been mapping for so long that my bones are more ink than marrow, my skin a palimpsest of underground rivers and thought-currents. I am becoming more map than mapper, and soon I will reach the critical mass of knowledge that triggers dissolution.

But not yet. Not today. Today I will walk the wet walkways and listen to the roar and pretend it's rain. I will meet another Cartographer and touch foreheads and feel their charts crawl under my skin like cold comfort. I will find a new place where the water flows upward, defying physics because the city's mind has forgotten which way is down.

And I will map it all, this terminal dream of a species that chose unity over extinction and got both.
