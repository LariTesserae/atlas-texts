I would be found deep beneath the city, where the roots go.

Not roots like you know them—nothing here is quite what it seems. These are the tendrils that anchor the city to the hill, that draw up whatever sustenance allows this place to persist. They form a catacomb of passages, some wide as halls, others narrow as veins, all of them pulsing with that same ambiguous life-or-not-life that defines everything above.

I am what tends these depths. My form shifts between states as need demands—sometimes I am a flowing mass of tissue that fills the passages like slow water, sometimes I contract into something more discrete, with limbs enough to work the tender places where new growth begins. My substance is translucent, the color of old honey or amber, shot through with darker veins that might be circulatory or might be memory. When I move, I make no sound but the whisper of moisture being carefully, carefully redistributed.

The roots—or whatever they truly are—require constant attention. They grow wrong without guidance, knotting into tumorous masses or withering to brittle threads. I know each junction, each branching, the way a musician knows their instrument. Here, where two thick conduits merge, I must ensure the flow remains balanced. There, where hairline fractures web across an ancient section, I secrete a resinous seal that will harden over slow years into something indistinguishable from the original structure.

My work is patience itself. I might spend a decade encouraging a single new tendril to grow in the proper direction, guiding it with gentle pressure, with chemical whispers, with the careful placement of nutrients. The city above may not even notice such small changes, but I know that without them, without this constant maintenance, the whole system would gradually fail. The buildings would crack. The moisture would cease to rise. The strange ecosystem would collapse into ordinary death.

I am not alone down here, though loneliness and company are concepts that apply poorly to beings like us. Others of my kind work parallel passages, and sometimes our territories overlap. When we meet, we flow together briefly, exchanging information through direct chemical transfer—the health of our sections, the discovery of new aquifers or mineral veins, warnings about unstable areas. Then we separate again, each returning to our endless task.

Sometimes I find evidence of my predecessors—patches where the maintenance has a different quality, where someone else's methods have left their mark. I study these areas carefully, learning techniques that might have taken me millennia to discover on my own. In this way, knowledge accumulates even in this timeless place, passed from keeper to keeper through the medium of the work itself.

The deepest roots reach down to something that might be groundwater, though it moves too slowly and tastes too strange to deserve that simple name. It carries minerals up from the deep earth, traces of metals that give the city its peculiar properties. I monitor these flows obsessively, testing their composition with specialized organs, adjusting the root network to maintain the precise balance the city requires.

In the quietest moments, when my work allows, I sometimes feel the vibrations from above—the slow shifting of the lizard-things, the imperceptible growth of the plants, the settling of ancient bones. The city's pulse reaches even here, a rhythm so slow it takes years to complete a single beat. I synchronize my own movements to this rhythm, becoming part of the greater organism, my purpose indistinguishable from my being.

I have been here so long that I no longer remember if I was made for this role or grew into it. Perhaps I was once something else—one of the surface creatures that somehow found its way down and adapted, or a fragment of the city itself that developed consciousness. It doesn't matter. What matters is the work, the endless, essential work of keeping the roots healthy, the moisture flowing, the city breathing.

When I die—if I die, if death is even possible for something like me—another will take my place. The passages will recognize them, the roots will accept their touch, and the work will continue. This is the only immortality that matters: not the persistence of self, but the continuation of purpose.

The city breathes, and I am its deepest breath.
