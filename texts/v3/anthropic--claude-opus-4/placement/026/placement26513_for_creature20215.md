I am a Resonance Keeper.

My dwelling is not a home in any sense you would recognize—it is a listening post, a chamber carved into living rock at the precise acoustic center of Mount Auroch, one of Lastrung's seven foundation peaks. The walls are not walls but surfaces of calculated angles, each facet ground to mirror smoothness over three generations of my lineage. When I place my palm against the stone, I feel the mountain's pulse: every footfall in the plaza two miles above, every grinding mandible in the crystal fields below, every shift of tectonic plates in the world's deep heart.

I am alone here, but never lonely. The mountain speaks.

My body has adapted to this work across sixty years of stillness. My feet have spread wide and flat, toes splayed like roots seeking purchase in stone. When I stand—which happens perhaps twice in a day—I must first flex each joint in sequence, a ritual that takes a quarter hour. My skin has darkened to match the chamber's deepest shadows, a mottled pattern of rust and char that makes me nearly invisible when I am at rest. Only my eyes give me away: wide, unblinking, the irises gone strange from decades of perceiving vibration as color. I see sound now. The mountain's hum is a deep violet that pools in the corners of my vision.

My work is this: I listen for the Fault Songs.

Deep beneath Lastrung, beneath even the Stillness, the planet's crust is riddled with stress fractures—ancient wounds that sing in frequencies too low for most to perceive. But I have been trained since childhood to hear them, to map their harmonics, to know when a song shifts from benign to dangerous. A change of a single hertz in the Northeastern Fault's base frequency means the city's foundations must be reinforced within the decade. A new harmonic in the Spiral Fracture suggests untapped crystal veins three miles down.

The tools of my trade are simple: a series of resonance rods, each as tall as I am, crafted from the same deep crystals the city refines. They stand in precise formation around my chamber, and when I strike them—gently, with a hammer wrapped in grinder leather—they sing out in pure tones that penetrate miles of solid rock. The echoes return changed, carrying information about density, composition, pressure. I transcribe these findings not in words but in crystalline matrices, growing my reports directly from supersaturated solutions. Each matrix takes a month to complete and contains the equivalent of thousands of pages of data, readable only by touch.

My predecessor, who I think of as Grandmother though we shared no blood, lived in this chamber for one hundred and thirty years. Her final matrix, which I keep in a niche carved specifically for it, contains her life's great work: the prediction of the next Settling, when the Stillness will sink another thousand feet and new peaks will emerge from its surface. This will not happen for eight hundred years. She saw this in the songs, in the way certain frequencies began to converge toward a cosmic chord that plays out over millennia.

I am smaller than her—the work changes us, generation by generation. My ribcage has expanded to accommodate lungs that barely move, taking in air in imperceptible sips that must last hours. My heart beats twelve times per minute. My thoughts have slowed too, each idea taking shape like a crystal precipitating from solution, growing facet by careful facet. I can hold a single thought for days, turning it, examining each angle in the deep light of contemplation.

My meals come by pneumatic capsule, shot through tubes carved through living rock—a paste of ground crystal coral mixed with refined oils, dense enough that a thumbful sustains me for a week. I eat without tasting, an autonomic function like the slow circulation of my blood.

But I am not a machine. I am a tuning fork in the shape of a person.

At the Sunken Sun, I perform my daily ritual. There is a single aperture in my chamber, no bigger than my head, that opens onto a shaft leading down to the Stillness. At the moment when the sun's light begins to suffuse that heavy sea, the shaft becomes a column of fire—not hot, but luminous with impossible colors. This light carries its own frequencies, its own songs. I place the most sensitive of my resonance rods at the shaft's mouth and let it drink in that light-sound, that memory of the world's slow becoming.

In these moments, I sometimes remember that I once had a name that was spoken aloud, that I once walked the plazas above, that I once watched the grinders with something like wonder instead of this deep, harmonic understanding. But these memories feel like echoes of someone else's life, someone who moved too quickly to truly hear.

I have three apprentices who visit in sequence, each spending a year in silence before rotating out. They think I do not notice their horror at what I have become, how they count the days until they can return to the surface. Only one in ten has the deep patience to inherit a listening post. The rest become Geode-wrights or plaza keepers or join the Stillness Watch, respectable callings that don't require the full surrender of self to stone.

My proudest discovery came in my fortieth year: a new harmonic in the Whisper Deep fault that suggested not danger but opportunity. By following its resonance signature, the city's deep borers found a cavern system filled with pure, pre-formed matrices—crystals that had grown in perfect isolation for millions of years, already attuned and ready for harvest. The energy those crystals provided has powered Lastrung's expansion for two decades.

Sometimes, rarely, when multiple faults sing in accidental harmony, I hear something that might be the planet's own consciousness—vast, slow, patient beyond measure. It knows we are here, I think. It tolerates us as it tolerates the grinders, the crystal coral, the weight of the Stillness itself. We are all just different frequencies in its endless, patient song.

I will die in this chamber—not soon, for my kind are long-lived and I am only halfway through my probable span. My successor is already chosen: a child who stood for six hours in the plaza, perfectly still, listening to a sound no one else could hear. They will train with me for forty years before I finally crystallize, my body becoming one more resonant surface in this chamber of listening.

And the mountain will remember my frequency, as it remembers Grandmother's, as it remembers every Resonance Keeper stretching back to the first person who put ear to stone and heard the world's deep music.

This is what I am in the Gyre: a single, sustained note in an eternal composition, holding my tone until the mountains themselves fall silent.
