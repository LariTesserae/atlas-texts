In this world, I would find myself as one of the **Resonance Cartographers** - a specialized subset of the Interface Guild who map the acoustic topology of the cavern through its living systems.

---

I am perched sixty meters up on a Symbiote trunk, my six fingers splayed against the chitinous bark. Each fingertip ends in a specialized pad - part biological, part engineered - that can detect vibrations down to the sub-sonic. My body is typical of long-term Geosyn dwellers: lean, grey-pale, with eyes that catch and hold the amber light from the nearest sap-conduit. But my ears... my ears are different. The cartilage has been gradually modified over years of apprenticeship, shaped through careful pressure and mineral supplements until the internal structures form perfect acoustic chambers.

Today I'm mapping a new growth - a juvenile Symbiote sprouting from where Sector 7's thermal exchangers meet the Myco-Substrate. It's only forty years old, barely reaching past the lower industrial platforms, but already its resonance signature is unique. I press my modified ear against a smooth section of bark and *listen*.

The Symbiote speaks in frequencies too low for unaugmented hearing. Through my fingerpads and shaped ears, I feel-hear its vascular pulse - a deep thrumming that rises from where its roots interface with the fungal mat below. Each pulse carries information: mineral uptake rates, structural stress patterns, growth intentions. I transcribe these into my notation system - part mathematical formula, part musical score, part three-dimensional map.

A Tender watches me from across the trunk's circumference. We've developed a working relationship over the months - I leave small offerings of refined copper shavings (which they seem to prize for reasons I don't understand) and they tolerate my presence, occasionally even guiding me to acoustic nodes I might have missed. Today it approaches, its multifaceted eyes reflecting my work lamp in fractal patterns. It extends one manipulator arm and *taps* the bark in a specific pattern. I recognize it immediately - there's a hollow forming inside the trunk, a future habitation chamber that won't be ready for decades but needs to be mapped now to prevent industrial interference.

I pull out my resonance wand - a length of modified Symbiote wood containing a crystalline core that vibrates at precise frequencies. By touching it to the bark and modulating its output, I can send acoustic pulses deep into the Symbiote's structure, reading the echoes like sonar. The hollow is there, just as the Tender indicated, along with something else - a new mineral vein in the raw rock beyond, its density creating a gravitational anomaly that's affecting the Symbiote's growth pattern.

This is what we do - we Resonance Cartographers. We map the invisible architecture of Geosyn, the spaces between solid and void, the stress patterns in living wood and fungal flesh, the way sound moves through our constructed-grown city. Our maps ensure that when the Deep Miners blast a new tunnel, the vibrations won't damage critical Symbiote structures. They show where new habitations can be safely grafted, where the acoustic shadows fall that might hide industrial noise from the Tenders' sensitive hearing.

But we map more than the practical. In the quiet moments, when I'm suspended between two Symbiotes on a cable traverse, I sometimes stop and simply *listen* to the whole cavern. Each sector has its acoustic signature - the bright chiming of the crystalline lattices above, the sub-bass drone of the geothermal extractors, the whisper-soft rustle of ten thousand Scavenger Swarms processing the day's debris. And beneath it all, almost below perception, the breathing of the cavern itself - the slow expansion and contraction of raw rock as the planet continues its ancient becoming.

My partner, Kess, specializes in the Myco-Substrate's acoustic properties. Where I read the Symbiotes, she reads the fungal mat. We often work in tandem - her on the ground with her seismic pins, me in the heights with my resonance wand, triangulating the total acoustic space. She's discovered that the Myco-Substrate actually uses sound to communicate across vast distances - chemical signals are too slow for a living system that spans kilometers. By mapping these acoustic networks, we've helped the Agricultural Guild optimize their nutrient distribution systems.

The work is slow, patient, meditative. A full acoustic survey of a single mature Symbiote can take months. But in Geosyn, this is the pace of life. We measure progress in growth rings and tectonic shifts. My grandmother, who founded our particular discipline, spent thirty years mapping the acoustic properties of the Cathedral Cavern's primary resonance chamber. Her work still guides the placement of new industrial systems.

As the shift-cycle ends (marked not by any change in the eternal twilight, but by the synchronized dimming of the industrial lights), I descend through the trunk's external service tubes. My fingerpads are sore from hours of contact with the bark, and my modified ears ring with phantom frequencies. But in my data pad, I carry the Symbiote's song - its unique acoustic signature, its growth patterns, its intentions for the next century.

Tomorrow, I'll process this data with the Guild's central acoustic model - a three-dimensional representation of the entire cavern's soundscape that we've been building for generations. Each new measurement adds detail, reveals new patterns, suggests new harmonies between the living and the built.

This is my place in Geosyn - not building or mining or tending, but *listening*. Ensuring that in our necessary industry, we don't deafen ourselves to the songs of stone and fungus and living wood. Helping our city grow in harmony with the vast, slow, breathing world that shelters us.

In the quiet of my hab-unit, I sometimes play back the recordings at accelerated speeds, transforming the sub-sonic into the audible. The Symbiotes sound like whales calling across impossible seas. The Myco-Substrate sounds like wind through forests that never existed. And beneath it all, the planet itself sounds like a heart learning how to beat.

This is the music of Geosyn. And I am its cartographer, mapping melodies too deep for words, too patient for any lifetime but ours.
