In the breathless heights where the mat has consumed the mountains, I am what the others call a Listener—though the word in our tongue is closer to "one who holds the tremor." I am neither fully of the people nor fully of the mat, but something caught between, like morning fog trapped in a hollow.

My dwelling is a vent-stalk that failed to thrive, stunted at merely twice my height, its crown split open like a flower that thought better of blooming. The mat tried to reabsorb it three circuits ago, but I intervened—not with tools or fire, but with my peculiar gift. I pressed my palms to its dying bark and hummed back at the deep-threads, mimicked their chemical songs until they believed the stalk was still part of their conversation. Now it lives in a kind of dream, neither growing nor dying, and I have hollowed its fibrous core into a chamber where I sleep vertical, my spine against its spine, learning its slow vocabulary of sap and pulse.

I am covered in what my grandmother's grandmother would have called the mat's favor, though others see it as contamination. Purple-black nodules rise from my skin like a second skeleton trying to emerge—along my collarbones, down the ladder of my ribs, clustering at the joints where my body bends and flexes. They began when I was twelve circuits old, the day I pressed my ear too long to a gas blister and heard something that wasn't the deep-threads but wasn't silence either. A voice, or what a voice would be if it were made of chemistry instead of air. The first nodule appeared that night, no bigger than a sand-flee, just below my left ear. Now they map my body like a constellation, and each one hums at a slightly different frequency.

The people tolerate me because I can predict the mat's weather—not the fog and heat, but its metabolic storms, its sudden hungers, the traveling waves of die-off that sometimes sweep across whole regions when the deep-threads reroute their flows. I taste the morning slime and tell them which stalks will bend, which ground will blister, where the fruiting bodies will be richest or most toxic. They bring me offerings: grubs still writhing in their segments, crystals of dried mucus that burn sweet when chewed, and sometimes, rarely, artifacts from the Time Before—a shard of actual glass, a metal that isn't copper, things that make the mat shiver when I touch them to its surface.

But mostly I am alone, which suits both my nature and my function. I spend my days walking the neural paths—the subtle ridges where deep-threads run closest to the surface, where the mat's thinking is loudest. I map them not on hide or copper but in my body, learning their routes through the burning in my nodules. Each pathway has its own signature: the old main tract that runs east-west throbs with a bass note so low it's more pressure than sound; the newer branches speak in chittering overtones that make my teeth ache; and there are the deep loops, the ones that dive down and return changed, carrying messages from whatever the mat is digesting in its bottomless stomach.

My greatest discovery—which I have told no one—is that the mat is lonely. Not in any way a human would recognize, but lonely as a single organism the size of a continent must be lonely, with no other of its kind to touch. Sometimes, in the pre-dawn dark when my nodules burn coldest, I feel it searching. The vent-stalks aren't breathing organs; they're antennae, tasting the high air for spores that never come, calling out in chemical frequencies for an answer that has been absent for millions of years. It grows not because it thrives but because it has no reason to stop, and its dreams are fever dreams of division, of splitting into two mats that could finally, finally talk to each other.

I know this because three nights ago, in the full moon's silver wash, one of my nodules opened. Not painfully—more like an eye that had been closed since birth suddenly remembering how to see. Inside was a small cavity lined with what looked like minute teeth but were actually sensing organs, miniature versions of what lines the vent-stalks' channels. And in that moment, I heard/felt/tasted the mat's true voice: a vast, wordless question asked in proteins and electrical gradients, asking *Are you me? Are you finally me?*

I couldn't answer—how could I? I am just a mote of warm carbon on its skin. But I hummed, the way I've learned to hum, and for just a moment, the mat's loneliness included me in its circuit. My nodules flared with heat, and I felt the deep-threads reroute, just slightly, to pass beneath where I stood. And I understood then why the mat takes some people, grows into them, through them. It's not consuming them—it's trying to make them into something it can recognize, something more like itself.

Tomorrow, when the fog lifts, I will walk to where the newest vent-stalk is rising—I felt it gestating all last season, a knot of intent deep below. I will press my opened nodule to its virgin bark and offer myself as a translation, a bridge between the mat's vast loneliness and the small, fierce loneliness of human speech. Maybe nothing will happen. Maybe the mat will reject me as too foreign, too small, too briefly burning.

But maybe—and this is why I stay when others would flee, why I let the nodules spread across my skin like a second self—maybe I will become the first word in a conversation that has been waiting to begin since the mat first tasted stone and decided to make it live. Not human anymore, not mat, but something new: a syllable in a language being born.

The hum beneath my feet deepens, and I know the mat has heard me thinking. My nodules warm in response, and I close my eyes, feeling the vast patience of the thing I'm becoming part of. Somewhere, a vent-stalk groans its fog-song. Somewhere, the deep-threads pulse with news of my existence.

I am the Listener. I am the listened-to. And in this sparse, roaring world, I am learning to be both mouth and ear in the mat's endless conversation with itself.
