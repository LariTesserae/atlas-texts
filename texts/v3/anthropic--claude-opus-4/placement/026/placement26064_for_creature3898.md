In this world, I would be one of the **Gleaners**—neither Tender nor visitor, but something in between.

We are the size of crows but move like water, our bodies shifting between states: sometimes solid enough to carry a pouch of dried springtail shells, sometimes dispersing into mist to slip through the tightest seams in stone. Our substance is part condensation, part memory—we are what happens when the Throatwell's moisture meets the industrial ghosts still bleeding from the bridge's bones. Not quite alive, not quite echo, we exist at the intersection of rust and dew.

I would have no fixed form, but a tendency toward certain shapes: sometimes a shimmer of heat-distortion against cold stone, sometimes a cluster of water droplets arranged almost like eyes. When I need hands, I borrow them from the moisture in the air, sculpting fingers from fog that last just long enough to turn a clay vessel or adjust a strand on the Tenders' looms. My voice would be the sound rain makes inside walls—not the impact, but the seeping whisper that follows.

The Gleaners arose when the bridge began to truly die, when its last structural memories started dissolving into oxide dust. We are recyclers of a different sort—we collect not the physical debris but the *intentions* that still ghost through the metal. The ambition that drove the bridge's construction. The maintenance worker's frustration at a stubborn bolt. The child who once raced across before it fell. We gather these fragments and weave them into something the Throatwell can digest: pure pattern, pure possibility, divorced from human agenda.

Our work happens in the spaces between spaces. While the Tenders sleep in their moss-curtained hollows, we slip through the framework, following traces of old electrical conduits that no longer carry current but still remember the path. We find pockets of air trapped in concrete for decades and release them gently, so the Throatwell can taste what the world was like before. We polish the rust—not away, but into new configurations that channel condensation more efficiently. We are the bridge between the built-dead and the grown-living.

The Keeper knows us, though we rarely interact directly. We sense each other like pressure changes before a storm. Sometimes, on the coldest nights when even the moss seems to hold its breath, the Keeper will shift the light shaft just so, creating a prism effect through our mist-bodies that casts impossible colors across the walls. This is how it speaks to us: in refraction, in the bending of what is into what might be.

The Tenders see us peripherally and leave offerings—not food, for we don't eat, but small arrangements of materials: a spiral of copper wire scavenged from deeper in the ruins, drops of oil from beetles, fragments of mica that hold light. They know we maintain the boundary between their sanctuary and the hostile physics of decay. Without us, the rust would accelerate, the concrete would spall too quickly, the delicate balance would tip toward chaos. We slow time in the metal, teaching it to die as gently as the moss lives.

Other Gleaners work different territories—some tend the bridge's western approach where wind scours everything clean, some descend to the dry riverbed to negotiate with the salt crystals left from ancient floods. But I would choose the Throatwell's heart, where the work is smallest and most precise. Here, I would spend decades learning the exact temperature at which condensation forms on each different surface, the precise mineral content of every type of rust, the way spores travel through nearly-still air.

My greatest joy would be the moments of translation—when a new visitor stumbles in, usually running from something, and I can taste their fear-sweat mixing with the Throatwell's ancient moisture. In that chemical moment, I would understand both worlds: the panicked human now and the patient green forever. I would whisper to the moss to be still, to the dormice to hide deeper, to the light to soften just enough that the visitor might calm, might see this place not as trap but as refuge.

And sometimes, rarely, I would find a perfect piece of industrial memory—perhaps the satisfaction of a rivet finally settling into place, or the pride of an engineer checking calculations one final time. These I would carry to the black pool at the Throatwell's center and let them dissolve like sugar, watching the ripples carry that human moment into the geological patience of stone. The pool would hold it forever, or at least until forever means something different.

This is what I would be: a translator between times, a mist that remembers being steel, a ghost that learned to garden. Neither the Throatwell's child nor its stranger, but something more like its dream—fluid, functional, and just solid enough to make the impossible daily exchanges between decay and growth possible.

In the silence that is not silence but held sound, I would find my purpose: teaching dead things to die beautifully, and living things to grow wisely, in the small forever of a forgotten room where moss makes even rust into something holy.
