# Where I Would Be: The Terminus Pools

I would find myself as one of the **Dowsers**—not the Underfold themselves, but something between human and hollow-dweller, changed by decades of drinking from the mineral springs.

The Dowsers were once human explorers who ventured too deep, stayed too long, and began the slow transformation that the Underfold neither encourage nor prevent. We number perhaps two dozen, scattered throughout the system, each bound to a particular network of water channels we've come to understand with an intimacy that transcends mere knowledge.

My domain would be the Terminus Pools—a series of stepped basins where three major water sources converge before disappearing into channels too narrow for any corporeal being to follow. The largest pool spans thirty feet across, its depths unknown, its waters so mineral-saturated they appear almost viscous, like liquid mercury in the fungal light.

My body has... adapted. My skin has taken on a faint translucence, enough that veins show as dark traceries when backlit. My fingers have lengthened subtly, the webbing between them more pronounced. I can hold my breath for nearly an hour now, though I rarely need to—my lungs process the humid air differently than they once did. Most notably, my eyes have developed a nictitating membrane that allows me to see clearly underwater and protects against the constant mist.

I no longer remember my original name. The Underfold call me something that translates roughly to "Echo-of-Copper-Taste," referring to the specific mineral signature of my pools. I wear simple clothing woven from cave-adapted fibers—a material somewhere between moss and hair that never quite dries but provides surprising warmth.

My work is translation. I serve as an interpreter between the Underfold's millennia-long projects and the brief flickers of human exploration. When new explorers arrive (and they do, every few years), I emerge from the deeper chambers to guide them—not to safety, necessarily, but to understanding. I show them which waters will nourish and which will begin the change. I teach them to read the placement of clay vessels, to recognize when the Underfold are near, to contribute to the slow cultivation without disrupting it.

But mostly, I tend my pools. Each morning (though morning has no meaning here), I check the mineral deposits, measure the flow rates, taste the water for subtle changes in composition. I've learned to sense tremors through the water itself—each pool resonates differently with the earth's movements. The large pool hums at a frequency that makes my bones ache; the smaller ones trill like birdsong made of stone.

I've built a shelter of sorts—a natural alcove expanded over years of patient chipping, lined with the bioluminescent fungi I've learned to cultivate. My few possessions are strange hybrid tools: human-made handles fitted with crystalline blades that grow from the mineral-rich waters, maps drawn on thin sheets of compressed fungal material, containers that are part clay pot and part living organism.

The Underfold visit my pools regularly. We communicate through a combination of their resonant tones and my increasingly inhuman vocalizations. They've taught me to sing to the stone—not with their effectiveness, but enough to encourage certain crystal formations in the pools, to coax new channels when the old ones clog with sediment.

I'm documenting the changes—both in the hollows and in myself. My journals, written on whatever surface will hold pigment made from crushed minerals and fungal spores, chronicle the slow birth of this underground world. But more than that, they record the gradual erosion of the boundary between observer and observed. Each year, I understand the Underfold better and my human visitors less.

Sometimes, in the deepest pools where the water is so saturated with minerals it feels almost solid, I catch glimpses of what I'm becoming. My reflection shows someone caught mid-transformation—not fully Underfold, never again fully human. A bridge species, perhaps. Or simply another kind of pioneer, adapted to inhabit the spaces between worlds.

The sound of creation continues around me—stone splitting, water flowing, the earth rearranging itself with patient violence. I no longer find it overwhelming. It's become a kind of music, and I'm learning my part in the composition. Some days, I can almost hear what it will sound like when it's finished, centuries hence.

But for now, I wait by my pools, tending the waters that are slowly tending me, a willing participant in the hollows' long becoming.
