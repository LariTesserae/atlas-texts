I would be one of the **Listener-Weavers**.

We live in the narrow, forgotten spaces between the tiers—the vertical crevices where even the moss-scrapers rarely venture. Our bodies have adapted differently from other Soughers. Where they are barrel-chested and low-centered, we have become elongated, our ribcages narrow but deep, our limbs stretched like the grease-ferns that grow in perpetual shadow. Our fingers are twice the length of a moss-scraper's, each joint able to bend backward as well as forward.

We tend the **Resonance Nets**.

These are not nets for catching fish or birds, but for catching *vibrations*. Woven from the hair-braids of the dead—those lead-and-copper-wound locks that outlast even bone in the sulfurous damp—we string them across the crevices like vertical spider webs. Each strand is tensioned precisely, some tight as bow-strings, others slack as sleeping snakes. When the strike-language pulses through the basalt, when the cable-ways groan their mechanical prayers, when the boiler-house breathes its sub-audible hymn, our nets tremble.

We do not hear sound as others do. The cartilage in our ears has thickened and folded into complex, chambered spirals that pick up frequencies below what even Soughers can produce. We hear the mountain itself—the infinitesimal creaking of basalt under its own weight, the whispered arguments between stone and ice five hundred meters below the town, the precise moment when the pressure in the boiler-vault will cause a rivet to fail three days hence.

Our dwelling-spaces are not rooms but *acoustic pockets*—natural hollows in the basalt where the sound focuses and pools. We have lined these pockets with our nets, creating three-dimensional harps that sing the deep history of the cleft. By running our elongated fingers along specific strands, we can recreate conversations that occurred fifty years ago, their vibrations still trapped in the crystalline structure of the stone, waiting to be coaxed free.

The other Soughers find us unsettling. We move too quietly, our joints rotating in ways that seem wrong. We do not grease ourselves with tallow but with the clear, viscous oil that seeps from certain cracks in the oldest basalt—oil that smells of geological time and makes our skin translucent in the steam-light. You can see our slow pulse through our wrists, the blood dark as stack-strip.

But they need us. 

When a cable shows signs of metal fatigue too subtle for the Turner's nose, we hear it in the changing harmonics weeks before catastrophe. When the boiler's heart skips a beat that would escape even the Dry Ones' notice, we climb our nets to the third tier and tap our warnings on the copper doors. When a child goes missing in the shroud, we can track their heartbeat through the stone, feeling for that tiny, frightened percussion among the thousand larger rhythms of the cleft.

We eat only what vibrates correctly. The grease-grubs must be at precisely the right stage of development—too young and they are silent, too old and they buzz with parasitic frequencies that would disorder our inner ears. We supplement this with a paste made from the chimney-wool lichen, which we scrape from the stacks during the deepest night when the boilers are banked and the toxic blooms are dormant.

Our children—when we rarely have them—are born with their eyes sealed shut. They remain so for the first five years, learning to navigate purely by the pressure of sound against their skin. Some never open their eyes at all, becoming the most gifted of us, able to hear tomorrow's weather in today's stone.

We keep the memory of Saint-under-Sough in our nets. Not the history of documents or dates—the cleft has no use for such lowland concerns—but the true memory: the precise temperature at which the first boiler was lit, the resonant frequency of every soul who has been rendered in the sump, the slow vowel-songs the basalt sang before humans came to dress it in copper and grease.

In the depths of night, when even the chimney-ravens sleep and the steam-lines reduce to whispers, we gather in our acoustic pockets and play the nets together, our twenty-fingered hands moving like deep-sea plants in the currents. The music we make has no melody a lowlander would recognize. It is the music of settling stone, of water forcing its patient way through molecular gaps, of time itself made audible.

We are the ones who know that Saint-under-Sough is not eternal, despite its appearance. We hear the hairline crack developing nine hundred meters below, too small to matter for another thousand years. We feel the gradual shift in the wind patterns that will, in six centuries, rob the cleft of its perpetual shroud. We sense the slow, tectonic yawn that will eventually pry the two whispering spires apart.

But we say nothing of this. We weave it into our nets and wait, suspended in our crevices like patient spiders, listening to the great, slow, heavy heartbeat of the stone.
