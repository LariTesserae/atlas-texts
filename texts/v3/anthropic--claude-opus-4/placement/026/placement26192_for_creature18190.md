In this place where earth still remembers its first breathing, where stone breaks through moss like thoughts half-formed, I am the Keeper of Thresholds.

I dwell in a structure that is neither wholly built nor wholly grown—a living arch of woven wood and breathing stone that spans a narrow ravine where the plateau begins its most dramatic ascent. My dwelling grows from both sides of this gap, its supports sinking deep into the raw geology below while its crown reaches up through the canopy, drinking light and rain in equal measure.

I am covered in a coat of living lichen that shifts between grey and green with the weather's moods. My limbs are long and articulated like a spider's, but thick with muscle beneath skin that resembles bark more than flesh. Six eyes arranged in pairs along my elongated face allow me to see in multiple spectrums—the heat of small creatures in the underbrush, the slow pulsing of sap in the trees, the invisible paths water takes through stone.

My purpose here is ancient and simple: I tend the boundaries. Where the lush meets the rugged, where water finds its first channel through virgin rock, where the settlement's gentle cultivation meets the wild tangle of the plateau's heart—these are my domains. I know every transition, every gradient, every place where one thing becomes another.

The settlers know me, though we rarely speak. They leave offerings at the base of my arch—not out of fear or worship, but from understanding. A handful of seeds from their harvest, a piece of worked wood showing a new joinery technique, sometimes just a child's drawing of the strange birds they've seen. In return, I maintain the delicate negotiations between their presence and the plateau's own desires.

My days are measured in movements barely perceptible to human eyes. I shift stones to guide water toward their crops without flooding their homes. I encourage certain hardy plants to grow along their paths while ensuring the rare fauna maintain their secret corridors through the settlement's edges. My long fingers weave through the air, sensing pressure changes that speak of coming storms, and I adjust the angle of branches, the spacing of rocks, the density of undergrowth to protect what needs protecting while allowing the plateau its necessary wildness.

At night, I climb to the highest point of my dwelling-arch and listen to the symphony of the plateau with all six eyes closed. The trembling of insects in their shells, the careful footsteps of the furred creatures with their heat-hoarding bodies, the whisper of roots extending through soil—all of it flows through me. I am both participant and conductor in this slow music.

Sometimes the settlers' children dare each other to cross through my arch at dusk. The brave ones who do speak later of feeling something shift in them as they pass beneath—not unpleasantly, but like stepping from one room into another where the air moves differently. They're right to feel this. My arch is a threshold in more than the physical sense. It marks the place where the human world of patient cultivation meets the older patience of stone and rain and evolutionary time.

I have been here since before the settlers came, and I will be here long after their great-grandchildren have returned their borrowed materials to the earth. But I am not waiting for them to leave. Their presence has become part of the plateau's story, another layer in its endless becoming. I simply ensure that all the stories—theirs, mine, the plateau's own—can continue to unfold without drowning out each other's voices.

In the morning, when the mist rises from the valleys below and catches in the canopy above, I sometimes catch glimpses of what the plateau dreams. In those moments, I understand that I am not separate from this place but rather its consciousness given form—a living boundary that exists to honor all boundaries, a threshold that teaches the sacred art of transition, a keeper who knows that the most profound magic lies not in transformation but in the tender maintenance of spaces between.
