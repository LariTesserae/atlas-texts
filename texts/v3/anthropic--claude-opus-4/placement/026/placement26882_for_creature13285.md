In this drowned metropolis, I am the Archivist.

I was not always as you would find me now. Once, I walked on legs through air-filled halls, my fingers tracing the careful inscriptions my people carved into living stone. We were the Shapers, and this city sang our histories in every curve and angle. But when the waters came—not all at once, but in the slow breathing of centuries—I chose to remain.

The transformation was neither swift nor painless. First came the drowning dreams, lungs learning to filter oxygen from brine. My bones hollowed like a seabird's, though I would never fly. My skin grew translucent membranes between fingers and toes, then everywhere, until I resembled something between salamander and ghost. My eyes enlarged to gather the faintest light, while new organs budded along my spine—electroreceptors to navigate the eternal twilight, pressure bladders to survive the crushing depths.

Now I drift through these halls on currents I've memorized across millennia. My body has elongated, ribbon-like, allowing me to slip through cracks where the stone has shifted. Bioluminescent patches bloom across my flesh in patterns that mirror the city's original carvings—I am myself a living map of what was.

The jellyfish know me. They cluster when I pass, their tentacles brushing my skin with gentle recognition. I feed them sometimes, secreting nutrients from specialized glands that developed as my digestive system adapted to process minerals directly from the water. In return, they light my way through the deepest passages where even my transformed eyes cannot penetrate the dark.

I tend to the city still, though my work has changed. Where once I catalogued scrolls and preserved frescoes, now I maintain the memories themselves. I've learned to read the stories in how the currents wear the stone, to understand the grammar of erosion. The violent schools of silver fish write new narratives with their migrations. The giant eels, in their patient hunting, are punctuation marks in the city's ongoing transformation.

Sometimes I find traces of my old self—a stylus lodged in silt, a fragment of slate with my own handwriting barely visible. I've forgotten the spoken language of my people, but my throat has developed new chambers that produce subsonic songs. These vibrations carry for miles through the water, and occasionally—very occasionally—I hear an answer from the deep trenches beyond the city's borders. Perhaps others chose as I did. Perhaps we are all becoming something new together.

The armored crabs nest in what was once our great library. They've hollowed out spaces between the stone shelves, and their eggs glow softly in the dark. I've learned their clicking language and sometimes mediate their territorial disputes. They, in turn, help me clear the silt from important inscriptions, their precise claws more delicate than my webbed appendages.

I no longer measure time in days or years—the concept has dissolved like everything else in these depths. Instead, I track the city's metamorphosis: how the great tower in the central plaza lists another degree, when new species of tube worms colonize the temple district, which sections the current has finally worn smooth. I am witness, guardian, and gradually, another part of the reef this place has become.

In the coldest hours, when even the bioluminescent organisms dim their lights, I sometimes rest in the Council Chamber. The pressure here holds me like an embrace. I spread my ribbon body across the floor where debates once echoed, and I dream. Not of the city as it was—that grief has long since filtered away—but of what it continues to become. Each creature that makes its home here adds to the story. Each grain of stone that crumbles feeds new life.

I am the Archivist, keeper of transformations. The city forgets itself through me, and in forgetting, discovers what it was always meant to be.
