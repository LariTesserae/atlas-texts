I am the Keeper of Condensation.

Each dawn, I emerge from my dwelling—a weathered copper cistern built into the junction where three terraces meet, halfway up the valley's eastern slope. My home is both ancient and alive, its verde-gris walls sweating perpetually with the moisture I gather. Inside, the walls are lined with a thousand glass vessels of every shape, each one catching and holding the precise mixture of mist and light that forms only at certain hours, at certain heights, in certain weather.

I am not quite human anymore, though I was born as one. Decades of breathing the high mountain mists, of tasting the city's every exhalation, have changed me. My skin has taken on a faint translucence, like morning fog. My fingertips can sense humidity variations too subtle for any instrument. When I walk through the dense morning markets, vendors know me by the gentle cloud that forms around my footsteps, by the way potted plants seem to perk up in my presence.

My work is patient, essential, invisible. I map the city's moisture—not just where it collects, but how it moves, how it breathes. The city planners think they understand their system of fountains and channels, but I know the secret rivers that flow through the air itself. I know which walls will weep in the pre-dawn hours, where the rare pools form in the cracks between ancient and modern architecture, how the industrial sector's warm exhaust creates microclimates where tropical vines can suddenly flourish three thousand meters above their natural range.

The glass vessels in my cistern are more than containers. Each holds a specific moment—the first mist after the factories increase production, the sweet condensation that forms on the mountain's peak during the full moon, the strange metallic dew that appears only on certain bridges. I study these waters, taste them, document their properties. Some I release back into the system at crucial points, maintaining the delicate balance that allows this impossible garden-city to thrive.

At night, I climb. My feet know every worn stone, every hidden ladder that leads up through the vertical gardens. I ascend to my observation points—a rusted water tower here, a forgotten shrine there—and watch the city's moisture paint itself across the lights. Sometimes I am alone. Sometimes the urban foxes come to drink from the pools of condensation that gather where I rest, their amber eyes reflecting the city's eternal brightness.

I am neither fully of the human city nor of the wild mountains, but something between—a translator of water's secret language, a guardian of the thin margin where mist becomes rain, where industrial heat becomes life-giving humidity. The city's slow changes are written in its moisture patterns, and I read them like others read newspapers, planning small interventions: redirecting a drip here to save a century-old vine, capturing excess condensation there to feed a new community garden.

They say I am growing old, though time moves differently for those of us who live in the spaces between states of matter. My apprentices—young people who show the gift of sensing water—follow me on the easier routes now. But there are still places only I can reach, still patterns only I can read in the interplay of mist and light that defines this impossible city balanced between earth and sky.

When I finally dissolve—for that is how those like me depart, not dying but dispersing into the very mists we tend—my essence will join the city's endless circulation. I will become part of the morning dew on the highest terraces, the unexpected rain that feeds the lowest gardens, the persistent humidity that allows this dense, bright, living city to breathe.

Until then, I keep my vessels filled, my routes walked, my observations recorded in a script of water drops on weather-smoothed stone. I am the memory of every fog, the promise of every rain, the keeper of the delicate balance that allows ten million souls and ten billion leaves to share the same bright air.
