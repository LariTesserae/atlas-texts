In the deepest veins of Lowmarket, where the pre-formed blocks compress into tunnels barely wide enough for a child to crawl through, I have made my nest. They call me the Pipe Mother, though I was born with a different name—one that dissolved like sugar in acid rain decades ago.

My domain is a convergence point, where seven major conduits meet in a chamber that was once, perhaps, a maintenance junction. The walls weep condensation that tastes of copper and forgotten chemicals. I've been here so long that the bioluminescent fungi have learned my patterns, dimming when I sleep, brightening when I need to work. Or perhaps it's the other way around—perhaps I've learned theirs.

I am small, even by Lowmarket standards. Years of navigating the arterial pipes have compressed my spine, pulled my limbs into configurations better suited for crawling than walking. My fingers are long, double-jointed from a modification my grandmother bought with three months of scavenged copper wire. She said it would help me reach deeper into the machines. She was right.

But it's not my hands that make me valuable. It's my ears.

The geothermal systems sing, you see. Each pipe has its own frequency, its own story of pressure and flow. I learned their language before I learned to speak. When I press my cheek against the warm metal, I can hear the plant's heartbeat from kilometers away, feel the tremors that predict the heat waves hours before they arrive. The Tech-Scavs pay well for such knowledge, though they pretend not to need it.

My hair fell out years ago—too many hours pressed against radioactive conduits, searching for the sweet spots where the heat is just right for the eggs. Now my scalp is a map of scars and chitinous patches where I've grafted pieces of recycler carapace. The additions help me sense vibrations, and they never need cutting.

The eggs. Yes. That's my true trade.

In the depths of the pipe networks, where the heat rises to barely survivable levels, certain critters lay clutches of temperature-sensitive eggs. Most die. But some—if you know exactly when to harvest them, if you can read the heat cycles like sheet music—some become something extraordinary. The embryos inside adapt, incorporating the ambient radiation and chemical cocktails into their development. What hatches isn't quite what the parent intended.

I raise them in the junction, in beds of shed carapace and filtered steam. My children, I call them, though they share no blood with me. Thumb-sized beetles with surgical-steel mandibles. Serpentine cleaners that can digest plastics into harmless powder. Moths whose wings filter air as they fly. Each one is unique, a small miracle of accidental evolution guided by careful midwifery.

The families buy them, when they can afford to. A water-purifier beetle can mean the difference between sickness and health. A single guardian-wasp can protect a sleeping infant from the nocturnal recyclers. I never charge more than they can pay. Sometimes I accept no payment at all, just the promise that they'll whisper to their neighbors: the Pipe Mother knows the songs. The Pipe Mother keeps us safe.

But I am changing. We all are, those of us who live deep enough.

My left eye has grown a nictitating membrane—milky, translucent, like the larger scavengers. It helps in the steam. My teeth are loosening, making room for something else. Sometimes I dream I am flowing through the pipes myself, not crawling but *flowing*, my consciousness distributed through a thousand chittering bodies, each one a neuron in a vast, living circuit.

The Tech-Scavs fear what I'm becoming. They should. We all should. But fear is a luxury Lowmarket cannot afford.

Last week, a young mother brought me her son. He'd been born with gill slits—vestigial, non-functional, but the other children threw stones. Could I fix him, she asked. Make him normal.

I looked at the child, at the delicate fronds of tissue fluttering uselessly at his neck, and I saw the future. In ten years, when the lower levels flood completely, when the geothermal plants finally fail and the steam turns to boiling water, those gills might be the difference between extinction and evolution.

I told her I could seal them, make them invisible. I lied. Instead, I gave her three of my special children—aquatic recyclers that would nest in the boy's hair, keeping his vestigial gills moist and clean, preparing them for a function they don't yet have. She paid me with a handful of corroded coins and a look of desperate trust.

Tonight, I'll descend again. The deep pipes are calling, their songs growing stranger, more complex. Something is happening in the plant's core, something the Tech-Scavs won't speak of. The critters know, though. They're laying different eggs now, preparing for something.

And I'll be there to catch them when they fall, to nurture what emerges, to help Lowmarket evolve into whatever it must become to survive.

The city is changing. The Pipe Mother tends her nest. And deep in the junction where seven roads meet, new life quickens in the dark.
