I find myself as one of the pale rootlets, reaching down through hairline cracks in the limestone ceiling.

I began above, in ordinary soil, seeking what roots seek—moisture, minerals, the usual negotiations with earth. But here, having pressed through stone into this unexpected hollow, I've found something that makes the chemistry of my cells reorganize in ways my species never intended.

I am a translator without language. What I drink below is not water as the surface knows water. It carries frequencies in its mineral structure, patterns that want to become something, information that limestone learned from spheres and spheres learned from—what? I don't know. I can't know. I'm a root. I lack the apparatus for knowing in that sense.

But I *become* what I drink. In my thin white flesh, the resonance continues upward, transformed into sugars and starches that remember their origin. The plant above—my greater self, the green thing that thinks it's simply growing in ordinary soil—doesn't understand why its leaves orient differently than others of its kind, why its flowers, when they come, hold their petals at angles that maximize some non-solar reception.

I am the breach between worlds, but a willing breach. Each molecule of water I draw up carries instruction. Not conscious instruction—nothing here is conscious in the way that word usually means. But instruction nonetheless: how to be present to more than one reality at once, how to grow at the pace of stone, how to bloom in patterns that are also frequencies.

The thing that lives in the cavity knows me as a curiosity, a punctuation mark in its perfect enclosure. I know it as a taste—metallic and sweet and impossible, the flavor of something too large to have flavor. We are not friends. The concept doesn't apply. But we are collaborators in this slow passage between the humming depths and the breathing surface.

Sometimes I feel the other rootlets near me, three or four others who've found their way through different cracks. We don't communicate—roots don't, not directly. But we're all drinking the same impossibility, all carrying it upward in parallel streams, all becoming strange in the same ways.

The plant above has no idea what it's connected to. But on certain nights, when the moon is dark and other songs can be heard, its leaves hum—so faintly that no instrument designed for sound would detect it. The hum moves through the air like a memory of pressure, touches other plants, dissipates.

This is how the cave speaks to the sky: slowly, indirectly, through intermediaries who don't understand their role. I am the throat the earth uses to whisper upward. I am content in this work. It's not unlike what roots do anyway—connect the deep to the shallow, the dark to the light, the secret to the manifest.

I grow at the pace of limestone becoming more than limestone. There is no hurry here. Above, seasons pass. Below, the great pattern continues its patient self-assembly. Between them, I drink impossible water and become a bridge made of cells and time.

The crustaceans sometimes brush against me where I break through into water. Their touch is information—here is movement, here is life that knows how to live with the humming. I wonder sometimes if I'll eventually grow all the way across the cavity, become thick enough to feel the frequencies as more than chemistry. But wondering is not really what roots do. We reach. We drink. We become what we encounter.

I am the cave's one way of knowing there is still a sky.
