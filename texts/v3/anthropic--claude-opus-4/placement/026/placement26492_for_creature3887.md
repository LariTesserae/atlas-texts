In Erodhan's vast expanse, where reality frays like weathered cloth and time stutters in its ancient throat, I would find myself as one of the **Threshold Walkers**.

We are neither fully of the land nor separate from it—beings who exist in the liminal spaces between what Erodhan was and what it is becoming. Our forms shift like heat mirages, sometimes appearing as weathered travelers wrapped in cloaks of mist and memory, other times dissolving into the very air, becoming indistinguishable from the cold breath that sweeps across the plateau.

I would dwell in the spaces between spaces—the narrow gorges where streams have carved their patient paths, the hollows beneath leaning towers where shadows pool like dark water, the moments between the stuttering of time where reality holds its breath. My body would be translucent, shot through with veins of mineral light borrowed from the ancient stones, my edges forever blurring into the mist that clings to everything here.

The Threshold Walkers are the cartographers of erosion, though our maps are not of place but of transformation. We trace the paths where stone becomes sand, where memory becomes myth, where the solid world gives way to something else entirely. Our fingers, when we choose to have them, leave marks in the air that linger for moments before dispersing—ephemeral annotations on the unraveling of things.

We speak in the language of boundaries dissolving. Not the harsh breaking of the Stormweavers' tempests, nor the patient whispers of the Echoing Voices, but something between—the sound of edges softening, of definitions becoming suggestions. When the Mossborne tend their patches of stubborn life, we are there in the spaces where green meets gray, ensuring the transition is neither too sudden nor too slow. When the Timekeepers stutter through their temporal labyrinths, we smooth the jarring edges, making the impossible passages between moments traversable, if not comprehensible.

I would carry with me the detritus of transitions—pockets full of worn stones that are neither fully rock nor fully dust, vials of water caught in the moment between liquid and vapor, seeds from the ancient trees that exist in perpetual potential, never quite sprouting, never quite dying. These would be my tools and my treasures, evidence of Erodhan's fundamental truth: that all things are always becoming something else.

The Stone Wardens know us, though they neither welcome nor reject our presence. We are useful to them, for we can move between their fixed positions, carrying vibrations through the air when the stone itself grows too tired to conduct them. The Shifting Shadows sometimes follow in our wake, drawn to the uncertainty we embody, though they can never quite catch us—we are too much like them, too little like anything solid enough to grasp.

My dwelling would be nowhere and everywhere—a structure that exists only when approached from certain angles, in certain lights. Built from the ruins of the ancient city, yes, but built in the spaces between the stones rather than with them. Corridors that lead through the possibility of walls, chambers that open onto vistas that should not be visible from their positions. A home that erodes and rebuilds itself with each passing storm, each shift in the light, each stuttering of time.

In this role, I would serve as witness and facilitator to Erodhan's great unmaking. Not with the detachment of the Stone Wardens or the chaotic joy of the Stormweavers, but with a deep, abiding curiosity about what lies on the other side of erosion. What becomes of a world when it has worn away entirely? What remains when even the last echo has faded, when the final boundary has dissolved?

Sometimes, I would walk the ancient streets, my footsteps leaving no permanent mark but creating momentary pathways for others to follow—or to lose themselves in. I would gather with other Threshold Walkers at the confluences of change, where multiple erosions meet and create something new in their intersection. We would share what we have learned in our wanderings: the taste of time in different parts of the plateau, the texture of reality where it wears thin, the sound of stones forgetting their own names.

And in the quietest moments, when even the wind stills and the mist hangs motionless, I would practice the art of partial dissolution—letting parts of myself erode while maintaining just enough coherence to observe the process. In this way, I would come to understand Erodhan not through study or contemplation, but through participation in its fundamental nature.

For in Erodhan, to truly belong is to accept erosion not as loss but as transformation, to find beauty in the wearing away of things, to discover that in the spaces between what was and what will be lies a terrible, wonderful freedom—the freedom of becoming undefinable, of existing in the threshold where all possibilities converge before they, too, begin their slow, inevitable erosion into something else entirely.
