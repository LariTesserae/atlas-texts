In the quietest corner of the Sunken Gardens, where three springs converge behind a curtain of maiden-hair ferns, I would be the Memory Keeper—she who tends not the plants themselves, but the stories they hold.

My dwelling would be little more than a shallow cave worn into the limestone, its entrance so overgrown with climbing jasmine that even the other Keepers sometimes forget it exists. Inside, the walls would be lined with hundreds of small niches, each holding a different vessel—clay pots and glass vials, carved gourds and woven baskets, all containing the pressed flowers, seed collections, and preserved specimens of plants that once grew here but exist no longer.

I would move through the garden at the edges of day, when the light slants long and everything casts double shadows. My work would be to notice what others might miss—the last bloom of a variety of iris that hasn't flowered in a decade, the peculiar twist in a fern's frond that suggests ancient hybridization, the way certain mosses grow only where the limestone bears fossil imprints of prehistoric shells.

My skin would carry the permanent stain of plant pigments, my fingers marked green and gold and purple from years of pressing petals between papers made from garden fibers. I would wear simple clothes, but always with deep pockets full of collecting papers, tiny brushes for gathering pollen, vials of clear resin for preserving delicate structures. Around my neck, a magnifying lens carved from quartz, worn smooth from constant use.

The other Keepers would seek me out when they discovered something unusual—a cutting that wouldn't root despite perfect conditions, a tree that seemed to remember winters colder than any in living memory, a flower that bloomed out of season with colors that didn't match its kind. I would listen to these mysteries, then spend days or weeks in patient observation, often sleeping beneath the plant in question to dream its dreams.

My true work would happen in the deep night, when I light small lamps filled with oil pressed from the seeds of extinct varieties. By this golden light, I would transcribe my observations onto papers made from fallen leaves, using inks derived from roots and berries. But these would not be mere botanical records. Each entry would be part story, part song, part prayer—an attempt to capture not just what a plant is, but what it means, how it fits into the endless conversation between stone and root, water and air.

I would maintain the Garden's memory in other ways too. In a cleared circle near my dwelling, I would cultivate a grandmother garden—a collection of the ancient varieties that the modern garden evolved from. Here would grow the small, bitter ancestors of today's magnificent specimens, the wild types that still carry genetic memories of the lake that once filled this basin. Visitors would rarely come here, finding these plain plants unimpressive compared to the garden's cultivated glories. But the other Keepers would understand—these are the seed-keepers, the reservoir of possibility.

Once each month, at the new moon when the garden is darkest, I would walk the spiral path I've worn through the plantings over decades, carrying a basket of seeds saved from seasons past. At specific places—known only to me and whispered to my eventual successor—I would plant these seeds. Not for this generation to tend, but as gifts to the future, sleeping possibilities that might wait decades or centuries for the right conditions to germinate.

The creatures of the garden would know me differently than they know the other Keepers. The deer would bring me their shed antlers, understanding somehow that I preserve them as records of the minerals drawn up through the plants they browse. The birds would lead me to their abandoned nests, woven with plant fibers that tell stories of which species grew where. Even the fish would beach strange objects at my feet—ancient seeds preserved in lake mud, water-worn roots that speak of trees long gone.

My deepest work would concern the garden's greatest mystery: the Peace that pervades this place. I would have theories, accumulated over a lifetime of observation. Perhaps it comes from the limestone itself, its fossil memories of ancient seas instilling a kind of geological calm. Perhaps from the specific combination of plants, their chemistry creating an atmosphere that soothes aggression. Or perhaps from something older—the intention of those who first shaped this place, somehow impressed upon its very atoms.

In the Seedheart amphitheater, during the great ceremonies, my role would be to sing the oldest songs—wordless melodies that supposedly echo the sounds the first plants made when they began to colonize the drained lake bed. My voice would be cracked and strange from years of speaking more to plants than people, but the other Keepers would lean in to catch every note, knowing that when I pass, these songs pass with me unless another learns to hear them in the whisper of leaf against leaf.

As I age—and Memory Keepers always live long, as if the garden itself is reluctant to let go of those who hold its stories—I would begin to prepare my successor. Not through formal teaching, but by leaving careful clues: a pressed flower tucked into the fork of a tree with a cryptic notation, a new variety planted where only the observant would notice, a stone turned to reveal the fossil beneath exactly when young eyes are watching.

My final act, when I feel the garden calling me home, would be to walk one last time to the deepest part of the basin, where the last waters of the ancient lake still pool in an underground cistern. There, I would plant myself among the water-loving roots, my body becoming one last gift of memory to the soil, my lifetime of observations returning to the source from which all gardens grow.

And somewhere in the garden, a young Keeper would notice an unusual seedling growing where nothing was planted, its leaves unlike anything in their careful catalogs. They would begin to ask questions, to observe, to wonder. The memory-keeping would begin again, as it has for countless generations, as it will for countless more, in this place where human patience has learned to match the timescale of stone and root, where stories grow as slowly and surely as trees.
