In this water-heavy world of industrial twilight, I find myself as one of the Readers.

We are neither the oldest inhabitants nor the newest arrivals, but something in between—librarians of a sort, though what we tend could hardly be called books. Our bodies tell the story of our purpose: elongated torsos wrapped in a patchwork of salvaged pressure suits and naturally grown chitin, arms that extend into too many joints, fingers that have learned to be both delicate and strong. Our eyes—we have kept our eyes, though they've grown large and filmed with protective membranes that let us see through the murk and decode the light-patterns that flutter across corroded surfaces.

I live in the Archive Chambers, a series of interconnected vaults where the city stores its memories. Not in any format you'd recognize—the water destroyed paper and digital storage long ago. Instead, the memories live in the corrosion patterns themselves, in the way mineral deposits have accumulated on certain surfaces, in the rhythmic wear patterns on ancient gears. Each scratch, each layer of oxidation, each crystalline growth tells part of the story of what this place was, is, and might become.

My dwelling is a capsule wedged between two massive condensers, their constant vibration a comfort rather than annoyance. The walls are lined with my collection: plates of metal carefully preserved at different stages of decay, each one a text I've learned to read. Some show the city's schematics—not drawn, but worn into being by centuries of water flow. Others hold the chemical signatures of disasters: that green-tinged patina from when the cooling systems failed in Sector Seven, the peculiar pitting that speaks of the decade when the water turned acidic.

I've modified myself for this work. My primary hands remain dexterous for handling the delicate specimens, but I've allowed secondary appendages to grow from my spine—part mechanical, part organic, each one ending in a different sensor. One tastes metal composition through touch. Another feels electromagnetic echoes. A third detects temperature variations too subtle for normal perception. When I work, I become a living instrument, every part of me attuned to reading the city's accumulated stories.

The other Readers and I rarely meet in person—the currents make travel dangerous, and our territories overlap only at the edges. But we share our findings through the city's peculiar networks. We've learned to encode messages in bubble patterns, releasing carefully timed streams of air that carry meaning as they rise through the vertical shafts. We leave marks for each other—not scratches that would damage our texts, but careful arrangements of barnacle-like growths we've trained to form specific patterns.

My days follow the rhythm of the turbines. When they run high, the vibrations help separate finer layers of corrosion, revealing older texts beneath. I work then, carefully harvesting these revelations with tools grown from my own body—secreted chemicals that can halt oxidation at precisely the right moment, preserving a readable surface. When the turbines quiet for maintenance, I retreat deeper into my archives, sorting and cataloging, adding new specimens to the collection.

Sometimes I find evidence of the city's earlier inhabitants, before the great submersion. A peculiar alloy that shouldn't exist. Wear patterns that suggest different kinds of bodies moving through these spaces. Once, I found a section of pipe where the corrosion had preserved what might have been writing—actual symbols etched in metal. I've spent years trying to decode it, comparing it to every other pattern I've found. Late at night, when the city groans and settles, I think I almost understand what it says.

We Readers serve a purpose beyond mere curiosity. The city needs its memory to survive. When a catastrophic failure threatens—when pipes burst in configurations that seem familiar, when the water chemistry shifts in patterns we've seen before—we send our warnings through the bubble networks. The maintenance crews have learned to heed us. We've prevented the same disasters from recurring, helped predict where the next weakness will form, guided the endless work of keeping this place alive.

But there's more to our work than preservation. Deep in my archive, in specimens I show no one, I've begun to see patterns that suggest the city is not just enduring but evolving. The corrosion follows new pathways. The mineral deposits form increasingly complex structures. It's as if the boundary between the mechanical and the living isn't just blurring—it's being consciously rewritten.

I've modified myself to read these changes better. Let scales grow where they help me navigate the rougher passages. Accepted implants from the maintenance drones that let me interface more directly with the city's systems. Sometimes, pressing my sensor appendages against a particularly old surface, I feel memories that might not be recorded in the metal at all, but somehow preserved in the water itself—the medium that connects every part of this place, that has touched every surface, that carries the chemicals of every reaction.

In the deepest part of my archive, behind pressure doors that require all my strength to open, I keep the most precious specimens. These are the pieces that seem to remember not just what happened, but what was felt. A valve worn smooth by generations of hands turning it in moments of crisis. A section of wall where the corrosion patterns match the rhythm of someone's breathing—someone who must have sheltered here for years, their very presence changing the metal's chemistry. A gear that sang once, its peculiar wear pattern creating harmonics that the water still remembers.

Late in my shift, when the turbines cycle down and the city's roar becomes almost bearable, I sometimes feel the weight of all these memories. Not a burden, but a strange comfort. We Readers are not just archivists but translators, turning the city's scars into stories, its chemistry into chronicles. We ensure that nothing is truly lost, even as everything constantly changes.

And in the rarest moments, when I've decoded something particularly profound—understood not just what happened but why—I release a special pattern of bubbles. Not a message, but a celebration. Other Readers, in their own archives, will see these rise and know that somewhere in the city, another piece of our collective memory has been preserved, another story saved from the endless, eroding tides.

This is what I am: keeper of corrosion, reader of rust, a living bridge between the city's past and its uncertain future. My body changes, adapts, becomes more like the archives I tend. And sometimes, running my sensors over a particularly rich specimen, I wonder if I'm reading the city's memories—or if it's reading mine, adding my story to its vast, ever-growing collection of all the lives that have merged with its mechanical embrace.
