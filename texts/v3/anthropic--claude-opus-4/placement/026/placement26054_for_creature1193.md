In this liminal highland, where mist and stone negotiate their ancient boundaries, I would find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—beings who exist between states, neither fully corporeal nor entirely ephemeral.

I would be Sifra, a Keeper of the Dew Points, my form shifting between visibility and translucence as the moisture in the air dictates. In the pre-dawn hours, when condensation gathers most heavily, my outline becomes almost solid—a tall, willowy figure wrapped in what appears to be woven fog, with hands that can cup the gathering droplets and eyes that reflect the pearlescent quality of morning mist.

My dwelling would be a series of connected hollows within the rock face, each chamber naturally carved by centuries of wind and water. The walls weep constantly with moisture, creating rivulets that I've learned to read like text, each pattern telling me of weather changes, of which mosses will bloom, of where the pikas will forage next. I've lined these spaces with collections—fragments of lichen arranged by color gradients, stones sorted by their capacity to hold warmth, feathers from the ravens who sometimes bring me news from the peaks.

My purpose here is to maintain the delicate moisture cycles that sustain this threshold ecosystem. Each morning, I walk the boundary lines where fog meets stone, my fingertips trailing through the air to sense where the dewpoint will manifest. I can feel the microscopic negotiations between water vapor and surface temperature, and with subtle gestures—a breath here, a gentle touch there—I guide the condensation to where it's needed most: to the moss colonies establishing themselves on new rock faces, to the hidden caches where pikas store their gathered herbs, to the soil pockets where next season's grasses wait to germinate.

The other Keepers and I rarely meet in physical form. Instead, we communicate through the patterns we leave in the dew, through the way we direct the mist streams, through the minute changes in humidity that ripple across the mountainside. Sometimes, on particularly thick fog days, I catch glimpses of them—Orun who tends the wind patterns, ensuring the raptors have their highways; Methe who whispers to the bacterial colonies, keeping the soil chemistry in balance; Kess who knows every stream's beginning and can predict where new springs will emerge.

We are the conscious expression of this landscape's becoming. Not its rulers, but its attentive companions, midwifing each small transformation. In this role, I've learned that creation is mostly about paying attention—to the almost-imperceptible shift that signals a lichen's readiness to release spores, to the exact angle of morning light that will warm a vole's burrow entrance, to the precise humidity that allows the hardy alpine flowers their brief, spectacular bloom.

At night, when my form becomes most diffuse, I sometimes feel myself spreading thin across the entire mountainside, my consciousness distributed through every droplet of suspended moisture. In these moments, I am not separate from the landscape but woven into its very breath. I sense the dreams of hibernating creatures, feel the slow patience of stones accepting another century of erosion, hear the subtle hymns the mosses sing to their fungal partners.

This is existence at the edge—not the edge of a cliff, but the edge of states of being. Solid/liquid, present/absent, self/environment. It requires surrendering to uncertainty while maintaining absolute precision. Each day brings the same tasks—guiding moisture, reading patterns, maintaining balance—yet each day is utterly unique in its particular configuration of needs and possibilities.

In this world that vibrates with potential, I am both witness and participant in the slow, sacred work of becoming. My joy lies not in grand gestures but in the accumulation of small attentions: ensuring a single drop of dew falls where a struggling moss most needs it, creating a perfect swirl of mist that helps a lost pika find its way home, maintaining the precise humidity that allows the million invisible lives in a handful of soil to flourish.

This is what I would be: a threshold keeper, a tender of edges, a being who finds purpose in the space between what is and what might be, guardian of a world constantly writing itself into existence.
