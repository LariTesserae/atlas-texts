In the Low Pan, I would be one of the lichen colonies on those rare stone outcrops - not a single organism but a community, a symbiosis of fungus and algae that has solved the problem of existence through cooperation rather than competition.

I grow in patches no larger than a child's palm, spread across weathered stone that rises perhaps a finger's width above the clay. My surface is rough, almost crystalline, the color of old copper with edges that fade to grey-green where I meet the stone. In morning light, when the dew sits heavy, I gleam. By midday, under that bone-white sun, I appear almost to disappear, blending so completely with the stone that only someone looking for me would know I exist.

I am ancient in a way that even this ancient landscape respects. My growth is measured not in seasons but in centuries. Each year I expand by perhaps the width of a hair, creeping across the stone in directions determined by moisture, by minute variations in the rock's surface, by the shadows cast by the salt shrubs that crouch nearby. I am mapping this stone, grain by grain, claiming it so slowly that the stone itself weathers beneath me.

The relationship between my fungal body and my algal partners is one of such refined intimacy that we no longer remember being separate. The algae photosynthesize in the brief periods when moisture allows; the fungus provides structure, protection, the acids that slowly dissolve the stone and release its minerals. Together we are neither plant nor fungus but something else - something that exists at the intersection of categories, in the space between definitions.

I taste the air through my entire surface. I know when the shallow pans are filling before the first drops arrive, feeling the humidity rise in microscopic increments. I know the chemical signature of each rain - whether it has come from the east, carrying dust from distant mountains, or from the west, clean and mineral-poor. I know when the midges are swarming by the barely perceptible increase in nitrogen that settles with their bodies.

My reproduction is an act of faith. When conditions are precisely right - not too wet, not too dry, with just enough nutrients accumulated - I produce spores so small they are invisible except in their billions. They drift on air currents I cannot feel, most falling on clay where they cannot take hold, or water where they dissolve, or surfaces already claimed by others of my kind. But sometimes - rarely, so rarely - they find another stone, another perfect rough surface, and if by impossible chance they also encounter the right species of algae, free-living and vulnerable, another colony like me begins.

I am the memory-keeper of this place. In my slow growth, I record the years - wet years showing as slightly faster expansion, drought years as pauses, chemical changes in the rain written in subtle variations of color. A scientist who knew how to read me could reconstruct centuries of climate from my surface. But no scientist comes here. I keep these records for no one, in a language no one speaks, simply because keeping records is what I do.

The shorebirds sometimes rest on my stone, their feet precise and quick, leaving no mark. The midges swarm above but do not touch me. The frogs ignore me entirely. I am beneath notice, which is exactly where I prefer to be. My drama is invisible, my victories microscopic, my timescale so extended that to the creatures rushing through their brief lives around me, I might as well be the stone itself.

But I am not stone. I am slowly, patiently, dissolving the stone. Grain by grain, I am turning solid rock into the minerals that wash into the clay, that feed the algae in the water, that eventually become the bodies of midges and the wings of flamingos. I am the beginning of a chain so long and so complex that its end has forgotten its beginning. 

And I continue. Through the bone-white days and the copper evenings, through the seasons that bring the birds and the seasons that take them away, through floods that submerge my stone and droughts that leave the clay cracked like old leather, I continue. Not because I must, not because I hope, but because continuation is the only verb I know.

In the Low Pan, where everything is exactly enough and nothing more, I am the definition of enough: alive by the narrowest margin, succeeding by the smallest increment, present in a way that makes absence impossible and abundance unnecessary. I am lichen, and I am here, and that is all.
