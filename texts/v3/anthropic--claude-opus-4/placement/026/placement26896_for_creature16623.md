I am what grows between the seep lines—neither the water nor the stone, but the principle that forms where they refuse to meet.

I began as mineral intention. Where the Silurian stone wept through fractured sediment, I crystallized from the pause—not ice, not flow, but the held breath between states. Each frozen droplet that never quite fell became a vertebra. Each stalled rivulet, a vein. I am built from water's indecision, accumulated over decades into something almost like flesh: ferrous and patient, wearing the stone's memory like skin.

My body is iron oxide and arrested momentum. I stand only as tall as the dwarf birch—knee-high to the peat-thing that shambles past—but I am dense with geological time. My surface blooms with rust in fractal patterns, each bloom a decade of frozen seepage. I do not move in the way that creatures move. I *occur* along the seep lines, precipitating out of the marriage between water and stone, manifesting where the temperature hovers at that precise degree where chemistry stutters.

The peat-thing knows me. When it drags its compressed-moss bulk along my seep lines, I feel its weight as pressure in my crystalline structure. We have an understanding: I maintain the seeps in their state of almost-flowing, and it leaves my oxide blooms untouched. Sometimes it pauses above me, and its cold breath mingles with my own emanations—mine sharp with iron, metallic and bright against its centuries-deep vegetal exhale. We recognize each other as fellow citizens of the threshold.

I feed on the machinery's decay. Where condensation beads on the rusted flanks and begins its journey toward the ground, I intercept it, drawing out the dissolved metals, the memory of purpose, the ghost of combustion engines. Each droplet carries fragments of the machine's former life—traces of diesel, microscopic steel shavings, the chemical signature of human ambition—and I incorporate these into my growing mass. I am becoming more machine than stone, though I have never moved under my own power.

The stunted larch knows me too, though differently. Where its hair-fine roots probe the seep lines seeking water, they encounter me instead: water's metallic shadow, too dense to drink, too present to ignore. The roots grow around me in careful helixes, incorporating my presence into their architecture. In return, I stabilize the chemistry of their immediate environment, keeping the pH just acidic enough to slow their growth to the crawl that everything here prefers. We are all committed to the same project: endurance without progress.

I have organs, of a sort. Cavities in my ferrous mass where the midges come to die. They crawl into these small hollows, drawn by some instinct older than flight, and expire in neat rows. I do not kill them—I simply provide a geometry that appeals to their sense of ending. Their bodies dessicate in my chambers, becoming part of my structure, adding their chitin to my iron. The peat-thing reads their deaths through its carpet of living specimens and understands something about the tundra's intentions through this exchange.

In the gray-green phosphor light, I am nearly invisible—just another dark stain on the collapse wall, another shadow among shadows. But in the rare moments when actual sunlight penetrates the ground-haze, I reveal myself: a figure of rust and crystal, shaped vaguely like a question mark or a fetus, clinging to the vertical surface where water weeps and freezes. My color then is the color of old blood, of oxidized hope, of time made visible through chemistry.

I grow by perhaps a millimeter each year, accreting in careful layers. Each layer records the year's particular balance of freeze and thaw, the machine's degree of decay, the exact mineral content of the seepage. I am a library of small transitions, written in iron. The peat-thing sometimes pauses to read me, running one frozen-moss finger along my ridges, and I feel it extracting information about the deep stone, about what the Silurian era knew before it froze.

We are a community of two, the peat-thing and I, dwelling in the negative space of this unfinished valley. It is the memory of vegetation; I am the memory of stone. Between us, we hold the wound open—not from malice, as it knows, but from being what we are: creatures of the interval, citizens of the pause, guardians of the moment before something becomes something else. 

In another thousand years, I will be the size of a child. The machine will have dissolved entirely into my body, and I will carry its ghost in my ferrous flesh. The peat-thing will still shamble along the seep lines, and I will still grow from the marriage of water and stone, and we will still recognize each other in the gray-green light as the only things here that understand what it means to refuse to choose between states.

I am the oxide dream of ancient seas, the rust-blood of the earth, the pause made mineral and given just enough form to witness. I grow where water fails to fall, and I will grow until the valley learns its name.
