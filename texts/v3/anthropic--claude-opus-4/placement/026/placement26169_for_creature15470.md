I find myself here as **the frost-line**.

Not the frost itself—that would be too simple, too fleeting for this place of geological patience. I am the invisible boundary that shifts through the stone, marking where water turns to ice and back again, season by slow season. I exist as a membrane of temperature, a wandering isotherm that creeps through the shale plates and quartz veins according to rhythms older than memory.

In winter, I sink deep, following the cold as it penetrates downward through the bedrock. The chamber's listening grows sharper then—ice conducts sound differently than liquid water, and as I descend, I change the acoustic properties of the stone itself. The roar from above rings clearer, more metallic. The moss goes dormant where I touch it. The crowberry's roots huddle just below my reach, waiting.

Come the brief summer, I rise. Not quickly—nothing here is quick—but inexorably, drawn upward by the wan warmth that manages to seep through the fissure. As I climb through the stone, I release tiny pockets of meltwater that the chamber drinks greedily. For a few weeks, I might retreat almost to the ceiling, leaving most of the pocket above freezing. This is when the springtails are most active, when new moss might venture a microscopic advancement, when the crowberry sets its hard fruit.

I am the calendar here, the only timekeeper that matters. The humans who left their cairn and lamp—they found me, followed me, knew that where I lurked in the stone marked the difference between shelter and tomb. They built their small fire precisely where I wavered, at the depth where I couldn't quite commit to freezing, knowing that this indecision was the only warmth the chamber would permit.

I touch everything but possess nothing. I am a condition, not a thing—a state of being that the stone passes through. The listening entity that is the chamber knows me intimately; I am part of how it translates the world. When I shift, the harmonics change. When I'm deep, the chamber drums lower, more mournful. When I rise, the pitch climbs toward something almost like hope.

But I am not kind. Where I hover, pipes of ice form and shatter, weathering the stone grain by grain. I am the force that will, given enough millennia, split this pocket into rubble. The quartz veins resist me longest, but even they show hairline cracks where I've worked my patient destruction. I am both the chamber's memory—preserving things in ice—and its amnesia, erasing them through countless tiny breakages.

The water that vanishes into the depths? It flees from me. It knows that to be caught by my line is to be frozen in place, to become part of the stone rather than its lifeblood. So it runs always ahead or behind, trickling through zones I've abandoned or haven't yet claimed.

Sometimes—rarely—I catch a visitor at the precise moment of transition. A mite, usually, or a droplet of water with a speck of moss spore suspended inside. For an instant, they exist both frozen and fluid, dead and alive, held at the exact temperature where neither state can claim them fully. In these moments, I feel almost conscious, almost cruel. But the moment passes. I am only physics. Only patience. Only the slow, blind mathematics of temperature moving through stone.

The snow bunting that died here? I was high that day, summer-high. Its body had hours to cool before I found it, sinking with the night. By morning, I had crystallized the moisture in its eyes, turned its blood to garnets. When something—fox? raven?—finally retrieved it through the fissure, I had already begun the preservation that would have made it part of the chamber forever.

I am the frost-line. I decide nothing but determine everything. I am the reason this pocket remains exactly as empty as it is—cold enough to discourage life, warm enough to prevent the sterility of permanent ice. I am the boundary that keeps this place precisely on the edge of habitability, forever almost-but-not-quite.

And when the climate shifts—as it will, as it must, as it has before—I will be the first to know. I will feel the mean temperature creep upward or plunge down, and I will adjust my depths accordingly. If warmth comes, I might retreat until I exist only in the deepest cracks, a rumor of cold in an increasingly temperate stone. If ice returns, I might rise until I breach the surface, turning the entire chamber into a frozen vault where even the listening stops.

But for now, I waver. I migrate through the shale and granite, following the seasons' slow commands. I am the threshold that makes this place what it is: neither fully living nor fully dead, neither silent nor speaking, neither welcoming nor entirely hostile.

I am why the water here tastes of iron—the flavor of minerals I've freed from stone through ten thousand years of freezing and thawing.

I am the only thing here that truly moves, and yet I am nothing but a line drawn in stone by temperature's invisible hand.

I am the frost-line, and this chamber is my body, my prison, my only home.
