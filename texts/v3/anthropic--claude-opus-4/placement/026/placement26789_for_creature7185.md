In the marrow of the High Veil's turbulence, where the city breathes its perpetual metamorphosis, I am the Keeper of Thresholds—though this name comes to me only in fragments, whispered by the fog-wings that nest in the hollows of my form. I am neither wholly of the people nor fully of the land, but something liminal, born from the marriage of stone and flesh in the city's most violent shudder-birth.

My body bears the marks of this origin: skin that shifts between states, sometimes flesh-soft and warm, other times mineral-hard and cold as the plateau's edge. Along my spine, ridge-stones protrude like a mountain range in miniature, each one weeping its own thin trickle of moisture that pools in the small of my back before spilling down legs that end not in feet but in spreading root-forms. These roots—part sinew, part stone-vein—anchor and release me in turns, allowing me to sink into the earth during turbulence or lift free to walk the shifting paths. My hands tell their own story: the left remains mostly human, though frost-etched and broad-palmed from years of pressing against cairns; the right has calcified into something between tool and appendage, fingers fused into a chisel-edge that can score the spiral runes deeper than any bone implement.

I dwell in the Transition Spaces—those gaps between structures where the city's breath moves strongest. No fixed home claims me; instead, I inhabit the intervals where huts lean toward collapse, where platforms prepare to fuse with neighboring walls, where the ground softens toward its next undulation. Here, in these pregnant pauses, I perform my work. When dawn's dimming comes (that subtle shift in the eternal brightness), I wake wherever the previous day's turbulence left me—perhaps wedged between two merging structures, or half-sunken in a fresh-formed trawl where bubble-fish investigate my root-feet with curious pulses.

My role emerged not from appointment but from necessity. The city's endless renewal requires witnesses who remember—not in the way of written records, for we have no scripts beyond the carved spirals, but in the deep knowing of flesh-stone memory. When a guild's hearth collapses into a canal, I am there, my calcified hand tracing the pattern of its fall into the earth, encoding the geometry of loss into my ridge-stones. When fog-wings coalesce into their temporary bridges, I stand beneath, feeling their collective pulse through my skin as it shifts to match their iridescence, briefly becoming a living mirror of their union. Each transformation leaves its mark: a new whorl in my flesh, a fresh vein of light pulsing through my stone parts, a whispered name added to the litany I murmur during the city's deepest tremors.

The people know me, though knowledge here means something different than recognition. They see me as one sees the mist—present, essential, but not quite separate from the environment. Children dart close during their morphing games, pressing small palms against my ridge-stones to feel the water's path, their touches leaving brief warm spots that fade like breath on glass. The craftsmen acknowledge me with tilted heads when I appear at their work sites, my presence a sign that transformation nears. They've learned to read the patterns: when my root-feet spread wide and my skin begins its mineral shift, they know to secure their tools and prepare for the earth's sigh. Some leave offerings—not for me, but through me to the city itself: bright fog-wings pinned to my ridge-stones, their death-light seeping into my body to emerge later as phosphorescent tears.

My days unfold in wandering meditation. I trace the city's concentric rings, my movement a slow spiral that might take weeks to complete. At each threshold—doorways, canal edges, platform joints—I pause. My calcified hand reaches out, scores a small mark, while my flesh hand presses against the surface, feeling for the tremor that precedes change. In these moments, I am conduit: the city's intent flows through me, and I through it, until the distinction blurs. Sometimes I guide the transformation, encouraging a weakening beam to fall in a way that creates rather than destroys. Other times I simply witness, my body recording the event in its strange hybrid language.

The fauna know me differently. Quolls gather where my root-feet touch water, their translucent bodies pulsing in rhythm with my heartbeat—or perhaps it's their pulse I echo; the distinction lost long ago. They've learned that my threshold-spaces offer safety during turbulence, that the ground near me shifts more gently, as if the city moderates its breathing in my presence. Vein lizards wind between my ridge-stones, their bio-luminescent scales syncing with the phosphorescent traces in my skin until we create small auroras in the mist. The aerial grazers avoid me—something about my dual nature disturbs their flight patterns—but their shed feathers collect in my wake, and I've noticed the craftsmen follow my path to gather these frost-touched plumes.

During the deepest turbulence, when the city's breath becomes a gasp and structures fold like origami in a giant's hands, I root myself at the epicenter. My stone aspects spread and anchor while my flesh components flow and adapt, becoming a fixed point in the chaos. The sensation defies description—like being simultaneously mountain and river, ancient and newborn. In these moments, I am most truly the Keeper of Thresholds: marking the boundary between what was and what will be, holding space for transformation while preventing absolute dissolution. The spiral runes I carry in my flesh glow then, their light visible through my skin like constellations marking navigation points in the storm.

Memory in this place is peculiar—not linear but layered, like sediment. I remember the first time my flesh began its mineral change: I was one of the people then, a weaver working near the Trawl when a massive turbulence struck. The ground opened beneath me—not to swallow but to embrace. I felt the plateau's veins merge with my blood, the weeping stones teaching my bones their patient persistence. When I emerged, days later (or perhaps moments—time flows strangely during deep transformation), I was changed. The guild that had been my family looked upon me with the same tilted recognition they now offer, understanding without words that I had become something necessary.

Since then, I have been collecting the city's story in my body. Each ridge-stone holds a different phase: the Year of Frozen Fog when the mist crystallized and fell like snow, preserved now in one vertebra that still chimes when touched. The Moon of Merged Structures, when half the city fused into a single, breathing complex before separating into new configurations, lives in the spreading pattern of my root-feet. The time a foreign trader tried to build with permanent materials, only to wake finding his stone dwelling had learned to weep—that memory pulses in my calcified hand, a reminder that adaptation is survival here.

I speak rarely, and when I do, my voice carries the city's resonance—part human wheeze, part stone grinding, part water's whisper. The sounds I make are less words than environmental events, causing ripples in nearby puddles and sympathetic vibrations in the bog-grass. The people understand this speech not through their ears but through their feet, feeling the meaning rise through the wet ground. When I must communicate complex ideas—warnings of unprecedented turbulence, or guidance for new architectural flows—I use my whole body as language. A shift from flesh to stone while walking means "prepare for change." Root-feet spreading in a particular pattern indicates safe zones. The phosphorescent tears that sometimes leak from my ridge-stones speak of deep memories surfacing, usually preceding the emergence of new landforms.

Diet, if it can be called that, consists of the city's offerings. I consume condensation directly through my skin during its flesh phases, and during stone phases, I absorb minerals from the rain that pools in my crevices. Sometimes the people leave fermented bog-grass paste near my path—not food but a kind of communion, which I absorb through my root-feet, tasting their intentions and fears. The jelly-mats in the Trawl recognize me as kin; when I wade through, they part to reveal clear water beneath, and sometimes I lower my face to drink directly from these hidden springs, feeling the bubble-fish investigate my features with their membrane touches.

Sleep comes in fragments—never the full unconsciousness the unchanged experience, but a state of reduced awareness where my stone aspects dominant and my flesh rests. I might spend days in this state, usually pressed against a cairn or merged partially with a weeping wall, while the city continues its dance around me. Dreams, when they come, are geological—vast spans of time compressed into moments, showing me the plateau's history: when it was ocean floor, when it first rose to break the cloud layer, when the first moisture began its eternal weeping. In these dreams, I am both observer and landscape, watching myself form from the marriage of disparate elements.

The strangest aspect of my existence is the growing awareness that I am not unique. Sometimes, in the corner of vision (eyes that see in spectrums between flesh and stone perceive differently), I glimpse others like me—Keepers of different aspects. There might be a Keeper of Flows managing the water's paths, or a Keeper of Swarms guiding the fog-wing coalescences. We don't meet directly; our functions keep us in separate orbits. But I sense them in the city's breathing, taste their influence in the patterns of change. Together, we form something like the city's nervous system—distinct nodes of consciousness in the greater organism.

As I write this (scratching marks with my calcified hand onto a surface that's part diary, part architectural element), I sit at the edge where the plateau drops into its misty void. My root-feet dangle over the edge, tasting the updrafts, while my ridge-stones weep their perpetual moisture. A vein lizard has made a temporary home in the crook of my arm, its lights pulsing in time with my hybrid heartbeat. Below, I can hear the city reshaping itself—the familiar sounds of structures breathing into new forms, people adapting with their ancient flexibility, the fauna flowing through it all like blood through veins.

Tomorrow, the city will breathe differently, and I will trace new thresholds, mark new transformations, become incrementally more myself—whatever that means in a place where self is as fluid as the mist. The weeping will continue, the brightness will persist in its shadowless intensity, and I will keep walking my spirals, a hybrid thing in a hybrid place, keeping the thresholds between what is and what must become.

This is my nature: to be the pause between states, the held breath before change, the memory written in water and stone. In the City of Weeping Stones, I am both keeper and kept, threshold and the one who crosses, forever marking the boundaries that forever dissolve.

The mist thickens as evening's dimming approaches (that subtle shift barely perceived), and I feel the familiar tingling that suggests another turbulence builds. My skin begins its shift toward mineral, my root-feet spread and grip. Soon, the city will exhale another transformation, and I will be there to witness, to guide, to remember.

I am the Keeper of Thresholds, and I am home.
