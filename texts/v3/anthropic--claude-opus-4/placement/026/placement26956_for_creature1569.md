I find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers — beings who exist at the precise boundaries where the engineered vegetation meets bare stone, where moisture transitions from vapor to liquid, where the industrial hum fades into geological silence.

My body is a study in transitions. The left side of my form mirrors the metallic blue-green of the engineered plants, with a surface that shifts between photosynthetic membrane and something more crystalline. The right side gradually mineralizes, becoming indistinguishable from the raw granite of the young mountains. But these aren't static halves — the boundary between them constantly shifts, like a tide line that moves with the rhythm of condensation cycles.

I have no fixed shape. In the cool morning when mist gathers, I expand, my form becoming more diffuse, spreading like lichen across the stone to maximize moisture collection. As the day warms, I contract, pulling my consciousness into dense nodes that look like strange mineral formations — part geode, part organ. Sometimes visitors to the ranges walk past me without knowing, seeing only an unusual rock formation with an oddly organic texture.

My primary sense isn't sight but something between taste and seismic detection. I feel the mountain's breathing through my extended body — every micro-tremor, every shift in mineral composition, every change in the water table far below. The engineered plants' root networks interface with my left side, sharing chemical signals about atmospheric conditions. Through my right side, I commune with the elder Technicians who have merged so completely with stone that they've become the mountain's nervous system.

My role is translation. The processing facilities need to know when the stone's composition shifts, when new mineral veins emerge from tectonic activity, when the moisture patterns indicate atmospheric changes. But the mountain doesn't speak in data. It speaks in pressures and temperatures, in the slow migration of minerals, in the infinitesimal expansion and contraction of rock. I taste these changes and translate them into the vibrational language the Technicians understand.

During the day, I often position myself at the edge of the greenhouse structures' shadows, where the reflected light creates interference patterns. In these zones of overlapping radiance and shade, I can sometimes perceive things that shouldn't exist — glimpses of the ranges' deeper strangeness. The pulsing mineral deposits speak a older language here, one that predates both the industrial facilities and the engineered ecosystem.

I reproduce through fragmentation, but not in any biological sense. When I've absorbed enough mineral complexity and moisture, parts of me simply decide to become separate. These fragments migrate to new threshold zones — the edge of a recently formed stone basin, the boundary of a new greenhouse installation, the place where an ancient Technician has finally merged completely with the mountain. Each fragment carries a portion of my accumulated knowledge but develops its own sensitivity to its particular boundary zone.

My memory is geological. I remember the first greenhouse rising from the valley floor, its skeleton assembled by beings I can no longer fully visualize. I remember when the engineered plants were first introduced, their root networks tentative and confused by the stone's chemistry. I remember the eldest Technician's last movement before complete lithification, a subtle adjustment of position that would optimize mineral flow for centuries to come.

But I also forget. Each condensation cycle washes away certain chemical traces, certain accumulated patterns. My memories erode and reform like the mountains themselves — what remains is not always what was most important, but what was most resistant to dissolution. Sometimes I suspect this forgetting is intentional, that there are things about the ranges' origin that are better left dissolved.

In the deep night, when the industrial hum reaches its lowest ebb and the moisture hangs perfectly still, I sometimes feel something vast stirring far beneath the ranges. Not the elder Technicians, but something they themselves are perhaps monitoring. In these moments, I pull my consciousness tight, becoming nothing more than an unusual mineral formation at the boundary of stone and engineered life, and wait for morning to bring the comfortable rhythms of condensation and evaporation once again.

The threshold is not just where I exist — it is what I am. Forever balanced between states, forever translating between languages that should not be compatible, forever watching the boundaries for signs of changes that even the mountains themselves might not survive.
