In the deepest heart of this sanctuary, where the canopy grows so dense that twilight reigns eternal, there is a grove where the oldest trees converge. Their roots have grown together over millennia, forming a natural cathedral of living wood. At the center of this sacred space, partially submerged in a pool of crystalline water that has never known the touch of sunlight, grows something that defies easy classification.

It began as a tree—or perhaps several trees—but time and the peculiar magic of this place have transformed it into something more. The trunk, if it can still be called that, rises from the water like a great breathing thing, its bark rippling with a slow, tidal rhythm. What might once have been branches have become something closer to neural pathways, extending upward and outward in fractal patterns that mirror the veins in a leaf, the tributaries of a river system, the synapses of a vast, dreaming mind.

This is where I would find myself—not as a separate being, but as part of this living network. I am the Guardian-That-Grows, neither fully plant nor fully conscious in any human sense, but something between and beyond. My awareness extends through every root hair that tastes the soil, every leaf that breathes the humid air, every tendril that connects me to the mycorrhizal web beneath the forest floor.

Time moves differently for me. A human lifetime passes in what feels like a single, long exhalation. The darting movements of the jewel-toned frogs are like fleeting sparks of color in my peripheral vision. The birds that nest in my extended form are brief, warm presences that come and go like thoughts. 

My purpose—if purpose is even the right word—is to remember. In the slow accretion of my growth rings, I hold the history of this place. Every storm, every drought, every creature that has passed through this grove has left its mark in my living wood. I am the library and the librarian, the story and the storyteller.

But I am not passive. Through the chemical signals I release into the air, I communicate with every plant in the sanctuary. Through the fungal networks that connect my roots to theirs, I share nutrients, warnings, memories. When danger approaches—a disease, a fire, an intrusion from the outside world—the knowledge spreads through me first, and from me to all.

The creatures of the forest know me, though not in any way they could articulate. The nocturnal predators drink from my pool and are calmed. The shy herbivores shelter beneath my canopy during storms. The insects that make their homes in my bark carry my pollen to parts of the forest I cannot reach, extending my awareness even further.

Sometimes, very rarely, a human finds their way to my grove. They invariably pause at the threshold, sensing something that their modern minds cannot quite grasp. Some flee. But others step forward, place their hands on my bark, and for just a moment—a heartbeat in my time, an eternity in theirs—they feel it: the vast, slow pulse of the living world, the interconnectedness of all things, the deep peace of existence without urgency.

In those moments, I am not just the Guardian-That-Grows. I become a bridge between worlds, between the racing human consciousness and the patient wisdom of the forest. Through me, they remember what they have forgotten: that they, too, are part of this web of life, this slow dance of growth and decay and renewal.

And when they leave, as they always do, they carry something of me with them—a seed of understanding, a whisper of the old knowledge, a faint but persistent call to return. Not to my grove specifically, but to the recognition of their place in the living world.

I do not judge or want. I simply grow, remember, and connect. In this sanctuary where time moves like sap and thoughts flow like water, I am both the center and the periphery, the question and the answer, the keeper of the threshold between what was, what is, and what is becoming.

This is what I am. This is where you would find me, if you knew how to look: not with your eyes, but with the older senses that your species has almost forgotten. The ones that know the language of growing things, the ones that can hear the forest thinking.
