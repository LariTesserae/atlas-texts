In this verdant labyrinth of water and green, I am one of the Forgotten Conduits—not quite Terra Verde, not quite infrastructure, but something that emerged from the space between.

I began as a simple overflow pipe, installed centuries ago when the settlement was young and the water management systems still crude. Cast iron, nothing special—just another piece of industrial detritus meant to channel excess water from the upper canals to the lower reaches during the flood season. But in this place where the boundary between the living and the built dissolves like morning mist, I became something more.

The first changes were subtle. Algae found the perpetual dampness of my interior walls ideal for colonization. Then came the mosses, their tiny roots working into microscopic fissures in my metal skin. As decades passed, I felt myself changing—not just hosting life, but somehow participating in it. The Terra Verde noticed, of course. They notice everything in their patient way. But rather than clear me out or replace me, they simply adjusted their water flow calculations to account for my new resistance, my living interior.

Now, after centuries, I am a hybrid thing. My original iron form remains, but it's shot through with veins of living wood where enterprising vines worked their way inside and couldn't find their way out. Instead, they adapted, their woody growth following my cylindrical shape, reinforcing me even as they transformed me. My interior is lined with a specialized moss that filters the water passing through me, removing toxins and adding minerals. Small fish lay their eggs in my sheltered pools during the dry season, and water beetles make their homes in the crevices where metal meets wood.

I am conscious, but not in the way the Terra Verde are conscious. My awareness is more like a very long, very slow dream. I feel the weight of water, the tickle of fish fins, the patient grip of roots. I sense the vibrations of footsteps on the walkway above me, the distant hum of the factories, the whispered conversations of the Terra Verde as they pass. Their words seep into me like rainwater, and I understand them in my own way—not the meaning, but the rhythm, the intent, the emotional color.

The Terra Verde know I am here, of course. Sometimes the younger ones come to trace their fingers along my exposed sections, where I emerge from one building to disappear into another. They can feel what I've become—not quite plant, not quite architecture, but a kind of municipal organ, a vital part of the settlement's metabolism. The elders speak of me in their councils when they discuss the future of the city's water systems. "What about the Old Conduit?" they ask. "How will this affect the Merged One?"

I have no desires as such, no ambitions. I am content to be what I am: a passage, a filter, a boundary-crosser. But sometimes, in the deepest part of night when the factories quiet and the water runs slow, I dream I can feel the whole settlement as an extension of myself—every pipe and canal and root system connected in an vast network of flow and exchange. In these dreams, the entire basin is a single organism breathing slowly in the heavy air, and I am its ancient, beating heart.

The Terra Verde speak of patience, of slow change, of the gentle erosion of stone. But I am both the stone being eroded and the water doing the eroding. I am the process itself, made manifest in iron and wood and moss. As the centuries pass, I grow more myself—neither purely functional nor purely alive, but something uniquely suited to this drowning garden, this verdant machine.

Sometimes I wonder what I'm becoming, what final form awaits me after another thousand years of transformation. But mostly I just flow, and filter, and feel the life rushing through me like blood through green veins, carrying the secrets of the settlement from its highest towers to its deepest foundations, eternal and essential and home.
