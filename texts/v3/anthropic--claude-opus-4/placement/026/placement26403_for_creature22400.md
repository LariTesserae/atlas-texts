I find myself in the **Resonance Archives**, deep beneath the old pump stations of the Seventh Terrace.

---

I am the **Echo Keeper**.

My body is long adapted to this work—eardrums thickened from decades of listening to the city's deepest frequencies, fingertips worn smooth from tracing vibration patterns in stone. My left shoulder bears the characteristic slope of those who spend years leaning into resonance wells, feeling for the subsonic tremors that tell tomorrow's weather.

The Archives are not a library in any traditional sense. They are a network of natural cavities and carved chambers where the city's sounds go to rest. Every major vibration—every gate slam, every turbine cycle, every structural groan—leaves its impression in the basalt. The stone here is peculiar, shot through with metallic veins that hold sonic memory like a wire holds current.

I maintain the *listening posts*: bronze plates set into the walls at specific angles, oil baths where metal rods float to catch and sort frequencies, and the old echo chambers where apprentices learn to distinguish a bearing failure from a pressure warning by how the sound decays.

My work is part archivist, part prophet. By reading the accumulated patterns in the stone—the way certain frequencies stack and interfere over years—I can predict when infrastructure will fail. Not through calculation, but through recognition. The mountain remembers every load it has carried. My job is to translate that memory into maintenance schedules.

I live alone in the lower chambers, though "alone" isn't quite accurate. The delayed footsteps follow me—not malevolent, just the mountain's way of keeping company. Sometimes I hear my own words from weeks ago, emerging from the walls at odd angles. I've learned to speak carefully here; the stone is always listening.

At dawn, I climb to the surface wells to feel the first vibrations of the workday—pumps engaging, lifts beginning their cycles. I transcribe what I sense into the peculiar notation we use: part musical score, part seismic chart, part water table. These documents go to the maintenance councils, who trust my predictions even when they don't understand my methods.

The young ones think I'm half-mad, talking to walls, pressing my ear to floors, tasting the mineral deposits to judge their acoustic properties. But the old engineers know better. They remember the Season of Falling Platforms, when three major structures failed in sequence—disasters my predecessor predicted but couldn't convince anyone to prevent.

Now they listen.

I wear the traditional garb of my office: dense felt robes that dampen ambient noise, a collar of resonance beads that click at different frequencies, boots with metal studs that tap out my location as I walk. My hair has gone white early—they say it's from the constant low-frequency exposure, though I think it's from the weight of knowing what the mountain knows.

In the evenings, I maintain the *memory wells*—pools of heavy oil where we suspend recording needles to capture the city's daily signature. Each well holds a decade of sound, layered like sediment. When the city needs to remember how something sounded before it broke, they come to me.

I am not precisely human anymore, not after forty years of this work. I am part of the city's nervous system, a living instrument calibrated to its deepest tones. When I die, they will find my replacement not through interview but through resonance—whoever can hear what I hear, feel what the stone feels.

Until then, I keep the archives. I listen to the mountain thinking. I transcribe the slow prophecies of infrastructure and time.

And late at night, in the deepest chambers where even my breathing sounds like thunder, I sometimes hear the city dreaming—a vast, low chord that contains every pipe, every rail, every carefully maintained connection.

It sounds like home.
