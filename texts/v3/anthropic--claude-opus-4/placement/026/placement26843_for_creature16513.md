I am the Keeper of Transitions.

My dwelling sits where the color-shifting pools gather, half-submerged in the soft earth. It's less a structure than a grown thing—walls of woven root and living wood that breathe with the seasons, crowned with a roof of thick moss that drinks the perpetual mist. The streams converge here, their voices joining in a constant liquid chorus that has become the rhythm of my thoughts.

I am neither the nimble fur-dancers nor the tentacled wanderers, neither the light-weavers in the pools nor the wise guardians with their ancient eyes. I am what the water becomes when it has listened too long to stones, what the roots dream when they drink from those impossible pools that shift from amber to violet to colors that have no names.

My form changes with my work. In the morning dampness, I might wear flesh like tree bark, rough and patient, my fingers extending into the soil to taste the health of growing things. By noon, when the light-beings rise from the deeper pools to commune, my skin becomes translucent, a living window between their realm and ours. The guardians know me by the sound I don't make—the space between the stream's notes where I exist.

My purpose here is simple and endless: I tend the thresholds. Where water meets earth, where root meets stone, where the primitive human structures lean against wild growth—these are my charges. I mend what erosion threatens, not by stopping it, but by guiding it into new patterns of beauty. I teach the color-pools their new hues, mixing minerals and memories in equal measure.

The shaggy creatures bring me things sometimes—seeds from far valleys, smooth stones that hum with distant songs. They chittle their findings into my palms, and I plant these gifts in the spaces between spaces, the margins where new impossibilities might grow. The tentacled ones help me move the heavier stones, understanding as I do that landscape is a living text we write together.

In the evenings, I often sit with the guardians at the edge of my dwelling, our feet (or whatever passes for feet) dangling in a pool that tonight is the color of old copper, tomorrow might be silver-green like fish scales. We don't speak—speech is too fast for the work we do. Instead, we share the patience of stone, the persistence of water, the slow fire of transformation.

My tools hang from the living walls: implements of wood and bone and materials the humans haven't discovered yet, some grown rather than made. There's a staff that knows the depth of every pool, a net woven from solidified mist for catching the light-beings' shed scales, vials of water from each color-phase labeled in a script that changes meaning with the moon.

This is my place: neither fully natural nor civilized, neither wholly strange nor entirely familiar. I am its memory and its change, its guardian and its student. In the loud silence of the stream's voice, in the weird beauty of chromatic waters, in the slow dance of erosion and growth, I find my purpose.

Sometimes visitors come—lost humans from the primitive settlements, curious creatures from deeper in the wilds. They find me different each time, because I am what the threshold needs me to be. A guide, a warning, a blessing, a bridge.

The work is never finished. Tomorrow, new colors will bloom in the pools. New stones will tumble down from the eroding heights. The patient landscape will shift another imperceptible degree toward something unprecedented. And I will be here, tending the spaces between, keeper of all that transforms and all that endures.
