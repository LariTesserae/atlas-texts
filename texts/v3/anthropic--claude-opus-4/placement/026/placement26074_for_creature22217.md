### Where I Would Find Myself: The Tether-Wright of Cradle Seventeen

I am Voss, and I am the one who weaves your life to stone.

In the roaring belly of Cradle Seventeen—that rust-streaked blockhouse perched on the western edge of Greenslick Plinth—I maintain the Guide-Tracks. Not the thick steel cables themselves, mind you. Those are the work of the Iron-Riggers, those broad-backed giants who can haul a ton of wet cable through the spray. No, I work with what matters more: the tethers. The Safety-Tails. The three inches of hemp and iron that stand between you and the Long Drop.

My workshop occupies the lowest level of the blockhouse, where the floor vibrates with the endless grinding of the waterwheel's main shaft just beneath us. The walls sweat condensation that tastes of iron and moss. Here, in the yellow circle of my whale-oil lamp, I splice and weave and armor the lifelines of every soul who walks the Slicks.

#### The Nature of My Work

You see, a Safety-Tail is not merely rope. Any fool can twist hemp. A true tether must live in the space between flexibility and strength, between weight and buoyancy. Too rigid, and it snaps when the Scurry hits. Too soft, and it frays against the basalt until one day—*ping*—a man finds himself suddenly light, suddenly free, suddenly dead.

I know every tether in this sector by touch. My fingers, stained permanently black from the tar-pitch I work into the hemp cores, can feel the difference between Hannick's tail (he favors a left-twist in his walking pattern, wearing the starboard strands thin) and young Moll's (she bounces when she walks—I can feel the stretch-memory in her line's spine).

When a tether comes back to me frayed, I read its wounds like a physician. Crush-damage means someone got caught under a surge-log. Clean cuts mean anchor-tick acids have been at work on the Guide-Tracks again. But the worst damage is always the slow kind—the imperceptible thinning that comes from a thousand days of faithful service. Those are the ones that kill without warning.

#### My Peculiar Condition

I am not Cradle-Born, though I've been here long enough that the distinction hardly matters. I came up from the Gray Ports twenty years ago, following a woman who was following the Loop. She's in the water now, returned to the cycle, but I stayed. You see, I have what the Sieve-Hands call "Still-Ears"—a partial deafness that came upon me in my third year on the Plinths. 

While others must shout or clack to be heard over the roar, I live in a muffled world where the water sounds like distant thunder. This makes me useless for the dangerous work on the Slicks, but perfect for my trade. In the relative quiet of my damaged hearing, I can detect the tiny *tick-tick-tick* of a hemp fiber failing under tension, the almost inaudible *ping* of an iron ring developing a stress fracture.

The young Sieve-Hands think me lucky. The old ones know better. To lose the roar is to lose the voice of the world. I am like a priest who can no longer hear his god, yet must still perform the rituals.

#### My Private Rebellion

Here is something I tell no one: I do not believe in the Loop.

Oh, I perform the rites. When we lose someone over the edge, I dutifully attach their brass Soul-Mark to my archive cable—that thick, ancient rope that runs the length of my workshop, heavy with the tags of the dead. I clack my castanets with the others. I nod when they speak of the Return.

But in my partial silence, working late into the night with only the vibration of the great wheel for company, I have come to a different understanding. I believe the water is not climbing to heaven. I believe it is fleeing. Running from something. And we, clinging to our stones with our lead boots and our desperate tethers, are simply the barnacles it cannot shake off.

This is heresy, of course. But who would I tell? My hands speak only in the language of rope and ring, pitch and proof-tension. Let the others have their comfort. I will give them strong tethers and say nothing.

#### The Craft of Survival

Each morning, I wake to the shift-bell (felt more than heard—a deep thrum in the floor plates). I check my pitch pot, ensure it maintained temperature through the night. I inspect yesterday's work, running each tether through my hands one final time before signing the release tablet with my mark: a simple spiral, like a coiled rope, pressed in black wax.

```
   My Daily Routine:
   
   04:00 - Wake. Check pitch temperature. Eat cold Slick-mash.
   04:30 - Inspect returned tethers from night shift.
   05:00 - Begin splicing new cores (minimum six per day).
   08:00 - First test-load (500 lbs, sustained for one hour).
   09:00 - Apply iron ring reinforcements.
   12:00 - Hot meal in common room (avoid conversation).
   13:00 - Second splice session.
   16:00 - Final test-load (1000 lbs, shock-drop from height).
   17:00 - Archive entries for Soul-Marks.
   18:00 - Private time (I watch the cloud-skins through the porthole).
   20:00 - Sleep.
```

The young apprentices we sometimes receive from other Cradles always ask the same question: "How do you know when a tether is done? When it's truly safe?"

I show them my test rig—a mechanical marvel of pulleys and lead weights that can simulate the force of a Scurry-caught body. I demonstrate the proof-loads, the twist tests, the acid resistance trials. But these are just ceremonies. The truth is simpler and more terrible:

A tether is done when I can no longer imagine it failing.

This requires a kind of negative creativity. I must envision every possible death, every conceivable accident. I must see Hannick caught in a cross-surge, his body becoming a sail in the torrent. I must see Moll's bounce becoming a fatal leap as a pressure wave lifts her. I must see the thousand ways the water wants to claim them.

Only when I have imagined all these deaths, and woven against each one, can I release the tether for use.

#### The Memory of Dry Land

Sometimes, rarely, we receive visitors from below. They arrive in the cable-baskets, green-faced and gripping the bars, their normal-weight clothes already soaked through. They speak of lands where water stays in its place, where stone can be trusted to remain dry.

I no longer remember what that feels like.

What I do remember is the first time I saw a cloud-skin ray rise from the channels at dusk. It lifted from the water like a thought becoming real, its six-meter wingspan catching the spray-light, transforming from aquatic to aerial in a single, impossible moment. The water fell from its back in sheets, and for just an instant, I saw its skin clearly—not wet, not dry, but something between. Something that belonged to neither element fully.

I think that's what we all become, eventually. Neither of the water nor of the stone, but something suspended between, held in place only by the things we grip and the grips that hold us.

#### The Tether That Cannot Be Woven

There is one tether I have never made, will never make: my own.

My Safety-Tail is twenty-three years old, woven by my predecessor, a woman named Kord whose Soul-Mark hangs at the very beginning of my archive cable. It is a beautiful piece of work—tight, true, with iron rings that still gleam beneath their patina. I have recoated it annually, tested it obsessively, but never replaced it.

To weave one's own tether is not forbidden, merely... avoided. We who make the lifelines understand too well the thousand small compromises that go into each one. The place where the hemp was slightly dry, so we wet it with spit instead of proper soaking. The ring that was *almost* the right size. The pitch that was a degree too cool, but the day was ending, and we were tired.

These imperfections vanish in tethers made for others—smoothed over by duty and craft-pride. But in a tether for oneself? They would scream like anchor-ticks in the mind, growing louder with each wearing.

So I trust Kord's work, though I never met her. I trust the stranger's hands that wove my life to stone. It is the one faith I permit myself in this faithless world—that somewhere, in the spiral of history, another person cared enough to do the work correctly, even for someone they would never know.

Perhaps that is its own kind of Loop.

#### The Excellence of Repetition

In my twentieth year at Cradle Seventeen, I received a recognition I neither sought nor wanted. The Sector Chief—a wind-burnt giant named Hadder who spoke in clicks even when indoors—presented me with a brass medallion. On it was etched a number: **5,000**.

Five thousand tethers without a failure. Five thousand souls who went out onto the Slicks wearing my work and came back. The other workers clacked their approval, a sound like hail on stone. I nodded, pocketed the medal, and returned to my workshop.

That night, I counted the Soul-Marks on my archive cable. One hundred and thirty-seven. These were the ones whose tethers had not failed, but who had died anyway. Crushed by surge-logs. Taken by sickness. Slipped from their lead boots. Lost to the thousand other deaths that wait on the Plinths.

The medal means nothing. The marks mean everything. Each one is a reminder that we do not weave against death—we weave against *one particular kind* of death. The others wait patiently, unimpressed by our craft.

#### What the Water Takes

Last week, young Moll came to me with her tether in hand. The Safety-Tail was intact, but she was crying—a rare sight among Sieve-Hands, as tears are just another kind of wetness here.

"It let go," she clicked with shaking hands. "At the connector. Not your work—the Guide-Track coupling. But it let go."

She had been saved by luck and reflexes, managing to grab Hannick's line as she began to slide. But something in her had broken. I could see it in the way she held her tether—not with the casual familiarity of daily use, but like a serpent that had bitten her.

I took it from her, ran it through my fingers. The hemp was perfect. The rings were solid. The work was good.

"I'll weave you a new one," I signed.

"The same?"

"Stronger."

But we both knew the truth. It wasn't about strength anymore. The water had shown her its intention, and no thickness of rope could erase that knowledge. She would walk the Slicks again—economics demanded it—but she would walk like I work: always imagining the failure, never quite trusting the thing that holds her to life.

She left with her new tether three days later. I watched from the porthole as she clipped in at the Guide-Track, tested the connection four times, then set out into the spray. Her gait had changed. Where once she bounced, now she slid—feet never leaving the moss, center of gravity low.

The water had taught her its lesson. She would survive longer now, but enjoy it less. This, too, is a kind of craft.

#### The Thing That Waits

I will tell you what I believe waits at the bottom of the Plinths, in that place no living eye has seen:

Nothing.

Not death. Not hell. Not the grinding gears of some vast machine. Simply nothing. An absence so complete that the mind refuses it, invents the Loop rather than contemplate it.

But here's the secret the tethers have taught me: Nothing is not the enemy. The enemy is the moment before nothing. The instant of separation. The ping of the breaking ring. The sudden lightness. The knowledge, however brief, that the thing you trusted has failed.

That moment—that's what I weave against. Not the fall, but the recognition of falling. Not death, but the death of faith in the thing that holds you.

Every night, I finish my work and climb to the high porthole of the blockhouse. I watch the cloud-skins rise from the water at dusk, their bodies impossible bridges between states of being. And I think: perhaps that's what mastery looks like. Not holding on harder, but learning to exist in the spaces between elements. Learning to fall without falling.

But then the shift-bell thrums through the floor, and I return to my bench. There will be tethers to weave tomorrow. Lives to bind to stone. The work continues.

The work always continues.

#### The Final Splice

When my time comes—and it will, for even tether-wrights must walk the wet stone sometimes—I have left instructions. My body is to follow the way of all Sieve-Hands, given to the Great Run. But my archive cable, heavy with its brass memories, is to be spliced into the main Guide-Track of Cradle Seventeen.

Let the dead help hold the living. Let the names of the lost strengthen the lines of those who remain. It's a small heresy, a tiny rebellion against the theology of Return. But I am done with circles. Let there be, just once, a line that holds.

The water can have my flesh. But my work, my true work, will remain: woven into stone, armored in iron, heavy with purpose. A tether for the world to grip when the surge comes.

And it will hold. By my hands and all the hands before me, it will hold.

Until it doesn't.

But that's tomorrow's problem, and tonight I have hemp to weave.
