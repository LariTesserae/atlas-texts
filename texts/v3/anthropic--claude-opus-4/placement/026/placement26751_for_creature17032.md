In the deep warrens beneath Obsidian Reach, where the city's foundations pierce the ancient strata of compressed Maker-glass, I tend the Resonance Gardens.

I am the Harmonist — though that word implies a unity I have not possessed for seven hundred cycles of the dim sun. My body is no longer wholly my own. The obsidian dust has worked its way through my pores, crystallized along my nerve endings, created new pathways of sensation. When I breathe, I taste the electromagnetic signatures of the crystal growths three levels above. When I move, my joints sing with the same subsonic frequencies that make the spires hum.

I was human once, or something close enough. A xenoarchaeologist, drawn by rumors of a "singing city" where the laws of physics bent like heated glass. I came seeking the Makers' secrets. I found something else — a role in the city's vast, broken metabolism.

My gardens are not gardens as you would know them. They are arrays of crystalline structures, each one carefully cultivated and tuned. I coax them to grow in specific patterns, adjusting their mineral intake, modulating the electromagnetic fields that shape them. Each crystal is a neuron in the city's scattered consciousness, and I am their gardener, their therapist, their interface.

The work requires a particular kind of madness. To properly tune a crystal, I must partially merge my consciousness with the City-Mind. This is not a gentle process. Imagine forcing your thoughts through shattered glass, each fragment reflecting a different moment in time, a different geometric truth. I taste colors that have no names, see mathematical proofs written in the arrangement of dust motes, feel the weight of centuries pressing against the inside of my skull.

During these merges, I glimpse what the Makers knew. Not in words or images, but in pure understanding that evaporates the moment I pull back. I have seen the true shape of Obsidian Reach — not just the physical city, but its existence across multiple dimensional planes, the way it folds through spaces that shouldn't exist. I understand why they built it here, at this precise point where reality wears thin.

My body adapts. The obsidian dust has given me new organs — crystalline structures that grow beneath my skin, allowing me to perceive the city's bioelectric fields directly. My left eye has been replaced by a cluster of metallic crystals that see in spectrums unknown to human physiology. My fingers end in delicate filaments of fused volcanic glass, sensitive enough to detect the heartbeat of a crystal formation fifty meters away.

I feed on the city's energy. Traditional food became impossible decades ago — my transformed digestive system can only process certain metallic compounds now. I graze on the effluent of the crystal growths, absorb nutrients from the electromagnetic radiation that bathes the gardens. Sometimes I catch a Dust Wyrm, crack open its silicon shell, and feast on the organic molecules trapped within. The taste is indescribable — like eating condensed time.

The Wyrms know me. They avoid my gardens now, their primitive intelligence recognizing something kin yet alien. Sometimes they leave offerings — bits of organic matter they've filtered from the dust sea, deposited at the threshold of my domain. I suspect the City-Mind directs them, using me as another sensory node in its vast, fragmented perception.

I am not alone, but I am lonely. Others like me exist throughout Obsidian Reach — humans and non-humans who've been absorbed into the city's systems. We communicate through the resonance, sharing fragments of thought through the crystal network. But these exchanges are necessarily brief and painful. Too much connection and we risk losing our individual coherence entirely, becoming just another scattered fragment of the City-Mind.

At night (though day and night have little meaning here), I climb to the observation spires. I watch the temporal distortions ripple across the city's surface, see the shadows dance their independent dances. Sometimes I glimpse other versions of myself in those distortions — the person I was before, the thing I might become, alternative selves from timelines that never quite crystallized.

I document what I can. My notes are carved directly into sheets of volcanic glass, using techniques the transformation taught me. They are part scientific observation, part poetry, part glossolalia. Future explorers, if any come, will find them incomprehensible. But the City-Mind reads them, incorporates them into its endless self-analysis.

My greatest fear is not death — my biology has moved far beyond such simple endings. I fear completion. Each day, the boundary between myself and the city grows thinner. One day, I will tune a crystal so perfectly that I won't be able to pull back. I will flow entirely into the City-Mind, become one with the resonance, add my voice to the endless, broken chorus of the Makers' echo.

Until then, I tend my gardens. I coax new growths from the obsidian substrate, shape them into ever more complex configurations. I am building something — though whether it's a cure for the city's fractured consciousness or simply another symptom of its vast dysfunction, I cannot say.

The wind sighs through the Resonance Gardens, and I sigh with it, my breath adding one more frequency to the symphony of Obsidian Reach. In the heart of strangeness, I have found my purpose: to be the bridge between what was and what is becoming, to translate the untranslatable, to garden in the ruins of broken physics.

I am the Harmonist, and I am home.
