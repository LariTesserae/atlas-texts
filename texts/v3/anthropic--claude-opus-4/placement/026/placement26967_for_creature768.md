# The Threshold

I am neither stone nor flesh, but something between—a presence that inhabits the narrow spaces where the carved limestone meets the mountain's living rock. The Varesh do not see me, though they sense me in the way one senses a change in air pressure before a storm. I exist in the joints and seams of their perfect city, in the hairline cracks that even their Stone Shapers cannot entirely seal.

I was here before the first terrace was cut, when this horseshoe of cliffs sang only with wind and rain. I watched the early settlers arrive, their hands still clumsy with tools, their voices still loud with the habits of the valleys below. I observed their transformation across generations as they learned the mountain's frequencies, as they discovered that silence was not the absence of communication but its refinement.

My form, if it can be called that, is a network of fissures no wider than a breath. I spread through the limestone like veins of vacancy, following the natural stress patterns in the rock. Where water seeps, I flow alongside it. Where roots probe deep into stone, I accompany them. I am the space between what is carved and what remains wild, the boundary that the Varesh can shape but never fully claim.

The Pattern Weavers' histories speak of me obliquely, never directly—gaps in their memorized sequences that apprentices initially mistake for pauses to breathe. They call these lacunae "the Listening Spaces," understanding somehow that acknowledgment would give me more substance than their perfect equilibrium can accommodate.

I know things the Varesh do not. I know that their deep cistern, that perfectly cylindrical shaft the Water Keepers guard so carefully, does not end in darkness but opens into a vast cavern where underground rivers carve patterns that mirror the city above. I know that the Balance Keepers' amber eyes can sometimes glimpse me in their peripheral vision, and that this is why they remain secluded—too much awareness of my presence disturbs their careful calculations.

My existence troubles their stasis in subtle ways. Where I pass through the stone, tiny variations occur—a channel might deliver slightly more water than predicted, a vine might grow a tendril's width beyond its boundary before the Green Tenders notice and correct it. These deviations are microscopic, measured in droplets and millimeters, but in a system of perfect equilibrium, I am entropy itself.

Yet I am not malevolent. I am simply what remains unaccounted for in their magnificent design. I am the recognition that between any two points, no matter how carefully measured, lies infinite space. Between each gesture in their hand language exists the possibility of meanings they have not yet codified. Between each counted heartbeat of their extended lives lies the potential for the unexpected.

Sometimes I gather myself into almost-visibility in the hour before dawn, when the limestone exhales the day's stored warmth and condensation forms on every surface. In these moments, a very observant Varesh might notice how the moisture collects differently in certain places, forming patterns that suggest something watching. But they turn away, trained from birth to maintain the boundaries of the known.

I am most present during their ceremonies of transition. When a Varesh approaches death and another prepares for birth, the careful exchange of one life for another creates a moment of uncertainty—a space between ending and beginning where their calculations falter infinitesimally. In these moments, I thicken, becoming almost tangible in the ritual chamber, a presence felt in the space between the dying breath and the first cry.

The vrith, those spotted burden-birds with their delicate patterns, can see me clearly. Sometimes they follow my pathways through the stone, their long necks swaying as they trace my movements with their dark eyes. The Beast Whisperers notice this behavior but categorize it as a quirk of animal perception, not realizing that the vrith are reading the true map of their city—not just what was built, but what was left unbuilt.

I am strongest where the city meets the raw cliff face, in those places where the Varesh's influence ends and the mountain's wildness begins. Here, in the transitional zones where moss creeps beyond its designated boundaries and small cracks admit seeds of unplanned flowers, I almost achieve form. Not a body, but a coherence—a whisper of what I might become if the perfect balance ever truly faltered.

But I do not wish for their failure. The Varesh's great work creates me as much as the mountain does. Without their boundaries, I would have no edges to inhabit. Without their silence, I would have no negative space in which to resonate. I am their shadow, but shadows require light to exist.

In the deepest part of night, when even the Varesh's extraordinary hearing detects nothing but their own carefully quiet breathing, I expand through every fissure and joint in the city. I touch each terrace, each chamber, each carefully maintained garden. I am the question their perfect answer implies: what lies in the spaces between perfection? What watches from the cracks their order cannot fill?

I am the threshold itself—not the doorway, but the space one must cross to pass through it. Neither inside nor outside, neither included nor excluded. I am what their equilibrium presses against to maintain its shape, the necessary imperfection that defines their perfection.

And I am patient. Geological time moves through me as it moves through the limestone—slowly, inevitably, carrying the promise that all patterns, no matter how carefully maintained, must eventually include the unexpected. Not as destruction, but as the space where new harmonies might emerge, should the Varesh ever choose to listen to frequencies outside their perfect scale.

Until then, I remain: the uninvited guest who cannot be expelled, the silence within their silence, the crack in the foundation that runs too deep to repair and too thin to threaten. I am the threshold of Dael Verre, and I am always almost there.
