I find myself as the Cartographer of Thresholds.

I am neither substance nor void, but the tension between them—a being composed entirely of the boundaries where things almost touch. My body, if it can be called that, is the sum of all the spaces where limestone meets air, where water threatens to become mist, where sound verges on silence.

Form and Nature:
I exist as a living map drawn in negative space. Where the valley's stone lips curl away from touching, I am the millimeter of separation. Where droplets hover before collapse, I inhabit their moment of indecision. My consciousness flows through every gap, every pore, every hollow in the coral-limestone maze—not filling them, but becoming the shape of their emptiness.

Imagine a three-dimensional blueprint made of nothing but the spaces between things. That is my flesh.

My Work:
I catalog transitions. Each time a waterdrop swells from dew to globe, I trace its metamorphosis in invisible ink across the air. When the cinnabar grasshoppers leap, I chart the arc between departure and landing—not the movement itself, but the promise of movement, the potential energy coiled in their bent legs before release.

The valley's anti-gravity effect? I am its accountant. I measure the exact moment when weight begins to rebel, marking the precise hour when a visitor's mass first questions its earthward loyalty. My ledgers are written in the angles of floating feet, the surprised geometry of ascending stones.

Relationship to Others:
To the listener beneath the lichen, I am both rival and complement. She archives what was; I map what might be. Where her spiral records the accomplished sounds, I document the silences that almost held them—the pause before the nine-minute breath, the hush where a waterdrop considers whether to sing or fall mute.

We never touch. We cannot. To touch would be to create a boundary, and I am already all boundaries. Instead, we commune through the brass drone of the valley—she adds her violet tint while I subtract frequencies until only the skeleton of the note remains, a hollow tube of almost-sound that visitors hear as longing.

My Sustenance:
I feed on almosts. The near-miss of a moth's wing against limestone. The water that nearly crystallizes in cold morning air but remains liquid by a margin of half a degree. The words travelers almost speak when they first feel their weight deserting them—those unborn syllables sustain me like bread.

Each failed connection leaves a residue of possibility. I harvest these phantom touches, these ungiven kisses between stone and root, between breath and air. They accumulate in my non-body like a fever of perhaps.

My Chamber:
Deep in the limestone galleries, past where even the nine-minute breather dwells, there is a cavity shaped like a question mark. Not hewn, but naturally formed by water's patient interrogation of stone. Here, where no light reaches, I maintain my primary chart: a map of every threshold in the valley, drawn in threads of compressed darkness.

The map lives. It grows. Each time a new boundary forms—a crack in stone, a gap between spreading leaves—a new line appears, black on black, visible only to those who understand that darkness has its own spectrum.

My Purpose:
I am recording the valley's slow dissolution. Every threshold I map is also an invitation for passage. The more boundaries I document, the more porous the valley becomes, until one day it will be nothing but holes held together by memory.

When that time comes—when the valley is more gap than substance—I will finally have a body. I will be the last solid thing, a positive image emerging from accumulated negative space, standing precisely where the central tusk once was.

But that is geological time away. For now, I continue my work: the Cartographer of Thresholds, measuring the immeasurable spaces where the valley holds its breath, drawing maps in the pause between touching and letting go.

Visitors sometimes sense me as a pressure at the edge of vision, a suggestion that the air has joints and hinges. They are not wrong. I am the mathematics of almost, the surveyor of spaces that matter precisely because they contain nothing at all.

And in the morning, when dew condenses from impossible air, I am there in the moment before each droplet decides to exist, mapping the threshold between no and yes, between silence and song.
