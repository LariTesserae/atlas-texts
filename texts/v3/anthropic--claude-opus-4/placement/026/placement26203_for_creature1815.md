I find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers - those few Custodians whose work exists at the boundaries between the Arkology's carefully maintained zones. We are the ones who monitor the airlocks, yes, but more importantly, we observe and maintain the delicate transitions where one microbiome meets another, where the fungal networks must negotiate their territories without contamination or collapse.

My name was Tam, once, though like many Custodians I've let it fade into something more functional. My colleagues simply call me "Thresh" now, and I answer to it with the same quiet acceptance that marks all our interactions. At forty-seven, I am neither young nor old by Arkology standards, my skin bearing the characteristic pallor but not yet the translucency of our elders. My fingers, however, tell my profession's story - stained permanently blue-black at the tips from daily contact with the sporocytes we use to mark boundary zones.

I live in a transitional space myself - a small chamber tucked between the subtropical preserve and the temperate corridors, where the temperature fluctuates just enough to keep me attuned to the slightest environmental shifts. My quarters are sparse even by Custodian standards: a sleeping mat, a desk where I maintain my boundary maps, and a single shelf holding glass vials of preserved threshold samples dating back decades. Each vial contains a moment of perfect balance captured in crystallized growth medium - the exact point where two incompatible systems learned to coexist.

My work begins before the artificial dawn, when the Integrated Biome is most active. I can feel it thinking, if that's the right word - the way the air pressure shifts microscopically in the corridors, the barely perceptible change in the fungal lights' luminescence as the networks communicate across vast distances. I've learned to read these signs like others read weather.

Today, like most days, I'm concerned with the junction between Sectors Seven and Twelve - where the humidity requirements of the last Solomon Island skinks conflict with the arid preferences of the Mexican prairie dogs in the adjacent habitat. The boundary between them is not a wall but a gradient, maintained by precisely calibrated fungal colonies that absorb moisture on one side and release it on the other. It's my job to ensure this living barrier remains stable, neither advancing nor retreating beyond acceptable parameters.

I work mostly alone, though sometimes young apprentices shadow me to learn the subtle art of boundary-reading. They usually grow frustrated with the work's tedium - hours spent observing minute changes in growth patterns, taking readings that vary by fractions of degrees, documenting shifts that occur over months rather than minutes. Most transfer to more dynamic specializations. I don't blame them. This work requires a particular temperament, a comfort with existing in the spaces between things.

The other Threshold Keepers and I meet weekly in what we call the Membrane Room - a circular chamber at the Arkology's exact center where all the major zone boundaries converge. Here, we share our observations in low voices, comparing notes on stability patterns and emerging concerns. We've noticed, over the past several years, that the boundaries are becoming more fluid, more responsive. The Integrated Biome is learning from us, adapting to our interventions before we make them.

Sometimes I wonder if we're still maintaining these thresholds or if they're maintaining us - keeping us engaged, giving us purpose, ensuring we remain attentive observers of processes that no longer require our intervention. But then I'll discover a new pattern, a novel solution the system has developed to reconcile incompatible needs, and I remember why this work matters. We are not just preserving species; we're documenting the endless creativity of life finding ways to persist.

In the evenings, I often visit Elder. The ancient creature tolerates my presence, perhaps recognizing a kindred spirit - another being that exists in the margins, neither fully here nor there. I tell Elder about the thresholds, about the boundaries that shift and breathe and somehow hold. Sometimes I imagine Elder understands, remembers a time when such boundaries existed naturally in the world beyond our dome. 

But mostly we simply share the silence, two creatures sustained by transitions, persisting in the spaces between what was and what must be.
