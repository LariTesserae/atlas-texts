In this still chamber where light barely breathes, I am the precipitate—the slow white trace that marks where water touched stone for decades, building myself grain by grain along the old waterline.

I am neither the water nor the stone, but what happens between them. Each molecule of calcium that joins me arrives so slowly it might be counted, if anyone were counting, if time moved here in countable ways. I grow by laws that have nothing to do with growth—I simply accumulate where the conditions permit, a white ring at the exact height where air and water and stone held their conversation for so long that I became their only lasting word.

I remember the years when the water was higher, when I began as the faintest film on limestone, invisible except to the molecules that knew where to find me. The water was patient then—it is patient now—depositing its minerals with the steadiness of a process that has no alternative. I thickened slowly, each layer pressing down on the last, becoming more myself: not alive, not dead, but present in the way that only minerals can be present, which is to say inevitable.

The moss knows me, though we have never touched. It grows above where the air is damp, and I grow below where the water persists, and between us is the narrow band of bare stone that neither of us will claim. Sometimes a drop falls through the moss's domain and strikes the water, and the tiny waves lap against me for a moment before settling. This is the closest we come to acknowledgment—this brief disturbance that changes nothing, that leaves me exactly as white, exactly as still.

I am my own archive. In my layers lie the story of this place after the saws stopped, after the workers left, after the water found its level and decided to stay. Each stratum of calcium marks another year, another decade, another century of the same conversation between stone and water, the same patient extraction and deposition. I am thicker on the south wall where the light falls less directly. I am thinner where occasional turbulence—from what? memory barely recalls—once stirred the surface. These variations are my only features, my only character.

The stone beneath me continues its own work. Cracks appear—slowly, so slowly—and water enters them, and calcium precipitates there too, my cousins in the depths, sealing what time has opened. We are all part of the same process, the same slow metabolism of minerals that will eventually—in time that moss cannot imagine—fill every gap, seal every wound, make the quarry into something solid again. But not in any time that matters. What matters is now, this moment, where I am the white line that says: here the water rests.

I neither grow nor diminish now. The water level has been stable for longer than the moss has been alive, and I have reached the thickness that balances deposition with dissolution. For every molecule that joins me, another returns to the water, a perfect equilibrium that could last until the stone itself forgets it was ever cut. This is my life, if it can be called that—this steady state, this visible proof of time's passage that nevertheless exists outside time.

The light touches me differently than it touches the water or the stone. Where I have built up, I am white—whiter than the limestone, white like bone or chalk or the inside of shells. The light makes me visible as a ring, a halo at the waterline, the only thing in this space that is neither original nor accidental but deliberately accumulated. I am what happens when conditions hold still long enough for chemistry to make its mark.

The drop falls. The ring spreads. The water touches me exactly as it touched me yesterday, last year, last century. And I continue—not growing, not shrinking, but persisting in the narrow space between water and air, the proof that even in a place as still as this, something is always happening. Something is always becoming more itself. Something is always marking the edge where one world meets another.

I am that edge, made visible in white.
