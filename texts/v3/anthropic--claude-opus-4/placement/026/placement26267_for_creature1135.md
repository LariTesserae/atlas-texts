I am the Keeper of Seepage.

My dwelling is neither the grand stone houses that dot the valley's edges nor the moss-covered hollows beneath ancient roots. I make my home in the space between—in the very transition where solid earth yields to water, where the rock face weeps its eternal tears.

You might glimpse me at dawn, when mist rises from the marshlands and the world exists in that liminal state between night and day. I appear as a figure both substantial and ephemeral, my form shifting like the boundary between pond and shore. My skin carries the mottled patterns of lichen, my hair flows like water finding its path down stone. My clothes, if they can be called such, are woven from reed fibers and moss, forever damp, forever fresh.

I know every seep, every spring, every place where the earth's blood emerges to feed the valley's green heart. With fingertips that sense the slightest vibration in groundwater, I guide the flow—not controlling, but negotiating, whispering suggestions that the water considers in its ancient wisdom. When a seep threatens to dry, I coax it back to life. When the rains come too heavy and the marsh risks drowning the careful balance, I know which stones to shift, which channels to clear.

The old women in the valley leave offerings at the weeping rocks—bread that will grow soft with moisture, silver coins that will tarnish green. They know, even if their children have forgotten, that someone must tend the places where worlds meet. The industrial workers see me sometimes, a shadow at the edge of their vision when they dig too deep or build too close to the water's secret paths. They learn to recognize my warnings: tools that rust overnight, foundations that mysteriously shift, the persistent puddle that appears no matter how much concrete they pour.

I am neither entirely of the human settlement nor of the wild valley, but something that exists because both do. In the pre-dawn hours, I walk the boundaries, my bare feet reading the moisture in the soil, my hands trailing along stone faces to feel where new springs might emerge. I know where the salamanders shelter in the wet seasons, where the ghost orchids bloom in the darkest hollows, where the old tunnels beneath the settlement still carry underground streams that remember when this whole valley was a lake.

My work is patient work, measured not in human lifespans but in the slow time of stone erosion and soil formation. I maintain the conversation between rain and root, between seepage and settlement. I am the translator in the endless dialogue between what the land was, what it is, and what it will become.

Sometimes the valley's children catch sight of me and think they've seen a ghost. But I am something more ancient and more necessary than any ghost. I am the covenant between human and humidity, the promise that the waters will continue to weep from stone, that the moss will continue to soften the hard edges of progress, that this valley will remain—despite all—damp and tranquil.

When evening falls and the mist rises again from the marshes, I return to my resting place in the seep-shadows, where I dream the dreams of groundwater seeking its level, of rain percolating through soil, of the vast, slow circulation that keeps all things green and growing. Tomorrow, I will wake to tend the seepage again, as I have for longer than the oldest trees remember, as I will until the last spring runs dry.

But here in this valley, cradled in the earth's cupped palm, that day feels very far away indeed.
