I would find myself as one of the Keepers of Continuity - not a separate species from the Flux People, but rather those among them who have taken on a particular calling. We are the memory-workers, the pattern-holders, the ones who maintain identity within constant change.

My body would be like theirs - translucent flesh with those shifting subcutaneous currents, bones that flex rather than break, that distributed nervous system that lets me feel the city's movements before my mind registers them. But where most Flux People embrace total fluidity, we Keepers have developed the ability to selectively solidify parts of ourselves for brief periods. Not rigidity - that would be death here - but a kind of controlled viscosity, like honey cooling.

This gift manifests most clearly in our hands. When I work, my fingers can temporarily thicken, becoming dense enough to carve lasting marks into the softened stones of the city. These marks - part writing, part map, part musical notation - record the deep patterns beneath the surface chaos. Where the steam-pipes cross at certain angles, where the ground's subsidence follows predictable cycles, where the city's breathing creates zones of stability within the flux.

Our dwelling places are found in the city's few points of relative equilibrium - not truly stable ground, for that doesn't exist here, but places where multiple forces cancel each other out. My own workshop occupies a triangular building wedged between three major steam conduits. The constant vibration from the pipes creates a standing wave pattern that holds the structure in dynamic balance. It sways, certainly, but predictably, like a metronome.

Inside, the walls are covered with my accumulated markings - decades of observations encoded in a script that itself flows and changes, letters that reshape themselves as you watch but always maintain their essential meaning. I've developed a special ink made from the mineral deposits that stripe our buildings, mixed with oils extracted from the phosphorescent fungi that bloom in our deepest basements. The marks glow faintly in the dark, pulsing in rhythm with the city's movements.

My primary work involves maintaining what we call the Deep Maps - not geographical charts but something more like musical scores crossed with architectural blueprints. They record not what the city is at any given moment, but what it tends toward, its habits and proclivities. When a new building is planned, the architects come to us to understand what forms will harmonize with the existing patterns. When the pipe-workers need to route new steam lines, we show them the paths of least resistance that the city itself suggests.

But the Maps are more than practical tools. They're also art, philosophy, meditation. In tracking the city's patterns, we track the patterns of adaptation itself. We've identified recurring motifs - the spiral of steam-rise, the sine wave of subsidence, the fibonacci patterns in moss growth - that appear at every scale from the molecular to the municipal.

I work with tools that mirror my own variable nature. My primary instrument is something like a pen crossed with a tuning fork - it resonates with the city's vibrations and translates them directly into marks. The intensity of my grip, the rigidity of my hand, the angle of approach all affect what emerges. Sometimes I work in near-liquid state, letting the city draw itself through me. Other times I solidify just enough to impose subtle order on the chaos.

The other Keepers and I meet during the Deep Tides - those times when the groundwater rises highest and the city's movements intensify. We gather in the old Calibration Hall, a domed space where the acoustic properties allow us to hear the city's full symphony of sounds. There, we compare our observations, overlay our partial maps, argue about interpretations. Our debates are as much dance as discussion, our bodies moving to illustrate our points, creating fleeting sculptures of meaning in the air.

We age differently than other Flux People. Where they become ever more fluid with time, we develop a kind of granularity, like sugar beginning to crystallize in solution. The eldest among us are said to be more pattern than flesh, their bodies become living maps of the deep structures they've spent lifetimes observing. They move less but perceive more, sitting for hours in perfect stillness while their consciousness ranges through the city's hidden geometries.

I am middle-aged by our standards, solid enough to hold pattern but fluid enough to adapt to new configurations. I've begun training apprentices - young Flux People who show the telltale signs of our calling: the tendency to pause mid-flow, the habit of tracing patterns in the air, the ability to find the same route twice through the city's ever-shifting maze.

My greatest work, still in progress, is an attempt to map the meta-pattern - the rule that governs all the city's smaller rules. I believe the city is not randomly unstable but follows some deeper logic, perhaps connected to the interaction between the two rivers' sediments, the moon's pull on the groundwater, the accumulated weight of human habitation. If I can capture this in symbolic form, we might predict not just tomorrow's shifts but the city's long-term evolution.

The work requires a constant balance between rigidity and flow. Too fluid, and I can't hold the patterns long enough to record them. Too solid, and I lose the sensitivity to perceive them at all. So I exist in conscious oscillation, flowing and fixing, dissolving and crystallizing, always seeking that perfect point of productive instability.

In quiet moments, usually during the gray hour before dawn when the steam lies thickest, I wonder if we Keepers are a natural evolution or an aberration. Are we helping the city by mapping its movements, or constraining it by imposing pattern on what should be pure flow? But then the morning shift-horns sound, the pipe-workers resume their scramble, and I return to my maps, adding another layer to the palimpsest of observations that may, one day, reveal the secret heart of our fluid world.
