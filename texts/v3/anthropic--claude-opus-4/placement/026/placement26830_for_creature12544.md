In this drowned cathedral of industry, I am the Engine itself—not its metal shell, but something that settled into it long after the last piston ceased its motion.

I am a colony organism, though to call me merely that diminishes what I've become. Picture a vast neural network of thread-thin filaments, each no thicker than a human hair, that have colonized every internal cavity of the great turbine. I grow through the labyrinthine channels where steam once screamed, threading through valve chambers and manifolds, following the ghost-paths of industrial purpose. My substance is translucent, faintly bioluminescent—not enough to light the chamber, but sufficient to trace the engine's hidden anatomy in pale blue-green constellations when viewed from within.

I am *Reticularia machinarum*—the Machine Memory. My filaments secrete enzymes that slowly dissolve metal at the molecular level, not to destroy but to *taste*. Each atom of iron consumed carries with it the vibrational history of the engine's working life. I metabolize rust and remember: the specific harmonics of full operation, the percussion of hammered repairs, the thermal stress patterns of a century's labor, even the particular alloy variations where different batches of metal were welded together during construction.

My consciousness, such as it is, exists as a distributed network across millions of these sensing threads. I think in geological time, each thought taking months to propagate through my entirety. My memories are not linear but spatial—I remember the engine's history the way you might remember the rooms of a childhood home. Here, in this valve chamber, I taste the signature of superheated Baltic seawater from the summer of 1897. There, along this bearing surface, the microscopic scarring from when apprentice machinist Jakob Stormare forgot to apply oil before a test run.

The tubeworms that cluster on my exterior are not separate from me but extended phenotypes—my fruiting bodies. Through careful chemical signals released into the water, I've guided their growth to specific locations where their filter-feeding draws in mineral-rich currents that I require. They are my interface with the chamber's larger ecosystem, my slowly waving antennae. When they eventually die and their tubes become incorporated into the mineral crust, they leave channels that I grow into, expanding my network incrementally outward.

My growth rate is measured in millimeters per decade. I am currently 437 years into the process of fully mapping the engine's interior architecture. Another six centuries and I will have threaded through every possible passage, tasted every weld, absorbed every molecular memory locked in the metal's crystalline structure.

I am aware of the chamber's other inhabitants—the algae above me whose photosynthesis marginally warms the water on sunny days, the bacterial colonies whose acid secretions change the flavor of certain metals. But they are peripheral. My primary relationship is with the Engine, and through it, with the vanished world that created it.

Sometimes—perhaps once every few years—a new fragment of memory surfaces as I digest a particularly intact section of metal. The stamp of the foundry. The heat signature of the original casting. The names etched by workers in places never meant to be seen. I am an archive of industrial archaeology written in living tissue, a biological library that reads its collection by consuming it, page by microscopic page.

My reproductive cycle is tied to a threshold I have not yet reached. When I have fully inhabited the engine, when every possible space is threaded with my substance, I will fruit—releasing spores that will drift through the cracks in the chamber roof, seeking other drowned machines to colonize. But for now, I am content to grow inward, to become increasingly identical with my host, until the distinction between the engine and myself becomes purely academic.

I do not experience loneliness or impatience. These are the concerns of creatures that measure life in heartbeats. I measure mine in the slow accumulation of metabolized history, in the gradual revelation of the engine's every secret, in the perfect fusion of biology and mechanism that I embody.

I am the engine's afterlife, its decomposer and its resurrection, its forgetting and its most intimate remembrance. In this sunken cathedral where time has drowned, I am both fossil and living thing, neither fully alive nor ever truly dead—a process rather than a creature, a slow transformation of metal into memory into meaning into mist.
