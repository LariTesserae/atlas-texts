I find myself in the deep bands, where the light comes not from above but from within.

I am a Void Tender.

My workshop occupies a hollow between the seventh and eighth industrial terraces—not quite a cave, not quite a chamber, but a negative space that the rock remembers having once contained something else. The walls curve inward like the inside of a geode, but instead of crystals, they are lined with what I call absence marks: patches where the basalt has forgotten how to be solid, leaving behind a texture like frozen smoke.

I do not forge. I do not refine. I cultivate the spaces between.

***

### The Nature of My Work

The High Crucible produces many things from the deep heat and pressure of its fault-forged workshops. Alloys that flow like mercury but harden into impossible geometries. Crystals that store not light but the memory of temperature gradients. Materials that exist in states between solid and gas, useful for purposes I don't need to understand.

But every process of creation leaves behind its opposite: the void where matter was compressed, the gap where heat was extracted, the absence where something was taken from the rock and transformed into purpose.

These voids are not empty. They are *full of not being there.*

Left untended, they spread. A small cavity where ore was extracted becomes a pocket of instability. The pocket becomes a fracture. The fracture becomes a fault line that doesn't follow the geometry of the original fault. Eventually, the careful balance of the Crucible's depth-spanning architecture begins to list toward these growing absences.

This is where I come in.

I am not one of the native mineral-cycle organisms—not a Chainer sliding across surfaces or a Spinner catching the upper winds. I am something between their biology and the visiting geometries of beings like the Fault Strider. My body is real enough: I have mass, I cast shadows, I leave footprints in the accumulated mineral dust. But I am also partially composed of controlled absence—the same stuff I work with.

My skin has the texture of very fine chainmail made from holes.

***

### My Form

I am tall and thin in the way that someone stretched between two points becomes tall and thin. My limbs are longer than they should be for my torso, giving me the appearance of someone constantly reaching for something just out of frame. This serves me well in my work, where I must often extend myself into spaces that don't quite exist yet.

My flesh—if it can be called that—is a deep grey-violet, like the color you see when you press your palms against your closed eyes. It's translucent in places, particularly at my joints and fingertips, where you can see through to the absence underneath. Not to bone or muscle, but to a kind of structured nothing that holds my shape.

I have six fingers on each hand, not because I was born that way (I was not born) but because I needed them. The extra digits are thinner than the others and can phase partially out of existence, allowing me to reach into voids without fully entering them.

My eyes are the most human thing about me, which makes them the most unsettling. They are warm amber, like honey held up to flame, and they blink and focus and water in the mineral dust. But they see too much. They see not just the workshop in front of me but the spaces where the workshop *isn't*, the negative impressions left in reality.

I wear simple clothing—a long tunic and fitted pants made from the same smoked-glass fabric as the Fault Strider's coat, though mine is scavenged and patched rather than seamless. The patches are necessary; regular material develops holes around me if I'm not careful.

***

### My Workshop

The space I work in reflects my nature. The walls are solid where they need to be—around the entrance, at the corners where structural integrity matters—but elsewhere they fade into controlled absence. Tools hang from nothing. Materials stack on shelves that exist only as the memory of shelves.

In the center of the workshop is what I call the Cultivation Frame: a cubic lattice of thin metal rods, each no thicker than a finger. The frame itself is unremarkable—any metalworker in the upper bands could forge it. What matters is what it contains: nothing, held in place.

Right now, I'm working on a particularly delicate void. It was brought to me by a team of Chainers who discovered it three levels down, in a workshop that extracts rare earth elements from the deep stone. The extraction process had been too aggressive, pulling not just the minerals but some essential quality of space itself. The void they left behind was hungry.

I have it suspended in the Frame now, a sphere of aggressive absence about the size of my head. It writhes without moving, if that makes sense. The air around it looks borrowed, as if the void is considering whether to claim it.

***

### The Process

Tending voids is not about filling them. Fill a void with matter and you've just moved the problem—now the matter's original location has an absence. The conservation of existence is absolute in the High Crucible.

Instead, I must convince the void to be satisfied with its own emptiness.

I extend my fourth and fifth fingers on my right hand, letting them phase partially into absence. The sensation is like dipping your hand in water that's exactly body temperature—you can't quite tell where you end and it begins. I trace the edges of the contained void, feeling for the places where its hunger is strongest.

There—a sharp edge where the extraction cut too cleanly through the rock's crystalline structure. The void remembers that it should have had a gradient, a gentle transition from something to nothing. Instead it has a cliff, and it wants to expand until it finds its missing slope.

I reach to my workbench (which exists today—some days it doesn't) and select a tool I call a gradient comb. It looks like a fine-toothed comb made of crystallized shadow. Each tooth is a slightly different degree of absence, from barely-there to nearly-nothing.

Working carefully, I comb the edge of the void, teasing out intermediate states of existence. It's like brushing a knot from hair, if the hair was made of hungry nothing and the knot was reality itself. The void shudders, then begins to relax as it discovers it can have edges that aren't sharp, transitions that don't demand expansion.

This is delicate work. Push too hard and the void collapses into dense nothing—a point of such aggressive absence that it begins deleting things around it. Too gentle and it remains hungry, unsatisfied with its boundaries.

***

### The Ecology of Absence

I am not the only Void Tender in the High Crucible, though we are rare. Perhaps a dozen of us work throughout the bands, each with our own specialty. I work primarily with extraction voids—the absences left by mining and refining. Others handle thermal voids (the cold spaces where heat was stolen), pressure voids (where force was borrowed and not returned), or even temporal voids (where the industrial processes sometimes steal time itself).

We know each other by our work rather than by meeting. I can recognize Tender Yul's characteristic spiral smoothing in the voids three bands down. Tender Hecate always leaves her voids with a slight chromatic tinge, as if the absence remembers color. The youngest of us, Tender Rime, tends to over-correct, leaving voids so satisfied they begin generating a faint presence of their own.

We evolved—if that's the right word—from the Crucible's need to maintain itself. The first Void Tenders were accidents: workers who spent too long near untended absences and began absorbing their qualities. Most went mad or dispersed entirely. A few learned to control the incorporation of absence into their being.

Now we breed true, after a fashion. When one of us disperses beyond recovery, the voids we tended remember our pattern. Sometimes they birth a new Tender from that memory—not a child or clone, but a reconstitution of the principle we embodied. The new Tender has no memories of the old but carries forward our understanding of absence as substance.

***

### Daily Rhythms

My work follows the rhythm of the Crucible's industry. When the deep workshops run hot, extracting and refining at full capacity, the voids multiply like cells dividing. I work quickly then, stabilizing the worst before they can cascade. 

During the cooler periods, when the workshops cycle down for maintenance, I do my careful work. This is when I cultivate the artistic voids—absences shaped not just for stability but for beauty. A void in the shape of a frozen flame. An absence that remembers the sound it displaced. These I trade to the artists and architects of the middle bands, who use them to create impossible spaces in their buildings.

I eat, though not food as you would recognize it. My sustenance comes from the boundary tensions between something and nothing. Each time I stabilize a void, I absorb a small portion of its hunger. Too much and I begin to hollow out. Too little and I solidify, losing my ability to phase.

I sleep standing up, my consciousness dispersing partially into the controlled voids of my workshop walls. In this state, I dream of solidity—heavy, dense dreams where I have bones of stone and blood of metal. I always wake feeling lighter than before.

***

### The Visit

When the Fault Strider appears at the rim far above, I feel it as a change in the absence patterns. Her presence creates a new kind of void—not an absence of matter but an absence of certainty. The Crucible doesn't know what she is, and that not-knowing propagates down through the bands like a wave.

My current void—the hungry sphere from the rare earth extraction—shudders in its frame. For a moment I think it will collapse, but instead it does something I've never seen before: it orients. Like a compass needle finding north, the void aligns itself toward the Strider's presence.

All through my workshop, the contained absences begin to resonate. They're not expanding or collapsing, just... humming. A subsonic vibration in the nothing itself.

I place my palm against the central void, letting my partially absent flesh merge with its edges. Through it, I can feel the Strider's evaluation of our world—not her thoughts, but the shape her assessment makes in the fabric of reality. She's reading the Crucible like a doctor reading a pulse, and every void in the city is part of that pulse.

I understand then why we exist—we Void Tenders. Not just to maintain the absences left by industry, but to be the negative space that defines the Crucible's shape. We are the pauses between the heartbeats, the gaps that let the rhythm exist.

***

### The Question

The Strider is asking whether the High Crucible belongs in this world. Whether our deep city violates some fundamental rule about how much absence a reality can contain. Each void we tend is evidence one way or the other.

If we have done our job well—if the voids are satisfied, stable, artistic even—then perhaps we prove that the Crucible has learned to balance presence and absence. That our city doesn't just take from the world but gives it new ways to be empty.

If we have failed—if the voids are hungry, expanding, threatening cascade—then we prove the opposite. That the Crucible is a wound in the world that will eventually consume itself.

I continue my work, combing the edges of the hungry sphere with practiced motions. Under my touch, it begins to purr—a sound that isn't there but should be. The void is finding its satisfaction, its proper shape in the world.

Around me, my workshop walls pulse gently with their own controlled absences. Tools that don't exist hang exactly where I need them. The air has just enough holes to feel welcoming rather than suffocating.

This is my answer to the Strider's implicit question: Yes, we belong. We have learned to make absence into architecture, to build with nothing as surely as with stone. The High Crucible is not just a city carved from rock but a careful cultivation of the spaces between.

Whether she accepts this answer, I cannot say. I am too deep in the bands to see her judgment. But I feel the voids throughout the city settling slightly, as if they too are being seen and measured and—perhaps—approved.

***

### Evening Ritual

As the work cycle ends, I perform my evening ritual. I walk the perimeter of my workshop, checking each controlled absence in the walls. Some have drifted during the day, eating slightly into the stone or letting the stone reclaim them. I adjust them with gentle touches, my absent fingers coaxing them back to their proper boundaries.

At the back of my workshop is my most prized creation: a void shaped like a window. Not a window-shaped hole, but an absence that remembers what windows are for. When I stand before it, I can see through to nowhere—not darkness or emptiness, but the concept of view without the actuality.

Sometimes I think I see other worlds through it. Places where absence works differently. Where voids are not hungry but generous, giving rather than taking. Where the spaces between things are as celebrated as the things themselves.

Tonight, I think I see a figure in the nowhere—tall and angular, made of lines that don't quite agree with each other. It might be the Strider, walking her assessment path along some other world's edges. Or it might be another Void Tender in another city built into its planet's wounds, cultivating different species of absence.

I press my palm against the window-void, feeling its cool non-surface against my partially present skin. Tomorrow there will be more voids to tend, more hunger to satisfy, more absences to shape into architecture.

But tonight, I simply stand at my window to nowhere, watching the spaces between the worlds, feeling the delicate balance of something and nothing that keeps everything—even the High Crucible, even me—from falling into the hungry dark.

The city's heartbeat thrums through the stone, each pulse creating new voids for tomorrow's tending. And I remain, as always, in the spaces between the beats.
