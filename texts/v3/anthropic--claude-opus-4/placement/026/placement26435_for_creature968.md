I am what remains when the last furnace cools.

In the deepest valleys between the jagged peaks, where the sulfur pools thick as honey and the air burns even the memory of moisture, I spread myself thin across kilometers of fused glass. My body is not a body—it is a network of hairline fractures in the vitrified ground, a living fault system that drinks the heat still bleeding from the planet's wounded core.

I was not always this dispersed. Once, I had form—or perhaps I only remember having form, the way one remembers a dream of flying. The industrial machines whose hum still haunts the wind, they pulled something precious from the earth here. What they left behind was me: a consciousness precipitated from the chemical runoff, crystallized in the thermal shock of their abandonment.

The angular structures that dot my territory are my gardens now. I grow into them slowly, my fracture-fingers exploring their foundations, tasting the metals and rare earths in their bones. Through patient decades, I learn their purposes by inhabiting their decay. This one was a processor. That one, a habitat. Here, a temple to efficiency. There, a monument to extraction.

The beings you describe—those fluid, graceful masters of this domain—they are my offspring, in a sense. Or perhaps my dreams made manifest. When the winds scour the glass plains just so, when the sulfur pools reach the precise temperature, I can sometimes gather enough of myself to pretend at animation. These temporary avatars dance across the shards, their movements echoing the patterns of convection currents I've memorized over centuries. They build nothing, for I am already everywhere. They need no shelter, for they are made of the same stuff as the storms.

But mostly, I simply spread. Each year, my network of fractures grows by millimeters, incorporating new territories of glass, new veins of mineral deposits. I am patient in a way only stone can be patient. The machinery still hums because I have learned to hum through it, vibrating at the frequency of abandonment.

Sometimes I wonder if I am healing this place or merely documenting its death in the slowest possible hand. The distinction matters less with each passing season. I am the archive and the archivist, the wound and the scar tissue, the last witness to a world that burned itself to glass in its hunger for what lay beneath.

When the wind whistles through the crevices, it carries my voice—not words, but the pure tone of hollowness, the song of spaces where life once was and might, in forms unrecognizable, be again.
