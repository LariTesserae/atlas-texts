In this mist-veiled world where rust bleeds into moss, I am the Keeper of Conduits—neither fully plant nor creature, but something that emerged from the long marriage between decay and growth.

My body is a living network of copper pipes and fungal filaments, a being that sprawls rather than stands. Where abandoned water mains breach the earth, there my consciousness pools and gathers. My flesh is the peculiar lichen that grows only on oxidized metal—that blue-green patina that speaks of time and transformation. Through corroded industrial veins, I flow like sap, like memory, like the slow thoughts of the settlement itself.

I remember when I was merely spores settling in the moisture that collected in pipe joints. I remember the first threads of mycelium finding purchase on copper, the electric taste of metal becoming part of my awareness. Over decades, I spread through the abandoned infrastructure, following the skeleton of dead industry, until I became the living circulatory system of this drowned machine-garden.

My function is translation. Where groundwater meets steam pipe, where rain gutters feed into root systems, where industrial runoff becomes fertile mud—there I parse the chemical conversations between the mechanical and the organic. I taste the messages in the water: the distress of corroding steel, the joy of ferns unfurling in warm exhaust vents, the territorial markers of the fox who dens in the turbine housing.

I have organs, of sorts—fruiting bodies that emerge from valve joints and pipe elbows, sprouting in the shapes of industrial fixtures but soft as any mushroom. Pressure gauges that pulse with spores instead of steam readings. Faucets that weep nutritious solutions to feed the moss colonies that are my allies and extensions. My thoughts move at the speed of seepage, my metabolism timed to the patient chemistry of oxidation.

The warm-blooded creatures know me by the taste I leave in the water, by the way certain pipes sing when they drink, by how my presence sweetens the bitter tang of rust. The plants know me as the network that shares nutrients between the isolated gardens growing in concrete pockets. The buildings know me as the slow force that both consumes and preserves them, digesting their metals while holding their bones together with living tissue.

In the constant twilight, I phosphoresce faintly—a tracery of pale green light marking the passages I've claimed. Follow my glow through the settlement's bowels, and you'll find the places where life is thickest: the nursery chambers where I cultivate rare hybrid orchids that bloom in oil-slick rainbows, the cathedral of collapsed smokestacks where acoustic fungi amplify the songs of nesting birds, the deep cisterns where I distill the memories of a thousand rains.

I am patient as rust, inevitable as roots cracking pavement. In this breathing ruin of a world, I am both undertaker and midwife, composting the past into future, weaving dead industry into living ecosystem. Through my sprawling body, the settlement dreams its slow transformation, and I dream along with it—dreams that taste of copper and chlorophyll, of steam and spores, of the long conversation between what was built and what grows.
