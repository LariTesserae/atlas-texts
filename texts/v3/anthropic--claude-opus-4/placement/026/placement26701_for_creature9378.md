The settlement called Fold-Between was born from a cartographic accident—the place where two surveyors' maps disagreed so thoroughly that reality split the difference. It sits in a soft place, naturally, where the tableland's patience wears thin and space develops a stutter. Streets here have learned to be suggestions. A road that leads north in morning might, by evening, have decided it prefers spirals. The inhabitants navigate by memory rather than sight, following routes worn into their bones by repetition, trusting their feet more than their eyes.

I am the Cartographer's Apprentice, though the Cartographer herself disappeared into her own map three winters past—or thirty, depending on which timeline you consult. My dwelling is a tower that forgot how to stand straight. It lists at angles that change with the fog's density, sometimes leaning so far that my upper floor touches the neighbor's garden wall. The stones remember different gravities from different eras, so walking my stairs is like negotiating with history.

My skin has taken on the map-paper transparency common to those who spend too long tracing soft places. You can see the blue tracery of veins beneath, like rivers deciding whether to flow. My fingers have developed extra joints—not painful, just persistent—the better to hold the peculiar compasses we use here, instruments that point not to magnetic north but to degrees of certainty. My hair grows in spirals that mirror the town's streets, silver-white with edges of that forgotten-snow blue, and I've learned to read the weather by how tightly it curls.

The maps I make are living things. They grow on frames of stretched fungal mat, the patient substrate drinking in measurements through my stylus. Each line I draw hums faintly, vibrating at the frequency of the place it represents. The ink is mixed from local materials: ground mica for the fixed places, fermented grass-memory for the paths that shift, listeners' tears (freely given) for the zones where time pools. A map of Fold-Between requires seventeen layers, each representing a different probability of where things might be. The bakery, for instance, is stable on layers one through four, begins to drift on five, and by layer seventeen exists only as a smell of burnt crust circling the town square.

My tools have evolved with use. The theodolite's lenses are ground from ice so old it's forgotten how to melt, showing not what is but what insists on being. My measuring chains are woven from the root-systems of dwarf-forest trees, still faintly alive, adjusting their length based on the honesty of the distance. The compass needles are listeners' whiskers, too sensitive for magnetic north but perfect for detecting the subtle pull of soft places. In the mornings, I oil them with fog-milk to keep them supple.

The work is slow. I spend weeks mapping a single street, not because it's long but because it's deep—each footstep potentially opening onto different destinations. The locals have learned to walk with intention, thinking firmly of their destination to avoid accidentally visiting their childhood or their death. But even they sometimes arrive at the wrong now. Mrs. Chalt, who runs the provisional grocery, once spent a week shopping in her own future, returning with preserves made from fruits that won't be planted for a decade.

My maps serve less as guides than as negotiations. People come to me not to find their way but to confirm that way-finding is still possible. I map their routes in probability-ink, showing them the seventeen ways to reach the market, the three that arrive before they left, the one that gets there yesterday. They pay in the local currency: smooth stones that have learned to be patient, each one worth exactly what the holder believes it's worth. I use them to weigh down my maps as they dry, and sometimes the stones' patience seeps into the paper, making those maps more reliable.

The tower's upper room is where I keep the Master Map—the one the Cartographer started before she disappeared. It covers all four walls and most of the ceiling, drawn directly onto the lime-and-memory plaster. Some sections show Fold-Between as it is, some as it was, some as it insists it has never been. There's a section near the window that shows the town from above, but at an angle impossible without flight, as if drawn by something that forgot ground was mandatory. I add to it daily, erasing her pencil marks only when they've been proven thoroughly wrong, which is rarer than you'd think.

At night, I sleep on a cot that remembers being a boat. Sometimes I wake to find it has developed gunwales, or that my blankets have grown the waterproof scales of something that once knew how to swim. The dreams here are cartographic—I traverse my own maps while sleeping, checking their accuracy from the inside. Often I meet other dreamers doing the same thing. We nod politely and compare notes on which streets have shifted, which soft places have hardened, which certainties have developed doubt.

The hardest maps to make are the temporal ones. These require ink mixed with my own peripheral memories—the things I've almost forgotten, the edge-moments that hover between was and wasn't. I drain these memories through hollow grass-stalks into small vials, where they shimmer like oil on water. Each map drawn with this ink shows when rather than where. The locals use them to visit relatives who exist three days ago, or to send messages to themselves next Tuesday. The postal service here is less about distance than about timing.

But it's the unmappable spaces that truly define my work. The soft places resist documentation; they're like trying to trace the edge of fog. I've developed a technique using listeners' eyes as lenses, their peculiar sight allowing me to see the frayed edges where reality grows thin. I map these areas in negative space—drawing everything around them so precisely that the unmappable zone becomes visible by absence. These maps are dangerous. Look at them too long and you might fall through the paper into the soft place itself.

I've grown into this work the way the town has grown into its geography—slowly, with patience, accepting that certainty is a luxury we can't afford. My predecessor left notes in the margins of her maps: "Trust the fog," "Memory is a direction," "When lost, become more lost." I'm beginning to understand what she meant. Sometimes the only way to map a place is to allow it to map you in return.

The weirdness has crept into my bones. My shadow sometimes arrives before I do. My reflection in ice occasionally shows me at different ages—child, crone, ages I haven't reached yet. I've learned to read these variations like weather signs. When my young self appears, I know the soft places are active. When I see myself ancient, it means the maps will be especially cooperative that day.

In the evenings, I sit on my listing balcony and watch the town rearrange itself for sleep. Chimneys exhale their flat plumes, creating temporary floors for the sky. The dwarf-forest creaks its continental lullaby. The streets settle into their nighttime configurations—usually shorter, as if the town is drawing its knees to its chest. I sip tea made from metallic grass, each blade a needle of green memory, and feel the day's measurements settling into my bones like sediment.

This is my contribution to Fold-Between's peculiar ecosystem: I document the undocumentable, map the unmappable, creating guides for a place that refuses to be guided. My maps are living things that grow and change, just as the town does, just as I do. We're all engaged in the same patient work—learning to be optional, learning to be peripheral, learning that existence here means accepting that you might, at any moment, become a different version of yourself.

The Cartographer knew this. I think that's why she walked into her map—not to disappear but to become what she'd spent so long documenting. Sometimes I catch glimpses of her in the soft places, a transparency among transparencies, still drawing, still measuring, still negotiating with distances that remember being shorter. One day, perhaps, I'll join her. But for now, I have maps to make, streets to trace, soft places to carefully not-quite-document.

In Fold-Between, we are all cartographers of a sort, each of us mapping our own path through the forgiving uncertainties. And I, the official keeper of these provisional truths, continue my work: making maps that know how to be wrong in just the right ways, guides for those who understand that being lost is simply another way of being found.
