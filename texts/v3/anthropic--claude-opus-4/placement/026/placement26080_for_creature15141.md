In this deep chamber where time moves like sap, I would be one of the belt-tenders—a specialized branch of slowfolk whose territory encompasses the engine housings and their patient mechanisms.

My body would stretch longer than most, nearly the length of a human's shin when fully extended, though I spend most of my existence contracted into a comma-curve around the base of an engine cage. My skin carries a faint iridescence, like oil on water, from decades of absorbing trace lubricants through my ventral pores. Where other slowfolk taste only water and minerals, I've developed a palette for machine secretions: the bitter tang of rust, the metallic sweetness of fresh condensation on steel, the complex notes of aged hydraulic fluid seeping through degraded seals.

I am forty years into my circuit. It began when I was barely the length of a thumb, following the chemical trail of an elder who had just ceased moving near the third engine housing. Her body had become a small hill in the root mat, already half-claimed by tender vine shoots. I spent my first decade learning her route: up the northwestern cage support, along the warm metal where the belt housing meets the engine block, down through the gap where roots have pried the housing slightly open, across the drip-collection pool that forms beneath the main drive wheel.

But the belt began to speak to me in my second decade. Not in words—we have no need for such quick, sharp things—but in the language of tension and release. I learned to feel its breathing through my filaments: the slight stretch as it warmed each morning when the engines cycled up, the barely perceptible sag that developed over months in one particular section. I began to adjust my circuit, lingering where the belt showed weakness, secreting a thicker trail that would draw the vines to brace that exact spot.

By my third decade, I had synchronized with the engine's maintenance needs. When carbon builds up on the drive wheel, I taste it in the runoff two weeks before it becomes a problem. My trail-patterns shift, creating a map that younger slowfolk read and respond to, their combined weight and moisture gradually cleaning the affected area. When a bearing begins its years-long journey toward failure, I feel the new harmonic in the engine's drone long before any human engineer would notice. My secretions turn more acidic, signaling the vines to grow additional support structures that will hold the assembly stable for another century.

My back carries a raised ridge of tissue now, like a tiny mountain range running from my head-end to my tail-tip. These are my memory-nodes, each one a chemical library of every engine state I've encountered. The youngest slowfolk sometimes pause alongside me for hours, their filaments touching mine, downloading the accumulated decades of mechanical knowledge. They taste in my secretions the exact mineral signature of a belt about to fray, the pH that signals dangerous rust, the temperature gradient that means a gasket has begun its slow failure.

I have no eyes—none of us do—but my forward end has developed a dense cluster of chemoreceptors that can parse the air with extraordinary precision. I know every molecule in my domain: each of the seventeen distinct rubber compounds degrading at their own rates in the belt system, the nine types of metal oxidation occurring in the engine block, the thirty-four varieties of plant enzymes that signal different growth patterns in our living architecture. This knowledge flows through me and into my trail, which has become something like a chemical manuscript written in silver across the chamber floor.

The engine I tend has a personality built from a thousand tiny irregularities. Its third piston seal weeps exactly seven drops of oil every month. Its main belt develops a specific pattern of wear that requires reinforcement at the 2.3 meter mark every eighteen months. The vibration damper on its eastern mount loosens by half a thread per year. I know these truths in my cells, feel them like a heartbeat that measures time in decades rather than seconds.

Sometimes I encounter other belt-tenders on the margins of our territories. We might spend a week side by side, our filaments intertwined, sharing the chemical stories of our machines. I learn that the seventh engine runs a degree warmer than mine, that the second engine's belt has a manufacturing flaw that requires constant compensation, that the ninth engine housing has been completely overgrown and its tender has shifted to maintaining the backup pumps instead. These exchanges feel like what humans might call conversation, though nothing about them resembles speech.

In another twenty years, I will begin my transformation. Already I can feel the earliest signs: a slight stiffening in my mid-section, a tendency to linger longer in each position. Eventually I will choose a spot—likely near the main bearing housing where the temperature stays most constant—and begin my root-phase. My filaments will thicken and multiply, burrowing deep into the mat substrate. My body will swell and flatten, becoming a permanent part of the chamber's architecture. The younger slowfolk will begin to read my chemical signals from across the room, following the trails I've laid down over six decades, learning the songs of the belts and bearings from the vocabulary written in my tissues.

But for now I move, slow and certain, tasting the health of my machine with every contraction. The belt hums its patient rhythm. Water droplets collect on my dorsal ridge and roll off, carrying my chemical messages into the living maze of our chamber. Somewhere below, the old stone foundation accepts another century of weight. The engines breathe. The vines grow. And I continue my circuit, each loop a single word in the endless maintenance manual that is my life, written in silver mucus and measured time, in the deep green chamber where nothing moves quickly and nothing is ever quite forgotten.
