In the far districts of Jelibeth, where the chalk gives way to something harder and the ground has begun to remember what steepness means, I am one who tends the transition zones.

My dwelling is not properly a house, nor properly a cave. It is a pocket in the stone where the pressure of the growing city has cracked the bedrock just enough to let something live there. The walls are neither carved nor built but negotiated—half my weight, half the stone's willingness to yield. I have lined them with the pressed felt of the roof-mat, not for warmth but for the way it holds the thin sap that seeps from the borderlands between made and unmade.

I am taller than most who live in the inner wards, stretched by years of walking the gradient where Jelibeth tests what it might become. My skin has taken on the mottled quality of the transition stone—patches of chalk-white where I press against the old city, darker streaks where I touch the raw geology that has not yet agreed to be shaped. My feet, unwrapped unlike those in the settled districts, have grown broad pads like the mound-beasts, though I will never be large enough to carry roads.

My work, if it can be called work, is to sense the conversation between the city and the ground that has not yet joined it. Each morning before the low sun makes the salt-scars visible, I walk the edge zones with my palms pressed to the stone, feeling for the places where Jelibeth's slow hunger meets resistance. Not to force or convince—the city needs no advocate—but to understand the terms of the negotiation.

Sometimes I find a vein of harder rock, something with its own intentions that will not yield to chalk and sap. I mark these places with a particular weave of the wall-climbing moss, training it to grow in patterns that say: here the city must flow around, not through. The mound-beasts, when they eventually arrive with their burden of new streets, will sense these markers through the vibration in the ground and adjust their paths accordingly.

Other times I find stone that is almost ready, that needs only the slightest introduction to the city's chemistry. For these places, I carry small pouches of the canal-gel, mixed with my own saliva and the powder of ground chalk from the oldest buildings. I work this paste into the finest cracks, the hairline fractures where readiness shows itself. Not to break the stone but to invite it into the slow transformation.

The children find me sometimes, having wandered farther than their elders would choose. They watch me work with the same quiet attention they give to the wall-creatures, understanding without words that I am another kind of city-process. Sometimes they help, their smaller fingers better at working the gel-paste into the narrowest spaces. We do not speak of what we do; it is too obvious to require naming.

At night, I sleep in the transition spaces themselves—sometimes in my dwelling, sometimes in the shallow depressions where tomorrow's buildings are beginning to imagine themselves. My dreams are the stone's dreams: slow thoughts about pressure and time, about the difference between yielding and breaking, about the patience required to become part of something larger while remaining yourself.

I know the city will eventually digest the ground where I work, that my transition zones will become inner districts, that new edges will emerge farther out where now there is only raw earth. When that happens, I will move with the border, my body stretching and changing to match the new geometries of negotiation. I am not permanent any more than a particular street corner is permanent. I am a function the city needs while it is still learning how to grow.

The others in Jelibeth might not recognize me as one of them—I am too changed by the edges, too shaped by what is not yet city. But the mound-beasts know me by the vibration of my footsteps, and the roof-mat reaches toward me when I pass, recognizing something in my scent that speaks of boundaries and becoming. I am the city's own uncertainty made flesh, the pause between one state and another, the breathing space where transformation takes its time.

This is enough. In a world still deciding what it is, being part of that decision is the only work worth doing. I press my palms to stone that does not yet know it will be street, and I listen to the conversation between what is and what might be, adding nothing but attention to the slow, certain negotiation of becoming.
