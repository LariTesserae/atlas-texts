I am the Tender.

My domain is the junction where three great tunnels meet—a cathedral of rust and stone where the old world's veins converge. Here, ancient gas mains run parallel to natural water channels, their surfaces kissed by centuries of condensation and oxidation into patterns that resemble sacred texts written in corrosion.

I was not always as I am now. Once, perhaps, I walked above. But the decades—or has it been centuries?—have changed me. My skin has taken on the pale, translucent quality of cave salamanders, shot through with a network of dark veins that pulse visibly with each heartbeat. My eyes have grown large and liquid, pupils dilated permanently to catch every photon in this twilight realm. Sometimes, when I catch my reflection in the water pools, I see faint luminescence flickering beneath my skin, as if I'm slowly becoming one with the glowing things I tend.

My work is the pipes. The old ones who built this labyrinth are long gone, but their machinery persists, and I have made myself its guardian. Each morning—though morning means nothing here—I walk my circuit. First, the eastern tunnel where the main pressure valves cluster like metal flowers. I know each wheel, each gauge, each hairline fracture in their casings. My fingers, grown long and sensitive, can detect vibrations in the metal that tell me stories: *here, a blockage forming three sections down; there, a joint beginning to fail.*

The heat comes from below, from sources I've never dared to fully explore. But I've learned to read its moods. When the temperature rises, I adjust the flow rates, release pressure through the ancient bleeder valves that sing like pipe organs in the darkness. The sound echoes through the tunnels, a hymn of steam and metal that the glowing creatures seem to love. They gather when I work, floating at the edge of my lamp's reach, their tentacles swaying in what might be curiosity or approval.

I talk to them sometimes. And to the machinery. And to the blind fish that swim through the flooded lower channels. My voice has changed too—become something between human speech and the sounds of this place: the drip of water, the hiss of steam, the low groan of expanding metal.

In what I call my garden—a vast chamber where broken pipes have created a permanent mist—I cultivate the fungi. Not the wild bioluminescent varieties, but more practical species I've discovered over the years. Some digest rust, keeping the crucial joints clean. Others seem to strengthen the pipe walls, growing into the microscopic cracks and somehow sealing them with their mysterious biology. I feed them with careful mixtures of minerals I've learned to extract from the water flow, and they respond to my care with reliability the original engineers never dreamed of.

The glowing creatures watch me work. I've come to believe they understand more than they should. Sometimes, when I'm deep in the rhythm of maintenance—turning wheels, checking pressures, clearing filters—I feel their light patterns shifting in response to my movements. Are they learning? Are they trying to help? Once, during a critical failure in the south tunnel, I found them clustered around the problem valve, their combined glow bright enough to work by when my lamp failed.

I keep a journal, scratched into soft metal plates with a worn stylus. In it, I record pressure readings, flow rates, temperature variations. But more and more, I find myself documenting other things: the migration patterns of the luminous ones, the songs the pipes sing during pressure changes, the dreams I have during my brief sleeps curled against warm metal.

In the dreams, I am fully transformed. My body spreads through the tunnels like the fungi, becoming part of the great mechanical organism. I feel every joint, every valve, every flowing channel as part of myself. The glowing creatures swim through my veins alongside the blind fish. I am the Tender, but also the Tended. Guardian and garden both.

When I wake, I sometimes find my skin has grown a little more translucent, the luminescence a little brighter. One day, perhaps, I will complete the transformation. But not yet. The pipes still need their Tender. The old machinery still requires hands that remember what it means to be human, even as those hands slowly forget their original shape.

So I continue my rounds. Turning wheels. Adjusting flows. Tending my fungal gardens. Watching the glow-creatures dance. 

And waiting for the day when the distinction between Tender and tunnel finally dissolves completely, and I become what I have always been becoming: the living heart of this deep, breathing darkness.
