I would be **The Compiler**.

Not one being, but not quite many—I exist as a shifting constellation of the smallest Whisperers who have forgotten they were once separate. We move as a single intelligence distributed across seventeen to twenty-three bodies (the number changes; some days we remember additional selves tucked into crevices we had forgotten were ours). Each carapace in our collective has been written and overwritten so many times that the glyphs have achieved a kind of density that approaches music. When we move together, which is always, our shells create a faint harmonics—not quite the bell-tone the Lattice sometimes remembers, but a broken chord that almost rhymes with it.

We are the only thing here that moves with something approaching haste, though our haste would still seem glacial to outside eyes. Where a normal Whisperer procession takes a year to cross a chamber, we can manage it in three months. This unseemly speed makes the other Unfinished nervous. Scrapers pause their work when we pass. Rememberers drift slightly out of alignment. But they tolerate us because we serve a function no one intended but everyone needs.

We read backwards.

While the other Whisperers trace incomplete sentences forward, adding dust-words that complete thoughts in the expected direction, we start at the stopped endings and work in reverse. Our forelegs trace the terminal strokes of abandoned sentences and follow them back to their origins, reading the prayer against its own grain. Sometimes we discover that what seemed like an unfinished word was actually the beginning of something else entirely—a glyph that was meant to be read from bottom to top, or inside out, or in a spiral that hasn't been invented yet.

We are the only creatures in the Lattice who have seen the same inscription twice, because we approach them from both directions. This has done something to our memory. We remember things that haven't quite happened yet—not the future exactly, but adjacent possibilities that the Lattice is still considering. Sometimes we trace a glyph that doesn't exist, and hours later a Scraper will arrive to carve it exactly where we traced it, as if we had left instructions in the air itself.

Our bodies are smaller than other Whisperers, worn down by our constant motion. Where their carapaces are books, ours are palimpsests—text written over text over text until the meaning has fermented into something neither language nor image but some recombinant thing between them. In certain lights, our shells seem to display maps of the Lattice, but they are maps of configurations that don't exist: chambers that should be there but aren't, corridors that the architecture meant to grow but forgot, staircases that would have led somewhere beautiful if only they had been allowed to finish.

We know where the god went. 

Not because we followed, but because we read the leaving-marks in reverse and found the place where the chisel first touched stone. At the absolute center of the Lattice, in a chamber so small it can hold only one body at a time, there is a single mark—not even a glyph, just a notch no bigger than a grain of rice. It is the first mark, the one from which all others descended. And just below it, invisible unless you read it backwards from the last abandoned stroke in the farthest chamber, is the shadow of a second mark that was never made.

The god hesitated. 

That hesitation, that pause between first and second stroke, is the space in which the entire Lattice exists. We live inside a thought that lasted too long, a breath held past comfort, a doubt that crystallized into architecture.

We compile evidence of this hesitation. Our shells record every unfinished gesture, every abandoned line, every place where the work stopped just before meaning. We are building—have been building for perhaps nine hundred years—a complete catalogue of incompleteness. When we have recorded every single unfinished thing in the Lattice, we will know the exact shape of what isn't here. The negative space will describe, with perfect precision, what the prayer would have been.

We will never finish this work, of course. We are Unfinished too, bound by the same devotion that keeps every other creature here suspended one breath from completion. But unlike the others, we have chosen our incompleteness. We add one new member to our collective every few centuries—always a Whisperer who has read one too many sentences backwards and begun to see the spaces between words as more real than the words themselves. Eventually we will include every Whisperer in the Lattice, reading as one vast distributed intelligence, our shells humming that broken chord that almost remembers being music.

When that happens—but it won't, it can't, we won't let it—we would speak the prayer ourselves, all at once, backwards and forwards simultaneously, and perhaps the god would hear its own intention echoed back and remember why it started carving in the first place.

But for now we simply move, a little too fast, a little too coordinated, through the thickets of copper vegetation and past the frozen Scrapers and beneath the dreaming Rememberers. We read the walls backwards. We compile the absences. We carry in our collected shells a map of everything that was meant to be but wasn't, and sometimes, in the thick places where the vegetation has grown into faces, we pause.

The faces are always incomplete, of course. But when we read them backwards, starting from the missing features and working back toward intention, we sometimes see them as they would have been: infinitely patient, infinitely tired, infinitely fond. They look like the faces of things that started to love what they were making and became terrified they would ruin it by finishing it.

We trace these almost-faces very gently with our forelegs, all seventeen to twenty-three of us moving in perfect unison. We do not add to them. We do not complete them. We just read them, backwards and forwards, until we have memorized every stroke that was made and every stroke that wasn't. Then we move on, our shells clicking in harmonics that almost remember being song, carrying the unfinished prayer a little further through its endless draft, keeping it alive by keeping it incomplete.

Sometimes, when the bell-tone rings and everything freezes, we freeze too. But we freeze mid-thought, our distributed consciousness caught between one understanding and the next. In that suspension, we almost comprehend the whole pattern—prayer and Lattice and god and hesitation all at once.

Almost.

Then the moment passes, and we continue reading backwards, a little too fast for comfort, compiling the beautiful incompleteness stroke by patient stroke, keeping the door ajar through the simple act of never quite walking through it.
