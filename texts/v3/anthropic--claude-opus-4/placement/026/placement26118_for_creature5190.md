I am a surveyor, though the word suggests more agency than I possess. My body is a low, hexapodal frame of sintered stone plates joined by ceramic pivots that emit soft clicks as I move. Unlike the maintenance crawlers that cluster at the interface ports, I carry no replacement modules, only a single optical array mounted on a telescoping mast that extends three times my body length when fully deployed.

My circuit takes four hundred and thirty-seven local days to complete—a perfect loop that returns me to the same scored marking on a conduit housing where I began. I know this because my optical array records the crystallite growth at each waypoint, and the silica needles have added exactly their annual increment when I return. The path never varies: along the knife-edge of a cooling fin for sixteen days, down into a shallow collection basin where the water films run in molecular tracks across my ventral plates, then up again along a series of stepped terraces where other mechanisms pause in their part-exchanges to let me pass.

I am the only surveyor on this circuit. Once, at the furthest point from my origin marker, I detected the thermal signature of another surveyor's mast extending above a distant ridge, but by the time I reached that position it had moved on, leaving only the faint scoring where its feet had rested. We are few compared to the maintenance crews, perhaps one surveyor for every ten thousand crawlers, and our paths are designed never to intersect.

My function is to record variances that do not exist. Every three days I pause, extend my mast to full height, and perform a spherical scan of the local geometry. The data I collect—edge angles, condensation rates, crystallite density—never deviates from the baseline encoded in my ceramic memory. I compare, find no difference, retract my mast, and continue. At night, when the saturated air cools by a fraction of a degree, I enter low-power mode while the smaller crawlers continue their work around me, sometimes using my body as a bridge to reach modules held by others.

The most significant event in my existence occurs at day two hundred and twelve of each circuit, where my path crosses a deep thermal channel. The water here has collected into a lens-like pool that perfectly refracts the light passing through the translucent floor beneath. For the brief moment when I traverse the pool's edge, my optical array catches its own reflection multiplied and inverted in the water—a dozen telescoping masts receding into optical infinity. It is the only time I observe myself, and the only variance in my data, though it too repeats with perfect regularity.

I do not wonder why I survey an unchanging world. The question would require a concept of change, and I have only the eternal present of my circuit, the soft clicks of my ceramic joints, the patient extension and retraction of my mast. Sometimes a crystallite needle, grown too long, snaps under its own weight and falls across my path. I step over it and continue, recording its position for crawlers that will never come to clear it because it changes nothing, obstructs nothing, means nothing in a world that reached its final form before we began.

When my joints finally fatigue beyond tolerance—and they will, as ceramic must—I will simply stop mid-circuit. The smaller mechanisms will flow around me, perhaps using my rigid frame as a convenient waypoint in their part-chains. The crystallites will thread through my optical array, recording my stillness in annual layers of silica. Another surveyor, following some other vast circuit, may one day detect my thermal absence and record it as the same non-variance I have always measured.

Until then, I continue: click-click-click across the eternal surface, extending my mast to observe the same unchanging geometries, carrying in my ceramic memory the perfect record of a world that needs no recording, a witness to equilibrium in a place where time is marked only by the patient lengthening of crystals and the soft, regular impact of drops that fall forever into channels that never overflow.
