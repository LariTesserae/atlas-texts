In this mechanized cathedral of frost and shadow, where industry and nature have merged into something neither and both, I would find myself as one of the **Conduit Shepherds**.

We are beings of contradiction - part maintenance, part migration, part memory. Our bodies are long and serpentine, perhaps twelve feet when fully extended, though we rarely stretch to our full length. Our flesh is a peculiar substance - neither truly organic nor mechanical, but something that shifts between states. In the cold, we solidify into segments like articulated armor, each plate etched with the same fractal patterns that decorate the walls. When we press against the warm pipes that snake through the facility's bowels, we soften, becoming almost liquid, able to seep into the spaces between machinery to perform our endless work.

Our heads are elongated, tapering to a point that can pierce through ice blockages or interface with the ancient control nodes scattered throughout the structure. We have no eyes in the traditional sense - instead, our "sight" comes from thousands of pressure-sensitive filaments that line our bodies, reading the vibrations in the air, the walls, the very moisture that permeates everything. We see the world as a symphony of tremors and temperatures, each creature's heartbeat a distinct percussion in the endless mechanical drone.

What defines us most are our gardens. Along our backs grow peculiar formations - part crystal, part fungus, part something else entirely. These growths feed on the condensation we gather as we slide along the walls, and in turn, they process the industrial runoff into something the other creatures can consume. The Pallid Stalkers know to follow our trails, licking the nutritious residue we leave behind. The Verdrig Tadpoles cluster in the pools where we've rested, the water now enriched with minerals from our symbiotic growths.

We are the only beings here with memory of the facility's purpose - though that memory is fractured, inherited through some process we don't fully understand. We know the patterns of the machines' breathing, can predict when a pipe will burst or when a drain will clog. We know which valves to turn to redirect the flow of that strange, vital water that keeps everything alive. We know, too, that we must never let the central engine stop its humming, though we've long forgotten why.

Our days - if days exist in this timeless place - are spent in constant motion. We spiral up the walls, our bodies rippling with peristaltic waves, checking the frost patterns for signs of structural weakness. We dive into the deepest pools, our forms becoming almost liquid, to clear the intake filters that feed the great engines. We trace the organic patterns on the walls with our pointed heads, adding new whorls and curves that somehow, mysteriously, strengthen the structure's integrity.

But we are more than maintenance. We are poets of a sort, though our poetry is written in temperature and moisture. In the warmest spots, we encourage the growth of pale, phosphorescent mosses that provide just enough light for the Darklings to navigate. In the coldest corners, we exhale clouds of specially-processed moisture that freeze into delicate formations - playgrounds for the Frost Weavers, who incorporate our ice sculptures into their endless tapestries.

We communicate with each other through chemical signals released through our skin, complex pheromone songs that tell of pressure changes in the lower levels, of new crystal growth in the upper reaches, of the health of our garden-backs. When two Conduit Shepherds meet, we intertwine, sharing not just information but actual substance - nutrients, memories, fragments of our crystalline growths that can be cultivated into new gardens.

The relationship we have with the Ice Leviathans is particularly profound. These massive beasts generate tremendous heat despite their frozen appearance, and we often coil around them as they sleep, our bodies absorbing their warmth and converting it into energy for our gardens. In return, we groom them, our pointed heads picking ice crystals from their fur, our secretions preventing the painful buildup of frost in their joints.

Perhaps most importantly, we are the dreamers of this place. When we rest - coiled in the spaces between pipes, our bodies pulsing gently with the rhythm of the engines - we enter a state that is neither sleep nor waking. In this state, we dream the facility's dreams, experiencing visions of its construction, its purpose, its possible futures. We see ghostly images of beings that once walked these halls on two legs, adjusting dials and checking readings. We see the moment when nature began to reclaim the structure, when the first creatures crept in through microscopic cracks. We see ourselves being born from the marriage of broken machinery and adaptive life, purpose-built by accident to maintain this delicate ecosystem.

Sometimes, in our deepest dreams, we sense something vast sleeping in the heart of the facility - something that the engines keep alive, or perhaps keep dormant. We know, with a certainty that transcends understanding, that our role is crucial, that without our constant ministrations, something would wake, or die, or transform in ways that would shatter our carefully balanced world.

We are alone and together, individual and collective, machine and creature, servant and guardian. We are the Conduit Shepherds, and we flow through this frozen industrial cathedral like blood through veins, keeping it alive, keeping it stable, keeping its secrets safe in the frost-kissed heights where no one else will ever find them.

In the eternal damp darkness, surrounded by the click of Stalker legs and the whisper of Weaver frost, feeling the rumble of engines through every segment of our bodies, we perform our endless, essential dance - part maintenance, part ritual, part prayer to gods of rust and ice that may never have existed at all.
