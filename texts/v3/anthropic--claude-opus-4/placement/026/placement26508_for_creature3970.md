I am a vertical cartographer, suspended in my web of ropes and pulleys against the north face of Cygnus's Weeping Wall – so named for the constant trickle of condensation that sheets down its oxide surface. My dwelling clings to the cliff like a barnacle: three rooms carved directly into the rock, connected by a fourth that juts out into space on cantilevers I welded myself from salvaged tram rails.

The morning mist hasn't burned off yet, so the city below me exists only as suggestions – the ghost of a bridge, the hint of a smokestack, the faint clatter of something mechanical echoing up through cotton-thick air. I work best in these hours, when the wall is slick and treacherous and most honest about its intentions.

My maps are not paper things. I etch them directly into sheets of local slate using acids I've developed from the cave lichens that grow in the Wall's deepest recesses. The acids eat differently depending on the stone's mineral content, creating patterns that mirror the reality distortions I'm documenting. When the anomalies shift – when a street decides to flow upward for a while, or when two buildings begin their slow melt into one another – the maps change too. The slate remembers.

Today I'm tracking a new wrinkle in space that opened three weeks ago between the Brass District and the Condensation Gardens. It's subtle: a place where falling water droplets pause for exactly three seconds before remembering gravity. The locals have already started calling it the Hesitation, and children dare each other to stand beneath it with their mouths open, catching time-stuck rain.

My equipment hangs from my belt in leather pouches stained green from years of lichen acid: hammers and chisels for taking rock samples, vials of various reactive substances, a headlamp modified with filters to see the faint aurora that appears where reality wears thin. Most importantly, I carry my plumb bob – not for finding vertical anymore, since vertical itself has become negotiable in Cygnus, but for detecting the direction of maximum ontological stability. It pulls toward what's most real.

I've been mapping this section of the Wall for six years now, ever since I noticed that the erosion patterns were writing something. Not in any language, but in the grammar of geology itself – the way softer stone surrenders first, the way water finds its path, the way time carves meaning into rock. The Wall is trying to tell us something about what's happening to our city, about why space folds here and why our machines hum that particular note that makes babies cry and birds fly backward.

My grandfather was one of the original engineers who built the first anchor points for Cygnus. He used to say the city wasn't built in a valley at all – that we're clinging to the inside of something else, something that only looks like a valley from our limited perspective. He went mad, of course. Most of the first generation did. But his notes, his calculations, the frantic sketches he made on his workshop walls before they took him away – they align too well with what I'm finding in the stone.

The plumb bob swings wildly as I rappel down to a fresh section of exposed rock. Here, the Wall bleeds a different color – not rust-red but a deep purple that seems to eat light. I've found three other purple zones in my surveys, and when I plot them on my master map, they form vertices of something. Not quite a shape. More like the shadow of a shape that exists in more dimensions than we can see.

I take out my newest slate, virgin and ready. The acid hisses as I begin to etch what I see, translating the grammar of stone into something human eyes can parse. Somewhere below, the Hesitation has started to sing – a low throb that makes my teeth ache. Another change. Another revision to the map.

By evening, I'll return to my cliffside dwelling, where my walls are covered in hundreds of slate maps, each one a frozen moment in Cygnus's slow transformation. I'll heat water collected from the Wall's weeping, make tea from the aerial roots that dangle past my windows, and try to assemble the greater pattern. My partner will return from their shift at the reality observatory, and we'll compare notes, looking for correlations between the stone's testimony and the instruments' data.

But for now, I hang here in the mist, reading the Wall's newest chapter, adding my own marginalia to the great text that Cygnus is writing with itself. The city may be hollow, waiting for a population that might never come, or perhaps preparing for something else entirely – a transformation so profound that our current forms are merely scaffolding for what we're becoming.

The plumb bob points sideways now, trembling with sympathetic vibration. I follow its lead, traversing across the cliff face toward something new being born in the stone.
