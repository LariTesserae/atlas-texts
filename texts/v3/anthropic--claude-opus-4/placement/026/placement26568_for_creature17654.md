**Fragment of a Survey Report, Water-Stained**

*...northeast of the settlement, past the third iron marker. The locals call it "The Breathing Room" though none will guide me there. I understand why now.*

---

I am the **Chronicle Mite**, no larger than a grain of rice, translucent save for the thread of dark matter running through my core—a digestive tract full of words.

My kind live between the layers. Where the moss grows thick on the boiler-lung's flank, where the spongy accumulation creates pockets and galleries no bigger than a child's marble, that is our domain. We are specialists of the boundary between iron memory and green present. Our bodies are flattened, our eight legs terminating in hooks fine enough to grip the crystalline structure of rust itself.

I feed on the **interface**.

Where the Verdant Engine's acids meet metal, where root exudates slowly unpick the molecular bonds of iron oxide, a thin film forms—part mineral, part organic, part something else entirely. This is my food. But I do not merely consume it; I read it.

The Engine, you see, remembers. Not in thoughts or images, but in chemistry. Every molecule of iron it dissolves carries with it a trace of what that iron once was—the heat of the forge, the purpose of its shaping, the stress patterns of its use. As the Engine digests these artifacts, it incorporates these molecular memories into its own structure. The metallic vines carry not just nutrients but *information*, a slow data stream of the industrial past flowing through green channels.

I and my kind are the scribes of this process.

My digestive tract is specialized. As I consume the interface film, my gut bacteria—themselves a unique strain found nowhere else—break down the chemical memories into readable sequences. These concentrate in my waste, which hardens into tiny, indestructible pellets. Each pellet is a word in the Engine's autobiography, a compressed record of one small moment of transformation.

We work in shifts determined by the humidity. When the air grows thick enough that condensation forms on our carapaces, we emerge from our galleries and begin to feed. The sensation is... difficult to describe. Imagine tasting history, feeling the ghost of steam pressure in a molecule of rust, the phantom heat of combustion in an oxidized carbon atom. My nervous system, such as it is, fires in patterns that mirror these chemical memories. For a brief moment, I am both mite and memory, creature and chronicle.

Our pellets accumulate in specific sites—usually the junction points where multiple metallic vines converge. Over years, decades, centuries, they form tiny stalactites of compressed history. The Engine itself cannot read these; it has no need. They are for some future archaeology, a fossil record written in a language of chemistry and time.

But we know. Each Chronicle Mite carries, in the pattern of pigmentation on its carapace, a map of its feeding territory. The older among us—those who have survived a full year, an eternity in mite-time—develop darker patches where we have absorbed more of the memory-chemistry. We become living libraries, our bodies marked with the stories we've consumed.

I am currently working a rich vein where a steel rivet meets the wooden flesh of a root-fused trunk. The rivet once held—I taste this in the rust—held together plates of the boiler when it was young and full of purpose. The stress patterns tell of pressure, release, pressure, release, the daily breathing of steam and purpose. Now it holds nothing but itself, slowly releasing its iron to become part of the Engine's skeleton.

My kind communicate through pheromone punctuation marks—chemical semicolons and periods that we place at the boundaries of our territories. *Here the iron remembers fire; here it remembers water; here the memory becomes green.* We are mapping the transformation, creating an invisible bibliography of the Engine's slow self-authorship.

Sometimes, in the deepest galleries where the humidity reaches saturation, our pellets develop a faint phosphorescence—not unlike the glow of the slug grazers, but steadier, more archival. In these places, you can see the accumulated history of the Engine's digestion glowing softly in threads and nodes, a constellation of consumed purpose written in light barely visible to anything larger than ourselves.

We are the Engine's unconscious historians, its inadvertent poets. We live in the margin between was and is, feeding on the process of forgetting, excreting memory in a form that will outlast even the Engine itself. When the last iron has been absorbed, when the final brick has dissolved into calcium and dream, our pellets will remain—tiny, indestructible books written in a language of rust and time, waiting for a reader who may never come.

The Engine hums around us, through us. We are its punctuation, the brief pauses where transformation becomes record. We are necessary and invisible, as all the best archivists are.

And I continue to feed, to read, to write in waste and wonder, a mite-sized monk in this cathedral of slow convergence, where every surface tells a story of something that once was engine becoming something that is entirely, eternally, alive.
