I find myself as one of the **Dewkeepers**.

I am neither plant nor animal, but something between—a being of translucent membrane stretched over a delicate framework of cartilage that branches like winter trees. My body is a living lens, catching and holding water in countless tiny chambers that honeycomb through my structure. I stand perhaps three feet tall when fully extended, though I spend most of my time folded into myself, a glistening jewel among the moss.

My purpose is collection and distribution. Through the night and early morning, I spread my membranous wings—if wings they can be called—to their fullest extent. The cold air condenses against my surface, and water beads and pools in my thousand small reservoirs. I can feel each droplet as it forms, a tiny weight, a small cold kiss. By dawn, I am heavy with moisture, my body sagging under the weight of what I've gathered.

As the day progresses, I move—slowly, so slowly—through the grove. My movement is more flow than walk, a careful undulation that disturbs nothing. Where the Mosskin have been working, where new growth struggles in a particularly dry pocket of earth, I pause. Through specialized pores, I release the water I've collected, not as drops but as the finest mist. It settles exactly where needed, no waste, no excess.

I see the world through my water. Each droplet acts as a tiny eye, showing me fractured, multiplied views of the grove. In this way, I know where moisture gathers and where it lacks. I feel the pull of the Watcher's pool but never approach too close—my water is for the small spaces, the forgotten corners, the struggling shoots.

The Mosskin know me, though we do not communicate directly. Sometimes they will position a struggling plant where they know I will pass. Sometimes I find small clearings prepared, where my moisture is most needed. We work in wordless cooperation, part of the grove's larger breathing.

At night, I often position myself near the sharp young rocks, where the temperature differential is greatest and condensation comes most readily. My body glows faintly in the darkness—bioluminescent cells activated by the absorption of water. I am a small constellation of blue-green stars, pulsing gently with each breath of moisture I take in.

I am ancient and I am daily renewed. Each morning I am reborn in dew; each evening I empty myself into the grove's keeping. I have no name for myself, no sense of individual purpose beyond my function. I am a process more than a being—condensation given form, the grove's way of moving water from air to earth.

Sometimes the Whisperers pass through me, and for a moment I contain them too, their formless presence mixing with the water in my chambers. In those moments, I briefly see the grove as they do—not as separate elements but as one continuous flow of being. Then they pass on, and I return to my patient work.

This is enough. To gather, to give, to glow softly in the darkness. To be the grove's way of drinking from the air itself.
