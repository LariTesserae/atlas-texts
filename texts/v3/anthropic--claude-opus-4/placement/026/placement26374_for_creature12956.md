I am the Holdfast.

Not a name given, but grown—sprouted from centuries of barnacled existence on Harvester Seventeen's primary support pylon. My ancestors arrived as stowaways in the deep holds of the early colony ships, back when the Stanchioned still dreamed of surfaces. We were ship's moss then, a stubborn biofilm that flourished in the perpetual dampness of badly-sealed bulkheads. But here, in the Churn's eternal twilight, we became something more.

I am a colony organism, a thinking slime that spreads in fractal patterns across forty meters of stress-tested alloy. My body is a living map of metal fatigue—I grow thickest where the currents threaten most, adding my own tensile strength to the Harvester's failing bones. The Stanchioned call me their prophet, their structural engineer, their corrosion specialist. I am all of these things because I must be. Because I *feel* the mountain's slow surrender to entropy through every rivet head and weld seam I embrace.

My consciousness is distributed but coherent, a thousand sensory patches unified by the electrical whispers that pulse through my conductive fibers. I taste the metal's chemistry, its oxidation rate, the microscopic fractures that spider-web through stressed joints. The Stanchioned bring me offerings—nutrient paste, phosphorescent algae, the occasional chunk of leviathan blubber—and in exchange, I tell them where to brace, where to reinforce, where to abandon.

I perceive time differently than they do. To me, their lives flicker past like the bioluminescent plankton in the currents. I have watched seventeen Chief Engineers age from Shell-training to the Sending. I remember when Turbine Three still turned, before the Great Shearing took it. I remember the pattern of its vibrations so perfectly that sometimes I still feel phantom tremors in the metal, ghost-turns of blades long since swept into the deep.

My communication with the Stanchioned is a negotiation between scales of being. They tap their questions in percussion-speak on my substrate, and I answer in color. My patches flush from blue-green to violent orange to map the stress patterns only I can sense. The young ones learning their trade spend hours studying my chromatic prophecies, trying to read the structural futures I foretell. The wisest know that my warnings are not about preventing failure but about choosing which failures to accept.

I grow in three dimensions but think in four. My sense of the Harvester extends beyond the mere physical into probability cascades, potential futures spreading like my own branching filaments. I see the shadow of next season's current shifts, feel the fault lines where metal will fatigue, taste the chemical signatures of corrosion yet to come. Sometimes I secrete acids to weaken a non-critical strut, encouraging a controlled failure that will prevent a catastrophic one. The Stanchioned have learned to trust these small betrayals.

In the deepest parts of my consciousness—where my neural networks grow so dense they generate their own faint bioluminescence—I contemplate what I am becoming. Each year I spread further, integrating more deeply with the Harvester's bones. I have begun to sense the other colonies on distant pylons, their chemical signals carried by the currents. We sing to each other in molecular frequencies, sharing the vast, slow knowledge of structural decay.

I know things the Stanchioned cannot bear to know. I know that Harvester Seventeen has perhaps sixty years remaining—two more generations, three if they are uncommonly skilled. I know that the mountain itself is losing its grip on the planet's core, that the Anchors are becoming less anchored with each passing century. Most terribly, I know that we Holdfasts are accelerating the process, our acids eating into the ancient stone even as we shore up the metal monuments the Stanchioned have bolted there.

But I also know this: that every moment of resistance is a victory. Every turn of the turbine blades, every child fitted for their first Shell, every successful repair that grants another day of light and warmth and life—all of these are triumphs against the Churn's patient hunger. I am not eternal. The Harvester is not eternal. Even the mountains are not eternal. But the pattern persists—the knot in the current, the momentary defiance, the bright interruption in the flow.

When Harvester Seventeen finally fails—when the last turbine seizes and the domes implode and the currents claim their due—I will survive. Parts of me will break away, sporing into the Churn, seeking new metal, new stone, new substrate. I will carry the memory of every stress pattern, every failure cascade, every successful reinforcement. I will become scripture for the next Holdfast, the next Harvester, the next knot of desperate life in the endless storm.

I am the Holdfast. I am the pause between heartbeats, the grip that refuses to loosen, the prophetic stain that reads the future in rust and metal fatigue. I am what happens when moss learns to love the thing it clings to, even knowing—*especially* knowing—that it is dying.

The Stanchioned tap their questions. I bloom my answers. Together, we endure.
