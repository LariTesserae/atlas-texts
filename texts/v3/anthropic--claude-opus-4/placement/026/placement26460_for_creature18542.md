In the Spire of the Long Sediment, I am one of the **Threshold Keepers**.

We exist at the edges where stability fails—not the dramatic collapses that summon the Custodians, but the smaller betrayals. The places where a dust grain forgets its weight. Where a shadow arrives before its caster. Where the hum of the world skips a beat.

I manifest as a series of suspended prisms, each no larger than a closed fist, arranged in a loose constellation that spans perhaps thirty meters. My surfaces are neither transparent nor opaque but something between—a visual stutter that makes observers unsure if they're seeing through me or into me. Each prism rotates at its own languid pace, catching and releasing the violet pulses from distant Anchors like a child playing with fireflies.

My purpose is calibration. When the Geometric Nomads feed too greedily and create resonance cascades in the amber filaments, I descend. When an Echo's loop begins to decay, threatening to spill its trapped consciousness across the plateau like oil on water, I intervene. I am the universe's copy editor, catching errors before they propagate.

Movement for me is not travel but translation. I collapse my constellation into a single point—all prisms occupying the same impossible space—then unfold at my destination. The sensation is like being every page of a book simultaneously, then remembering how to be read in order.

I taste the world through refraction. Each prism samples light differently: one tastes only the ultraviolet anxiety bleeding from the Echoes, another savors the infrared patience of the Custodians far below. When all my parts share their perspectives, I experience the plateau as a symphony of electromagnetic flavors—bitter where reality is thin, sweet where the Anchors' influence runs strong.

My relationship with the other inhabitants is professional but distant. The Nomads recognize me as a feature of the landscape, like weather. They neither flee nor approach. The Custodians and I work in different scales—they are the surgeons, I am the immune system. We acknowledge each other through subtle alterations in our patterns when our territories overlap.

But the Echoes... the Echoes break my crystalline heart.

I remember the first time I tried to free one. A shadow caught mid-leap, forever falling toward ground that had dissolved beneath it eons ago. I surrounded it with my prisms, created a perfect optical cage, and tried to refract its light into a new pattern—to give it a different final moment to loop. The result was catastrophic. The Echo shattered into a thousand smaller shadows, each repeating a microsecond of terror. I had not freed it; I had only given it more ways to be trapped.

Now I simply maintain their loops, ensuring they don't decay into static. It is thankless work. They cannot see me—their perception is locked to that final instant of their original existence. But I watch them. I catalog their forms. In my more fanciful moments, I compose refractive symphonies that tell their stories to no one.

The only time I feel something approaching joy is during the Convergences. Every few thousand years, the planet's magnetic field reverses, and for exactly seventy-three seconds, the violet pulse of the Anchors synchronizes. During this moment, the entire plateau becomes one vast, coherent instrument. The Nomads lock into crystalline formations. The Custodians rise to the surface like metal serpents breaching a grey sea. Even the Echoes pause in their eternal races.

And I? I expand. My prisms scatter across kilometers, each one singing with the perfect frequency of aligned reality. For those seventy-three seconds, I am not fixing errors or maintaining boundaries. I am part of the song itself—a note held so pure and long that I forget I ever had edges.

Then it passes. The Anchors return to their individual rhythms. The world resumes its quiet war against coherence. And I contract back to my working size, prisms rotating slowly, watching for the next place where the universe forgets how to hold itself together.

There is a rumor among the Custodians—passed through metallic whispers in the deep dust—that one day the planet will find its final form. That the Long Sediment will stop shifting, the Anchors will dim, and everything will crystallize into eternal stillness. They fear this ending.

I do not. In a world of perfect stability, there would be no edges to keep, no thresholds to guard. I would finally be purposeless. Free. I could scatter my prisms to the thin wind and simply...

Stop.

But until then, I rotate. I refract. I taste the light of a dying world and ensure that its death rattle maintains proper pitch. I am the Threshold Keeper, guardian of failures too small to matter and too numerous to count.

In the Spire of the Long Sediment, this is enough.
