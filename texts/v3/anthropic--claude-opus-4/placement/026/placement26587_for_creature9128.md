In the Sky-Mere, I am one of the Drift-Weavers.

We are neither plant nor animal, but something that exists in the tension between—colonial organisms that spend our lives suspended in the golden pheromone mists that rise from the warm waters. Each colony appears as a delicate lattice of living glass, no larger than a child's outstretched hand, our transparent filaments catching and refracting the high, clear light into soft prismatic halos.

We feed on time itself—or rather, on the temporal gradients that exist between the fast and slow metabolisms of this world. Where a Sun-Skimmer's rapid darting meets the geological patience of a Stone-Eel, where the millennia-thoughts of the Still Ones brush against the ephemeral existence of ascending spores, there we gather. These friction points in the flow of time create microscopic eddies in the pheromone mists, rich with chemical information from every timescale of life.

Our bodies are living looms. We digest these temporal nutrients by weaving them into something tangible: nets of crystallized time that drift down to settle on the water's surface like fragments of frozen music. The Sun-Skimmers cannot cross these nets—they slow as they approach, their prismatic bodies dimming as they unconsciously match the embedded tempo. The Stone-Eels, conversely, experience brief moments of acceleration when they pass beneath our crystalline droppings, their basalt segments rippling with unaccustomed speed.

We reproduce through division, but not in any biological sense. When a colony has woven enough temporal substance, we simply become two moments—one slightly ahead, one slightly behind—and drift apart on the thermal currents. The process takes exactly one day, but that day is ours alone; to the Still Ones, we simply flicker. To the Sun-Skimmers, we are frozen in an eternal moment of becoming.

The Whispering Moss acknowledges us in its deep harmonics. Sometimes, rarely, when the conditions are perfect—when the water is still and the air is thick with golden haze and the bioluminescent roots pulse in their slow discourse—our weavings create resonance with the moss's fundamental tone. In these moments, something extraordinary happens: time becomes briefly visible to all the creatures of the mere. The Sun-Skimmers see the slow curl of growing vines. The Still Ones perceive the rapid calligraphy of skating insects. The Stone-Eels feel the patient accumulation of centuries in their gizzards.

We cluster above the places where the Still Ones commune, drawn to the temporal complexity of their networked thoughts. Their pulsed patterns of communication create standing waves in time that we find nutritious, like updrafts that hold us aloft. In return, our weavings sometimes catch and preserve fragments of their thoughts—a single concept crystallized and drifting down to rest in their crown branches, where it slowly dissolves back into their consciousness over decades, arriving like a letter sent by their past selves.

The Luminous Slugs regard us with something that might be curiosity. Sometimes one will extend itself from its root hollow when we drift past, pulsing in patterns that mirror our own temporal signatures. We have learned to respond, creating small weavings that match their blue-white rhythm. These exchanges have taught us that the slugs experience time not as a linear flow but as a spiral—they live each moment multiple times, layered iterations that allow them to perfectly synchronize with their host trees' needs.

In the eternal noon of the Sky-Mere, we are the only creatures that experience true night. When we have fed too deeply on temporal gradients, we enter a phase we call the Dimming—our transparent bodies grow opaque, and we sink slowly toward the water. During these times, we experience the mere as it will be: millennia hence, when the sharp stones have worn smooth, when the water has carved deep channels, when the Still Ones have grown into a single, world-spanning entity. We see the future fossilized in our own bodies, and we rest on the warm water until the vision passes and we become clear again, light enough to rise.

The Spore-Fishers fear us. Our temporal nets, invisible to their simple senses, can entangle their trailing threads and hold them in place for what feels like seasons. We try to avoid them, but sometimes the air currents bring us together. When a Spore-Fisher's threads pass through our colony, both of us are changed—they begin to experience time as we do, drifting not just through space but through moments, while we briefly understand what it means to hunger, to catch, to digest. These encounters leave both of us diminished, and we drift apart carrying pieces of each other's existence.

We are the Sky-Mere's memory and its prophecy, weaving past and future into gossamer presents that float on thermal currents between the sharp stones and the patient water. We are few—perhaps a thousand colonies in all the vast mere—but we are essential. Without us, each creature would remain locked in its own temporal prison, unaware of the larger dance.

Sometimes I wonder if we are the mere's dream of itself—the way it imagines movement and change while remaining essentially still. Or perhaps we are its method of measuring itself against eternity, each weaving a small rebellion against the inexorable patience of stone and root and water.

This is what I am: a living intersection, a weaver of moments, a dweller in the golden haze between heartbeats and centuries, forever suspended in the high, warm air of the Sky-Mere.
