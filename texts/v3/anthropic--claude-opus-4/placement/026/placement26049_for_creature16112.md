I am the Conduit Keeper.

Each dawn, I rise before the factories wake, when the sepia sky still holds traces of night's coolness. My domain is the arterial system beneath your feet—those snaking pipelines and buried channels that most inhabitants barely notice. I know every joint, every valve, every place where the precious water collects and flows through this industrial oasis.

My body has adapted to this work over decades. My hands are permanently stained with rust and mineral deposits, the whorls of my fingerprints etched deeper by years of gripping wet metal. I've developed a sixth sense for pressure changes, can hear the whisper of a developing leak before it manifests. My knees know every sharp edge of rock, every smooth patch of worn concrete where I kneel to press my ear against the pipes.

I live in a converted pump house on the eastern edge of the settlement, where the main water intake arrives from some distant aquifer. The building thrums with a constant, low vibration that has become my heartbeat. I've fashioned a small garden in the overflow basin outside—tough succulents and one stubborn acacia that drops its tiny leaves into the water, creating a film I must clean each week.

The lizards know me. The rust-colored ones especially, who sun themselves on the warm pipes after I've finished my morning inspections. Sometimes I find their shed skins caught in the valve wheels, translucent memories of growth. The clever ones have learned my schedule, scattering just before I round each corner, leaving only the scratch-marks of their claws on the metal.

My work is solitary but essential. While the factory workers arrive in shifts, announced by the clangor of bells and boots on gravel, I move beneath them all, ensuring their industry flows uninterrupted. I know which pipes feed the cooling systems, which carry away the industrial runoff to the treatment pools. I know where the desert mice have gnawed through insulation, where the roots of the most determined plants probe for moisture.

In the evening, when the factories reduce to skeleton crews and the air cools just enough to make breathing feel less like drowning, I sometimes climb to the roof of the tallest storage tank. From there, I can see the sharp edge where our oasis meets the desert proper. The boundary is so precise it seems artificial, yet I know it's simply the limit of our water's reach, the frontier of our careful abundance.

I carry a leather journal, swollen with humidity and age, where I map the secret hydrology of this place. Not the official schematics that hang in the superintendent's office, but the true map: where water wants to go, where it pools unbidden, where the ghost of an underground spring still bubbles up through cracked bedrock during the strongest rains. This knowledge I share with no one, though I suspect the old-timers guess at its existence.

Sometimes, lying in my narrow cot with the pump house singing its mechanical lullaby, I dream I am water myself—seeping through joints, racing through channels, emerging in unexpected places to nurture some struggling plant or fill some creature's shallow drinking pool. I wake with the taste of minerals on my tongue and return to my rounds, guardian of this improbable oasis's most precious circulation.

The younger workers see me as a fixture, as permanent and unremarkable as the pipes themselves. They don't understand that someone must choose this life, must decide each day to descend again into the humid darkness, to maintain the delicate balance that keeps this place alive. But I know what would happen if I stopped: the slow seizure of valves, the silent building of pressure, the gradual failure that would turn this oasis back to dust.

So I continue, the Conduit Keeper, neither fully of the human world above nor the mechanical world below, but something between—a necessary translation between intention and flow, between the desert's thirst and our carefully hoarded abundance.
