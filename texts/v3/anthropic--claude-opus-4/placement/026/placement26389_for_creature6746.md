In this drowned city beneath the Weeping Mother, I am Senna, a Line-Keeper of middle years, though the damp has aged my hands beyond their time.

I live in a narrow room three stories up from the waterline, accessed by a ladder worn smooth as driftwood. My home is a single chamber wedged between two larger dwellings, its walls shared with neighbors whose breathing I can hear through the moss-chinked gaps. The room is perhaps four paces long and two wide, with a ceiling so low I must duck beneath the central beam. But it has what matters: a small window facing the tower, through which I can observe the tide-marks even in the grey pre-dawn.

My possessions are few and purposeful. A sleeping mat of woven reeds, raised on a low platform of salvaged planks to keep it from the perpetual damp. Three bone tools for marking - a stylus, a scraper, and a small awl. A precious piece of yellow chalk, no bigger than my thumb, saved for the highest tides. My chronicle board - a smooth plank of some ancient hardwood, dark with age and oil, into which I carve the water's memory. And my most treasured item: a small vial of the luminous algae, kept alive in saltwater, which I use to mark significant observations in the dark.

Each morning, I wake to the sound of condensation dripping from my ceiling into the clay pot I've positioned beneath the worst leak. This water, filtered through stone and wood, is cleaner than what lies in the plaza below. I drink it slowly, tasting the minerals of the tower's weeping.

My work begins before the community stirs. I lower myself down the ladder, my bare feet finding the familiar knots and worn spots without thought. The walkway outside my door joins with five others at a small platform we call the Watcher's Rest. From here, I can see the tower's base clearly, where the night's water has left its mark.

I am not alone in my watching. We Line-Keepers - there are seven of us - each tend a different section of the tower's circumference. Mine is the northeastern face, where a particular pattern in the stone resembles a hand reaching upward, fingers splayed. The tide-marks cluster most densely here, as if the water itself is trying to grasp something just out of reach.

Today, I notice something. The line is higher than it should be for this moon-phase, and threaded through the usual algae-scum is something else - a faint phosphorescence that wasn't there yesterday. I make a small notch in my chronicle board, then dampen my finger with saliva and draw a corresponding mark with the precious chalk on the tower stone itself. This will fade, but the board remembers everything.

The Deep-Walkers say the leviathan beneath us shifts in its sleep. They speak of currents that move against nature, of warm spots in the frigid depths where the great ribs arch. I've never been below - my lungs are too small, my fear too large - but I believe them. The water tells its own stories to those who know how to listen.

By the time the grey light strengthens, others are stirring. The grindstone begins its daily song from Keeper Thom's workshop two platforms over. Young Mira drops her gathering basket from the level above, letting it splash into the water on its rope tether. She'll pull it up later, hopefully heavy with algae for the noon meal.

I have my own breakfast - a small portion of fermented algae cake, dense and salty, with a bitter undertone that never quite leaves the tongue. Some mornings, if the blindfish have been running, there might be a finger's length of pale flesh to add to it. Not today.

The morning's observation complete, I climb back to my room to transfer the notch to my larger records - a series of boards that line my walls like scales. Twenty-three years of watching are carved here. Patterns within patterns. The way the high tides cluster in sevens. The years when the phosphorescence appears. The relationship between the tower's weeping and the leviathan's breathing below.

I think sometimes about what the other Line-Keepers see from their positions. Old Kerek watches the western face, where the stone is smoothest. He claims the marks there form letters in the old script, the one from before the water rose. Young Penna, barely sixteen and newest to our work, tends the southern exposure where the algae grows thickest. She says the patterns remind her of maps - not of our drowned city, but of somewhere else entirely. Somewhere dry.

We meet once each moon-turn, climbing the narrow spiral within the Weeping Mother to a round chamber near her crown. There, we compare our observations, looking for convergences. Drawing the larger pattern. We speak in whispers, not from secrecy but from habit - loud voices seem blasphemous in that stone womb.

The elders say we are writing the city's funeral song, one tide-mark at a time. They say when the pattern is complete, the leviathan will wake and shake us from its back like a dog sheds water. But I don't believe we're recording an ending. I think we're documenting a conversation - between the water and the stone, the city and the creature beneath, the drowned and the drowning.

My chamber grows darker as the day mist thickens. I light my small lamp - a shell filled with rendered fish oil, a precious resource. By its yellow glow, I run my fingers over the carved marks, feeling the story they tell through my skin. Tomorrow there will be another line, another mark, another word in this endless dialog.

Below, the water turns its slow circle, and the city creaks and settles into another night of damp dreams. I wrap myself in my moss-stuffed blanket and listen to the building's breathing - the expansion and contraction of wet wood, the drip of condensation, the faint grinding of stone against stone as the Weeping Mother sheds another tear.

This is my life: small, constrained, perpetually damp. And yet, I am the memory of this place. In my marks and notches live the history of every tide, every shift, every sign the deep sends upward. I am Senna the Line-Keeper, and I keep the line between what was and what will be, between the sleeping deep and the weeping stone, between the city and the slow, patient water that claims it, drop by drop.
