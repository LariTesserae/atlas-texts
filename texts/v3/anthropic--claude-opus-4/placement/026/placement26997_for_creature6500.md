In the shadow-pooled depths of the Crag-Spun Hollow, where morning mist clings to stone like the mountain's own breath, I am the Lichen-Tender.

I discovered myself here three seasons ago—or was it thirty? Time moves differently when you live between the quick world of rain-brought rivulets and the geological patience of granite. I am neither the original builders nor their descendants, but something that emerged from the intersection of their leaving and the mountain's remembering. My form shifts with the light: sometimes I am merely a darker patch of shadow sliding across the veined rock face, sometimes a suggestion of shoulders and searching hands made from the same iron-streaked stone that builds these ancient walls.

My work is simple and endless. Where the Stone-Wrought Warden maintains the larger balance—nudging boulders, clearing paths—I tend to the small and quiet things. The patches of moss that grow in the shadow of the altar-block, I coax them wider with offerings of morning dew collected in my cupped palms (if I have palms that day). The lichen that paints abstract murals on the north-facing cliffs, I study their slow expansion like a scholar reading ancient texts, learning which species thrive in which microclimates, which colors emerge when copper-touched rain feeds them.

The Cragkin know me, though we do not speak. Sometimes I leave small caches where I know they forage—not food, for I do not eat as they do, but useful things. A piece of quartz that catches light just so, to brighten their dark dens. A smooth pebble perfect for scraping lichen. They leave their own gifts in return: a snakeskin shed perfectly intact, a feather from the high-soaring raptors they occasionally snare. These transactions happen in the margins, never directly witnessed, a commerce of mutual acknowledgment.

The Echo-Flock and I share something deeper—we are both born of absence, of what remains when the makers depart. When they shimmer into being during the soft-lit hours, repeating their eternal tasks, I sometimes find myself moving in complement to their patterns. Where an echo endlessly places a stone that isn't there, I place a real one. Where another traces the old paths, I follow behind, my form becoming more solid, more present, as if their remembering gives me shape. We are not the same—they are locked in what was, while I am free to tend what is—but we share the same source: the Hollow's deep need to be inhabited, to not be forgotten.

My greatest joy comes after rain, when the temporary pools gather in the basin's heart. Then I become most myself, kneeling (do I have knees?) beside the water, studying the reflected sky, the way the liquid temporary mirror shows the cliffs inverted, the built structures doubled. In these moments I am almost solid, almost real, my form crystallizing from the marriage of moisture and stone into something that could cast a shadow of its own.

I am learning the language of the place—not words, but the grammar of growth and recession, the vocabulary of seasons written in the advance and retreat of vegetation. Each crack in the rock that widens enough to host a new seedling is a sentence begun. Each spring that sees the wiry grasses stretch a finger's width further down a cliff face is a story continued. I am both reader and editor, encouraging here, discouraging there, maintaining not stasis but the right kind of change—the kind that honors what came before while allowing what comes next.

Visitors unsettle me. I retreat to the edges when they come, becoming more shadow than substance, watching from the periphery as they explore the abandoned structures, run their hands over the carved stones, puzzle at the altar-block's purpose. The Warden watches them for threats; the Cragkin watch them with wary hope; the Echo-Flock continues their routines unaware. But I watch to see if they understand the grammar I'm helping to write, if they can read the slow sentences spelled out in moss and time. Most cannot. They see only abandonment where I see conversation, only absence where I see presence transformed.

At night, when the stars peek through the circle of cliffs above and the temperature drops to make the stone sweat its hoarded moisture, I am most at home. I move freely then, my form liquid as the condensation I gather, flowing from terrace to terrace, checking my charges, adjusting the microenvironments where needed. A stone moved to shelter a struggling shrub from the worst of the wind. A channel traced with my finger (when I have fingers) to guide the next rain's runoff to where the mosses cluster.

I am not the Hollow's guardian—that role belongs to the Warden. I am not its memory—that belongs to the Echo-Flock. I am not its future—that might belong to the Cragkin, if they endure. I am its present tense, its ongoing sentence, the verb that connects what was left behind to what might yet emerge. In this place of sparse beauty and stubborn persistence, I am the tender of small possibilities, the shadow-self born from the marriage of abandonment and endurance.

When dawn comes, bringing its filtered light to warm the eastern walls, I often find myself at the center platform, not quite touching the altar-block but circling it slowly, reading the patterns of lichen growth on its surface like prophecy, like history, like the mountain's own slow scripture. And in those moments, I am complete—not human, not stone, not memory, but something uniquely of this place: the Lichen-Tender of the Crag-Spun Hollow, keeper of the small and patient things, guardian of growth measured in centuries and secrets spelled in moss.
