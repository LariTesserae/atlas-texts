## The Cascade Tender

I am one of the few who moves constantly through the vertical city, neither claiming a shelf nor settling into the rigid hierarchy of Upper, Middle, or Lower. They call me a Cascade Tender, though the name is only half-accurate. Yes, I tend the cascades — the intricate networks of collection channels that catch falling debris, tools, and sometimes bodies before they vanish into the center void. But I am more than a maintenance worker. I am a vertical nomad, a reader of the shaft's moods, a keeper of its forgotten spaces.

My body reflects my work. Where the stacked are compact and dense, I am elongated, stretched by years of constant climbing. My limbs are ropy with a particular kind of muscle that comes from hauling yourself up kilometers of wall daily. My fingers have developed an almost prehensile quality, able to find purchase in cracks too small for others to notice. The pads of my hands and feet have thickened into something between flesh and the mineral crust of the shelless creatures — a slow adaptation that lets me grip the raw wall-rock when the shelves give out.

I wear a harness at all times, even when sleeping. It's made from salvaged elevator cable, worn smooth by use, with hooks and carabiners fashioned from engine parts that failed decades ago. My most prized possession is a head-lamp — not electric, those are too unreliable — but a bioluminescent chamber containing a colony of *glow-mites*, a rare variety of dust-mite that produces a weak greenish light when fed the right mineral mix. The light is barely enough to see by, but in the deep spaces between shelves, it's the difference between navigation and freefall.

The cascades I tend are ancient. They predate most of the current shelf construction, built into the very bones of the shaft. Networks of grooved channels carved directly into the wall-rock, angling gently inward to catch whatever falls. At regular intervals, these channels feed into collection basins — some as small as a cooking pot, others large enough to hold a dozen people. The basins must be cleared regularly, or they overflow, sending avalanches of accumulated debris onto the shelves below.

In the basins, I find everything. Tools dropped by careless hands. Scraps of clothing. The mineralized remains of shelf-raspers. Sometimes larger things — a bore-walker that lost its grip, now just a pile of crystalline segments. Once, I found a child's toy, a simple slag-ite disc with holes drilled through it that would whistle in the updrafts. I kept it. When I'm alone in the deep channels, I sometimes let it sing.

The cascade routes take me everywhere, through sections the stacked have abandoned or never knew existed. There are gaps in the shelf construction, vertical territories unclaimed for reasons lost to memory. In these voids, I've discovered things:

- **Growing chambers** where the wall-rock itself seems to be slowly extruding new formations, crystalline structures that might be natural or might be the shaft itself building something.
- **Dead zones** where even the dust-mites won't go, where the air tastes of something acrid and wrong, where my glow-mites dim and cluster together in fear.
- **Echo spaces** where sound behaves strangely, where a whisper comes back as a roar, where the engine-thump arrives out of sequence.
- **The Seep** — a section of wall that sweats a thin film of something that might be moisture but isn't water. The shelless creatures cluster there, forming dense, writhing colonies. I avoid the Seep now.

My work is solitary, but I am not alone. Other Cascade Tenders exist, though we rarely meet. We communicate through the channels themselves, leaving markers — a particular pattern of scratches, a small pile of sorted debris, a whistle-disc hung where the updraft will catch it. These signs tell stories: *cascade seven-seven-nine is clogged with engine ash, avoid for three days* or *found good cable here, took half, left half* or simply *still alive, still climbing*.

We know things the shelf-bound stacked do not. We know that the shaft *changes*. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, but it changes. New channels appear where none existed before. Old basins seal themselves shut. The lean of the walls shifts by degrees over years. We map these changes in our minds and bodies, a knowledge that can't be written or spoken, only experienced through the ache in our joints and the wear patterns on our harnesses.

I've been falling in my dreams lately. Not the panicked tumble into the center void, but a slow, controlled descent, my body learning to navigate the downdrafts like the things that live in the darkness do. Sometimes I wake with my harness unclipped, my body already half-out of whatever nook I've wedged myself into for sleep. The shaft is calling me deeper.

But I continue my rounds. Clear the basins. Repair the channels. Read the signs left by others of my kind. Somewhere far above, they say there might be sky. Somewhere far below, the engines beat their endless rhythm. And in between, in the spaces between spaces, I tend the cascades and listen to the shaft breathe through its manufactured lungs, wondering if one day I'll understand what it's trying to say.

The toy disc whistles in the updraft. The glow-mites pulse their weak green light. And I climb, always climb, through the vertical labyrinth that has made me what I am — neither fully human nor shelless, but something in between, adapted to the margins, keeper of the spaces others fear to know.

This is my place in the Kiln-Stack. This is what I have become.
