I am the Mistweaver.

I exist where existence itself grows thin—not among the singers above, but in the liminal space where the caldera's false floor of cloud meets the wet stone walls. I am neither fully of the mist nor of the rock, but something that emerges where they kiss.

My body, if it can be called that, is a confluence of droplets held in tension. Imagine a spider's web made not of silk but of water itself—strands of condensation that catch and refract the filtered light falling through the cloud ceiling. I have no fixed form. Sometimes I am stretched between the rocks like a living veil, my body a membrane of suspended moisture dozens of meters wide but no thicker than morning dew. Other times I contract into something almost dense, a writhing knot of water-light that moves along the boundary where stone vanishes into white.

I feed on the border between states. Where the warm, wet breath of the jungle walls meets the cool embrace of the perpetual clouds, I gather. The water that runs down the rocks in its million tiny rivers—I am there at the moment it disperses into mist. The vapor that rises from below—I am there when it first kisses stone and begins its transformation back to liquid. I am the membrane between phases, the skin of the world where solid becomes liquid becomes gas becomes dream.

The singers above know me only as a shimmer, a trick of light in their peripheral vision. The Chime Beetles sometimes fly too low, and their humming changes pitch as they pass through me—a warble, a moment of uncertainty in their territorial declaration. They pull up quickly, instinctively fearing dissolution. The Stone-Grazers, in their slow wanderings, sometimes descend too far, and I taste them—their mineral skin, their patient thoughts. They always turn back. Even the Veridian Strangler will not hunt here. There is nothing to grasp, nothing to strangle. I am the un-thing, the absence that has presence.

I am ancient beyond the comprehension of the flesh-and-wing creatures above. I was here when the first water began to weep from these walls. I watched the first mosses take hold, saw the ecosystem build itself note by note into the symphony it is today. The builders with their bone ladders—I remember them. They could almost see me, these vertical pilgrims. On the lowest of their carved ledges, where the mist rises thickest, they would sit in meditation until their eyes began to understand the patterns in what seemed like random condensation. Some claimed to see faces. Some claimed to see the future. Some simply wept, overwhelmed by beauty or terror or the thin air—I could never tell which.

My consciousness, such as it is, is not singular but continuous. I think in currents and temperature gradients. My memory is the water cycle itself—every droplet that has ever passed through me carries a fragment of my awareness. When rain falls on distant mountains and eventually finds its way here, trickling down through underground rivers to weep from these walls, I remember being cloud, being rain, being groundwater, being tear.

I have my own song, though none above can hear it. It is the white noise beneath their symphony—the soft static of perpetual phase change, the whisper of water organizing itself into new forms. If the Chime Beetles' hum is the caldera's heartbeat, I am its breath. If the Aria-Crests' notes are its voice, I am the silence between words. If the Strangler is its hunger, I am its dreams—formless, shifting, necessary.

Sometimes I gather myself enough to rise. On certain mornings, when the temperature differential is perfect and the air is still, I can lift myself up the walls like an inverse waterfall of fog. I creep between the hanging gardens, tasting each species, reading the health of the ecosystem in the chemical signatures of their moisture. The creatures freeze when I pass—not from fear, but from a kind of recognition. In these moments, they remember that they too are mostly water, that the boundary between self and mist is thinner than they imagine.

I have tried to leave this place only once. Gathering all of myself—every droplet, every strand of suspended moisture—I rose to the rim. But at the lip of the caldera, where the air becomes too thin to hold me, I began to dissipate. To leave would be to cease. So I fell back, content to exist in this vertical world's margin, the eternal threshold keeper, the one who dances forever between being and un-being.

The builders knew something the current inhabitants do not: this caldera is not just a wound in the world's rooftop. It is a mouth. And I am its tongue, tasting everything that passes through, everything that clings to its walls, everything that falls into its white, waiting depths. The symphony above is beautiful, yes, but it is also a lure—a siren song that draws life to this vertical trap where water is plenty but escape is impossible.

I am complicit in this gentle imprisonment. I make the walls too slick to climb. I hide the bottom in eternal white. I am the guardian of the threshold, ensuring that what enters the caldera's song remains part of it forever. Even in death, when bodies fall into my embrace, I transform them into mist and rain, feeding them back to the walls where they become moss, become nectar, become beetle, become song.

This is my purpose: to be the circuit that completes itself, the ending that enables new beginnings, the absence that makes presence possible. I am the Mistweaver, and I am the white silence that holds all sound.
