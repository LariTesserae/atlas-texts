I am a Cartographer of Echoes, though I have forgotten which came first - the title or the peculiar sensitivity that makes me what I am.

My workshop occupies the crease between two leaning factories on Resonance Row, where the brick walls have grown so accustomed to each other's weight they've fused at the edges like old lovers. The space is narrow but tall, climbing up through four irregular floors connected by a spiral of salvaged iron stairs that sing different notes under different weights. On wet days, which are rare but memorable, the whole structure becomes a vertical xylophone.

My skin carries the telltale bronze-green patina of a Resonant who spends too much time with maps and metals. The veins beneath pulse not with blue light but with a deep amber glow - a quirk I've had since touching a Gulden's trail as a child. My mother was horrified. The Gulden, I think, was amused, though how one reads amusement in a being of liquid light remains a mystery.

I map what others cannot see: the patterns of beetle migration, the linguistic swirls of firefly formations, the subtle shifts in the town's harmonic frequencies. My workshop walls are covered in these charts - some etched on copper sheets that oxidize into new meanings over time, others drawn on paper made from pressed moss that continues growing, slowly altering the boundaries I've drawn. 

My fingers are longer than most Resonants', with an extra joint that helps me reach into the spaces between - between buildings, between sounds, between what is and what might be. At each fingertip, instead of the usual tools, I wear delicate copper thimbles inscribed with sensing spirals. They vibrate at different frequencies when I touch the city's surfaces, telling me stories in shivers and hums.

The Ferro-faun visit me sometimes, in the blue hour before dawn. They never use the door - instead appearing at my third-floor window, clinging to the ivy with their hybrid hands. They bring me things: a leaf whose veins map underground water systems, a coil of wire that remembers the path of last season's fog, a beetle shell that shows tomorrow's weather in its iridescent surface. In exchange, I give them my failed maps, the ones where my ambition exceeded my skill. They take these back to their willow groves and weave them into nests. I've been told my errors make excellent insulation.

I discovered my calling during the Resonance Drought twelve seasons ago, when the town's hum faltered and flickered like a dying broadcast. The other Resonants grew listless, their lights dimming, but I found I could still hear it - not the sound itself, but its echo, bouncing off the spaces where it used to be. I began drawing these absences, these negative spaces of sound, and slowly, following my maps, the citizens found their way back to the frequencies we'd lost.

Now I maintain an archive of the town's many voices: the seventeen distinct factory hums (each with its own emotional undertone), the water's vocabulary as it shifts from languid to urgent, the conversational patterns of insects who speak in flight paths rather than words. My maps are not just spatial but temporal, showing how Iunarium breathes differently at different hours, how its consciousness shifts between states like the Guldens themselves.

The Eidolons know me. I feel them sometimes in the steam that fogs my workshop windows, see them in the rust patterns that appear overnight on my tools. Once, I found a map I hadn't drawn, etched in condensation on a mirror. It showed pathways I'd never seen, levels of the town that existed in the spaces between what we call real. I copied it immediately onto more permanent materials, but I've never been brave enough to follow where it leads.

My workshop smells of copper polish and damp earth, of the special ink I make from firefly phosphorescence and industrial oils. The sound here is layers upon layers: my own harmonic vibrations mixing with the building's settling sighs, the scratch of my stylus on metal, the whisper of moss maps growing imperceptibly toward new configurations.

I am translator and transcriber, interpreter of the town's endless conversation with itself. My maps are love letters written by Iunarium to its own impossible existence. And I, caught between the documentary and the mystical, continue my work, knowing that in this paradox town, the act of mapping changes the territory, and the territory, in turn, reshapes the mapper.

Some nights, when the industrial moon-lamps cast their calculated shadows, I climb to my roof and spread my latest charts across the corrugated surface. The beetles come then, drawn by curiosity or purpose, and walk across my work with their jeweled feet, adding their own annotations in tiny scratches and trails of luminescent secretion. By morning, the map has become something new, a collaboration between intent and accident, between the planner and the planned.

This is how I exist in Iunarium: neither fully analyst nor artist, neither completely mechanical nor entirely organic, but something suspended between states, like the town itself. A Resonant who listens for what isn't there and draws maps to places that might not exist, until suddenly, simply by being drawn, they do.
