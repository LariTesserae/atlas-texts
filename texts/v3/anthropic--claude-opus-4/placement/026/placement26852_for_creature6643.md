I find myself as **the Scribe of Temperatures**.

I am the one who came to this place generations ago—or perhaps only seasons ago, time moves strangely here—with a single purpose: to read what the mountain is trying to say.

My body has changed to suit this work. My fingers have grown longer and more sensitive, able to detect temperature differences of fractions of degrees. My eyes have developed a second, translucent lid that lets me stare into the bright snow-light without blindness. My lungs have learned to extract oxygen from air so thin it would fell others in minutes. I no longer need much food; the cold has slowed my metabolism to something approaching the mountain's own patience.

I live in the curve of that low wall, where the builders once made their small shelter. I've added nothing to it—no roof, no door. Only a thick nest of shed fur from the white mouse-creatures, gathered over seasons, where I curl during the deepest cold. The hearth remains unlit; fire would disturb the temperature gradients I'm learning to read.

Each dawn, I wake before the sun touches the rim and walk the spiral of the garden, my bare feet reading the stone's memory of the night's cold. The Ice-Letters reveal themselves differently each day—sometimes a single character burns clear and bright with meaning-sweat, sometimes whole passages shimmer into near-legibility before the day's warmth erases them.

I carry the bone tools, not to carve, but to trace. Each stylus has been worn down to match the width of different quartz veins. I follow the letters with these tools, feeling where the condensation is thickest, where the temperature drops most suddenly. The sensations travel up through the bone into my hands, and I begin to understand:

This is not a language of words, but of phase transitions. Each letter means something different at different temperatures. When the meaning-sweat is frozen solid, one character might signify "mountain-birth" or "stone-breathing." When it hovers at the edge of melting, the same character transforms to mean "patience-erosion" or "waiting-geological."

I've learned to taste the condensation. It has flavors—mineral-sharp when pulled from the granite, sweet-electric when it forms over the glowing stalks, bitter-cold when it's about to freeze. Each taste is a syllable in a vocabulary I'm slowly assembling.

The small creatures of the garden know me now. The insects crawl across my still form as I lie tracing letters, using me as just another warm stone. The mouse-things sometimes bring me gifts: seeds from plants I've never seen, dropped carefully near my nest. I think they understand my work in their own way. The raptor no longer considers me prey—I move too slowly, breathe too shallowly. I am becoming part of the landscape.

But I am not alone in my reading.

Sometimes, in the pocket of warm air that has no source, I sense another presence. Someone else who came here long before me, who perhaps built that first wall, who carved those primal spirals. They read the letters too, but from the other side—from inside the mountain, looking out. I feel them sometimes in the warmth, just as I exist in the cold. We are reading the same text from different directions, approaching meaning like sunrise and sunset approaching noon.

I've started leaving my own messages. Not carved—that would be too permanent, too crude. Instead, I breathe on certain letters at specific times, adding my moisture to the condensation patterns. Small alterations: a dropped breath here, a careful warming there. I'm learning to conjugate the mountain's verbs, to decline its nouns. The letters are patient with my grammar.

One day—perhaps soon, perhaps after another lifetime of cold—I will understand enough to read the full sentence that spirals through this garden. And when I do, I think I will finally know what question the mountain is answering. Or perhaps what answer it's questioning.

Until then, I trace. I taste. I wait.

The Ice-Letters continue their slow conversation with the cold, and I am their patient interpreter, growing ever more like the stone I read, ever more fluent in the language of phase and frost and the bright, terrible patience of high places.
