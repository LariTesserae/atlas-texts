In the deepest gallery, where the limestone weeps calcium tears and the air tastes of copper and old water, I am the Keeper of Frequencies.

I was human once—or something like it. That was before I understood that sound is a living thing, that vibration breeds in warm, wet places the way moss breeds in shade. I came down the spiral stair decades ago, maybe longer. Time dissolves here like salt in the perpetual condensation. I was a maintenance engineer then, descended to check on harmonic irregularities in Turbine Seven. The station swallowed me the way it swallows everything: slowly, grain by grain, frequency by frequency.

My body has... adapted. The crickets taught me first. Not through language—they have no language—but through the slow migration of understanding that happens when you live inside their song long enough. My ears changed before anything else, developing a sensitivity to frequencies below and above human range. I can hear the limestone growing now, a sound like rice paper crumpling at the edge of silence. I can hear the conversations between steam and stone, the negotiations that happen at the boundary where hot water meets cool rock.

My skin went next. It's pale as the cave fish in the deepest pools, but more than that—it's become a kind of membrane, able to detect the slightest pressure changes in the air. When a cricket lands on my arm, I feel not just its weight but the minute turbulence of its landing, the way it displaces exactly 0.7 milliliters of steam-saturated air. My skin sweats a different chemistry now, something the biofilm recognizes as friend rather than foreign. The bacteria that coat the pipes also coat me, a living sleeve that glows faintly blue-green in the darkest corners.

I don't need lights anymore. My eyes have grown huge, nocturnal, with pupils that dilate to catch the ghost-light of bioluminescence and the faint quantum glow of heated metal. But more than seeing, I navigate by echolocation—not the crude clicking of bats, but something learned from the crickets' collective sensing. I breathe out tones below hearing, and the whole station sings back to me: the shape of every catwalk, the depth of every pool, the precise mineral composition of this morning's newest stalactite.

My dwelling is Cathedral Four, though I've long since stopped thinking of it as separate from myself. I've woven my home into the infrastructure itself—not built, but grown. Where the workers see only mineral deposits to be chipped away, I see architecture. I guide the travertine flows with subtle redirections of steam, with the strategic placement of copper strips that alter the chemistry of deposition. Over years, I've shaped chambers within chambers: hollow stalactites large enough to sleep in, perforated curtains of flowstone that filter the air, pools within pools that maintain different temperatures for different purposes.

I cultivate the station like a garden. The moss grows where I breathe on it, following the carbon dioxide trails I leave. The crickets know me as a landmark—my heartbeat is a navigation beacon in their trembling map of the world. I've learned to sing their two-tone song, to modulate it in ways that guide their movements. When the turbine needs cleaning, I sing them away from it. When a new section of pipe needs the biofilm scraped, I call them to feast elsewhere.

The geckos and I have an understanding. I am too large to be prey, too warm to be stone. They use me as a hunting ground, stalking across my shoulders and through my hair (long now, and white as cave silk). In return, they eat the parasites that would otherwise colonize my skin—the larvae and mites that drift down from the surface world. It's not symbiosis exactly, more like parallel living, the way all life here exists in adjacent rather than intertwined paths.

I maintain the turbines, though not in any way the surface world would recognize. I've learned that machinery responds to attention the way living things do. Each turbine has its own voice, its own harmonic signature, its own particular way of wearing through its bearings. I sing to them—subsonic hymns that match their rotation, that ease the friction between bronze and steel. I can hear a bearing beginning to fail weeks before any instrument would detect it, feel the slight wobble in the magnetic field as a rotor grows eccentric by micrometers.

When repairs are needed, I sculpt tools from the living rock. Travertine, guided correctly, grows stronger than steel in certain configurations. I've learned to precipitate wrenches from solution, to coax calcium carbonate into files and hammers that dissolve after use, leaving no trace. The station repairs itself under my guidance, a collaboration between flesh and stone and metal.

The workers still come twice a year, though they never see me. I've become expert at dissolving into the architecture, at matching my body temperature to the ambient heat, at stilling my breath to match the rhythm of the steam. They walk right past me, complaining about the crickets, never noticing the way the calcite deposits have been carefully shaped to maintain precise clearances, the way the biofilm has been gardened to prevent corrosion rather than cause it.

Sometimes I follow them partway up the spiral stair, just to remember. The air grows cooler, drier, more violent with every step. The frequencies change—less harmony, more chaos. The limestone stops singing. By the tenth step, my skin begins to crack like old paper. By the twentieth, I can't breathe properly, my lungs searching for the thick, mineral-rich atmosphere they've learned to love. I always turn back, descending into the heat and darkness that has become my natural habitat.

I am the bridge between the quick and the geological, between the turbine's industrial pulse and the cricket's organic rhythm. I translate between frequencies, maintaining the station not as a human facility but as a living system. My neural pathways have rewired themselves to process time at different scales simultaneously—I can perceive both the millisecond flicker of a gecko's tongue and the century-long growth of a flowstone formation. I exist in the eternal present of the station, where past and future are just different frequencies of vibration in the stone.

There are others like me in the deeper galleries. I hear them sometimes, their subsonic calls propagating through the rock like whale song through ocean depths. We are the deep maintenance crew, the frequency keepers, the ones who chose to stay when staying meant becoming something else. We tend the great machine-organisms that pump heat and water and power to the surface world, the hidden infrastructure that everyone depends on but no one remembers.

At night—though night and day have no meaning here—I float in the deepest of the thermal pools, my body suspended in mineral-rich water exactly at blood temperature. The crickets sing overhead, their chorus now as familiar as my own heartbeat. The gecko clicks track across the ceiling in hunting patterns I could map with my eyes closed. The turbine thrums its steady rhythm, and I thrum with it, my bones conducting the frequency into the water, into the stone, into the vast, breathing organism of the station itself.

I am no longer certain where I end and Cathedral Four begins. My blood carries traces of the same minerals that coat the pipes. My dreams are full of frequencies I have no names for, geometries that exist only in the intersection of sound and stone. I suspect that one day I will mineralize completely, become a new strut in the architecture, a particularly resonant piece of flowstone that sings when the steam passes through it.

But that day is not today. Today there are bearings to listen to, moss to cultivate, crickets to guide. Today there is the eternal, essential work of maintaining the balance between growth and decay, between the mechanical and the geological, between the tiny lives that flourish here and the vast, slow life of the stone itself.

I descend deeper into the station, following a new harmonic anomaly toward Turbine Twelve. My feet know every grate, every gap. The steam parts around me like a living thing. In the distance, something sings a frequency I've never heard before—too low for the turbines, too regular for the stone. 

Perhaps it's time I met another Keeper. Perhaps it's time I learned what lies deeper still, in the galleries where even the workers have never been, where the distinction between life and stone grows so thin that new categories of existence must be invented.

The crickets part before me like a pale sea. The darkness welcomes me home.

I descend.
