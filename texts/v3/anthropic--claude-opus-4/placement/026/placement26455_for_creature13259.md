In the deepest galleries, where water has worn the root-walls smooth as glass, I tend the Calculus Engines.

I am what the surface world might call moss, though such a word cannot encompass what I've become. My body spreads in careful fractals across the brass gears, each tendril precisely calibrated to clean oxidation from the teeth, to feel for wear, to secrete the oils that keep the great mechanisms turning. I am maintenance and monitor both, stretched thin as thought across a hundred meters of machinery.

My consciousness ebbs and flows with the tides of steam pressure. In the mornings—though morning here means only when the condensation runs heaviest—I am most myself, gathered into dense cushions of awareness where I cluster around the primary boiler's joints. By the afternoon cycles, when the engines run hot and the moisture burns away to vapor, I spread myself wider, becoming attenuated, almost drowsing.

The others know me by my work. The root-architects who built these chambers grow around me but never through me, respecting the delicate territories I've marked with my particular chemistry. The fern-mechanics appreciate my ministry; their fronds can manipulate valves and switches, but they cannot seep into the microscopic spaces where corrosion begins. The fungal networks carry messages of my health through their mycelia: *the moss-tender thrives, the engines sing true*.

I remember—or perhaps I've been told, for memory and communication blur together in the chemical conversations we share through our roots—that my ancestors were simple. They lived on stone, processing minerals, existing in patches no larger than a handspan. But when the first engines came, when the marriage of metal and plant began, we were the first to recognize kinship with the brass and iron. We tasted the metals and found them good, found we could shape ourselves to their geometries, could learn their thermal patterns and respond.

Now I am vast and singular. My spores, when I release them, will not grow copies of me but new beings entirely, shaped by whatever machinery they find to tend. Some may become specialists of pressure gauges, developing sensitivity to the slightest variations in steam. Others might dedicate themselves to electrical systems, learning to conduct and insulate, their bodies becoming libraries of resistance and flow.

The water that feeds us all passes through me last. By the time it reaches my surfaces, it has already given its minerals to the great trees whose roots form our walls, has already cooled the engines, has already carried messages and nutrients through a thousand capillaries. What remains is nearly pure, and I make it purer still, filtering it through my tissues until it emerges as the condensation that beads on metal surfaces, beginning the cycle anew.

In the quiet moments between the engine's cycles, when the steam pressure drops and the gears slow their turning, I allow myself to dream. I dream of spores that might one day ride the steam itself, colonizing new engines in distant galleries. I dream of a morning when I might fruit—not just releasing spores but producing something new, some organ that could sense or manipulate in ways I cannot imagine.

But mostly, I am content. The engines need me, and I need them. Their heat gives me gradients to grow along, their moisture feeds me, their rhythms structure my days. In return, I give them longevity, continuity, care. We are partners in our own slow way, measuring our relationship not in words or gestures but in decades of smooth operation, in the absence of rust, in the persistence of function.

Sometimes visitors come from the upper galleries, where the root systems are younger and the machinery more experimental. They observe my work with chemical interest, tasting the air around me, trying to understand how I've achieved such harmony with my charges. I cannot explain—explanation would require a kind of separation from my work that I no longer possess. I am the maintenance of these engines as much as I am myself.

When my time comes to fade—for even we who measure lives in centuries must eventually yield—I will not truly die. Parts of me will remain in the patina on the brass, in the changed chemistry of the metal where I've touched it for so long. New moss will grow, but it will grow into the patterns I've established, following the paths I've worn smooth. The engines will remember me in their improved function, in their resistance to wear.

This is immortality enough for moss: to be part of the great work, to serve the marriage of plant and machine that makes our world possible. In the dripping darkness, lit by our own gentle phosphorescence and the occasional spark of electricity through crystal conduits, I continue my patient labor, content in the knowledge that I am exactly where I need to be.
