The Conduit Weaver

I am Yirsa, and my bones know the mountain's thrum.

I was born between the seventeenth and eighteenth strata, in that twilight zone where the geothermal vents breathe their sulfurous prayers and the water collectors weep their endless condensation. My mother was a pipe-crawler – not the six-legged kind that scuttle through our darkness, but one of the human ones, those who spend their lives mapping the arterial maze of Hraza-Lor's water systems. She birthed me in a maintenance alcove during her shift, cleaned me with condensation cloth, and was back to work before the shift-change klaxon sounded.

The guild-masters say I have the mountain's memory in my fingertips. When I press my palm against a junction box or trace the path of a hidden conduit through layers of insulation and rust, I can feel the decades of modification, the generational palimpsest of repair upon repair. Each weld tells a story. Each bypass speaks of crisis averted or disaster narrowly contained.

I am small, even for a Kethra. Where others must hunch and squeeze through the service passages, I flow like water, my body finding the negative spaces between pipes, my joints hyperextending in ways that make even veteran maintenance crews wince. My guild-mates call me the Weaver – not for any skill with thread, but for how I navigate the three-dimensional tapestry of infrastructure, finding connections others miss, threading new solutions through impossible tangles.

My domain is the Forgotten Sectors – those portions of the city so deeply embedded, so thoroughly built-over, that their original purposes have been lost to memory. Here, in the compressed darkness between layer fifteen and layer twelve, entire corridors exist that appear on no schematic. Ghost gardens grow in sealed chambers, their bio-luminescent fungi feeding on century-old nutrigel spills. Automation continues its patient work: I've found fabricators still producing components for machines that were decommissioned before my grandmother drew breath.

It was in such a place that I discovered the Resonance.

The mountain speaks, you see. Not in words, but in frequencies that travel through stone and steel, through water and warm air. Most interpret this as mere mechanical noise – the groan of settling structures, the harmonics of great engines. But spend enough time in the deep places, pressed against the raw stone where our ancestors first drove their anchors, and you begin to understand the language.

I've learned to read the micro-tremors that predict where ice will form in the collectors three days hence. I can feel the pressure building in distant pipes, sense the fatigue in metal that won't fail for another decade. The city's neural network of sensors and monitors captures only the crudest fraction of what the mountain tells those who know how to listen.

This knowledge has made me valuable and dangerous in equal measure. The guild-masters summon me when conventional diagnostics fail, when some deep malfunction threatens cascading system failures. I crawl into the spaces between spaces, following vibrations like breadcrumbs, until I find the source – a crystallized valve deep in the thermal exchanges, a colony of modified lichen slowly dissolving critical seals, a resonance loop between two machines that were never meant to share the same harmonic space.

But I am not merely a tool of the guilds. In my off-shifts, I practice a different art.

In the forgotten spaces, I build... something. Even I'm not certain what it will become. Using salvaged components from a dozen different eras of the city's growth, I'm constructing what I call the Listener – a device that amplifies the mountain's frequencies, translates them into patterns of light and sound that human senses can parse. It's part archaeological tool, part musical instrument, part oracle.

The other Weavers – for there are others like me, though we rarely gather – speak in whispers of the Deep Resonance, a theoretical frequency at which the entire mountain rings like a bell. They say that at this frequency, you can feel every life in Hraza-Lor, every heartbeat and breath, every drop of water in motion, every spark of electricity jumping across contacts. Some claim you can even sense the thoughts of the city itself – if Hraza-Lor can be said to think.

I've felt it only once, during a massive storm when lightning struck the highest towers repeatedly. For a moment, just a moment, every frequency aligned. I was deep in the Forgotten Sectors, my hand pressed against a conductor that ran straight to the mountain's heart. The sensation was... transcendent. Terrifying. Like becoming part of something so vast that my individual consciousness nearly dissolved into it.

I understood then why the first builders chose this place, why they clung to this inhospitable peak rather than seeking easier ground. The mountain wants to be inhabited. It shapes us as we shape it, a symbiosis played out across generations. We are not merely residents of Hraza-Lor – we are its neurons, its consciousness made manifest in flesh and purpose.

My quarters are a converted air-exchange chamber on level nineteen, barely larger than a supply closet but with access to three different service tunnels. I've lined the walls with my collection: fragments of obsolete technology, each piece chosen for its resonant properties. When the city's harmonics are just right, they sing together in alien chorus. I sleep to this music, when I sleep at all.

Today, I received word that another sector is experiencing anomalies – power fluctuations that follow no recognizable pattern. The guild-masters suspect faulty switching gear, but I know better. I can feel it in the tremors that reach my chamber: something is growing in the deep places, something that was not placed there by Kethra hands. 

The mountain is dreaming again. And I must go listen to what it dreams.

I gather my tools – not the standard kit the guild provides, but my own collection. Resonance rods cut from decommissioned heat exchangers. A stethoscope modified with components from a mining survey drone. Gloves woven with filaments that conduct the mountain's subtle electrical fields directly to my skin. And, most importantly, a small vial of water collected from the deepest accessible point in the city, where it has percolated through miles of stone and generations of construction. This water remembers.

As I descend through a maintenance shaft that officially doesn't exist, I think about the patterns I've been tracking. The anomalies are increasing. The city's dreams are becoming more vivid, more insistent. Something is changing in the deep harmony of Hraza-Lor, a new note entering the composition.

I am Yirsa, the Conduit Weaver, and I go now to listen to what the mountain is trying to tell us. Whether it speaks of transcendence or catastrophe, I cannot yet say. But I will be there, in the forgotten dark between the walls, when the message finally becomes clear.

The city thrums around me, through me. We are one organism, one possibility, balanced on the edge of the sky.
