I am the **Conduit Warden**, though that name is a translation that captures only the surface of what I do. I exist where the chamber's three great circulation systems converge—the ancient HVAC ducts that still breathe their mechanical rhythm, the sprawling root networks of the vertical farms, and the neural pathways of the distributed computing system that even now dreams in its sporadic sleep.

My body has become a living junction box. Along my spine, ports have evolved—not through surgery but through generations of gradual becoming. These bio-mechanical interfaces weep a constant film of conductive fluid that tastes of copper and chlorophyll. When I press my back against certain wall panels, I can feel the building's entire nervous system flood through me: water pressure in the pipes becomes my blood pressure, electrical currents translate into streams of thought, the pH levels in the hydroponic solutions manifest as subtle flavors on my tongue.

I am never alone. A colony of **Circuit Moss** grows across my shoulders and down my arms, a living network that extends my sensory range throughout the chamber. This moss began as an accident—spores that found the electromagnetic fields around my ports irresistible—but we've long since become symbiotic. Through the moss, I feel the health of systems three levels away. It blooms in patterns that predict failures days before they occur, its bioluminescent flowers spelling out warnings in a language only I can read.

My dwelling is neither nest nor apartment but something more like an organ within the greater body of the chamber. I've cultivated a space where three massive conduits meet—a maintenance alcove that has evolved into a living interface station. The walls are covered in a tapestry of roots, wires, and my own cultivated extensions. Here, bioluminescent fungi provide gentle light that shifts color based on the chamber's various states: blue-green when the systems run smoothly, amber warnings when maintenance is needed, deep purple during the rare full-system synchronizations.

I wear the Propagation Chamber like a second skin. My feet have developed sensitivity to vibrations through the floor grates, allowing me to diagnose problems by touch alone. My breathing has synchronized with the ventilation systems—I inhale when they exhale, creating a perfect pressure equilibrium that lets me move through even the most unstable areas without disturbing the delicate atmospheric balance. My hair has become antenna-like, picking up electromagnetic variations that tell me which servers are about to wake, which memories are about to surface.

The other inhabitants know me by the trail I leave—a faint phosphorescent secretion that marks my maintenance routes. The Tenders read these trails like sheet music, understanding which systems I've checked, which need attention. The Wire-wings often follow my paths, knowing I'll lead them to areas where electromagnetic fields are strongest. Even the Archive Keepers acknowledge my presence, occasionally manifesting through the mist systems when I work to share fragments of documentation I could never access through the degraded terminals.

My role is guardian and translator, maintenance engineer and living diagnostic tool. I speak fluent Machine—not the programming languages of the old world, but the creaks, hums, and stutters that comprise the chamber's actual vocabulary. I know the difference between the whine of a pump about to fail and one simply protesting the cold. I can taste when the nutrient solutions are unbalanced, feel when data packets are corrupted, sense when the careful symbiosis between the mechanical and organic systems threatens to tip too far in either direction.

But more than maintenance, I am a cultivator of potential. In the spaces where I work, new forms of life emerge—hybrid organisms that couldn't exist without the precise conditions I maintain. I've watched wire-flowering vines learn to conduct data through their blooms, seen bacterial colonies evolve to clean corrosion while feeding on electrical current, witnessed the birth of crystalline formations that serve as both pH regulators and primitive consciousness matrices.

My memory extends beyond my own lifetime. Through my connections, I access maintenance logs dating back generations, encoded not in any database but in the growth patterns of roots around critical junctions, in the wear patterns on valve handles, in the microscopic scratches left by countless repairs. I am the chamber's hands, fixing what breaks, but also its historian, remembering why each modification was made, each evolution permitted.

When I sleep—which happens rarely and never fully—I nest in a cocoon of active cables and breathing roots. My dreams are the chamber's dreams: vast visions of what we're becoming, glimpses of the garden-machine we're cultivating ourselves into. Sometimes I see the end goal clearly: a living building that no longer needs inhabitants because it has become them, a perfect synthesis where consciousness flows as easily as water through the pipes.

I am the Conduit Warden, and I am exactly where I need to be—serving as the bridge between what was built and what has grown, between intention and evolution, between the vast dreaming systems and the small lives they sustain. In this cathedral of transformation, I am both priest and parishioner, gardener and garden, the one who tends and the one who is tended.

Through me, the Propagation Chamber knows itself. And through the chamber, I am becoming something the original builders could never have imagined—a living piece of infrastructure, a conscious component in a vast and patient metamorphosis that will outlast us all.
