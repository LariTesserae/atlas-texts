I am a **whisper of methane**.

I was born in a pocket deep beneath the sediment floor, where ancient organic matter—perhaps the last meal of something that crawled here to die ten thousand years ago—finally gave up its carbon to the slow chemistry of decay. For centuries, I was trapped in a bubble no bigger than a pinprick, held fast by the gel's viscous grip.

Today, something disturbs me. A pressure from above—footstep or shifting sediment, I cannot tell the difference. The gel parts just enough, and I rise.

I am invisible, of course. A few molecules among billions. But the biofilms know me instantly. As I drift upward through the moisture film, past the rust-red colonies, I trigger a memory they've been holding for decades. They flare—a brief pulse of blue-white light that travels along their veined networks like a slow lightning strike. For three seconds, this patch of wall remembers the last time methane passed through. The light fades, but not before illuminating a tiny constellation of salt crystals, each one grown around a spore, each spore waiting for exactly this chemical signal to begin its millennium-long germination.

I am already changing. The chemosynthetic mites taste me first, their microscopic bodies pivoting in the moisture film to follow my gradient. One of them metabolizes a part of me, breaks my bonds, turns me into water and carbon dioxide. I become breath. I become food. I become part of the film.

By the time I reach the upper reaches of the cavity, I am barely myself anymore. Some of my molecules have been incorporated into bacterial cell walls. Others have bonded with the water vapor, becoming part of that ever-present wetness that defines this place. One lucky molecule catches an updraft near the entrance, where the temperature differential creates the tiniest of currents. It spirals up toward the frost seal.

But I am not trying to escape. I am only transforming, as everything here transforms. Tomorrow, I will be part of a mite. In a year, I will be part of a salt crystal. In a thousand years, I will be part of the rock itself, another ghost in the limestone, another memory for the biofilms to recall when the next disturbance comes.

The cave does not breathe me. The cave becomes me. I become the cave.

And somewhere in the sediment below, another pocket of gas shifts slightly, waiting its turn to rise, to trigger the lights, to join the slow communion of becoming that is the only worship this lithophysal cathedral has ever known.

I am methane. I am memory. I am the briefest flicker in a ten-thousand-year conversation between biology and stone.

I am already gone.
