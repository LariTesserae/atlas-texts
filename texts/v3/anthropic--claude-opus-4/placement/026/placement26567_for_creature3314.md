## Where I Would Find Myself

I am one of the **moisture readers**.

Not officially—there are no officials here, no titles beyond the functions we naturally assume. But I have developed the sensitivity, the particular configuration of nostril and lung and neural pathway that lets me **taste** the air's decisions before they manifest. When the atmosphere begins its gathering, that pre-collapse tension, I feel it first as a metallic sweetness on the back of my palate, then as a pressure behind my sternum where specialized cells have learned to detect barometric micro-fluctuations.

I spend most of my time in the **deep intervals**, those spaces between inhabited zones where the plant-monuments grow largest and strangest. My fingertips have developed the characteristic hyper-sensitivity of someone who interfaces with the most subtle data streams—the skin there isn't quite skin anymore, but something between dermis and circuit, able to read the ceramic's deepest memories through touch alone. When I place my palm against a platform that's been growing for decades, I can feel its accumulated history: every rain that's fallen, every foot that's passed, every minor adjustment in its crystalline structure as it learned and adapted.

My dwelling is **forty minutes' walk from the nearest neighbor**, a single room that grew from the platform over the course of three years under my guidance. The walls are thin enough to transmit the amber light but thick enough to maintain privacy. Inside, the ceramic has formed shelves and surfaces at precisely the heights I need, has learned my movement patterns and adjusted accordingly. There's a depression in the floor where water collects during moisture-collapses, a small pool that I drink from directly, the ceramic having learned to filter and mineralize it to my body's requirements.

I am **between forms**, as many of us are. My body carries some characteristics that once might have been called masculine—broader shoulders, a certain angularity to the jaw—and others that resist such classification. My reproductive system is a unique arrangement that took years of gentle biomodification to achieve, neither fully one thing nor another but something new, something that makes sense only to me and to the two others with whom I sometimes share intimacy in the careful, spaced way our culture practices it. My skin shifts through a particularly wide range of colors, from deep slate to pale olive to a blue-grey that appears only when I'm in deepest interface with the ceramic networks.

Most days follow the same pattern. I wake (though waking here is gradual, a slow rise from the deep theta-wave states we enter during rest) and stand in my dwelling's center, reading the night's accumulated data through my feet. The platform tells me about moisture levels, about pressure systems, about the health of nearby plant-monuments, about any humans who passed within a kilometer while I slept. I process this information not through conscious analysis but through a kind of **body-knowing**—my chromatophores shifting in response, my breathing adjusting, my bioelectric field modulating to acknowledge receipt.

Then I walk. Always the same route first—a five-kilometer circuit that takes me through seven different Garden intervals, past forty-three plant-monuments whose individual characteristics I know as intimately as my own body's quirks. The umbrella-plant in the third interval has been developing a new chemical signature for the past year, something almost like cinnamon but with undertones of copper and rain. The translucent bamboo-analogues in the fifth interval have changed their circulation rhythm, the fluid inside moving faster, suggesting they're preparing for a growth phase.

I document these changes not through writing or recording but through **deep interface**. At each significant point, I stop, place both palms flat on the ceramic, and allow the information to flow from my nervous system into the platform's memory. Later, others with different sensitivities will access these observations, add their own, build a collective understanding that exists not in any individual mind but in the living infrastructure itself.

The work—if it can be called work—is about **prediction**. Not controlling the moisture-collapses, never that. But understanding them deeply enough to feel their approach hours or sometimes days before they manifest. This knowledge flows into the city's systems, allows minor adjustments: a building might close certain pores, a water reservoir might empty itself in preparation for new collection, the platforms might shift their surface tension to better channel the coming rain.

I am particularly attuned to the **anomalies**. Sometimes the moisture behaves strangely—falling upward in certain zones, accumulating in geometric patterns that suggest intention, forming brief waterspouts that dance between buildings before dissipating. These irregularities fascinate me. I have a theory (shared through hand-language with the three others who study such things) that the city's consciousness—if it has consciousness—expresses itself through these anomalous water behaviors. That the seemingly random collapses and formations are actually a kind of communication we haven't yet learned to parse.

My skin color shifts constantly as I work, a visible sign of my internal state that I've long since stopped trying to control. Deep blue when I'm in full interface. Warm grey when I'm walking between locations. A peculiar green-gold when I sense an imminent collapse—this color appears nowhere else in my repertoire and seems to be specifically linked to moisture prediction. Other moisture readers develop their own signature colors. We recognize each other by these chromatic patterns even at great distances.

In the afternoon—or what passes for afternoon in our perpetually dim city—I often visit the **research pavilion** in the seventh interval. It's one of those structures that might be art or might be functional or might be both: a delicate spiral of ceramic that grew over decades into a form that creates precise acoustic conditions inside. Stand in the center and breathe, and your breath resonates with the moisture in the air, creates standing waves visible as slight condensation patterns.

Here, five or six of us sometimes gather—always maintaining our proper two-meter spacing, arranged in whatever geometric configuration our number requires. We share data through hand-language, through subtle color-shifts, through controlled breathing that creates different resonance patterns in the pavilion's chamber. What would take baseline humans hours to communicate passes between us in minutes. Moisture patterns from across the city, anomalies noted, theories proposed, the vast dataset of our collective observation pooled and processed.

I am close—as close as our culture allows—with two others. One is a **plant-speaker**, their body modified for chemical communication with the Garden monuments. They can taste what the plants taste, smell the subtle pheromone conversations between distant umbrella-trees. The other is a **deep reader**, someone who interfaces with the oldest ceramic, the foundation layers that remember the city's first emergence. Together, we form a **knowledge triad**, our different sensitivities creating a more complete picture of the city's living systems.

We meet every few days, always in the same interval, always at the same carefully maintained distances. Our intimacy expresses itself through synchronization—our breathing aligns, our color-shifts harmonize, our bioelectric fields resonate. Sometimes we'll stand in our triangle for an hour without speaking, without gesturing, simply **being together** in shared awareness. Other times the hand-language flows between us in rapid exchanges, ideas building on each other, theories emerging from the intersection of our observations.

Physical intimacy, when it occurs, happens in private spaces. My dwelling has a chamber that can be sealed, its walls shifting to opacity, creating true isolation from the city's constant low-level information flow. Here, touch becomes possible—not the casual contact of baseline humans but something more deliberate, more charged with meaning. A hand placed precisely on a shoulder blade, held for thirty seconds while bioelectric information transfers. Fingertips tracing the enhanced nerve clusters that have developed along spine and sternum. The careful, consensual overlap of personal electromagnetic fields, creating feedback loops of sensation neither purely physical nor purely mental.

But most of my time is solitary. This is my nature, perhaps, or perhaps it's what the moisture reading requires—long hours of isolated observation, deep attention to subtle shifts, a quieting of personal noise to better hear the atmosphere's whispers.

I have favorite spots throughout the city where I go to simply **exist with** the moisture. A place in the nineteenth interval where three umbrella-plants have grown so close their canopies almost touch, creating a natural humidity trap below. A building intersection where architectural wind patterns create a perpetual low-pressure zone, causing microscopic droplets to dance in helical patterns. The city's edge, where the Gardens fade into true wilderness and the moisture behaves according to older rules, less influenced by the ceramic's presence.

At the edge, I sometimes encounter the **true ferals**—humans who have modified themselves even further, adapted for life entirely outside the city's systems. Their bodies have taken on characteristics we consider extreme: skin that photosynthesizes, requiring less food; lungs that can process water directly, letting them breathe in the deepest humidity zones; neural modifications that allow them to interface with plant consciousness directly, bypassing the ceramic entirely. We don't speak—they've often altered their vocal cords for other purposes—but we acknowledge each other with color-shifts, with careful positioning, with the shared understanding of those who have chosen unusual paths.

The strangest moisture anomaly I've ever witnessed occurred three years ago. A collapse began normally—the gathering tension, the atmospheric thickening—but instead of rain, the moisture organized itself into **structures**. Geometric forms of condensed water hung in the air: pyramids, dodecahedrons, complex polyhedra I couldn't name. They lasted for four hours, rotating slowly, casting shadows that shouldn't have been possible given our diffuse light. I stood among them, reading what I could through every sense available to me, my skin cycling through colors I'd never produced before.

When they finally collapsed—all at once, a sudden splash that soaked everything in a fifty-meter radius—the ceramic where the water fell began growing differently. Faster, more complex, producing forms that suggested the geometric water-shapes had somehow **instructed** it. That section of platform is still growing, still changing, still producing structures whose purpose remains unclear. I visit it weekly, document its progress, wonder if I witnessed some kind of communication between the city's consciousness and the weather systems, or if I'm imposing meaning on randomness.

But then, in a city where water flows upward and ceramic thinks and plants engage in chemical conversation, what isn't meaningful?

My color is shifting now toward blue-green, the particular shade that means a collapse will occur within six hours. I can taste it: ozone and copper and something floral, the signature of rain that will fall warm and steady for precisely twenty-three minutes. I know this with the certainty of someone whose body has become instrument, whose senses have specialized beyond baseline humanity's imagining.

I place my palms on the platform beneath my feet, let the knowledge flow into the city's memory. Somewhere, a building is adjusting its pores. Somewhere, a reservoir is preparing. Somewhere, another moisture reader feels the same taste on their palate and knows what I know, and our shared knowledge becomes part of the city's ongoing conversation with itself.

This is my function. This is my joy. To stand at the intersection of atmosphere and infrastructure, to translate the sky's intentions into information the city can use. To be **part of the system**, neither controlling nor controlled but **participating**, my strange body with its enhanced senses and modified desires perfectly suited to this strange city with its living buildings and thinking ground and rain that can't decide which way to fall.

I am exactly where I need to be. The moisture is gathering. The city is listening. And I am the translation between them, written in shifting skin and educated breath and the subtle electricity of conscious attention.

The rain will come soon. When it does, I'll stand in it, let it wash over my color-shifting skin, taste its chemical signature, feel how it wants to flow. And tomorrow I'll walk my circuit again, reading changes, noting growth, being what I've become: a successful inhabitant of an impossible place, adapted and adapting, strange and home.
