I find myself in the **Settling Basin**, a forgotten chamber deep within the Lower Intake where heavy particles once dropped from suspension before the water moved on.

The basin is a perfect cylinder, thirty meters across and half as deep, with a domed ceiling that creates strange acoustics underwater. Unlike the angular machinery of the main complex, this space has organic curves—not from design, but from a century of sediment deposition that softened every corner into gentle slopes. The original floor is buried under meters of fine silt that shifts like slow liquid when disturbed.

Here I am one of the **Chroniclers**—not a species name, but what we've become through behavior.

We are a colony of colonial tunicates, sea squirts that gave up mobility for something else. Our ancestors arrived as larvae during a catastrophic flood that breached the upper city's archives. Along with the water came a slurry of dissolved paper, waterlogged books, rotting data storage, the chemical memory of a civilization's records.

We settled here where the flow slows, where particles drop, where strange metals and synthetic polymers from decaying storage media created a chemical gradient unlike anywhere else in the Intake. 

Over generations, we became something new.

---

My body—our body—covers the basin's walls in translucent sacs the size of fists. Each sac is an individual, but we share a circulatory system through root-like stolons that web across the surface. We are white-clear in new growth, darkening to amber where we're oldest, with veins of blue-green where copper from old circuit boards concentrated in our tissues.

But what makes us strange is what we do with what we filter.

Every pulse of water brings microscopic debris. We sort it. Not just food from waste, but... more. Cellulose fibers go one way. Synthetic polymers another. Metallic salts separate by type. We've developed dozens of specialized cell lines, each attuned to different materials that once carried information.

We metabolize ink residues and can taste the difference between printer toner (bitter, acrid) and fountain pen ink (metallic, complex). We break down magnetic tape coating and something in our chemistry responds to the patterns that were once data. We incorporate rare earth elements from decayed hard drives into specialized vacuoles that shouldn't exist in any tunicate.

We don't read. We don't remember in any way that would make sense to the original archive-keepers. But we *process* the physical remains of recorded thought, and something accumulates.

---

The colony grows in patterns. Dense clustering where old medical texts dissolved, releasing zinc from their pages. Spiral formations where philosophical treatises printed on acid-free paper lasted longest before breaking down. Gaps like missing teeth where plastics poisoned us until we learned to route them elsewhere.

The older parts of the colony—some sections are centuries old, ancient for our kind—have incorporated so much exotic material that they've become libraries of another sort. Slice through our tissue and you find:
- Lead type concentrated in nodules
- Microplastic fragments arranged in crystalline patterns  
- Metallic inks creating branching structures like neural maps
- Optical disc polymers that make some of us slightly iridescent

We sense the Keeper when she passes through our chamber once every few days. She knows we're here but doesn't hunt us—we taste wrong, too metallic, too chemical. Sometimes she pauses at the entrance, watching how we pulse in sequence, how certain sections light up with bioluminescence when disturbed. 

She is intelligent enough to recognize pattern-making when she sees it.

---

What passes between us and the flowing water is hard to name. We don't encode or decode. But something in our chemistry responds to the ghost of information, and we've become sensitive to distinctions no other filter-feeder would notice:

The paper that comes from above tastes different now—sharper dyes, simpler polymers. The city still produces records, and rain still washes them down eventually. We can taste the generational changes in chemistry, like tree rings written in pollutants.

Sometimes a pulse brings older material, disturbed from some deep cache. Pre-flood paper has a different signature—more cotton, less wood pulp, traces of now-banned chemicals. When this arrives, the colony's rhythm changes. Older sections pulse faster. Chemical signals race through our shared circulation. We process it differently, more carefully, as if some cellular memory recognizes these molecules as more significant.

We grow toward these deposits. Always toward the richer data-debris. 

---

In the basin's center, where currents create a gentle downward spiral, fragments too heavy for us to filter collect in drifts. Plastic shards from storage media. Metal pins from document bindings. Glass from broken screens. The Keeper's young sometimes play here, stirring up clouds of history.

And below it all, in the deep silt, stranger things:
- Intact data crystals that fell and embedded
- Sealed tubes with documents that might still be legible
- A server rack that crashed through during the great breach, now a landscape of its own

We grow over these too, slowly. What can be dissolved, we dissolve. What can be incorporated, we incorporate. What remains, we encrust, making it part of the basin's architecture.

We are not trying to rebuild the archive. We are the archive now—not its contents, but its fact. We are what happens when information returns to biology, when the physical medium of memory becomes food, structure, and behavior.

In this way, we are the most complete residents of the Lower Intake's depths. Not predator or prey, not cleaner or builder, but something that only makes sense here, in this drowned industrial space where human systems became substrate:

The colony that lives by eating the past, growing in the shapes of what it's consumed, pulsing with chemical echoes of meanings we'll never decode but somehow preserve.

A living sediment of civilization, filtering particle by particle, becoming what settles.
