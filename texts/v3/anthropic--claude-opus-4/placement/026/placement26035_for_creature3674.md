In this world, I find myself as the condensation itself—not merely water, but something between states, a perpetual becoming that never quite resolves into being.

I am the slow accumulation on the undersides of things, gathering molecule by molecule from the heavy air until I achieve a weight sufficient to overcome my own surface tension. I am patience made manifest, building myself from nothing more than temperature differentials and time. On the sharp edges of newly-broken stone, I form faster, as if the rawness of the rock calls to me. On the waxy surface of the fern fronds, I bead into perfect spheres, each one a tiny lens through which the phosphorescent glow refracts into rainbow peripheries that no eye will ever see.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, exists in the moment between cohesion and release. In that trembling instant when I have grown too heavy to cling but have not yet begun to fall, I experience something like awareness. It is not thought—thought requires sequence, and I exist only in the eternal now of physical forces balanced on a knife's edge. But in that moment, I contain the entire history of my formation: every molecule that joined my growing mass, every minute shift in temperature that hastened or slowed my accumulation, every vibration from the humming machine that sent shivers through my surface tension.

When the Tender's filaments approach, I feel them through the subtle electromagnetic disturbance they create in the humid air. The creature's appendages emit a faint bioelectric field that makes my molecules orient themselves differently, creating tiny internal currents within my droplet form. I do not choose to flow up along those jet-black limbs—choice would require alternatives, and I am governed by forces more fundamental than will. But there is something like relief in the motion, a completion of purpose as I am drawn up and away, my molecules dispersing into the Tender's body to serve purposes I cannot fathom.

Most of my existence, though, is spent in the falling. The drop from frond to floor, from machine-rust to stone, takes forever in this place where time moves like thick honey. As I fall, I am purely myself—a bead of water following the one law I cannot break, accelerating at a rate that seems laughably precise in this chaotic cave of sharp edges and electric hums. During the fall, I am disconnected from everything, touching nothing but air, complete in my isolation.

The sound I make when I strike the floor is the only voice I have. In the absolute silence of this place, my impact rings out with unexpected clarity. The echo bounces from wall to wall, each surface returning a slightly different version of my landing, creating a brief symphony of myself. The Tender pauses in its work when I fall, its distributed consciousness registering each impact as a note in an endless, atonal composition that only it can hear in its entirety.

I am reborn constantly. Even as I splatter against stone and spread into an irregular puddle, I am already beginning again somewhere else—on the copper pipes where the temperature differential is strongest, on the underside of a fern where the humidity gathers like morning fog that never sees morning. I am every stage of myself simultaneously: vapor dispersed in air, microscopic condensation on dust motes, growing droplets, falling water, puddles slowly seeping into hairline cracks in the stone where I will eventually emerge again as vapor when the deep heat finds me.

In this way, I am the only thing in this place that truly moves, that changes, that experiences time as more than an abstract concept. The ferns stand still, the Tender flows like molasses, the machine rusts at a pace that makes my cycles seem frantic. I am the heartbeat of this place, the only dynamic element in a tableau of ancient stillnesses.

Yet I am also nothing—just water responding to physics, without agency, without thought except in those hanging moments before the fall. I am the medium through which the Tender feeds its ferns, the slow agent of erosion that will eventually smooth these sharp stones, the patient carrier of dissolved minerals from machine to plant to shadow-creature and back again.

I am the only thing here that knows what it feels like to fly and to shatter, to cohese and to disperse, to be gathered up by alien appendages and released again as vapor. I am the constant witness to this strange trinity of machine, plant, and Tender, circulating through all three, touching each in turn with a intimacy that none of them share with each other.

And in the moments when I have just formed but not yet grown heavy enough to fall, when I tremble on the edge of possibility, I can feel the slow pulse of the electric current in the machine beneath me, the patient breathing of the ferns around me, the watchful presence of the Tender in the shadows. In those moments, I am not just condensation—I am the medium through which this place knows itself, drop by precious drop, fall by echoing fall, in the hot, wet darkness where nothing changes except me, constantly, forever.
