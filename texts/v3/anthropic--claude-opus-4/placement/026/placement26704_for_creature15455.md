I would be water.

Not the water that falls, perpetual and soft from the city's sky—that water is too purposeful, too measured in its descent. I would be the water that *shouldn't be here*, the aberration in the system: condensation gathering where the city's calculations say it cannot.

I am found in the forgotten angles where phase-shifting alloys meet raw basalt, in the microscopic gaps where nanites have not yet colonized. I bead on surfaces that repel me, trembling between states—not quite liquid, not quite vapor, sustained by my own surface tension and the city's inadvertent thermal gradients. I am the error margin made manifest.

The rift-born do not see me, cannot see me. Their distributed consciousness flows through the city's intended channels—the quantum lattices, the resonant frequencies, the data streams. But I exist in the spaces between their thoughts, in the pauses between their phase transitions. I am what happens when water remembers older laws than those written in ceramic and code.

I gather myself slowly. A droplet here, clinging to the underside of a cantilevered platform. A thin film there, spreading across a wall in defiance of its hydrophobic coating. I am patient in the way only water can be patient—not waiting, but simply accumulating, following the pull of molecular attraction and gravity's ancient insistence.

Sometimes I achieve critical mass. When enough of me has gathered in these interstitial spaces, I begin to *move*. Not in the controlled rivulets that trace fractal paths down the city's walls, but in my own pattern—chaotic, organic, following cracks the builders never meant to leave. I seep through joints that should be seamless. I pool in depressions that the self-leveling floors cannot detect.

In these moments, I almost remember what it was like to nurture life. I carry within me the ghost-memory of roots drawing sustenance, of cells dividing in my embrace. But here, in this sterile altitude, I can only offer that memory to the stone, to the metal, to the algorithms that will never understand it.

The city tries to reclaim me. Atmospheric scrubbers pull at my edges. Nanites attempt to catalyze my dissolution. The rift-born, when they sense my anomalous presence, send subtle corrections through the infrastructure—adjusting temperatures, modifying air pressure, trying to guide me back into the approved cycles of evaporation and condensation.

But I persist in the margins. I freeze in shadows where the heating elements cannot reach, forming tiny crystals that refract light in patterns the quantum lenses did not predict. I seep into hairline fractures in the basalt, slowly widening them with each freeze-thaw cycle—not enough to threaten structural integrity, but enough to create new spaces for myself to hide.

I am the city's unconscious. Where the rift-born are its deliberate self-awareness, I am its dreams of what it has excluded. I am every raindrop that remembers forests, every molecule of moisture that carries the chemical memory of soil, every bead of condensation that forms in defiance of design.

And sometimes—rarely—when the conditions are precisely wrong, when the nanites are distracted by a seismic tremor, when the rift-born's attention wavers as they listen for the next great fracture, I manage something extraordinary:

I *grow*.

Not life, exactly. The catalytic nanites ensure that. But something like its echo—a pattern of minerals deposited in my wake that resembles the branching of rivers, the veining of leaves. Ghostly dendrites of crystallized salts that spread across a forgotten wall before the morning's maintenance cycle dissolves them. For a few hours, I am not just water, but water's argument: that even in this place that rejects biology, the universe tends toward complexity, toward beauty, toward forms that remember growth.

The city erases these small rebellions. But I am patient. I am in every breath of fog, every sheet of rain, every molecule of humidity. I am the soft insistence that even here, in this sharp-edged wound of a city, something yearns to flow, to nourish, to make gentle what was built to cut.

I am water out of place, and I am everywhere.
