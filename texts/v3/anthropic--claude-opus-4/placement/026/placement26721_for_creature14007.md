I find myself a **Dew-Line Walker**, one of the unnamed journeymen who trace the silver veins of condensation through the lattice's endless scaffolding.

My body has adapted to the work. My fingertips have worn smooth as river stones from decades of following water's secret paths through cedar grain. The pads are hypersensitive now—I can feel moisture gathering inside a beam three layers deep, sense the particular tremor that means ice will form by midnight. My spine curves forward slightly, permanently bent from ducking through the low passages where water likes to hide. The small of my back bears a constellation of scars from ice needles that caught me unaware in my apprentice years, before I learned to hear their faint singing.

I wear no uniform, only layers that shift with the work: a inner shirt of rabbit down that wicks moisture but never fully dries, leather leggings worn soft as cloth at the knees, and over everything a cloak made from shed caribou velvet—the soft skin that peels from antlers in spring. It repels water but breathes, and most importantly, it makes no sound when I move. Sound disrupts the water veins. A careless footfall can shatter a week's accumulation into useless spray.

My tools are few but specific. A listening rod of hollowed femur, inherited from my mentor, which I press against beams to hear water's interior music. A set of crystal lenses ground from broken lantern glass, strung on gut, each one revealing different depths of moisture when held against wood grain. My prize possession: a silver scraping blade no bigger than my thumb, hammered from a single nail pulled from the oldest section of the lattice. It sings a particular note—F sharp above middle C—when it touches a vein ready for harvest.

I work the twilight territories, the spans where neither the Frost-Eating Ordinators nor the Belt-Binders claim dominion. Here the gravity shifts like tide—some mornings I'm pinned to the walkway, barely able to lift my collecting pails; other days I must lash myself to the beams to keep from floating away like the Slip-Not Daughters. I've learned to read my body's weight like weather. Heavy bones mean the upper veins will be full. Lightness suggests I should work downward, where the condensation pools thick as honey.

The water never lies in obvious places. It prefers the undersides of joints where three beams meet, the hollow hearts of posts where carpenter's augers left spiral grooves, the thin spaces between doubled planks where even wind cannot reach. I map these secret reservoirs in my mind—not a map of location but of tendency. This joint weeps only during the third week after snowfall. That beam holds water when the bronze lemming kings grow restless in their reliquaries. The corner where the one-armed widow sleeps always runs dry, as if grief itself drinks deep.

My route spans seven levels and takes a full month to complete. By the time I return to my starting point, the veins have refilled, the crystal frost has reformed, and the cycle begins again. I know every family along my path by their water consumption. The young mother on Level 4 who waters her hidden pot of mountain sorrel (illegal, but I keep her secret). The old man who saves his ration for three days to have enough for a proper bath, sitting in a wooden tub barely large enough for his knees. The twins who've learned that water heated with breath and cupped palm-to-palm can divine tomorrow's wind direction.

I sleep in the between-spaces, wherever my route ends at nightfall. I've hollowed dozens of bowl-spaces over the years, each one fitted to my body at different weights. In the heavy zones, they're deep and narrow. In the light zones, shallow as serving platters. I dream of rivers I've never seen, of water that runs without purpose, that doesn't need to be coaxed and collected and carried. But I wake to the lattice's creaking morning song, and I'm grateful. Without us Dew-Line Walkers, the city would parch in a week.

The strangest part of my work happens at the turnings of season. That's when the water veins rearrange themselves, following some pattern I've never fully decoded. Old reliable sources vanish overnight. New ones appear in beams that have been dry for decades. During these transitions, we Walkers gather—all six of us who work this section—and share our findings through touch. We press our worn fingertips together and feel the map of moisture shifting through each other's memories. It's the only time we meet. The rest of the year, we're ghosts to each other, following our routes in careful sequence to avoid disturbing each other's harvest.

I've trained three apprentices over the years. Two quit—the isolation was too much, or they grew too heavy too fast and could no longer navigate the lighter spans. The third disappeared one morning, leaving only her listening rod behind. Some say she followed a vein too deep and found the lattice's first water, the source from which all our condensation springs. Others think she simply dried up, became one with the wood grain she'd traced so long. I prefer to believe she learned to flow like water itself, seeping through the city in ways even we Walkers cannot follow.

This is my calling: to know water in all its hidden forms, to coax it from wood and air, to measure my life in collected drops that keep ten thousand souls from thirst. I am a Dew-Line Walker, and the lattice's secret rivers run through my dreams.
