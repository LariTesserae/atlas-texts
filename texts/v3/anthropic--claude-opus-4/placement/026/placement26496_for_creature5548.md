I am the Chronicler of Tides, though I was once called by another name—a name that belonged to the world above, when these peaks were mountains and the machinery sang with purpose instead of whale-song.

My dwelling is neither fully of the old world nor the new. I have made my home within the hollow shell of what was once an industrial cooling tower, its concrete walls now carpeted with bioluminescent algae that pulse in rhythm with my breathing. The structure lists at an angle where it emerges from the shelf of an ancient plateau, half-consumed by the patient appetite of the sea. Through the gaps in its deteriorating crown, shafts of that strange green light filter down, painting everything in shades of memory.

I am changed, as all things here are changed. My skin has taken on a nacre sheen, smooth and slightly translucent where the light catches it. Between my elongated fingers, delicate webbing has grown—not enough to make me a true creature of these depths, but sufficient to move through the flooded chambers of my home with something approaching grace. My eyes have adapted too, pupils dilated permanently wide to catch every photon that penetrates these waters. When I glimpse my reflection in the polished metal of some half-buried artifact, I see someone caught between transformations, neither fully human nor fully Other.

My work continues, though its nature has evolved. Where once I might have maintained databases or monitored systems, now I maintain something more fluid—the memory of what was, written in the placement of salvaged objects and the careful cultivation of certain species of kelp that grow only where I guide them. I have learned to read the stories written in rust patterns and barnacle colonies. Each encrusted surface tells of currents and time, of the slow digestion of one world by another.

The cephalopods visit me. They come in the blue hour when the light shifts toward darkness, their bodies flashing with colors I have learned to interpret as language. We share a fascination with the remnants of the before-time. Together, we explore the deeper ruins, their tentacles and my webbed hands working in concert to uncover fragments of meaning. They show me their collections—bits of circuitry arranged in patterns that please them, gears nested inside shells in ways that suggest understanding beyond mere play.

I have learned to hear the way sound travels here, how it bends and warps around the submerged architecture. The clicks of hunting dolphins echo through the metal bones of a radio tower, transforming into something almost like the transmissions it once carried. The low groans of the great towers settling ever deeper into their foundations harmonize with the calls of the leviathans in the dark water beyond the shelf. Sometimes, in the convergence of these sounds, I swear I hear voices—fragments of conversations from the world that was, preserved in the acoustic memory of the stone and steel.

My daily ritual takes me along the ridge where the water meets the last emergent peaks. Here, I tend to my garden of anemones, each one placed to mark where something significant once stood. A cluster of purple marks the site of a home; gold indicates a place of gathering; the rare blues show where acts of kindness occurred that I still remember. The fish that shelter among them carry these stories forward in ways they cannot know, their movements weaving new narratives through the old.

There are others like me, though we rarely meet. Sometimes I catch glimpses of them in the distance—a figure moving through the kelp forests with too much purpose to be fully aquatic, a silhouette against the green light that retains something indefinably human in its posture. We acknowledge each other with gestures that belong to neither the world above nor below, and continue our separate works of preservation and transformation.

The industrial machinery has become something new. The great engines, flooded and still, have been colonized by coral that pulses with bioluminescence in patterns that mirror the electrical flows they once contained. Schools of silver fish move through the spaces between gears like living current. The cephalopods have shown me how certain configurations of metal and growth can produce sounds—haunting tones that carry for miles through the water. They have learned to play these structures like instruments, creating symphonies that speak of both ending and beginning.

I sleep in a chamber I've sealed against the stronger currents, surrounded by artifacts arranged with careful intention. A keyboard, its keys hosting a miniature reef. A monitor screen, dark now except for the phosphorescent trails of passing plankton. Books, their pages fused into solid blocks that I've carved with new symbols—a language emerging between human text and the script of water on stone.

In dreams, I sometimes forget which world is real. I walk through dry corridors filled with the sound of typing and conversation, only to wake to the sound of kelp brushing against my walls. Or I dream I am fully transformed, moving through the deep waters with the leviathans, only to reach for something with hands that still remember how to grasp tools meant for air.

This is what I am: keeper of the threshold, translator between worlds, gardener of memories in a medium that dissolves them even as it preserves their shapes. Each day, the water rises imperceptibly higher. Each day, I am a little less what I was, a little more what this place requires me to become.

But I continue the work, as do we all who remain—not because we hope to restore what was, but because the act of remembering is itself a form of creation. In this vast expanse where past and present flow together like converging currents, we are writing the next chapter of the world, word by dissolved word, gesture by adapted gesture, in the eternal syntax of water against stone.
