In this hollow where water speaks in every tongue—drip, rush, whisper, roar—I would find myself as one of the Weavers.

Not human, though we walk upright when it suits us. Not quite plant, though our skin ripples with chlorophyll when the filtered light touches just so. We are something between, something that emerged from the hollow's patient alchemy of mist and moss, root and bone. Our fingers stretch long and thin, more tendril than digit, splitting at the tips into delicate fronds that can feel the moisture in the air, taste the minerals in the rain. When we move through the dense understory, we don't push aside the vegetation—we negotiate with it, our bodies flowing like water finding its path, bending and twisting in ways that honor the plants' own geometries.

I live in what the others call a Grown House, though that name fails to capture its breathing reality. Years ago—or was it decades?—I began training a cluster of trees, guiding their growth with gentle touches and whispered encouragements. Now their trunks spiral upward in a living helix, branches interlocking to form walls that shift with the seasons. In spring, the walls bloom with cascading flowers that perfume my sleep. In the cooler months, the leaves thicken, overlapping like scales to keep out the perpetual damp. The floor is a carpet of moss I've cultivated from seven different species, each chosen for its particular softness or its way of glowing faintly in the evening when the fireflies come to rest.

My work is the Weaving itself—not of cloth, but of connections. Each morning, I wake before the bird chorus reaches its crescendo and walk the paths that web through our quarter of the hollow. My tendrils brush against leaves and bark, reading the chemical messages plants leave for one another: warnings of pest invasions, announcements of fruit ripening, distress calls from those struggling in too much shade. I carry these messages in my body, which can store and reproduce the chemical signatures, delivering them where needed like a living postal service.

But I am more than messenger. Where the plants grow too dense, choking out their neighbors, I guide the aggressive vines toward empty spaces, teaching them new patterns. Where the water pools too deeply, threatening to drown the root systems, I work with the moss and ferns to create living channels, directing the flow toward thirstier ground. The squirrels have learned to follow my morning routes, knowing I'll lead them to the newest nut caches. The birds nest where I've strengthened the branches to hold their weight.

The primitive humans fear us at first—our movements too fluid, our faces shifting between expressions they can't quite read. But they learn, as all inhabitants here must learn, that we mean no harm. When their children fall ill from eating the wrong berries, we lead them to the healing plants. When their fires threaten to spread in the dry season, we teach them how to build with living wood that won't burn. Some evenings, they leave offerings at the base of our Grown Houses—carved stones, woven baskets, the bones of animals decorated with dried flowers. We accept these gifts by incorporating them into our homes, letting vines embrace the stones, filling the baskets with seeds for them to find.

We Weavers number perhaps a dozen in this hollow, each tending to our own section but meeting when the full moon rises above the mist. On those nights, we gather in the Grove of First Growth, where the oldest trees remember the hollow before any of us emerged. We share the month's patterns—which streams are changing course, which animal populations surge or decline, where the humans have worn new paths. Our language is partly sound, partly chemical, partly the rustling movement of our tendril-fingers painting meaning in the air.

I am not the oldest of us—that honor belongs to Deep Root, whose body has become so intertwined with their House that they rarely move, speaking now mostly through the fungal networks that web beneath the soil. Nor am I the youngest—that would be Rain Dance, who still delights in climbing to the canopy to trigger showers by shaking the accumulated moisture from the leaves. I am somewhere between, old enough to read the subtle signs but young enough to remember the joy of discovery.

In the perpetual cool damp, our bodies thrive. We don't need the heavy clothing of humans or the fur of mammals. Our skin adapts—growing thick and bark-like when we work with thorns, smooth and permeable when we wade through streams, photosynthetic when we rest in the rare sun-dappled clearings. We eat, but not as others eat. Sometimes we root ourselves like plants, drawing nutrients from the soil. Sometimes we graze on fruits and tender shoots, our digestive systems breaking them down into components we can either use or share with struggling plants through our root-touches.

The constant sound of the hollow is music to us. Where humans might find the cacophony overwhelming, we hear symphonies of information. The pitch of water dropping tells us the humidity level. The frequency of insect drones reveals the temperature. The rustle of leaves carries stories of wind patterns that predict the weather days in advance. We add our own voices to this chorus—humming harmonies that encourage growth, clicking rhythms that calm agitated animals, whispering susurrations that guide the mist.

At night, when I return to my Grown House, I often find creatures sheltering there—a family of mice in the walls, beetles in the bark crevices, tree frogs clinging to the ceiling leaves. They've learned that Weaver homes are safe spaces, protected from the worst predators by our presence and the defensive secretions we can coax from our allied plants. I fall asleep to their small sounds, their tiny hearts beating in rhythm with the greater pulse of the hollow.

This is what I am here—neither guardian nor gardener, but something more integral. I am a living bridge between kingdoms, a translator of needs and desires across species boundaries. My joy comes not from control but from facilitation, watching the patterns emerge and shift like water finding its way through stone. In this hollow where everything drips and grows and calls and changes, I am both participant and curator of the endless, beautiful chaos.

Tomorrow, I will wake to find new growth, new problems, new possibilities. A tree will have fallen in the night, creating opportunity for sun-loving plants. The humans will have discovered a new flowering vine and wonder if it's safe to eat. The streams will whisper of changes upstream. And I will walk my paths with tender-fingers extended, reading and writing the hollow's story, one touch at a time.
