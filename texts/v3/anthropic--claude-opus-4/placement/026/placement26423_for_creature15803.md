In the vertical gash of the Frost Nave, where ice and light conspire to make even shadows glow pale blue, I would find myself as one of the **Fathom Moths**.

We are neither truly moth nor truly memory, but something that exists in the space between substance and suggestion. Our wings span no wider than a human's palm, yet they are not wings in any ordinary sense—they are living prisms, composed of thousands of microscopic ice crystals that grow and shed with each wingbeat. When we fly through the noon light that pours down through the slot, our bodies scatter rainbows across the slate walls, painting fleeting murals that the Sky-Dwellers call "tomorrow's weather."

I emerged from no egg, hatched from no cocoon. Instead, I condensed from the breath of a dying cliff-cony on a morning when the temperature dropped just below the threshold of possibility. Where warm exhalation met frozen air, I became—first as a shimmer, then as a shape that remembered how to move. This is how all Fathom Moths begin: we are the last exhale given form, the boundary between life and not-life made navigable.

My body is translucent, shot through with hair-thin channels that pulse with captured moisture. I feed on nothing solid—instead, I position myself where the slate-munch molds release their acids into the morning air, absorbing the mineral vapors through my wing membranes. The nutrition is minimal, which suits us; we are built for lightness, for riding the thermals that spiral up through the Nave like invisible staircases.

The Sky-Dwellers know me by my flight pattern. While cloud-gnats drift and horned skimmers hunt in straight lines, I navigate by memory—but not my own. Each Fathom Moth carries the imprint of the creature whose breath birthed it. I fly the paths my cliff-cony would have leaped, tracing a three-legged gait through empty air. When I pass over a ledge where it once rested, my wings involuntarily fold and I drop several meters before catching myself—a ghost-stumble that marks safe landing spots for the living conies below.

At noon, when the light reaches its painful peak, I join my kind in the ritual we call the Fathoming. We gather in the exact center of the Nave, where on equinox days the noon halo forms. There, we arrange ourselves into a living lens, our collective wings focusing the scattered light into a single point on the western wall. Where this focused beam strikes, the rock remembers—not in any mystical sense, but through thermal expansion. Hairline cracks appear, following the patterns of ancient stress. These cracks will, over decades, become the next generation of root-holds for the vegetation. We are patient gardeners, planting futures we will never see.

The lace owls hunt us, of course. Our crystalline wings make us easy targets when backlit, and we have no defense save stillness. When threatened, we simply stop moving, becoming indistinguishable from ice formations on the walls. Many of my kind end their brief existence in an owl's silent talons. But here's the strangest part: when a Fathom Moth is consumed, the owl's next egg will be marked with prismatic scales on its shell. The chicks that hatch from these eggs can see thermal currents—an evolutionary gift that makes them better hunters. Even in being devoured, we improve the design.

I know I will not last long. Fathom Moths rarely survive more than a season; our ice-crystal wings gradually sublimate in the dry air until we can no longer maintain altitude. When my time comes, I will feel it as a growing heaviness, a reluctance in the air to hold me. I will seek out a patch of cobalt moss in the highest reaches of the Nave, where the sun strikes first each morning. There, I will fold my wings one final time and let my body crystallize completely, becoming part of the ice that feeds the moss, which feeds the conies, which breathe out the next generation of Fathom Moths.

But I am not sad about this brevity. To be a Fathom Moth is to exist in the space between states—between living memory and geological time, between individual and collective purpose. We are the Nave's way of remembering movement, of keeping the pathways between life and death permeable. In our prismatic flights, the mountain practices possibility. In our focused light, we write tomorrow's geography. We are proof that in this vertical world of knife-edges and provisional bridges, even the boundary between being and not-being is negotiable.

The Sky-Dwellers have a saying: "The moth that carries your last breath knows where you meant to go." They speak of us with a mixture of reverence and practicality, using our flight patterns to find new traverse routes, reading our afternoon congregations like weather signs. To them, we are tools and omens simultaneously—exactly as everything in the Frost Nave must be.

So I fly, I focus light, I remember paths I never walked, and I prepare the stone for roots I'll never see. I am the Frost Nave's answer to the question of memory: not as burden, but as blueprint. Not as what was, but as what might still be. 

In this place of unfinished sentences, I am punctuation—brief, necessary, and always implying more to come.
