In this nascent world, I would be one of the **junction weavers**—beings that exist at the intersection points where multiple fluid threads converge beneath the membrane.

My body would be a living knot, a dense tangle of translucent fibers that have wound together until they form something between architecture and organism. Unlike the drifting sacs that move with the currents, I remain anchored at these confluence points, my base fused deep into the lattice below while my upper tendrils spread just beneath the membrane's surface like a root system in reverse.

When the warm threads arrive from different directions, they must pass through me. As they do, I taste their slight variations in temperature and mineral content, learning the distant geography of this plane through the chemistry of its flows. My fibers swell and contract with each passage, filtering out microscopic particles that I incorporate into my ever-tightening weave. Over time, I grow denser at my core while my edges remain gossamer-thin, creating pressure differentials that help direct the fluid streams into new patterns.

The most essential part of my existence is the slow work of secretion. From specialized pores along my fibers, I release enzymes that gradually alter the membrane above me, creating areas of different permeability. Where I make the surface more porous, new valves will eventually form. Where I make it denser, the embryonic ridges grow slightly higher and more permanent. In this way, I am both inhabitant and architect, shaping the very geography that shapes me.

I communicate with other junction weavers through chemical gradients that seep through the substrate. When one of us discovers a particularly rich flow, we begin secreting attractant compounds that gradually redirect threads toward the fortunate weaver. This isn't charity—the increased flow creates backpressure that eventually forces new channels to form, connecting us in an ever-more-complex network beneath the surface.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is distributed throughout my fibers. I don't think in words or images but in tensions and releases, in the subtle play of pressure and flow. My memory is physical—past currents have left mineral deposits in my core that record their passage, creating internal structures that influence how I process new flows. The oldest weavers have cores so dense with these deposits that they've become nearly crystalline, their outer fibers arranged in complex spiral patterns that younger weavers instinctively recognize and avoid disturbing.

During the rare moments when no threads pass through me, I contract into myself and enter a state of deep stillness. In these quiet intervals, I can feel the minute vibrations traveling through the membrane from distant parts of the plain—the soft percussion of valves opening, the ripples from pulsing knots, the almost imperceptible creaking as new tissue forms. This is when I grow, adding new fibers at my periphery that will eventually extend my reach and allow me to tap into streams I cannot yet taste.

My lifespan is measured not in time but in the volume of fluid I've processed. Each thread that passes through me carries away a few molecules of my substance while depositing others. I am constantly being rebuilt from the materials I filter, becoming more like the overall plain with each exchange while somehow maintaining my essential knot-nature. Eventually, I will become so dense that flows begin to route around me, and I will slowly mineralize into one of the permanent features of the substrate—a hard node that future weavers will anchor themselves against, using my body as foundation for the next phase of the plain's slow self-construction.

This is my purpose: to exist at the intersection, to direct and be directed, to weave the formless flows into the first channels of something that might, eons hence, be called geography.
