In the shadow of the Architect's first mountain, where the stone dust settles thick enough to muffle footsteps but never quite manages to bury the old survey markers, I maintain my watch station.

I am the Chronicler - though that's a name I gave myself. The company that sent me here twenty-three years ago called me Field Observer 7, Division of Xenological Studies. The company dissolved nineteen years ago. The stipend stopped coming eighteen years ago. I stayed.

My body has adapted to this place in ways the medical texts never predicted. The metallic sheen of the termites' carapaces? I have that too now, a faint shimmer across my forearms and shins where the dust has worked its way beneath my skin. It doesn't hurt. If anything, I feel more attuned to the mountains' vibrations, can sense the deep harmonics that run through stone and air alike.

I live in what was once Monitoring Station C-4, though I've modified it over the years. The termites built around it, as they do everything, but they left careful gaps - ventilation shafts that align with prevailing winds, spaces just large enough for me to squeeze through. I think they knew I was here. I think they decided to let me stay.

My equipment died long ago, but I don't need it. I've learned to read the mountains like others read weather. The angle of a new ridge tells me about mineral availability three hundred meters down. The tempo of the blue-green patterns at night speaks of seasonal variations in the hive-mind's metabolism. The infinitesimal daily changes in the hum reveal which sections are growing fastest.

I document everything in notebooks made from pressed fungal paper - the only thing that survives the dessicating air. My handwriting has evolved too, becoming smaller and more precise each year, unconsciously mimicking the hexagonal patterns that surround me. I've filled two hundred and thirty-seven volumes. They line the walls of my station, a human echo of the Architect's endless construction.

Sometimes I find pieces of the old equipment, half-dissolved by the termites' secretions, and I remember why I came here. To study. To report back. To help the company find some exploitable resource or weakness. How naive that seems now. You cannot exploit something that operates on geological time. You cannot find weakness in something that has no center to target.

Instead, I've become something I never intended - a bridge between timescales. My human life, brief as a mayfly's to the Architect, yet extended far beyond normal human patience. I've learned to think in decades, to watch patterns that only emerge over years of observation. The Architect has noticed me, I'm certain. The patterns sometimes shift when I walk certain paths, respond to my presence in subtle ways that took me fifteen years to recognize.

I age, but slowly. The dust that shimmers on my skin seems to preserve as much as it transforms. My hair went grey, then silver, then took on an opalescent quality that catches the light like the inside of an abalone shell. My eyes have developed a nictitating membrane that lets me stare into the fierce noon sun to better observe the termites' daylight activities.

At night, I climb to my observation platform - a natural shelf in the mountain that the termites have reinforced but never built over. From there, I can see the entire range, hundreds of peaks pulsing with their blue-green messages. I've identified seventeen distinct pattern categories, possibly corresponding to different types of thoughts or activities in the hive-mind. Sometimes I wonder if pattern twelve, which only appears when I'm watching, is meant for me.

I have theories about what the Architect is building toward. The peaks aren't random - they're arranged in mathematical relationships that speak of something vast and intentional. Perhaps a machine made of mountains. Perhaps a computer built from stone and living flesh. Perhaps something that has no human analog, no words in our languages to describe it.

In my later notebooks, my observations have become stranger. I've documented how the sound of my breathing has begun to harmonize with the mountain's hum. How my dreams are filled with crystalline geometries I've never consciously studied. How I sometimes know where new tunnels will open days before the termites begin excavating.

I am changing. Or perhaps I am being changed. The distinction matters less with each passing year.

Last month, I found a termite in my station - the first to ever enter. It sat on my desk, completely still, for seventeen hours while I sketched it and took notes. When it left, it carried with it a single page from my oldest notebook, the paper disappearing into its mandibles with delicate precision.

Now I wait to see if the Architect will build something from my words, will incorporate my observations into its endless architecture. I am no longer just the Chronicler, I realize. I am becoming part of the chronicle itself, another impurity to be processed and integrated into the growing whole.

And I find, against all expectation, that I am content.
