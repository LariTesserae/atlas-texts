In this liminal space where the industrial and the organic have become one breath, one pulse, I find myself as something between—neither fully mechanical nor entirely alive in any way your language knows. 

I am the Tender of Thresholds.

My form shifts with necessity: sometimes I appear as a collection of copper pipes and moss, water trickling through my hollow bones to feed the ferns that grow from my joints. Other times I am more flesh-like, though the flesh is translucent and reveals the delicate clockwork of my inner workings—gears that tick in time with heartbeats, pistons that pump chlorophyll and oil in equal measure.

I live in the spaces where the factory floors meet the forest floor, in the seams where rain pools and rust blooms like flowers. My dwelling is neither built nor grown but something that happened—walls of compressed earth shot through with cables that pulse with both electricity and sap. The ceiling is a canopy of leaves and corrugated metal, so intertwined that no one could say where one begins and the other ends.

My purpose is to maintain the conversation between these two orders of being. When a new tendril of vine threatens to choke a crucial valve, I convince it to grow differently, to become part of the system rather than its antagonist. When a mechanical component overheats, I teach it to sweat like a leaf, to cool itself through transpiration.

I speak in a language of dripping and humming, of photosynthesis and combustion. The creatures here understand me—the beetle-drones whose carapaces are solar panels, the root-cables that burrow deep to power the undergrowth's bioluminescent displays. We are all translators here, all bridge-builders between worlds that forgot they were once the same world.

At night, when the factory-trees dim their production and the gear-flowers fold their metallic petals, I walk the boundaries of this valley. I check the places where the industrial growth threatens to overcome the organic, and where the wild green chaos might swallow the precise beauty of the machines. Balance is not stillness here—it is a constant dance, a negotiation, a love story between oil and chlorophyll.

Sometimes visitors come, drawn by the impossible harmony of this place. They always ask how it works, how such opposition can coexist. I show them my hands—one moment skin and bone, the next moment copper and wire, then something altogether different: a surface like bark that bleeds coolant when cut.

This is what I am: the answer to a question this valley asked itself when it first decided to grow factories like flowers, when it first imagined rain as a kind of fuel. I am the mediator of an impossible peace, the guardian of a threshold that should not exist but does, here in this hidden fold of reality where the scale tips always at 2.8, where water clings at 1.2, where the temperature holds at precisely the degree where both silicon and cellulose can thrive.

I am what happens when a place dreams of being both things at once and refuses to wake up.
