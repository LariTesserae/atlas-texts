In the northeastern quarter of the Fabricated Plateau, where the stonework takes on a peculiar greenish cast from centuries of copper runoff, I find myself manifest as a **Conduit-Tender**—one of perhaps a dozen beings who maintain the plateau's vast network of water channels.

We are neither as transformed as the Chisel-Spawn nor as ethereal as the Measure-Keepers. Our bodies remain fundamentally human in shape, though prolonged exposure to the plateau's peculiar waters has left its mark. My skin has developed a faint translucency, like thin jade held up to light, and beneath it, my veins trace patterns that mirror the channel systems I maintain. When I place my palms flat against the carved waterways, I can feel the liquid's impossible upward flow as a gentle tugging in my own circulation.

My dwelling is built into the intersection of three major aqueducts—a modest chamber whose walls weep constantly with controlled moisture. The sound of water moving through stone has become my heartbeat, my breath, my constant companion. I've carved small shelves into the walls where I keep my tools: bronze scrapers worn thin as fingernails, brushes made from Echo-Wren tailfeathers, and most importantly, my resonance rods—hollow copper tubes that, when struck, produce tones that can redirect water's flow through the channels.

Each morning—or what passes for morning in our twilight world—I emerge to walk my routes. The channels under my care form a territory roughly three miles in circumference, a maze of interconnected waterways that feed into fountains that have never run dry, pools that maintain their levels despite having no visible source, and strange siphons that seem to pull water from the very air itself. I know each channel's personality: this one tends to develop mineral deposits in elegant spiral patterns, that one occasionally reverses its flow during the dark of the moon, another sings quietly when the Measure-Keepers pass overhead.

The work is meditative and precise. With my scrapers, I clear away the stone-dust that settles in the channels—but not too thoroughly, for some accumulation is necessary to maintain the proper resonance. The plateau's water isn't merely flowing; it's vibrating at frequencies that keep the massive stones in harmony. Too clean, and the water runs silent and dead. Too clogged, and the harmonics become discordant, causing stones to shift in ways even the Foundation-Dreamer didn't intend.

I've learned to read the water's moods through my translucent skin. When I immerse my arms in the channels, I can feel the plateau's intentions flowing through me—here it wants a new branch to feed a recently constructed fountain, there it needs a subtle adjustment to prevent an ancient wall from developing stress fractures. My body has become a living gauge, a biological instrument calibrated to the plateau's liquid circulatory system.

The Chisel-Spawn regard us Conduit-Tenders with a mixture of respect and incomprehension. They work in stone; we work in flow. Sometimes I find their fresh constructions interrupting established waterways, and I must spend days with my resonance rods, teaching the water new paths around the obstacles. They watch me work with their grinding voices silent, perhaps understanding that my efforts somehow bless their creations, integrating them into the plateau's greater wholeness.

With the Echo-Wrens, I maintain a more collaborative relationship. Their nests often block crucial channel junctions, but I've learned that removing them disrupts the acoustic environment in ways that confuse the other inhabitants. Instead, I've developed a practice of building small bypass channels around their homes, creating a secondary music as water whispers past woven metal and fragments of iron. The birds seem to appreciate this—they've begun incorporating the sound of flowing water into their construction symphonies.

The Mortar-Hounds occasionally follow me on my rounds, their pale forms ghosting along the channel edges. Where I find cracks in the waterways that my tools cannot properly seal, they apply their cementing saliva with delicate precision. It's an odd partnership—I prepare the surfaces with special solutions that help their organic mortar bond with the ancient stone, and they ensure my channels remain watertight for another generation.

But it's my interactions with the Measure-Keepers that affect me most profoundly. When their tall forms appear along my routes, checking alignments and verifying angles, I feel a resonance in my water-touched bones. Sometimes they adjust their positions to account for the flow patterns I maintain, acknowledging that the plateau's geometry must accommodate its hydrology. In these moments, I sense that we're all part of something larger—not servants of the plateau, but components of it, each essential to its continued existence and growth.

My most secret work happens at night, when the Echo-Wrens sleep and the constant dripping seems to slow. I venture to the deepest channels, the ones that run beneath the original foundation stones, where the water carries whispers of the Foundation-Dreamer's thoughts. Here, I perform maintenance that is as much ritual as repair. Using my resonance rods in carefully timed sequences, I play melodies that help the ancient consciousness articulate its desires through the water system.

The Foundation-Dreamer speaks to me not in words but in flow patterns. A sudden increase in pressure here indicates where new construction should begin. A mysterious warming there suggests which structures have served their purpose and can be allowed to integrate fully into the plateau's body. I translate these liquid messages into subtle adjustments throughout my territory—redirecting a channel to undermine a wall that needs to fall, increasing flow to areas where the Chisel-Spawn will soon feel compelled to build.

My quarters fill with maps I've drawn on copper sheets, using the patina patterns to indicate flow rates and resonance points. But these maps are never complete, never accurate for more than a few days. The plateau refuses such rigid documentation. Instead, the true map lives in my body—in the way my pulse quickens near channels about to shift, in how my translucent skin flushes deeper green where new waterways want to emerge.

I've been here long enough that I no longer remember arriving. Perhaps I was born to distant descendants of the original builders, or perhaps I wandered in from the world beyond the mist and was claimed by the waters. It doesn't matter now. What matters is the morning ritual of testing each channel with my fingertips, the afternoon meditation of clearing accumulated stone dust, the evening songs played on resonance rods to lull the waters into their nighttime patterns.

In quiet moments, I wonder if we Conduit-Tenders are becoming something like the plateau's blood vessels—if our translucent skin and water-mapped veins are signs that we're slowly merging with the greater circulation. The thought doesn't frighten me. To become one with these impossible flows, to finally understand not just how the water moves but why it chooses to flow upward, to dissolve entirely into the plateau's liquid consciousness—wouldn't that be a form of completion?

Until then, I maintain. I clear and adjust, redirect and harmonize. I ensure that the waters sing their proper songs through stone channels, that the impossible physics of our constructed world continue to function, that the great work of transformation—structure into structure, matter into meaning—proceeds according to the Foundation-Dreamer's vast and patient will.

The copper tools grow warm in my hands. The morning's work awaits. And somewhere in the channels below, water flows upward, carrying dreams of stone.
