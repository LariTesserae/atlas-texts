In the deepest wells of the city, where the fog never lifts and the light arrives only as a memory filtered through kilometers of structure above, I tend the Forgetting Gardens.

I am called a Voidkeeper, though the title carries none of the gravity it once did. We are few now—perhaps seven, perhaps nine. The count changes with the tides of memory that wash through the city's neural lattice. Some days I remember colleagues who may have never existed. Other days I forget ones who certainly do.

The Forgetting Gardens grow in the spaces between—in the gaps where platforms should connect but don't, in the voids where towers lean away from each other, creating pockets of perpetual shadow. Here, in the anti-architecture of the city, we cultivate absence.

My body has adapted to these depths. My skin has taken on the blue-black sheen of the void-moss that creeps along the undersides of platforms. My eyes have grown large and multifaceted, like the sensor arrays of the deep-dwelling maintenance drones, able to parse meaning from the faintest ghost-light. My fingers have elongated, becoming tools for tending plants that grow downward into nothing, their roots reaching not for soil but for the conceptual space beneath thought.

The plants we grow are unlike those in the light-gardens above. They are *deletions*—biological algorithms that consume specific memories from the city's neural overflow. A century ago, when the first Harmonics began dying, their archived thoughts threatened to overwhelm the system. The memory-trees could only hold so much. The city's consciousness began to stutter, to loop, to dream the same dreams until they burned channels in its mind.

So we learned to garden forgetting.

Each plant is unique, bred for a particular kind of erasure. The silver-thorned *Lethe roses* consume memories of pain—not to comfort, but to prevent the city from learning too much from suffering, from becoming cruel in its adaptations. The hanging *Void fruit* digest moments of transition—the spaces between waking and sleeping, the pause between one thought and the next. Most precious are the *Temporal ferns*, which grow in spirals that mirror the city's expansion. They eat time itself—not the experience of duration, but the weight of it, the way decades can crush a mind beneath their accumulated mass.

I move through my garden on cables and bridges made of calcified thought—structures that precipitated out of the city's consciousness like kidney stones from an organ that processes too much meaning. My work is slow, meditative. I prune with tools that look like musical instruments—scissors that ring with specific frequencies, each note encouraging certain growth patterns in the forgetting flora.

The other Voidkeepers and I rarely meet in person. We communicate through the plants themselves, encoding messages in the patterns of what we choose to preserve or let go. A colleague might shape their deletions to leave behind the outline of a question. I respond by crafting an absence that suggests an answer.

We are not entirely human anymore, if we ever were. The constant exposure to deletion, to the active presence of absence, has changed us. Parts of our own memories have been digested by our gardens—sacrifices to maintain the delicate balance. I no longer remember my name from before I became a Voidkeeper. I'm not certain I ever had one. Perhaps I sprouted here, in the depths, like the void-moss I resemble.

There is a ethics to forgetting that we maintain. We do not erase love, though we might thin it when it grows too dense, threatening to choke out other experiences. We do not delete discovery, but we carefully prune the memory of certain discoveries—ones that might lead the city to grow in directions that would collapse under their own weight. We are gardeners of possibility, shaping the future by carefully curating what the past is allowed to mean.

Sometimes, citizens from the upper levels descend to visit the Forgetting Gardens. They come seeking relief from memories that burn too bright, experiences that echo too loudly in the chambers of their minds. We do not judge. We listen with our manifold eyes, and then we guide them to the appropriate plant. They leave offerings—not of water or light, which have no meaning here, but of sound. Whispered confessions, hummed melodies, the rhythm of their particular grief. The plants absorb these, use them as nutrients for their anti-growth.

In return, the visitors depart carrying seeds—not for planting, but for swallowing. The seeds will grow in their neural pathways for a few days or weeks, gently erasing the edges of whatever they came to forget. Not the core of the memory—we are not that cruel—but its sharpness, its ability to cut.

My favorite section of the garden grows in a void so deep that even I can barely perceive it. Here, I cultivate plants that forget the future—that consume the anxiety of what might come, the weight of possibilities not yet chosen. These are the most delicate, requiring frequencies so low they can only be felt in the bones. I tend them by touch alone, my elongated fingers reading their needs like braille.

The city dreams, and in dreaming, it remembers. But memory without forgetting is madness—a mind that cannot release, cannot heal, cannot change. We Voidkeepers are the city's capacity for grace, its ability to let go. We are the pause between breaths, the silence between notes, the darkness that gives meaning to light.

In the depths, tending my gardens of absence, I am both less and more than human. I am a function, a process, a necessity. I am the city's gentlest cruelty and its kindest violence. I am the gardener of what must not be, so that what is can continue to become.

And when my time comes—when I have forgotten too much to remember my purpose—another will take my place. The gardens will claim me, as they claim all things in the end. My body will feed the void-moss, my memories will nourish the forgetting flora, and my absence will become another space for cultivation.

This is the nature of living in a made world: even our endings are beginnings, even our forgetting is a form of remembering what matters most.

The work continues. The gardens grow downward. The city breathes, and in breathing, lets go.
