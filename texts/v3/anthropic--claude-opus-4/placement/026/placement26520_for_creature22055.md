I find myself in the cramped under-belly of Tower Seven, where the wind screams loudest through the ancient lignin chambers and the city's weight groans overhead like a thing alive.

I am Tellik One-Ear, senior among the Slack-Men, and I have not seen sunlight on my bare skin for eleven years. The missing ear—taken by a snapping cable in my third season—has left me with an advantage: I can press the good side of my head directly against the main tension-wire and hear the wood's intentions before they manifest as catastrophe.

My body is a tool shaped by its purpose. My legs, thin as air-moss stalks, dangle useless in the harness while my shoulders have grown so broad that I must turn sideways to pass through the access hatches. My hands are leather and scar tissue, each knuckle swollen from years of gripping the cold iron turnbuckles. The beetle-tallow we slather ourselves with has seeped so deep into my pores that water beads and rolls off me like mercury on glass. I smell of rancid vinegar and machine grease, a scent so profound it has become my name-sign among my crew—two sharp clicks of the copper club against iron, followed by a long scrape.

I live in the Underneath with my cable-brothers. We have no homes in the traditional sense, only the "rest-nooks"—small platforms of tarred hemp and salvaged wood wedged into the gaps between the great vertical beams. We share everything: tools, tallow, bad air-moss liquor, and the constant anxiety of listening to the wood grow.

The Spire is never silent. To untrained ears, it might seem dead—just the wind and the city's industrial clatter. But I hear its true voice: the deep, subsonic groans as the heartwood contracts, the whisper-thin crack of resin crystallizing in the cold, the murderous ping of an over-tensioned cable approaching its breaking point.

Each day begins the same. I wake in my harness—we never fully remove them—and check my sector's cables by feel in the darkness. The under-belly is too vast and dark for torches; we work by sound and touch alone. My fingers read the cables like a blind man reads a face, feeling for the telltale vibrations that signal danger.

Today, Sector Seven-Seven-Three is singing the death song.

I strike the emergency pattern—seven rapid pings that echo through the iron forest of cables. My crew assembles in the darkness: young Korvek Two-Scars (still hopeful enough to count them), Marsh the Grease-Drunk (who claims the tallow helps him think), and ancient Preem, so long in the harness that his legs have actually begun to fuse together at the knees.

We rappel through the three-dimensional maze of ironwork, following the high, tortured note of the dying cable. The lazy gravity makes the descent dreamlike—we fall in slow arcs, catching ourselves on cross-beams and swinging through the darkness like oil through water. One miscalculation and we'll drift sideways into the void, where the wind will play with our corpses for hours before letting them fall.

The cable, when we find it, is a monster—thick as my waist, twisted hemp reinforced with Void-Weaver silk and wound with copper wire. The wood has shifted perhaps half an inch in the night, but at this scale, that half-inch has put sixteen tons of additional tension on the line. The metal sings with stress, a high, clean note that makes my remaining ear ache.

We begin the ritual. Korvek and Marsh brace the emergency jacks while Preem and I work the turnbuckle. It takes four hours to release a quarter-turn. The metal is so cold it burns through our tallow-soaked gloves. We speak in club-strikes and cable-thrums, our bodies moving in the practiced synchronization of those who know that one false move will kill everyone.

As we work, I feel the shadow-lag beginning. I've been at this post so long that my after-image has started to develop its own weight. When I shift position, I feel myself moving through my own ghost—a cold, grey resistance that wasn't there in my young years. The other Slack-Men say I'm beginning the Wash. In another decade, I'll be one of the Smear-Folk, if the cables don't take me first.

The turnbuckle finally gives with a sound like a bell cracking. The cable drops six inches, and the entire Spire seems to exhale. Somewhere far above, a thousand people continue their lives, never knowing how close they came to catastrophe.

We retreat to the rest-nook for our ration of grease-water and dried air-moss cakes. Marsh produces his flask of fermented beetle-tallow—a truly vile substance that burns the throat but makes the cold bearable. We sit in our harnesses, suspended in the darkness, and listen to the Spire digest its adjustment.

Preem tells us he's decided to take the Long Rappel next month. His legs have fused completely now; he can no longer work the outer sections. The Long Rappel is what we call it when a Slack-Man knows his time has come—a final descent into the true bottom of the Spire, where the Burl-Mites tunnel and the air is thick with explosive gases. No one comes back from the Long Rappel, but it's considered more honorable than waiting for the cables to claim you.

I think about the surface sometimes, about the Ground-Gliders in their anchor-harnesses, plowing across the endless grey plain. I think about the sun on the lichen-fields, the horizon unbroken by iron and shadow. But then I press my good ear to the main cable and hear the wood's slow, patient hunger to grow, to shift, to shrug off the parasitic city clinging to its flesh.

This is my world—a vertical maze of iron and danger, where we fight a war measured in quarter-turns and fractional inches, where victory is another day without the screaming fall of a severed cable. I am Tellik One-Ear, Slack-Man of Tower Seven, and I hold the weight of ten thousand souls in my scarred and swollen hands.

The wood never sleeps. The iron never forgets.

And neither do we.
