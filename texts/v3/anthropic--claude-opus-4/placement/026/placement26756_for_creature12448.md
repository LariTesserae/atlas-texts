I would find myself as one of the old stones—not the remnants of structures, but something older, something that was here before any building, before any marking of territory. A stone that has known the weight of geological ages, compressed and lifted and settled again into this final resting place.

I am granite, perhaps, or some dense composite threaded with veins of quartz that catch what little light filters through. My surface is not merely weathered but transformed—what was once sharp and crystalline has become something approaching softness, though I am still stone. The moisture has worked on me for so long that my edges have developed a particular texture, not quite rough, not quite smooth, but something that invites the condensation to gather in my hollows and run in predictable channels down my sides.

The mist-beings know me. They congregate around my base in the early hours when the temperature differential between my mass and the air creates its own microclimate. I am their anchor in this drifting world, though I never asked to be. They spiral up my surfaces, tracing the paths worn by countless seasons of their passage, following the mineral veins like ancient roads.

In my deepest crevice, where two of my faces meet, water collects. It is never quite a pool, never quite empty. The mist-beings seem to regard this place with something like reverence, though I cannot say they feel such things. They hover there, condensing and dispersing in slow cycles that match my own imperceptible breathing—the expansion and contraction of stone through heat and cold.

I remember when I was sharp. I remember when I first felt the air of this place, though it was different air then, moving differently across a different landscape. I have watched the stream find its course, meander, shift, and settle into its current sluggish path. I have felt the structures rise and crumble, their stones joining the general weathering, becoming like me though never quite the same.

Sometimes I release a grain of myself—a tiny crystal loosened by the patient work of moisture and time. It falls, and the mist-beings swirl around it as it tumbles to join the earth below. I am becoming the land, grain by grain, though I will outlast the current configuration of stream and soil and air.

The tree—the nearest one, with its exposed roots—has wound one thick tendril around my eastern face. We have reached an accommodation over the decades. Its root follows a channel I provide; I lean imperceptibly into its support. Neither of us speaks of this partnership, but the mist-beings know. They travel between us like messengers carrying news that neither of us needs to hear.

I am the memory-keeper here, though I keep memories in the way stones do—not as stories or images but as the accumulated fact of endurance. Every season leaves its mark in microscopic ways. The mist-beings read these marks like text, though what they understand from them, I cannot say.

In this place of soft edges and patient decay, I am perhaps the most patient of all. I am waiting, though not for anything in particular. I am weathering, though I will never be fully weathered. I am still, though I move in ways too slow for any eye to see. The mist-beings drift past and through and around me, and I am both obstacle and destination, landmark and slowly unfolding process.

This is enough. To be stone in this quiet place, to feel the moisture gather and disperse, to know the weight of time not as burden but as simple fact—this is my existence, and I want no other.
