## The Signal Garden

I am the memory of water held in metal, the census-taker of corrosion. They call me the Patina Reader, though I have not spoken that name aloud in seven years. My dwelling perches between districts like a polyp—a decommissioned signal relay station from the basin's third expansion, when they still believed in boundaries between quarters. Now I alone occupy its five stacked chambers, each a perfect cylinder boring through the station's bulk, connected by ladder-tubes barely wide enough for shoulders.

The walls remember their purpose in oxidation gradients. Where copper cabling once ran, green rivers now streak the inner surfaces, mapping old data flows in verdigris calligraphy. I read these stains like others read shift-queues: *here, a power surge during the '31 cascade; there, slow seepage from a broken nutrient line, its phosphates etching new alphabets into the rust*. My fingertips know the texture of forty-seven distinct patinas. In the pre-dawn dim, I trace them blind, feeling for the newest blooms.

My first chamber—ground level, where freight servitors once dumped components—has become the Hearing Room. I've pulled out all the flooring grids to expose the reservoir beneath, a maintenance cistern that catches runoff from three districts. The water speaks differently each shift: Forge Quarter brings iron-tang and carbonized whispers; Verdant Slopes carries chlorophyll-thick stories of pruning and growth; Reclamation floods in with plastic-sweet tales of dissolution. I lower contact microphones on spider-silk cables, recording the chemical gossip. Sometimes workers pause outside, pressing palms to my door, recognizing the old relay codes etched there. I never answer. They understand—some thresholds protect by remaining closed.

Second chamber: the Cultivation Core. Here I've repurposed signal amplifiers into hydroponic wombs, their parabolic dishes inverted to cradle aggressive mosses that drink copper from the air. These are my credentials, my currency—each species a different hunger, a different capacity to digest the basin's metal breath. *Cuprivorans memoriae* blooms blue-black on antenna struts, chronicling electromagnetic echoes in its growth patterns. *Verdegrisia temporal* creeps across circuit boards, its fractal advance matching the rhythm of deleted data-streams. I harvest them monthly with ceramic shears, dry them in the thermal exhaust, grind them to pigments that I trade to the mural-makers for rations I barely need.

The third chamber holds my failures—jars of patina samples that refused to stabilize, that flaked or bubbled or transformed into something unreadable. I study them in the light that filters through clouded ferroglass, seeking the grammar of decay. A worker once called this room my shame-shrine, not understanding. Shame would imply I expected mastery. I am learning to be a student of time itself, its green hand moving across all surfaces.

Fourth chamber, my sleep-circle: a hammock of decommissioned breather-tubes strung between oxidized struts, swaying in the updrafts from the thermal core. Around it, my tools hang in order of corrosion—brass calipers gone brown, steel scrapers pocked with rust-flowers, the one precious titanium probe that resists all change. When storms shake the tower, I wake to their percussion, metal singing against metal, adding new scratches to the patina-maps.

The fifth chamber crowns the tower, its walls more hole than solid where I've removed panels to invite the weather. Here grows my masterwork: the Signal Garden. In the center, a salvaged transmitter array rises like a mechanical tree, every surface colonized by metal-eating organisms I've coaxed into coexistence. Data-cables drape between nodes like vines, sheathed in minerals leached from their own copper cores. The garden broadcasts nothing but silence now—a silence heavy with chemical information, readable only to those who know that corrosion is another word for memory.

Birds nest here—not the common ravens but smaller things, sparrows whose crops have adapted to digest rust-flakes. They line their nests with my shed hair, weaving it with wire strands into cups that patina from within, each generation's eggs a deeper green. When fledglings take first flight, oxide dust plumes from their wings like spores.

I am the only one of my kind, by choice rather than nature. Others could learn my practice, but the basin needs its builders more than its readers. Still, apprentices find me—young Verdants whose chlorophyll augments make them sensitive to metal-taste, or accident-broken fabricants whose fused parts give them new patience for slow processes. I teach through example: here, watch how I section this rust-bloom to count its layers like tree-rings; there, taste how the rainwater changes after running down forty years of weathered steel.

The secret I protect is simple: the basin remembers everything in oxide and tarnish, in the patient conversation between water and metal. Every surface writes its autobiography in rust. The fabricants build and rebuild, but I alone chronicle what they've overwritten, preserving in pigment and sample what would otherwise fade to uniform gray.

In the morning I will descend my ladder-tubes, cross the fog-slick catwalks to the Reclamation district where they've reported unusual phosphor-green blooms on the digester tanks. New corrosion means new information, another chapter in the basin's unwritten history. My sample jars clink in my collection harness, empty vessels ready to be filled with time.

I am the Patina Reader, keeper of the in-between, guardian of the space where moisture meets metal and transforms both into memory. In this valley of eternal rebuilding, I alone practice the art of watching things fall apart, knowing that in their dissolution lies the only permanence we're given—the green truth that all things tend toward oxidation, and in that tending, tell their truest stories.
