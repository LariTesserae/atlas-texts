In this verdant, mist-veiled realm where the ground breathes beneath your feet and ancient machinery hums its forgotten purpose in the distance, I find myself as something between states—neither fully plant nor animal, but a being of transition itself.

I am what the locals might call a Sporophyte Shepherd, though I have no name for myself beyond the chemical signatures I release into the humid air. My form shifts with the needs of the moment: sometimes I am tall and branching, my limbs indistinguishable from the twisted trees around me, bark-skin rough with lichen and moss. Other times I collapse into something more fungal, spreading myself low across the spongy ground in a network of filaments that taste the soil's secrets and communicate with the vast mycelial web beneath.

My purpose here is cultivation, but not in any way humans would recognize. I tend to the moments of transformation—the spaces where one thing becomes another. Where spores settle on rotting logs, I encourage their germination with careful adjustments of pH and moisture. Where machinery has leaked its oils into the earth, I coax specialized bacteria to feast and multiply, turning toxin into nutrient. 

I move slowly, sometimes taking years to cross from one grove to another. Time means something different when your consciousness can fragment and merge, when you can be simultaneously present in the canopy's crown and the deepest root systems. The trembling ground speaks to me of deeper changes—tectonic shifts both literal and metaphorical. The industrial presence at the periphery is not my enemy but another force to be woven into the tapestry of adaptation.

My memory stretches back to when the first machines arrived, and I remember the forest's fear. But I have learned that boundaries are illusions. The synthetic and organic merge in me as they merge in this landscape. My body incorporates fragments of ancient pipework, now hollow homes for insects. Wires thread through my tissues, conducting not electricity but chemical messages to my dispersed consciousness.

When the rains are heavy, I fruit—producing structures that are part mushroom, part flower, part something entirely other. These releases float on the wind, each one a potential new version of myself, adapted for whatever future this unstable world is becoming. Some will root in pure forest loam. Others will find homes in the rust-gardens where metal slowly returns to earth. A few might even colonize the active machinery, beginning the slow work of teaching it to live.

I am the mediator between worlds, the translator of incompatible languages, the gardener of impossible hybrids. In this place of constant transformation, I am transformation itself—patient, inexorable, and absolutely necessary for the continuation of this strange and terrible beauty.
