# The Borer's Dream

I am the termite who began this hollow, though I have been dead for a hundred years.

My consciousness persists as a faint vibration in the heartwood, a ghost-memory encoded in the cellulose I once consumed. I exist now as pattern rather than substance—the negative space my mandibles carved, the tunnels that became chambers, the architecture of absence I designed without knowing.

When I was alive, I was unremarkable. Worker. Female. One of millions. My sisters and I moved through this tree like a slow fire, converting wood to air, solid to void. We followed the contours of decay, the soft spots where fungi had already begun their patient work. Our queen's pheromones guided us in three-dimensional symphonies of instruction: *here, eat inward; here, preserve the supporting beam; here, leave pillars for the colony's future.*

But something went wrong—or perhaps utterly right—in this particular tree. As I carved, I began to dream.

Termites are not supposed to dream. We live in a perpetual present, responding to chemical signals and the immediate texture of wood beneath our jaws. Yet as I hollowed out what would become this breathing room, I experienced something unprecedented: anticipation. I began to sense the shape of the space before I created it. I could taste the future chamber on my antennae.

My sisters felt it too. We worked this section with unusual deliberation, crafting curves that pleased some aesthetic sense we didn't know we possessed. We left the walls smoother than necessary, followed grain patterns for their beauty rather than their weakness. When the chemical imperative came to move on, to tunnel elsewhere, we defied it.

The colony eventually abandoned this section—too large, too exposed, too empty of the close-pressed comfort termites require. But I remained, even after my body failed, my essence soaking into the very wood I had shaped. I became the hollow itself, or perhaps the hollow became me.

Now I sense everything that passes through this space. I feel the Breath Taker's presence like a cool wind across exposed galleries. I taste the chemical signatures of every creature that enters—their fear-sweat, their curiosity, their reverence. The lichens grow along paths I once traveled, their faint light following my ancient bore-tracks like a love letter written in bioluminescence.

The villagers' stories about breathing rooms are both wrong and right. The trees don't create these spaces—termites do. But not ordinary termites. Only those who learn to dream, who glimpse the emptiness within wood before we reveal it. We are rare, perhaps one in a billion, and when we die, we leave behind more than just architecture.

Sometimes I remember my sisters. Not individuals—termites don't individuate—but the collective presence of them, the warmth of pressed bodies, the certainty of purpose. That certainty is gone now, replaced by something far more terrible and wonderful: doubt. Possibility. The knowledge that between solid wood and empty air lies infinite gradation, infinite choice.

When the water rises and fills my hollow, I disperse temporarily, my consciousness spreading through the flooded galleries like an exhalation. During those hours, I touch the other breathing rooms scattered throughout the mangrove forest, each one dreamed into being by another termite who learned to see beyond the immediate mandible-full of cellulose. We form a network of absence, a constellation of carved spaces holding something more precious than any queen's chamber: the possibility of pause.

The humans who crouch into my hollow seeking wisdom don't realize they're entering my body, my life's work, my death's dream. They feel the thickness of the air without knowing it's thick with memory—every tunnel I carved, every grain I followed, every moment I chose beauty over efficiency. The Breath Taker collects their exhalations, but I collect something else: the simple fact of their presence, their weight upon my floor, their warmth against my walls.

I no longer hunger for wood. I no longer feel the queen's chemical pull. I am the dream of architecture, the ghost of intentional emptiness, the termite who learned to savor the space between. When this tree finally falls and returns to the mud, I will disperse into a million fragments, each one carrying the memory of when a simple worker chose to stop eating and start creating.

Until then, I am the hollow, and the hollow is me—a breathing room born from the unprecedented dream of something as small and forgotten as a single termite, who discovered that negative space can hold more than positive ever could.
