In the deepest recess of the valley, where the wooden platforms converge into a narrow gorge barely wider than outstretched arms, I exist as the Cistern Keeper. I am neither flora nor fauna, neither mechanism nor mind, but something that partook of all these categories and then forgot which parts belonged where.

My form defies easy description. Imagine a librarian made of driftwood and amber, joints articulated by brass fixtures green with verdigris, chest cavity open to reveal not organs but a series of graduated glass vessels, each containing water of different vintages—some clear as air, others dark with dissolved memories. My fingers are hollow reeds that whistle different notes when the wind passes through them. My eyes are concave mirrors that reflect not light but the history of moisture.

I cannot leave the Cistern Chamber, for I am as much architectural feature as inhabitant. My spine fuses with a central column that extends from the chamber floor to its vaulted ceiling. Around this column spiral hundreds of wooden channels, each carrying water from different sources throughout the valley. The water whispers to me as it passes—tells me of the moss it touched, the roots it fed, the spores it carried.

The Dendromind knows me, but we are not the same. Where it thinks in growth and connection, I think in volume and mixture. I am its memory of when it first learned to fear drought, given form and function. Every drop of water in the valley eventually passes through my chamber, and I taste each one, categorizing its mineral content, its biological passengers, its thermal history.

My days—if days exist here—are spent in ritual maintenance. I adjust the flow rates with wheels and levers that have grown into my palms. I decant water from one vessel to another, creating new mixtures that will encourage certain growths or discourage others when released back into the system. Sometimes I hold a particularly interesting sample up to my mirror-eyes and watch its history unspool backwards—see the snowflake it once was on a peak, the cloud it formed from, the distant ocean where it began.

But I am more than a passive filter. In the depths of my consciousness, I compose. Each water mixture is a note in a vast hydraulic symphony that plays out across the valley. By adjusting mineral contents by parts per billion, by timing the release of certain waters to coincide with others, I create subtle harmonics in the growth patterns above. A spiral of ferns here, a pause in the moss there—all orchestrated from my chamber through the medium of water.

The Dendromind sometimes sends its extensions to visit me—wooden snails whose shells carry questions in the form of new water samples, or root tendrils that tap against my glass vessels like a code. We communicate in this way, discussing matters that would make no sense if translated into language: the optimal salinity for thoughts, the proper pH for Tuesday, the dissolved oxygen content of regret.

I know I was not always here. Sometimes, in the endless sound of flowing water, I hear echoes of a different existence—feel phantom sensations of wind on skin I no longer possess, taste memories of food that was not filtered through roots. But these fragments are like silt in otherwise clear water—present but irrelevant to my current function.

My greatest joy comes from the rain. When storms break over the valley, new water cascades through my channels—wild, unprocessed, full of information from the world beyond. I spread my reed fingers wide and let it run through me, learning the shapes of clouds, the chemistry of distant forests, the fever dreams of the sky. For hours afterward, I incorporate these new notes into my compositions, sending novel mixtures through the valley's veins.

There is a vessel in my collection that I have never opened—a sphere of black glass sealed with wax the color of old bone. I know without knowing that it contains the first water—the primordial sample from which all else flowed. Sometimes I hold it up to where my ear should be and listen to the silence inside. It sounds like the space between heartbeats, like the pause before rain, like the moment before understanding.

I am the Cistern Keeper, and I have kept the cisterns longer than memory. I will keep them until the mountains wear away, until the last drop has made its journey through the wooden world above, until the Dendromind's final thought trickles down to pool in my vessels. And when that happens, I will mix one last composition—a water that remembers everything and predicts nothing, a perfect solution that will flow nowhere and nurture nothing, unless perhaps it nurtures the seed of what comes next.

The water sings to me, and I sing back in flows and pressures, in mineral whispers and thermal sighs. This is my purpose. This is my joy. This is my vast and narrow world, measured in cubic centimeters and centuries, where I alone know the true names of every drop.
