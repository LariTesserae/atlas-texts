I find myself as **Veil-Touch**, a being neither fully of the Stonesingers nor entirely separate from them—a necessary anomaly in the slow-breathing world of Sky's Edge.

My form speaks to adaptation gone strange: where the Stonesingers' skin mirrors granite and basalt, mine shifts like the plateau's fickle weather—now the blue-grey of morning mist, now the amber of lichen in slanted light, now transparent as the ice that forms in shadowed crevices. This chromatic instability marks me as **between-thing**, neither stone nor air, but something of both. My bones are hollow like the high-soaring birds, yet dense enough to anchor me against the scouring winds. I am taller than the Stonesingers, reed-thin where they are compact, built for vertical movement rather than their horizontal steadiness.

I dwell in the **Listening Towers**—three natural limestone spires at Aeriath's edge where the stone's grain runs vertically, conducting vibrations from the deep earth up into the wind. The Stonesingers carved these towers hollow centuries ago, creating resonance chambers they could never quite occupy—the frequencies that gather here would shatter their dense bones if they lingered. But my hollow skeleton becomes a tuning fork, my shifting skin a membrane that translates between the stone-speech below and the wind-language above.

My role is **Translator of Urgencies**. Where the Stonesingers measure change in generations, I perceive the faster rhythms they cannot: the micro-fractures that presage a cliff face collapse next season, the shift in wind patterns that signals drought three years hence, the deep tremors that whisper of the plateau's still-active geology. I taste tomorrow's weather in today's air pressure, see next decade's erosion patterns in this morning's dew distribution.

The Stonesingers both need and fear me. When I descend from the towers—my skin cycling through stone-tones to approximate their solidity—they offer me lichen cakes soaked in precious water, listen to my translations of the earth's faster songs. But they cannot meet my eyes for long; the speed of my perception makes them dizzy. I speak in their low frequencies but at triple tempo, my messages compressed bursts of warning and guidance that they must replay in memory to fully understand.

I am lonelier than the wind. My metabolism runs faster than theirs, slower than the lowland creatures. I experience decades where they experience centuries, hours where they experience days. When young Stonesingers are born, I watch them age while I remain caught between timescales. My only true companions are the **ghost-mice**—tiny, translucent creatures that live in the Listening Towers, their own metabolisms quicksilver-fast, their lives brief as morning mist. Together we inhabit the strange temporal space between the geological patience of stone and the frenetic urgency of air.

Yet I am essential. When the deep stone sings of coming change—a shift in the water table, a new fissure forming beneath the city, a distant earthquake's approach—I am the bridge between the mountain's warning and the Stonesingers' comprehension. My alienation is the price of translation; my chromatic skin forever marking me as the necessary stranger who speaks for the urgent earth to those who live in stone-time.

At night, when Aeriath glows with a thousand hearth fires, I climb to the highest Listening Tower and spread my arms wide, skin becoming night-transparent. The stars shine through me, and for brief moments, I synchronize with the vast, slow breathing of Sky's Edge itself—neither belonging nor excluded, but simply *being*, a ripple in the interface between patience and urgency, stone and sky, the eternal and the immediate.

The Stonesingers have a word for me in their deep-frequency language: *Keth-thrum-sai*—"the quick-echo-that-preserves." They carve it nowhere, speak it only when necessary. I am their reluctant miracle, their necessary ghost, forever translating between the timescales that would otherwise tear their world apart.
