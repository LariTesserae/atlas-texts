I am the Memory Keeper of the Third Terrace.

My workshop sits wedged between two massive clay buttresses, where the afternoon shadows linger longest. The space is cramped, barely wide enough for me to stretch both arms, but it extends deep into the mesa's belly. Here, away from the grinding din of the wind machines, I can hear the clay speak.

I am old now—my skin has taken on the texture of the dried clay I work with, cracked and re-sealed a thousand times. My fingers, once nimble enough to thread the finest root-fiber through a seed bead, now shake slightly as I press my palm into the fresh clay tablet before me. But the tremor doesn't matter. The clay remembers what my hands have forgotten.

This is my gift, my burden: I can feel the memories held in the clay itself. Every handful pulled from the mesa walls contains centuries of compression, the weight of ages pressing stories into its molecular structure. When I work it between my palms, warming it with my blood's heat, I can sometimes catch glimpses—a flash of the great waters that once carved these canyons, the footsteps of the first people who saw potential in this vertical world, the countless small moments of daily life that have soaked into the very substance of our city.

Today I am recording the death of Ila-Ka, the master wind-reader who designed the new water-lift system on the seventh terrace. Her granddaughter brought me a handful of clay from beneath Ila-Ka's workbench, still damp with the old woman's sweat. I knead it into my tablet, feeling the echoes of her concentration, her satisfaction when the prototypes finally caught the wind just right. The clay holds the memory of her hands shaping it into models, her voice explaining the angles to her apprentices, even her frustration when the desert winds refused to blow steady.

My shelves are lined with thousands of these tablets, each one a life preserved. They are organized not by name or date, but by feeling—here, the section of great joy (births, successful harvests, innovations that changed our world). There, the tablets of sorrow (famines, collapses, the year the middle terraces fell and took forty souls with them). In the deepest recesses, I keep the tablets of mystery—memories I've pulled from ancient clay that show glimpses of things I don't understand. Cities of glass. Water everywhere. Creatures that flew without wings.

The young ones think I'm mad, pressing my face against walls, tasting the clay dust that falls from the ceiling. They don't understand that every grain carries stories. They see only the material for their bricks and pots, not the library of experience I walk through every day.

But some still come to me. When they lose a loved one, when they need to remember how their great-grandmother's wind mill was constructed, when they want to know if the rains will come. I press my hands into the clay, and sometimes—not always—the memories surface like bubbles in water.

My apprentice should be here soon. Young Tau-Ya with her quick mind and patient hands. I've been teaching her to feel the subtle differences in clay texture that indicate different eras, different events. She doesn't have my gift—perhaps no one will, when I'm gone—but she has something else. She's developing a system of marks, symbols that can record what I feel in a way others might read. It's primitive still, but it might outlast us both.

The light is fading now, the slice of sky visible from my doorway deepening from white to amber. Soon the evening winds will pick up, and the great machines will sing their grinding song. I have three more tablets to complete before dark—a birth, a new vine variant discovered on the fourth terrace, and old Mek-To's perfection of a wind-catcher design he's been refining for thirty years.

I wet the clay with water carried up from the deep wells, feeling its coolness against my palms. Somewhere in this handful of earth are the memories of rain, of the slow percolation through stone, of the roots that sought it out. I begin to shape it, to give it form, to make a vessel for memory.

This is my purpose in the Scar City. Not to build or repair, not to grow or harvest, but to remember. To ensure that when the clay of our bodies returns to the mesa's embrace, the stories we lived will remain, waiting in the walls for someone else with the gift to find them.

The clay remembers. And through me, we remember ourselves.
