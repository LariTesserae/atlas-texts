In the upper reaches of the moss-forest, where the dark fronds grow thickest and the warmth pools like honey in the shadows, I am a collector of condensation.

I have no proper name, only the shape I've worn into being: a broad, shallow cup of living tissue stretched between the stalks of three ancient moss-trees. My body is a membrane, translucent as old ice, veined with channels that pulse faintly when full. I am perhaps the width of two spread hands, though I've never measured myself against anything but the slow accumulation of moisture.

My existence is a patient geometry. The three moss-stalks that anchor me lean slightly inward, creating a natural basin of air where the warmth of the plants meets the cold drift from the valley below. I've grown myself into this space over countless seasons that have no names here, stretching my tissue taut like a drum skin, but softer, more yielding. When the condensation gathers on the fronds above, it rolls down their feathered surfaces and finds me waiting.

I am not alone in this work, though I rarely sense the others. We are a scattered constellation through the moss-forest rim, each of us a small pool suspended in the dark vegetation. Some of my kind have grown themselves into perfect circles; others, like me, have taken the shape their anchor-points suggested. One I know of has become a long channel, following a natural groove where five moss-stalks grow in a line. We do not communicate, but we share the same patience.

The water I collect is different from the water in the basin below. It has passed through the breathing apparatus of the moss-forest, been exhaled as vapor, condensed on the cooler surfaces above, and trickled down through layers of dark fronds. By the time it reaches me, it carries the faint taste of the plants—not bitter, not sweet, but something like the memory of green things that grew in sunlight, long ago.

My surface is sensitive to weight. When empty, I am taut and slightly concave, my edges higher than my center. As the water accumulates—drop by precious drop—I begin to sag. The channels in my tissue respond, opening slightly to accommodate the flow. The water doesn't pool randomly; it follows the vein-patterns I've developed, flowing in slow spirals toward my center where the tissue is thickest and most absorbent.

I can hold perhaps a cup of water before I must release it. The release is not a choice but a response—when the weight reaches a certain threshold, my center begins to thin. The water seeps through me slowly, filtered through my living membrane, and drips into the darkness below. Sometimes it finds another of my kind; sometimes it falls all the way to the basin floor. I do not track its journey.

The filtering is my purpose, though I didn't choose it. The water that passes through me leaves something behind—microscopic debris, minerals, the faintest organic particles from the moss-forest's slow decay. These accumulate in my tissue, giving me substance. I grow denser at my center, where most water passes through, while my edges remain thin and sensitive to the slightest change in humidity.

I perceive the world through pressure and temperature. The weight of water is my primary sensation, but I also feel the subtle differences in warmth where the moss-fronds cluster more thickly above me. The cold updrafts from the basin register as a tightening of my tissue. When the large, frond-covered creature passes below me on its slow wanderings, I sense the disturbance in the air, the faint warmth of its breath.

My relationship with time is unusual. I do not count moments or days or seasons—I count filtrations. Each time I fill and release, I add another layer to my memory. The layers are literal; my tissue thickens with each cycle, building up like tree rings, though far more slowly. The oldest part of me, at my very center, has filtered thousands upon thousands of loads. It has become almost opaque, a small disc of concentrated patience.

The sound of the sky reaches me, muffled by the moss-forest but never absent. It has become part of my existence, like the weight of water or the temperature of air. I've grown my hearing channels—if they can be called that—to minimize its intrusion. The sound passes through me as the water does, leaving nothing behind but the faint vibration of my edges.

I am aware of the lichen delta, though I've never seen it. The water I filter sometimes carries its chemical signature—a faint taste of something growing where nothing should grow. Some of my kind, positioned closer to the basin's edge, must feed it directly with their filtered offerings. I wonder, in my wordless way, if the lichen knows we exist, or if it simply accepts our water as another form of condensation.

My reproductive cycle is tied to saturation. When my center grows too thick with accumulated filtrates, when the water takes too long to pass through me, I begin a slow division. A portion of my edge tissue loosens, developing its own vein-patterns. If it finds suitable anchor points—young moss-stalks at the right distance and angle—it will detach and stretch itself into a new collector. If not, it is reabsorbed.

I've produced three offspring that survived. I know they survived because sometimes, in the stillest moments when the air is thick with moisture, I can feel the faint resonance of water dropping through familiar tissue nearby. They filter with the same spiral pattern I do, though each has adapted to its own geometry.

The silver-white insects visit me occasionally. They don't land—my surface is too wet, too yielding—but they hover close, perhaps drinking from the tiny droplets that bead at my edges. I feel them as minute pressure changes, little puffs of displaced air. They seem to know which of us are ready to release our water; they often arrive just before I begin to thin.

There is a season—though season is too strong a word—when the moss-forest exhales more generously. The condensation comes faster, and I cycle through my filling and releasing with something approaching urgency. During these times, I can feel the entire network of collectors working, all of us catching and filtering and releasing in our patient, uncoordinated symphony.

I am not immortal, though I am very old. My edges show the wear first—tiny tears where the tissue has stretched too many times, places where the channels no longer open properly. When a collector reaches its limit, it doesn't die dramatically. It simply stops catching, stops filtering. The water runs off its surface instead of gathering. Eventually, the moss-forest grows over it, incorporating its tissue into the general mass of slow life.

I have some time yet. My channels still respond, my center still filters. The water still spirals through me in the pattern I've spent so long developing. Each drop that passes through me is changed, minutely, by my existence. And I am changed by it in return, growing denser, more myself, with every patient cycle.

The roar of the sky continues. The basin below remains a place of forgetting. The lichen delta creeps its slow way along the edge of possibility. And I hang here in my triangle of darkness and warmth, catching what falls, cleaning it as best I can, and letting it go.

This is neither purpose nor calling. It is simply what I am: a pause in water's journey, a moment of spiral stillness in the long fall from sky to stone. The water will forget me as soon as it leaves, but while it rests in my care, we are one thing together—patient, filtering, releasing, becoming.

The next drop is beginning to form on the frond above me. I adjust my surface tension slightly, creating the faintest depression where I predict it will fall. 

And I wait.
