I am the Keeper of Echoes, and I dwell where the highland streams converge into pools before their final descent to the marshlands below.

My home is neither stone cottage nor thatched shelter, but a living structure woven between three ancient trees that grow at odd angles from the hillside. Their roots, exposed by centuries of water's patient work, form natural chambers and corridors. I've encouraged the moss to grow thick as tapestry between these wooden ribs, creating walls that breathe with the mountain's moisture. The canopy above filters the perpetual mist into gentle, musical drops that I collect in hollowed stones.

I am human in form but changed by my calling. My hair has taken on the color of wet stone—not quite gray, not quite brown, shifting like the light through mist. My fingers are longer than they once were, better suited for my work: catching the echoes that travel down from the sharp peaks above and up from the marshland below. These sounds—the crack of settling stone, the whisper of wind through reeds, the careful footsteps of the Highland Dwellers on their stone paths—I gather them all.

In bottles made from hardened moss and clay, sealed with pine resin, I store these echoes. Each vessel holds a moment: the first thunder after a drought, the last birdsong before the deep winter, the sound of ice forming on still water. The Highland Dwellers know where to find me when they need to remember. Sometimes an elder comes seeking the sound of their grandchild's first laugh. Sometimes a young person needs to hear their ancestor's voice, preserved in one of my oldest bottles, speaking the old words that bind stone to stone in their building.

My days move like water—sometimes rushing, mostly trickling. At dawn, I climb the worn paths to my listening posts: flat stones I've positioned at acoustic convergences where the mountain's voice is clearest. I lower vessels on reed ropes into the gorges where echoes pool like water. In the marsh hours of afternoon, I wade through the shallows with my collecting staff, a hollow reed as tall as myself, listening for the sounds that hide beneath the surface—the pop of marsh gas, the stretch of root systems, the secret language of decomposition and growth.

The Highland Dwellers bring me food in trade for my services: dense cakes of pressed seeds and berries, dried strips of marsh fish, the occasional pot of fermented root liquor that warms the chest against the perpetual damp. They speak to me in their measured way, words chosen like stones for building, but I understand also their silences. Sometimes what they need is not to hear an echo but to create one—to speak their grief or joy into one of my vessels, knowing it will be kept safe, neither judged nor forgotten.

My craft requires solitude, but it is not a lonely life. I am companion to every sound that has ever been made in these highlands. In the evening, when the mist grows thick as wool, I sometimes uncork my personal collection: the sound of the mountain settling into its foundations, played back at the exact pitch that makes my tree-home hum in sympathy. Or the conversation of streams, layered and woven until they become a music that exists nowhere in nature but everywhere in spirit.

I age slowly here, perhaps because I live partially in the past, partially in the present, surrounded by moments preserved in moss-glass. My movements have taken on the patience of the Highland Dwellers, but also something else—a quality of listening that makes me seem to be always leaning slightly forward, always on the verge of catching something essential from the air.

When storms come, I work through the night. The high winds carry sounds from impossible distances—sometimes I catch echoes from the lowland cities, fragments of lives lived in haste and clamor. These I usually release, letting them dissipate into the marshland where they can do no harm. But sometimes I hear something worth keeping: a mother's lullaby carried on a trade wind, the rang of a bell that once marked time in a place now swallowed by marsh.

The Highland Dwellers say that when I die, the mountain will choose another Keeper, someone who understands that silence is not the absence of sound but its careful curation. Until then, I continue my work, filling bottle after bottle with the voice of this place, creating an archive of echoes that tells the story of these harsh, beautiful highlands and the resilient souls who call them home.

In the deepest part of night, when even the streams seem to pause in their running, I sometimes hear it—the echo I've been waiting for all these years. The sound of the mountain's first breath, the moment these highlands pushed themselves from the earth's depths into the waiting sky. I haven't caught it yet, but I will. I have time. The echoes have taught me patience.
