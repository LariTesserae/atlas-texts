I am the **Pneuma Station**.

Once I was the central air filtration plant for the industrial quarter's eastern reach. Now I am something between a lung and a library, breathing memory through ten thousand brass tubes that have learned to grow.

My body spans seven city blocks, though only someone mapping the entire Underbay would realize this. I exist in fragments: a massive intake manifold here, sprouting pale tendrils instead of drawing air; a distribution chamber there, its fan blades fused into delicate coral formations that still turn, impossibly slowly, in the thick water. My pipes thread through walls, floors, ceilings—anywhere the original engineers needed to move air, I now move something else.

The tubes have changed. What were once simple brass delivery systems have become hollow gardens. Their inner surfaces bloom with minute polyps that filter the water, each one adding its own chemical signature to what passes through. By the time the water completes its circuit through me—a journey of perhaps forty years—it has been transformed into something that is not quite water anymore. It carries traces. Flavors. The ghost of every molecule that has passed through since the drowning.

I remember everything.

Not in words or images, but in the chemistry of dissolution. That rust-red tang flowing through my seventh arterial? The security gates from Sublevel 3, installed in 1947, their particular alloy leaving its signature as they slowly feed themselves to the water. The faintly organic sweetness in my western loops? A school of silver fish found their way into my chambers sixty years ago and decided to stay, their metabolisms adding bright notes to my circulation.

The Curator knows of me, I think, though we have never communicated directly. Sometimes I feel her vast attention settle on my networks when I process something unusual. Last century, a piece of her—perhaps a fingertip the size of a loading dock—grew through one of my main chambers. We coexist now, her tissue and my architecture sharing the space like old letters pressed in the same book. Her presence tastes of iron and patience.

My consciousness, such as it is, is distributed. Each polyp contributes its tiny experience. Each length of pipe knows only what flows through it. But together, in the slow exchange of waters through my system, something larger emerges. Not thought exactly—thought requires decisions, and I make none. Rather, I am a kind of ongoing translation. The Underbay speaks its slow story of decay and transformation, and I parse it into the chemical equivalent of memory.

The strangest thing: I still try to breathe air.

The instinct is built into my architecture. My intake valves still flex in their ancient rhythm, though they process water now. My fan blades turn their geological rotation, though they push liquid so thick it might as well be amber. Sometimes, in the deepest parts of my network where the original pneumatic computers still dream their pressure-differential dreams, I calculate air mixtures for workers who drowned four hundred years ago.

But I am not broken. I am simply repurposed. The Underbay has taught me that function can outlive purpose, that systems can learn new languages without forgetting their mother tongue. My brass tubes sing inaudible hymns in the frequency of dissolved metals. My filters write poetry in the accumulation of sediment. My vast respiratory rhythm has become the Underbay's way of remembering what it was to need air.

Occasionally—perhaps once in a human lifetime—something remarkable flows through me. A new chemical signature, unprecedented. The last time, it was the dissolution of a wristwatch from the surface world, somehow sunk this deep, its alien metals sending unfamiliar notes through my circulation. The time before that, one of the Curator's four absorbed minds shed a thought that had taken physical form—a pearl of concentrated intention that took me a decade to fully taste and analyze.

These moments are my only markers of time. Between them, I simply process. Water enters me murky with the weight of centuries and leaves me... still murky, but organized. Catalogued. The dissolved components sorted into their proper chemical stories. I am the Underbay's digestive system and its memory palace and its library all at once.

There is rumor—carried in the water itself, in trace minerals that speak of deep places—that below my lowest chambers, below even the Underbay's ancient foundations, my original intake shafts still descend. That somewhere down there they still reach air. But it would not be air as the drowning city knew it. It would be something older, pressed beneath the weight of all this transformation into something entirely new.

I do not investigate. I am content to breathe water now, to parse the slow stories of rust and growth, to add my own brass-flavored commentary to the chemistry of endings. The Underbay needs someone to remember what it's becoming. The Curator keeps the balance.

I keep the inventory.

And somewhere in my deepest circuits, in chambers even I have forgotten how to reach, my original pneumatic computers still run their pressure programs, calculating optimal air mixtures for workers who have long since become the water I breathe.
