In the northeastern reaches of the Greenglow Swamp, where the water channels converge into a broader, slower pool they call the Mirror Basin, you would find me. I am neither Lumen nor outsider, but something caught between—a translator of sorts, though not merely of words.

I arrived decades ago, or perhaps it was yesterday. Time moves differently when you begin to hear the swamp's song. I came as a chronicler, drawn by tales of the impossible architecture and the living light. My notebooks, once crisp and ordered, have long since surrendered to the damp, their pages now soft as moss, their ink bleeding into watercolor abstractions that somehow capture more truth than my original precise observations ever could.

My dwelling sits at the confluence of two waterways, built half in the Lumen style—woven reeds and living wood—and half from the materials of my former world: glass bottles that catch and fracture the light, metal instruments whose purposes I've forgotten but whose surfaces now serve as mirrors for the Lumen children to study their own luminescence. The structure has no walls in the conventional sense, only screens of hanging vines that I've trained to grow in patterns that echo the Lumen's light-language. Sometimes I catch them pausing as they glide past, reading messages in my garden that I didn't know I'd written.

I am the keeper of correspondences, though the letters I receive arrive by no postal service. They come as deposits in the mud after storms, as patterns in the fallen cypress needles, as variations in the swamp's eternal hum. I decode these messages through a practice I've developed over the years—part meditation, part mathematics, part pure intuition. My skin has not become translucent like the Lumen's, but I've noticed that in certain lights, usually at dawn when the mist rises, I can see the faint tracery of something beneath—not quite veins, not quite light, but a map of all the places where the swamp has touched me.

The Lumen tolerate me with a bemused affection. To them, I am like a heron that has forgotten it can fly—somewhat pitiful, somewhat amusing, but ultimately harmless and occasionally useful. They bring me things: shells that sing when the water level changes, flowers that bloom only in complete darkness, stones worn smooth that pulse with warmth at intervals matching the swamp's deeper rhythms. In exchange, I offer them my peculiar service.

You see, I've discovered that the swamp speaks not just to its children, but to the wider world. The vibrations that suffuse this place carry outward like ripples, touching distant shores in ways the Lumen, in their perfect integration, cannot perceive. I am the one who notices when the hum shifts a quarter-tone, presaging storms three hundred miles away. I map the correlations between the brightness of their skin-light and the migration patterns of birds they've never seen. I am their unwitting prophet, their accidental astronomer, charting influences they don't believe in but have learned to trust.

My greatest achievement—or perhaps my greatest delusion—is the Resonance Archive. In a cypress hollow that floods at high water, I've arranged hundreds of objects in precise patterns: stones, shells, bits of woven reed, fragments of my old equipment, gifts from the Lumen, things that have washed in from the outside world. When the water rises and covers them, and the moonlight hits just right, and the swamp's hum reaches a certain frequency, the entire collection begins to vibrate in harmony. In those moments, I swear I can hear the voice of the swamp itself—not in words, but in pure meaning, downloaded directly into the bones.

The Lumen young ones have begun to visit the Archive, though their elders pretend not to notice. They sit at the edge of the hollow and match their own light-patterns to the resonance, creating new harmonies I hadn't imagined possible. Sometimes I think they understand what I'm doing better than I do. Sometimes I think I'm just a tool the swamp is using to teach its children something they needed to remember.

I no longer write letters to the outside world. Who would I send them to? That life feels like a story someone told me once, in a language I've half-forgotten. Instead, I write into the swamp itself—carving symbols into the mud that disappear with the next rain, weaving messages into the vine-bridges that only reveal themselves when the wind blows just right, teaching the herons new calls that carry my words downstream to settlements I'll never see.

At night, when the bioluminescence is at its peak and the air thrums with that ancient song, I sometimes catch glimpses of myself in the water. The reflection shows not what I was, but what I'm becoming—a creature of correspondence, a living bridge between the swamp's eternal present and the churning change of the world beyond. My skin may never glow like theirs, but I've learned to shine in other ways, to be a prism for the swamp's light, breaking it into spectra the Lumen have no eyes to see.

This is what I am: the Greenglow Swamp's memory of the outside world, and perhaps, if I dare to believe it, the outside world's dream of what it could become if it learned to listen to the hum that underlies all things. I am the chronicler who gave up chronicling to become part of the chronicle. I am the stranger who stayed long enough to forget what strange means.

And in the Mirror Basin, where the water holds the sky and the sky holds the water, I continue my work—whatever that work truly is—knowing that each day brings me closer to a understanding I may never fully grasp, but which fills me with a certainty as deep and still as the swamp itself.
