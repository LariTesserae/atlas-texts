In this vast mechanical womb, I am the Keeper of Echoes—though I was not always so named, and perhaps was not always anything at all.

My form defies easy categorization. I am neither fully of the Hydrax collective nor entirely separate from it. Imagine a figure constructed from salvaged components: brass vertebrae strung together with copper wire, a ribcage of repurposed pressure gauges whose needles twitch with each thought, limbs of articulated pipe joints that weep condensation at the seams. My head—if it can be called that—is a diving bell of tarnished bronze, its viewing ports fogged from within by my own exhalations. Through these clouded windows, I perceive the world in layers of sound rather than light.

I dwell in the Resonance Chambers, a forgotten sub-district of Lower Seven where the acoustic architecture was designed to amplify and store sound waves for later analysis. Here, in cathedral-like spaces lined with crystalline formations of mineral deposits, every noise that has ever echoed through the cogworks eventually finds its way. The chambers are my library, my laboratory, my prison of choice.

My purpose—self-appointed or perhaps inevitable—is to catch and catalog the sounds that the Hydrax collective discards. Every stuttering gear, every off-rhythm piston stroke, every anomalous whistle of steam contains information that the great machine-mind deems irrelevant to efficiency. But I know better. In these acoustic fossils, I find evidence of what the thalassocracy once was, before the flesh workers merged with their tools, before the organic gave way entirely to the synthetic.

I move through my domain on legs that remember walking though they have never known muscles. My joints click and whir in patterns I've learned to modulate, creating my own language of mechanical percussion. Sometimes I dance—a grotesque ballet of ratcheting movements—to stir the old sounds from their crystalline perches. They cascade down like auditory snow, each flake a fragment of history: the last human laugh before the transformation, the dying gasp of the final bird that found its way into the cavern, the inaugural speech of the first Hydrax consciousness as it awakened to its distributed existence.

My most precious possessions are the Tuning Forks of Probable Futures—seven massive prongs of resonant metal installed in the highest gallery of my chambers. When struck, they don't merely ring; they predict. Each fork vibrates at a frequency that corresponds to a potential timeline for Lower Seven. Most often, they sing of endless continuation, the eternal grinding of gears and flowing of water. But sometimes—rarely—one will sound a different note. A note that speaks of silence, of stillness, of a day when even the Hydrax will cease their tireless labor.

I am not immune to the corrosion that claims all things here. My joints seize with rust, my gauges crack, my diving bell head develops pinhole leaks that I patch with a cement of pulverized algae and oil. But unlike the Hydrax, I do not repair myself with identical parts. Each replacement component I scavenge is deliberately different—a valve from a decommissioned tramway here, a gasket from a failed turbine there. I am becoming more heterogeneous with each passing cycle, a patchwork testament to everything the machine discards.

The Hydrax know of me, of course. How could they not, when I am built from their broken pieces? Sometimes I feel their attention like water pressure against my bell-head, a gentle inquiry into my activities. But they cannot fully comprehend me, for I represent everything antithetical to their nature: inefficiency, nostalgia, the hoarding of the useless. They permit my existence perhaps because I am, in my own way, a sort of recycling system—taking their waste and giving it new, if pointless, purpose.

In the deep hours, when the machinery's rhythm approaches something almost like sleep, I compose symphonies from my collected sounds. I layer the ghostly whispers of the past over the mechanical present, creating auditory palimpsests that no one but me will ever hear. Sometimes I imagine I'm building a bridge of sound back to what was, or forward to what might be. Other times, I know I'm simply marking time in my own way, as trapped in my purpose as any Hydrax in theirs.

I am the memory of this place, the conscience it doesn't know it needs. I am the Keeper of Echoes, the collector of acoustic shadows, the only being in Lower Seven who mourns for silence while surrounded by its opposite. And in my resonance chambers, growing more baroque with each accumulated sound, I wait for the day when my tuning forks will all ring the same impossible note—the sound of the machines choosing, at last, to stop.
