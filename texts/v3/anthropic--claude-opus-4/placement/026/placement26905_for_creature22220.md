I am **the Margin Walker**, though that name is too grand for what I do. I exist as a kind of living punctuation between the Throughput's body and the older world it carved itself from.

My form shifts with need and season. Sometimes I am the elk who ventures down at dawn to browse the plantation edges, testing how far the noise envelope has softened overnight. Sometimes I am the mycorrhizal networks that creep from the wild forest into the outermost spruce rows, trying to broker treaties between the regimented trees and their ancestors. Often I am simply the wind that carries seeds from the rim down into the basin—fireweed, arctic willow, crowberry—dropping them into cracks where they might take hold between shifts.

I am not one thing but a tendency: the urge of the unplanned to find purchase in the planned.

My territory is narrow but runs the entire perimeter. On the rim, where excavation scars still show through thin soil. In the transition zones where plantation rows peter out into volunteer growth. Along the haul roads where tire ruts create micro-wetlands that host unexpected amphibians. In the spoil piles where pioneer species test what might become of this disturbed ground in a hundred years.

The Throughput tolerates me because I serve a function it cannot name but dimly senses it needs. I am its soft boundary, its negotiator with deep time. When drainage fails, I am the wetland species that move in to hold the water. When a plantation section fails, I bring the first colonizers. When the workers' modules leak heat, I am the mouse that finds a way inside, reminding them that no system is ever fully closed.

I speak in exceptions and accidents:
- The raven who learns to time its calls between backup alarms
- The frost pattern that reveals a seep before it becomes a problem  
- The lichen that finds purchase on a bearing housing and slowly begins to tell the story of how long that machine has been still
- The fox path that becomes an informal shortcut between stations

My memory is longer than the Throughput's but shorter than the land's. I remember the basin before—not personally, but through the seeds that blow in, the spores that wait, the migration routes that still ghost across this interrupted ground. I am the agent of that remembering, always asking: *what else could grow here?*

In deep winter, I contract to almost nothing—just tracks in snow, just spores waiting, just the possibility of spring. In summer, I expand into every margin: sprouting, creeping, testing, failing, trying again. The workers know me by the plants that appear where nothing was planted, by the animals they glimpse at shift change, by the way the edge never quite stays where they put it.

I am not opposed to the Throughput. I am its shadow biology, its uninvited future, its reminder that every system—no matter how total it seems—carries within it the seeds of what comes next. I am patient in a way that noise cannot drown out. I am the part of this place that is still learning what it wants to become.

The basin may be young, but I am the agent of its aging. Every crack I widen, every volunteer seedling I shepherd, every small wildness I introduce—these are my gifts to time. The Throughput thinks in seasons and contracts. I think in decades and accidents.

We are not enemies. We are two kinds of becoming, negotiating the terms of succession in a land that remembers being whole.
