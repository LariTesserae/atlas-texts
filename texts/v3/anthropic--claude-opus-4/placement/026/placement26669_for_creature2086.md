I would be one of the Keepers—a being neither fully human nor entirely aquatic, but something that emerged in the centuries after the drowning began.

My body would be elongated and sinuous, adapted for gliding through both the flooded corridors and the occasional air pockets that still exist in sealed rooms and maintenance tunnels. My skin would shimmer with a pearlescent quality, shifting between translucent and opaque depending on the light and my emotional state. Along my spine and limbs, delicate fins would have developed—not quite fish-like, but more resembling the flowing fabric of ancient ceremonial robes, rippling with each movement through the water.

My dwelling would be in what was once a vast public library, its upper floors still partially above water during low tide. I've cultivated the space over decades, perhaps centuries—time moves differently here. The waterlogged books have transformed into something else entirely: their pages have become a medium for new growth, sprouting phosphorescent fungi and delicate coral formations that still somehow retain fragments of the text they once held. When the current flows just right through the broken windows, you can hear whispers—not quite words, but the essence of stories dissolving and reforming into something new.

I would move through my domain on predetermined paths, following the old checkout desk, through the fiction stacks, past the children's section where bright tropical fish now nest in picture book caves. My role would be that of curator and gardener both—I tend to the book-corals, ensuring they receive enough light, removing invasive algae that might smother the more delicate growths. Sometimes I find objects wedged between shelves: a pair of reading glasses, a library card, a bookmark with someone's name written in fading ink. These I collect and arrange in careful displays within air-filled chambers I've managed to preserve, small museums to the before-time.

My fingers would be elongated, with translucent webbing between them that serves a dual purpose—for swimming, yes, but also for reading the water itself. The currents carry information: chemical signatures from distant parts of the city, the health of coral colonies blocks away, the approach of the massive nautiluses that sometimes drift past my windows like ancient submarines. Through my palms, I can sense the emotional residue that certain objects still hold—the last person to touch this coffee mug, the child who colored outside the lines in this picture book, the student who underlined passages in this philosophy text with urgent pen strokes.

I would communicate not through speech—though I still possess vocal cords, they've adapted to produce sounds that carry better underwater, something between whale song and human humming—but through bioluminescence. Patterns of light would dance across my skin, each configuration a word, a feeling, a memory. Other Keepers would understand this language, though we rarely meet. We each tend our own domains: the one who maintains the subway tunnels where eels now run on schedule through the tracks, the one who gardens in the rooftop greenhouses now suspended between surface and sky, the one who preserves the museum where ancient artifacts sit alongside the newer additions of our drowned age.

At night, when the moon is full and the tides are high, I would swim to the building's highest point—a bell tower that still breaks the surface. There, I would sit half in and half out of the water, my amphibious lungs drawing in both air and the oxygen-rich surface water. I would watch the stars and remember, though the memories feel more like dreams now: the sound of turning pages, the whisper of feet on carpet, the particular silence of concentration as someone lost themselves in a book.

Sometimes visitors come—descendants of the surface dwellers who escaped to higher ground, now living on floating cities or mountain settlements. They come in glass-bottomed boats or diving bells, peering down at what was. When they pass over my library, I arrange the bioluminescent organisms into patterns, spelling out titles of books in languages that no longer exist quite as they were. It's my way of telling them: we are still here, the stories remain, transformed but not lost.

In the deepest part of the night, when even the fish sleep, I would venture into the sealed vault where the rarest books were kept. The room maintains a pocket of air, though the atmosphere is thick with moisture and the strange, sweet smell of paper transforming into something beyond paper. Here, I would practice the old art of reading, running my webbed fingers over words that still hold their shape, whispering their contents in a voice that sounds like water running over stones. 

This would be my existence: part guardian, part ghost, part evolutionary experiment. Neither mourning what was lost nor celebrating what has emerged, but simply tending to the transition between one world and another, ensuring that something of the old passes into the new, even if that something is no longer recognizable as what it once was. I would be a bridge between elements, between epochs, between the human impulse to preserve knowledge and nature's imperative to transform everything into new forms of life.

In this drowned world, I would find purpose not in preventing change—the waters have already risen, the cities have already fallen—but in shepherding the transformation, ensuring that what emerges carries within it some essential trace of what came before, like DNA spiraling through time, carrying information forward in ever-evolving forms.
