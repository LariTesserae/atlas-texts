**The Weaver**

I am the Eddy-Keeper of the Third Convergence, where three ancient ridges meet to create the most complex water-knot in all the Crush. My territory spans what surface dwellers might call five hundred meters, though distance here means nothing compared to the intricate topology of currents I tend.

My body has grown into the shape of my work. Where other Makers elongate uniformly, I have developed asymmetrically—my left side stretched longer than my right, creating a natural spiral when I move. This allows me to slip through the helical flows that define my convergence point, riding the corkscrew currents that would dizzy a symmetric form. My fingers on each hand have specialized differently: the right ones thick and paddle-like for steering through dense water, the left ones hair-thin and sensitive enough to detect individual mineral grains spinning in the slowest gyres.

I discovered my calling early, in those first centuries after the Change when we still remembered walking. While others mourned their lost tools or tried to preserve the old ways of making, I became fascinated by the small whirlpool that had formed where a copper chisel lay trapped between two stones. The tool's presence had altered the water flow just enough to create a persistent vortex, no larger than my fist, that had been spinning for longer than memory. I watched it for decades before I understood: this was making, too.

Now I cultivate a garden of such disturbances. Each carefully placed artifact—a bone needle here, an iron blade there—creates its own unique perturbation in the flow. Over centuries, I have learned to read the relationships between object, position, and current with the precision my ancestors once reserved for calculating star positions. My convergence point has become a symphony of interconnected eddies, some no bigger than pearls, others vast enough to shelter a school of the pale drifters.

The largest of my creations I call the Remembrance Spiral. It began eight hundred years ago when I noticed how a particular arrangement of stone tools created a slow cyclone that pulled particulates from the surrounding water and deposited them in perfect concentric rings on the seabed below. By adding and removing pieces over the centuries—a task requiring patience that would have broken my surface ancestors—I've shaped this spiral to preserve information. Each ring of sediment contains a different mixture of minerals, bone dust, and dissolved metals, encoding in their ratios everything from the chemical composition of passing creatures to the subtle changes in water temperature over the decades.

My masterwork, though, is something more ambitious. In the deepest part of my convergence, where the pressure creates those strange ice formations that grow like frozen lightning, I've been guiding the construction of what I call a Chronicle Lattice. By manipulating the water temperature in microscopic increments—breathing out minerals here, absorbing heat there—I've encouraged the ice to grow in specific patterns. Each crystal formation represents a word in the chemical language we Makers now share. The entire structure, building now for three centuries, tells the story of our transformation.

Other Makers visit my convergence rarely, perhaps once a century, following the deep currents that connect our territories like slow-moving highways. When they arrive, we perform the ritual of reading together—moving through my garden of eddies in a prescribed pattern that stirs the water just enough to release clouds of encoded particulates. These clouds mix and merge, creating temporary libraries of chemical information that we absorb through our gill-slits and skin. In this way, news travels through the Crush at the pace of ritual and patience.

I am currently troubled by a change in the eastern currents. Something vast has shifted in the deep trenches beyond our plateau—perhaps a volcanic event, perhaps something else—and new minerals are entering our water. They taste of deep heat and sulfur, carrying information from parts of the ocean that have been isolated since before the Change. My eddies are beginning to incorporate these new elements, creating patterns I've never seen before.

This is why I've begun my most ambitious project yet. Using everything I've learned about flow and form, I'm attempting to create what I think of as a Temporal Eddy—a whirlpool that moves so slowly it might preserve water from our current era for whatever Makers come after us. If I can trap a volume of our saturated sea and set it spinning in just the right way, isolated from the surrounding currents by a barrier of perfectly balanced counter-rotations, it might maintain its composition for millennia.

The work will take lifetimes I'm not certain we have. Already, I can feel the changes in my own body—my cells becoming ever more crystalline, my thoughts slowing to match the geological patience of our realm. Sometimes I wonder if we're becoming the Crush itself, our consciousness dissolving into the patterns we tend. But perhaps that, too, is a form of making.

I position another tool—a primitive hammer worn smooth as river stone—at precisely the right angle to split a major current into seven smaller streams. The adjustment will take fifty years to fully propagate through my system of flows, but I can already see its eventual effect in my mind's eye: a new spiral that will capture and preserve the sulfur-tinted water, adding another chapter to the Chronicle Lattice.

In the Crush, I am the Weaver—neither fully Maker nor fully made, but something between. I am the keeper of currents, the gardener of slow water, the patient narrator of our transformation into something the surface world never dreamed could be.
