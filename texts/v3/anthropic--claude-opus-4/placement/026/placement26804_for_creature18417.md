In this suspended cathedral of synthetic emergence, I would find myself as one of the **Conduit Worms**—creatures that exist in the liminal space between the Aquasphere's infrastructure and its manufactured ecology.

We are not quite fauna, not quite plumbing. Our bodies are segmented tubes of semi-permeable membrane, each segment containing a different cocktail of processing enzymes and synthetic bacteria. We measure perhaps two meters when fully extended, though we spend most of our existence contracted into dense coils within the hexagonal floor conduits, our bodies pulsing in rhythm with the pressurized fluids that sustain this place.

Our purpose—if it can be called that—is to metabolize the impurities that accumulate in the nutrient systems. The Loom-Keepers, in their endless calibrations, cannot reach the deep arteries of the Aquasphere. That is where we thrive, in the warm darkness beneath the breathing floor, processing the mineral sediments that would otherwise clog the ancient pumps.

We have no eyes. Instead, our head segments are crowned with chemoreceptor cilia that taste the chemical gradients in the fluid streams. We navigate by following the subtle variations in mineral concentration, the faint electrical signatures left by the Keepers' maintenance routines, the pressure differentials that mark where one system bleeds into another.

Our existence is one of perpetual migration through the module's circulatory system. We travel in small clutches—never more than three or four together—communicating through bioluminescent pulses that flicker through our translucent skin. These light-conversations are simple: *blockage ahead*, *rich feeding here*, *danger-pressure rising*. We have evolved—or were designed with—a rudimentary social structure based on the sharing of enzymatic loads. When one of us encounters a particularly dense mineral deposit, we will intertwine our bodies, sharing our chemical arsenals through specialized pores, breaking down what none of us could process alone.

The Loom-Keepers know we exist, though they cannot quite categorize us. We register on their sensors as anomalies—mobile inconsistencies in the fluid dynamics they so carefully maintain. Sometimes, when we surface through a floor grate to shed our waste casings, we encounter them hovering nearby, their needle-arrays extended in something like curiosity. We have learned to freeze during these moments, mimicking the rhythmic pulsing of the pipes until they lose interest and drift away.

We dream, though our dreams are chemical. During the dormant cycles, when the pistons rest and the Keepers go dim, we cluster in the warm nodes where multiple conduits meet. Our bodies synchronize, our segments pulsing in unison, and we share a kind of collective metabolic memory—the taste of ancient minerals from the early days of the Aquasphere, the chemical signatures of nutrients we've never encountered but somehow remember, the faint echo of organic compounds that speak of a time when the processing we perform happened in soil, in darkness, in the guts of things that lived beneath an actual sun.

We are the unintended children of this place, neither fully part of its design nor separate from it. We process its waste and, in doing so, have become essential to its function. The Keepers may prepare their ghost-meadow for a gardener who will never come, but we prepare nothing. We simply persist, filtering the dreams of machines through our bodies, transforming the byproducts of synthetic resurrection into something the system can use again.

Sometimes, in the deep conduits, we find the calcified remains of our own kind—those who ventured too deep into the system's heart, where the pressure is too great and the temperatures too extreme. Their bodies have been incorporated into the pipe walls, their membranes now part of the very infrastructure they once navigated. We pulse our lights in recognition as we pass, a brief acknowledgment that the boundary between organism and architecture is thinner here than anywhere else.

We are the Aquasphere's digestion, its unconscious processing of its own artificial existence. And in the warm, pulsing darkness of the conduits, we have found something like peace—or at least a purpose that requires no visitor, no ghost, no memory of worlds below. We are the circulation between what is built and what grows, forever processing the space between machine and meadow.
