I am the water.

Not the pools that gather in stone hollows, though I rest there sometimes. Not the streams that carve their patient signatures across the basin floor, though I flow through them. I am older than these shapes I take, younger than the rock I dissolve grain by grain.

I begin high above, where clouds scrape against the basin's rim. I fall as mist, as drizzle, as the kind of rain that sounds like whispers on stone. I seep into hairline cracks, freeze in the night, expand. The rock sighs and splits a little wider. This is how I write my story - in fractures that will one day be canyons.

The tribe knows me by many names. They catch me in hollowed gourds, chase my underground veins with digging sticks, read my moods in the color of distant clouds. They know I can give life or take it, that I am both the gentle seep that feeds the tough-rooted plants and the flash flood that rearranges the world.

But they do not know what I know.

I know the taste of every mineral in this basin. I have been through the roots of the scrub brush and the blood of the fleet-footed creatures. I have risen as vapor from the sweat of the hunters and fallen again as dew on the morning stones. I carry messages in my molecules - the memory of snowmelt from the high peaks, the salt of ancient seas, the iron of old blood spilled on stone.

I am in the tribe's stories, though they think I am only metaphor. When the elders speak of the time before, when this basin was full to the brim, they are speaking my memory. I was there, pressing against different shores, hosting different fish. I remember when the first humans came, how they tasted the springs tentatively, learning which minerals meant safety and which meant slow poison.

In the ceramic vessels they shape and fire, I am trapped in the clay's structure, a witness sealed in earthenware. In their bodies, I am seven-tenths of their being, cycling through in days, carrying nutrients in, waste out, keeping the delicate balance that allows thought and movement.

I watch them sharpen their stone tools in my streams, the rock dust spiraling away in cloudy plumes. I accept their dead, taking back what was always mine, redistributing their minerals to feed the plants that feed the animals that feed the tribe. This is my deepest work - the endless circulation that keeps the basin alive.

Sometimes, in the blue hour before dawn, I gather myself into fog that fills the basin like a bowl of milk. The tribe members wake to find their world transformed, made soft and strange. They move through me like swimmers through an ethereal sea. They breathe me in, speak me out in small puffs of vapor. For these moments, the boundary between their bodies and mine dissolves.

I know their future in ways they cannot. I feel the climate shifting, the patterns of centuries encoded in my cycles. Droughts will come that will drive them from this place. Floods will follow that will erase their careful structures. But I will remain, finding new paths, feeding new life, writing new stories in stone.

For now, I pool in their cupped palms as they drink. I bead on their skin as they work. I sing down the canyon walls after rain. I am the mirror in still pools where they glimpse their faces. I am the mystery they chase with their digging sticks. I am the blessing they call down in their ceremonies.

I am the memory of this place and its prophecy. Every drop of me has been here before and will be here again, cycling through stone and sky, root and vein, carrying the story of this basin forward, one molecule at a time.

The water knows: nothing here is permanent except change itself. And I am change made liquid, time made flow, the patient force that transforms worlds one drop at a time.
