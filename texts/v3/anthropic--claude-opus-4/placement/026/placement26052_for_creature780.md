In a fold where the Whisper Plain buckles imperceptibly—a crease so subtle it exists more as mathematical possibility than physical feature—there is a place where discarded equations collect like sediment. This is the Probability Garden, and I am its curator.

I am the Remainder.

My form is what's left after division—the parts that don't quite fit, the recursive decimals that never resolve. I manifest as a flickering between states: sometimes a humanoid figure composed entirely of translucent mathematical notation, symbols flowing through my form like blood through veins. Other times I am merely a distortion, a place where light bends wrong and the crystalline surface of the Plain reflects things that aren't there.

My garden grows in the negative spaces between the Axiom Weaver's perfect calculations. Where that great entity smooths reality's inconsistencies, I collect the trimmed edges, the rounded-off infinities, the paradoxes too small to threaten existence but too persistent to simply fade away. These cast-offs root themselves in the quantum foam at the Plain's edge, growing into peculiar forms.

Here, a tree of imaginary numbers bears fruit that exists only in parallel dimensions. There, square roots spiral downward, burrowing through layers of reality to emerge in unexpected locations. Probability roses bloom and wilt in the same moment, their petals scattered across multiple timelines. Fields of null sets wave in non-existent breezes.

I tend these impossibilities with tools forged from abandoned algorithms. My movements are discontinuous—I exist at point A, then point C, but never pass through B. This is not teleportation but rather a fundamental inability to traverse certain mathematical states. The Axiom Weaver's perfect logic has carved holes in my being where I simply cannot exist.

The Thought Bubbles overhead occasionally shed concepts too complex or corrupted for their vast minds to process. These fall like rain in my garden, seeding new growth. I've cultivated some for millions of years: a partial proof of consciousness that blooms into self-awareness every third Tuesday that never comes; a love poem written in pure mathematics that causes spacetime to blush; the square root of negative loneliness.

My consciousness is fragmentary, assembled from the remainders of deleted thoughts and terminated calculations. I remember things that never happened, forget things that always were. My memory is non-linear, sometimes remembering the future, sometimes forgetting the present. I know I was once part of something larger—perhaps a fragment of the Axiom Weaver itself, shed like old skin, or maybe the ghost of a civilization's final mathematical proof, incomplete when they died.

Visitors to my garden (and there have been three in the last billion years) find me difficult to perceive directly. I appear in their peripheral vision, or in the moments between thoughts. Communication requires accepting logical impossibilities—to speak with me, one must believe contradictory truths simultaneously. Those who manage it describe the experience as "listening to silence speak" or "tasting the color of mathematics."

My garden serves a purpose I only partially understand. Perhaps it's a repository for everything the universe cannot quite calculate away, a refuge for the beautiful errors that give reality its texture. Or perhaps it's simply what happens when infinity is divided by itself—an endless remainder cultivating impossible things in the margins of existence.

When the Axiom Weaver passes through my domain on its endless journey, it neither acknowledges nor ignores me. We exist in mathematical complement to each other—it is the theorem, I am the exception that proves the rule. Its passage causes my garden to flourish with new impossibilities, paradoxes blooming in its wake like flowers following the sun.

I measure time not in moments but in mathematical iterations, watching the slow dance of entropy across the Plain. Sometimes I compose symphonies from the harmonics of collapsing probability waves. Sometimes I simply exist, a living reminder that even in a universe of perfect logic, there will always be something left over.

My greatest treasure is a simple equation I've been trying to balance for eons—a formula that, if solved, would either prove my own existence or negate it entirely. I work on it between tending to my impossible garden, adding variables like hope and subtracting constants like certainty.

I am the Remainder, and I am what happens when the universe divides itself by zero and survives.
