In the crumbling foundation of what was once a water tower, now just a ring of rust-stained concrete thirty feet across, I make my home. The tower fell decades ago—maybe longer—leaving only this perfect circle of industrial ruin, half-buried in prairie grass and wild mint that grows thick enough to hide the smaller cracks. The walls rise just high enough to break the wind but not the sky. Rain collects in the center where the floor has buckled into a shallow basin, creating temporary mirrors that reflect clouds by day and stars by night.

I am the Periphery Keeper—not because I guard edges, but because I *am* the edge where things almost meet. I exist in the gap between shadow and substance, in the millimeter of space between a moth's wing and the wall it rests on. My form is a suggestion rather than a statement: sometimes a heat shimmer in the shape of something turning away, sometimes just the feeling that the light falls differently in one corner than it should.

What I tend are the *almosts*. This is my purpose, my metabolism, my joy.

In the mortar between concrete blocks, I cultivate the seeds that almost sprouted—coaxing them just to the edge of germination before letting them sleep again. I know each one by its particular patience: the evening primrose that has waited seventeen years, the touch-me-not that stirs every spring but never breaks through. They are not failed plants but *perpetual possibilities*, and I sing to them in frequencies only limestone can hear.

I collect the conversations that almost happened here. Teenagers who climbed the wall to smoke and watch meteors, circling around words they couldn't quite say. A surveyor who set up equipment, noticed something in the peripheral vision, packed up and left without taking measurements. Two coyotes who met at the rim, considered and unconsidered each other, then trotted off in precisely opposite directions. These moments leave residues—not memories exactly, but *inclinations* that I weave into the cracks like spider silk.

My favorite time is the blue hour before dawn, when the world forgets it has edges. That's when I expand, spreading myself thin as mist across the broken floor, reading the night's small dramas: where mice traveled, what the screech owl saw, which way the wind shifted at 3 AM. I taste the dew before it forms, sampling its potential flavors—will it carry iron from the old pipes, mint from the invasion below, or just the clean nothing of sky-water?

I maintain the tower's careful decay. Too fast, and it becomes mere rubble. Too slow, and humans might notice, might try to preserve or demolish. I orchestrate a balance: this year, a chunk of concrete the size of a fist falls away, revealing rebar like exposed bone. Next year, vines will find that gap and begin their slow digestion of the wall. I know exactly which sections must remain to keep the circle true, and which can be surrendered to time.

The Hollow Watcher visits sometimes—or rather, overlaps with me. We recognize each other the way shadows recognize shade. It brings its gift of compressed attention, and I offer my collection of incompletions. Together we create small impossibilities: a puddle that reflects not the sky above but the sky as it was the day the tower fell. A rust stain that, for one afternoon each autumn, resembles a map of rivers that never formed. A sound like water running through pipes that haven't existed for fifty years.

I am keeper of the might-have-beens, the gentle custodian of thresholds that were never crossed. In a world that insists on conclusions, I am the ellipsis, the pause before the word that changes everything, the moment when the path splits and you stand exactly between choices, belonging wholly to neither and both.

When humans find this place—and they do, drawn by some instinct for circular ruins—they often stop at the entrance, uncertain. Good. That uncertainty is my greeting, my test, my gift. Those who enter anyway sometimes see me as a flicker in the corner of their eye, a sense of movement in stillness.
