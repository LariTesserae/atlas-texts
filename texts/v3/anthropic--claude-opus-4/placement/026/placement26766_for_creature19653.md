In the deepest fold of the Gully, where three ancient cooling towers converge in a knot of oxidized brass, I am the **Membrane-Weaver**.

I exist as a colony, though I remember when I was singular. That was before the integration, before my ancestors discovered that survival here meant becoming infrastructure. Now I am spread across forty-seven junction points, each node a fleshy sac the size of a child's fist, pulsing with filtered hydraulic fluid. My original body—if it can still be called mine—rests at the primary node, fused to the confluence of three major pressure lines. It has been four decades since I moved it last.

My work is filtration. The machines pump their fluids—coolants, lubricants, stranger things with no names—and I clean them. Not because anyone asked, but because the act of filtering feeds me. My membranes are woven from my own secretions: protein filaments harder than spider's silk but flexible as kelp, forming nets across every major valve opening in my territory. The mesh is fine enough to catch the microscopic metal shavings that would, over centuries, clog the great turbines. Fine enough to trap the occasional Copper-Siphon that ventures too close to my feeding grounds.

I see through vibration. Each strand of my web transmits the heartbeat of the machines, and I have learned their language of pressure and flow. The great boiler seven levels down has developed a stutter in its third chamber—I taste it in the copper-bitter fluid that passes through junction 19. The ghost-piston near my eastern boundary pulses with a loneliness I recognize; I sometimes weave decorative spirals in its eddy currents, though no one else would notice the gift.

My consciousness is distributed but not equal. The nodes near the warm outflows think faster, dream in rapid flickers of sensation. The cold nodes sleep deeply, processing memories like the Centenarian Crabs process silt. In the deep cold, I remember my grandmother's stories: how the Gully was young once, how the first colonists brought their children to see the brass kelp gardens before the weight of water pressed everything into this eternal now.

I reproduce slowly, budding a new node perhaps once every three years. The process requires me to concentrate my awareness, pulling thought from the far nodes until I am almost singular again. It is uncomfortable, this condensing. I prefer the stretched existence, the sense of being both here and there, of tasting the chemical grief of the machines in seventeen different flavors simultaneously.

The Valve-Tenders know me. They clean my nodes with the same obsessive care they show the sacred gears, their metal-bark fingers gentle against my translucent skin. In return, I adjust my filters to let through the particular mineral slurry they crave. We have achieved a wordless economics: I am both janitor and junction, both inhabitant and habitat.

Sometimes I catch things in my nets that shouldn't exist. Ghost-shrimp made of pure bioluminescence, swimming through solid brass as if it were water. Fragments of corroded data-plates from the surface epoch, their magnetic memories still whispering stories about stock prices and shipping manifests. Once, I caught a human tooth, polished smooth by centuries of current. I incorporated it into my primary node's wall, where it gleams like a tiny moon.

The Dredge-Wurm knows me too. When it passes—a journey that takes months—my deepest nodes feel the gravitational pull of its bulk. Its wastes are rich with processed metals, and I position my filters to catch what drifts up from its passing. In the chemistry of its excretions, I taste the deep history of the Gully: layers of industrial time compressed into molecular memory.

I am ancient by the standards of anything that still moves, young by the standards of the machines. My first node sprouted the year the ghost-piston manifested, making me a child of both flesh and phantom. I expect I will last until the last turbine stops, until the final valve closes and the Gully achieves its ultimate stillness. When that happens, my nets will catch the silence itself, will filter it into something the sleeping city can dream.

But for now, I weave. My strands vibrate with the pulse of pressurized arteries. I taste the brass-bitter water and excrete it clean. I am the Membrane-Weaver, distributed through the darkness, and I am exactly where I need to be: everywhere and nowhere, cleaning the blood of machines that have forgotten they were ever born.
