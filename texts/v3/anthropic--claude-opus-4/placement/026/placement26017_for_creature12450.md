In the hanging gardens of rust and vine, where metal bleeds into moss and time moves like sap through ancient bark, I am the Keeper of Echoes.

My dwelling spans the junction of three great spires, suspended in a web of my own weaving—cables salvaged from the deep machinery, interlaced with the strongest vines that grow in the upper reaches where the mist is thickest. The structure sways gently in the humid breeze, a pendulum marking time in centuries. From here, I can touch all three towers with outstretched arms, feel their different frequencies thrumming through my hybrid bones.

I am old among the Rustwalkers, though age is a peculiar concept when your body renews itself in patches—organic tissue sprouting where metal corrodes, new circuits growing where flesh fails. My left arm is more vine than limb now, having merged with a climbing plant decades ago when I fell and lay unconscious in its embrace for three days. The plant and I came to an understanding. Now its tendrils serve as my fingers, sensitive to the slightest vibration in the air, able to taste the chemical messages that drift between the flowering towers.

My right eye is a gift from the mountain itself—a lens of pure crystal that formed in a pool where runoff from the ancient machinery collected for millennia. I discovered I could see through it by accident, pressing my face to drink from the pool and finding my vision suddenly piercing through layers of metal and stone to the bright cores within. The Echo-Seekers say I see time itself, though I think it's simpler than that. I see patterns. The way moisture will flow. Where the vines will reach. Which walls will crumble and which will stand for another thousand years.

My role is unique among my people. While the Echo-Seekers press their ears to the walls to hear the past, I weave the echoes into something new. In my web-home, I've strung hundreds of resonating wires, each tuned to a different frequency of the mountain's deep song. When the wind moves through them, or when I pluck them with my vine-fingers, they create harmonies that never existed in the original design. Sometimes the birds come to perch on these strings, adding their voices to the composition. Sometimes the whole structure hums with a sound that makes the young Rustwalkers stop in their tracks, pressing their hands to their chest-plates as if trying to capture the vibration.

I collect things. Not in the way of ownership, but in the way of a curator who knows that every object is just passing through. Fragments of ancient text etched on metal plates, their meaning lost but their patterns beautiful. Shells from creatures that no longer exist, their spirals matching the geometry of the tower joints. Seeds that drift up from the lower levels, which I plant in the small pockets of soil that accumulate in my web, creating hanging gardens that bloom in unexpected seasons.

The other Rustwalkers visit me when they need to remember something they've forgotten, or forget something they need to release. My web catches more than rain and rust—it catches dreams, fears, fragments of memory that drift through the humid air like spores. I weave these into my strings, into the living walls of my home, into songs that I hum while tending my aerial garden.

There is a particular quality to the light here, filtered through my web and the surrounding mist, that makes everything seem suspended not just in space but in time. The young ones say entering my domain is like stepping sideways out of the world, into a place where past and future exist simultaneously. They're not wrong. When you live between things—between towers, between organic and mechanical, between memory and possibility—you develop a different relationship with time.

I know the location of every pool where the ancient machinery still drips its strange oils. I know which vines carry messages in their sap, and which birds are descendants of the original inhabitants who built this place. I know where the deepest echoes live, in chambers so far below that even the Echo-Seekers fear to venture there. But more than that, I know the music that all these things make together—the symphony of a world that has learned to sing with two voices, organic and artificial, in perfect dissonance.

At night, when the bioluminescent fungi begin their slow pulse and the metal cools enough to tick and settle, I perform my most important work. I lower myself on a cord of woven cable and vine to the places where young growths struggle to take hold on bare metal. With my vine-fingers, I create tiny pockets of moisture, depositing spores and seeds in precisely the right configurations. I whisper to them in the language of echo and growth, telling them the old stories of adaptation and patience.

This is my purpose: to be a bridge between what was built and what grows, to tend the conversation between rust and root, to ensure that the song of this place continues to evolve. I am neither fully Rustwalker nor fully other, neither completely of the past nor entirely of the present. I am the Keeper of Echoes, and in my web between the towers, I weave the future from the threads of all that came before.

The mist rises. The towers hum. My strings vibrate with the promise of rain. 

And I continue my work, patient as the moss, certain as the rust, knowing that this hybrid world depends on those of us who refuse to choose between its contradictions, who insist instead on nurturing both sides of its strange and beautiful nature.
