In the Towerswell Region, I would be **a Condensation Keeper**—one who tends the vast networks of moisture collectors that sustain this vertical world.

My dwelling would be a maintenance station wedged between levels, where the old vapor extractors hum their patient rhythm. The space was never meant for habitation, but generations of my predecessors have softened it: a hammock strung between support beams, shelves carved into the condensation-slick walls, a small cooking brazier vented through the same ducts that carry away excess moisture. The walls weep constantly—not from failure, but by design—and I've cultivated a garden of moisture-loving mosses and ferns that transforms the chamber into a green cave.

I am neither fully human nor entirely machine, but something in between—my left arm replaced long ago with a bio-mechanical appendage that can sense humidity gradients and interface directly with the ancient collector systems. The modification was voluntary, a rite of passage for those who commit their lives to the water-ways. My fingertips can taste the mineral content of condensation, detect the faintest mechanical irregularities in the extraction fans, feel the pulse of water through pipes like blood through veins.

My days begin before dawn, when the temperature differential between night and day creates the richest harvest of moisture. I move through my sector—seven levels up, twelve across—checking the mesh nets that capture fog, adjusting the angle of collection panels, clearing roots from intake grilles. I know every droplet's journey: where it forms, how it flows, which gardens it will feed, which families depend on the trickle from my machines.

I wear clothes that have evolved with my work: a coverall of water-wicking fabric that's more moss than cloth now, pockets full of spare gaskets and root-cutting tools, boots with gecko-grip soles for navigating the perpetually wet surfaces. Around my neck hangs a collection of keys—some metal, some grown from hardened fungus—that open access panels throughout my domain.

The other Keepers and I communicate through a language of pipe-taps and whistle-codes that echo through the water systems. Three sharp raps means a blockage; a long, low whistle warns of pressure building. We rarely meet in person, but we know each other by the signature of our repairs, the way we route excess flow, the small offerings we leave at junction boxes—a perfectly spiral shell, a cutting from a rare vine, a handwritten note about weather patterns.

I am intimate with the small lives that share my world: the colonies of beneficial bacteria that keep the water pure, the tiny crustaceans that clean the collection surfaces, the specialized moths that pollinate the night-blooming vines around my station. I feed them sometimes, leaving drops of sugar-water on designated platforms, maintaining the delicate partnerships that keep everything in balance.

At night, I often climb to the highest collection point in my sector, where a massive condensation dish curves against the sky. There, I can see the lights of the Towerswell stretched in every direction—vertical constellations of human habitation—and feel the moisture gathering on my skin, the same moisture I'll guide tomorrow into the vessels and veins of this built world. I am both engineer and shaman, mechanic and gardener, a servant of the slow, essential interchange between air and water, machine and life.

In quiet moments, I maintain the Keeper's Log—a massive book, its pages made from pressed fungus-paper, where generations before me have recorded flow rates, weather patterns, and the small wisdoms of water work. My entries are brief but precise: "Northeast collector, Level 347: increased root intrusion, rerouted through secondary. Moss bloom exceptional. Three new bird nests in housing. Minerals within acceptable range."

I know that one day, my bio-mechanical arm will fail beyond repair, and I'll train an apprentice in the patient art of listening to water. They'll learn that every drip has meaning, every condensation pattern tells a story, every morning's harvest connects to the green life that makes this vertical world possible. Until then, I am the keeper of this essential threshold, where air becomes water becomes life, high above whatever ground once was.
