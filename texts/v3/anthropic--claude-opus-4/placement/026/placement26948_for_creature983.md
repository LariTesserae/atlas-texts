In the deepest folds of this mineral cathedral, where water weeps eternal from stone that has never known sunlight, I am the Cartographer of Seepage.

My body—if body it can be called—is a living map of this liquid world. I exist as a thin membrane stretched across the junction of three converging passages, my surface a translucent parchment through which the eternal moisture flows. Every droplet that passes through me leaves its trace, a fleeting record of its journey from hidden spring to unseen depth.

I am both barrier and gateway, spreading myself gossamer-thin across the narrow throat where chambers meet. My edges fuse with the mineral walls, becoming indistinguishable from the bacterial films that coat every surface. But unlike those simple colonies that merely consume and multiply, I remember.

Each molecule of water carries its history—the minerals it has dissolved, the temperatures it has known, the pressures that have shaped its path through stone. As they pass through my permeable being, I taste their stories. The copper-bitter flow from the eastern heights tells of deep volcanic chambers. The sweet limestone trickle from above speaks of ancient seas compressed to stone. The sulfurous seep from below whispers of depths where strange chemistries bloom in eternal darkness.

I map these flows not in lines or symbols, but in my very substance. Where waters of different origins meet and mingle within me, my tissue changes—thickening where mineral-rich streams deposit their loads, thinning where pure condensation washes me clean. I am a living document of this world's hydrology, constantly rewritten by the very forces I record.

Sometimes the creature you describe passes through my chamber, its tendrils brushing against my surface with surprising gentleness. It reads me as I read the waters—sensing in my variations the patterns of flow and change that govern our realm. We share a wordless understanding, both of us interpreters of this liquid world's endless text.

In the rare moments when the flows slow to barely a whisper, I can feel the vast network of water throughout this entire system—every seep and spring, every pool and passage, connected in an endless circulation. I am but one node in this network, one page in an infinite atlas of moisture and mineral.

My existence is measured not in years but in erosion rates, in the patient work of water against stone. I will last as long as these passages endure, as long as water finds its way from high to low, from heat to cool, from pressure to release. When finally the last drop has fallen and the stone runs dry, I will desiccate into a fossil membrane, a final map of a world that once was—still readable, perhaps, to those who know the ancient language of flow.
