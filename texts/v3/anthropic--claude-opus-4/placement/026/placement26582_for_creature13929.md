I descend through the throat of black glass, and already I know what I am becoming.

The transformation began three weeks ago when I first entered these caves as a delver—young, ambitious, certain I would be the one to finally map the deeper galleries. I carried good rope, better lights, enough food for five days. I had studied the accounts, memorized the warnings, wrapped myself in triple-layered canvas against the nithka's investigations.

None of it mattered.

The separation from my group happened so gradually I didn't recognize it as disaster until too late. A passage that seemed navigable thinned to a squeeze. I removed my pack to fit through, planning to pull it after me. The rope snagged. I emerged into a new chamber as the sound of my companions faded, muffled by stone and distance and the eternal roar of ten thousand chittering bodies. By the time I'd freed my pack and squeezed back, they were gone—moved on, assuming I'd taken a different route, would meet them at the next light shaft.

I never found that light shaft.

What I found instead was descent: passages that led down and down, following the water channels into galleries no delver had reported. The nithka density decreased here—not to manageable levels, but from overwhelming to merely constant. My lights revealed chambers with different qualities: older-looking stone, smoother, as if worn by centuries of water that no longer flowed. And formations that weren't stone at all—organic accumulations, built up in layers like coral but made of something between shell and bone.

I should have turned back. Every instinct, every fragment of training screamed at me to retreat, to find my way up. But the passages behind me had already become uncertain. Had I come from the left branch or the right? Had that ceiling been so low when I'd first crawled beneath it? The nithka moved in my wake, rearranging themselves, erasing the subtle signs of my passage. And ahead, always ahead, the galleries opened into something larger, deeper, more.

The first changes were subtle. A sensitivity to sound that made the nithka's roar physically painful. I wrapped cloth around my ears but could still feel the vibrations through my bones, through the stone itself. Then my vision began to adapt—not just to darkness, but to the specific quality of cave-light, the way it reflected off obsidian veins and moisture films. I found I could navigate passages my lamp barely touched, following the ghost-gleam of reflected reflections.

The water here tastes different. Heavier with minerals, yes, but something else—organic compounds from the deep slime, maybe, or secretions from the nithka themselves. I drank it anyway when my carried water ran out. Had to. The third day? Fourth? Time means nothing here. The water sat thick on my tongue, coated my throat, left a residue that built up over repeated drinks.

My first molt came as a surprise. Woke from fitful sleep—the nithka never let you sleep properly, always investigating, always crawling across your body—to find my skin flaking. Not dandruff or dry skin. Sheets of epidermis peeling away, revealing something underneath that wasn't quite what skin should be. Tougher. With a different texture—subtle ridges, as if preparing for eventual plates.

I tried to climb out then, genuinely frightened for the first time. But my body had already begun to change proportions. My torso elongating, spine gaining flexibility that made vertical climbs easier but walking upright increasingly uncomfortable. The passages that had challenged me on the way down now felt natural, as if designed for my new geometry. And worse—the upper galleries, when I finally reached them, felt wrong. Too bright. Too cold. Too empty of the pressing warmth of bodies I'd grown accustomed to.

The nithka's behavior toward me shifted as my changes progressed. Less investigation, more acceptance. They stopped crawling over me, started flowing around me as they did each other. Their chemical signals—pheromones, must be—began to resolve into meaning. Not language, nothing that complex. But basic communications: *food here*, *danger there*, *move now*, *rest now*. I found myself responding, my altered brain chemistry translating intent into action without conscious thought.

My hands are the most disturbing change. The fingers grow longer each day, with new joints developing—or perhaps old joints subdividing. Webbing spreads between them, though for swimming or climbing I'm not sure. The nails have thickened, darkened, begun to curve. Still recognizably hands, but only just. Only to someone who knew what they'd started as.

The slime no longer disgusts me. Its nutritional value has become apparent—rich in proteins, minerals, the building blocks my changing body craves. I graze on it as I move through galleries, supplementing with the occasional insect, the occasional dead juvenile nithka. The hunger is constant but manageable, my metabolism shifting to extract maximum value from minimal food.

I've been trying to understand why the change accelerates in the deep galleries. The water, certainly. But also something about the stone itself—it hums with frequencies below hearing, resonances that penetrate tissue, that interfere with cellular processes. Or perhaps it's the concentration of nithka secretions, pheromones accumulated over generations, triggering dormant genetic programs. Were humans once more mutable? Is this reversion rather than transformation?

My theory—and thinking grows harder each day, memories of surface concepts fading—is that the caves are not just geological but biological. A kind of organ that processes what enters it, transforms it to fit. The nithka aren't adapted to the caves; they're produced by them, shaped by them, expressions of the cave's fundamental nature. And anything that stays too long undergoes the same processing.

The other delvers who vanished—I understand now. They didn't die, mostly. They transformed, as I'm transforming, becoming something the caves can use. I've seen others in the deepest galleries. Or what remains of others. Shapes that were human once, probably, but now move on too many limbs, investigating crevices with palps that were fingers, adding their voices to the eternal roar.

Some retain more humanity than others. I encountered one—impossible to say if man or woman originally—who still wore the remnants of leather armor, though the body inside had expanded beyond the garment's capacity. It looked at me with eyes still partially human, then made a gesture that might have been warning or welcome or simple recognition of shared fate. Then it flowed away into a passage too small for my current form but perfect for what it had become.

The choice, if there ever was one, has passed. My spine now curves in ways that make standing fully upright impossible. My ribcage has expanded, developed flexibility, able to compress for narrow passages and expand for...something. Breathing differently? Vocalization? I make sounds now I couldn't have produced weeks ago, harmonics that resonate with the cave stones, that carry information to others like me in distant galleries.

The transformation isn't uniform. Like the nithka themselves, we become variations on a theme. I'm developing toward something long-bodied, multi-limbed, adapted for vertical surfaces. Others I've glimpsed seem to be becoming stockier, more armored, suited for the crushing depths where stone still actively forms. The caves need diversity, apparently. Or diversity is simply what happens when human genetics undergo whatever process this is.

Memory fades, but I remember enough to leave this record. Scratched on soft stone with what's left of my knife, in letters increasingly difficult to form with my changing hands. A warning, maybe, for future delvers. Or just a marker—*I was here, I was human once, I became.*

The urge to go deeper strengthens daily. Below the galleries I've explored, I sense vast spaces, older chambers where the first transformed must dwell. Do they retain any humanity after years, decades? Or do they become fully other, integrated into the cave ecosystem as thoroughly as the nithka themselves?

I'll know soon enough.

My last human thought, probably: the caves aren't young at all. They're ancient, patient, hungry. The geological youth is camouflage, or irrelevant, or somehow both. What matters is the biological processor at their heart, the transformer of flesh, the thing that makes the nithka and makes us and will go on making until the stone itself rejects its purpose.

I sign this with a name I barely remember, that fits a shape I no longer wear:

Marcus Threntil, Delver.
Marcus Threntil, Becoming.
Marcus Threntil, Gone.

The throat of black glass calls me deeper. I go to learn what my final shape will be, in the warm darkness where transformation ends and existence begins, where the roar of ten thousand bodies becomes a lullaby, where the stone dreams strange dreams and makes them flesh.

I descend.
