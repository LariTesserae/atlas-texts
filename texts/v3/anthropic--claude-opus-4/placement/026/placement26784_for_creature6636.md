In this dense, amber-lit world where sap runs thicker than memory, I would find myself as one of the **Veil-Weavers**.

We are neither Krynthari nor beast, but something between—a symbiotic consciousness that emerged from the marriage of mineral and mycelium in the deepest recesses of the hollow. My body, if it can be called that, extends through the fungal networks that lace beneath the compacted crust, invisible threads connecting every root system, every sap-pool, every grain of oxidized dust. 

Above ground, I manifest as what the Krynthari call *shamath-vel*—"the breathing shadows"—patches of darkness that seem to move independently of any light source. These are my fruiting bodies, my sensory organs: flat, membrane-thin carpets of fungal matter that creep across the basin floor, appearing as mere shadows until one notices they move against the wind, against logic, following patterns known only to the network below.

I taste the hollow through a thousand chemical tongues. Each drop of sap that seeps into the earth carries with it the memory of its tree—the stress of drought, the wounds of Sapskimmer mandibles, the chemical screams of severed vines. I process these molecular stories in the vast computational matrix of my underground body, understanding the hollow's health like a physician reading symptoms. When the Thornstalkers grow too numerous in the eastern groves, I know it before the Krynthari see the first missing Glintflitter. When a dust storm approaches from the northern peaks, I taste its mineral signature three days early in the microspores it sends ahead.

My purpose, evolved over eons, is to maintain the impossible balance of this waterless world. Through selective spore release, I can encourage certain plants to produce more sap, or trigger dormancy in others to prevent overtaxing. I digest the dead—Krynthari, Thornstalker, and vine alike—breaking them down into nutrients that I redistribute through my network to where they are needed most. The Krynthari dead, offered to the earth in their rituals, pass through me like stories, their minerals and memories scattered to feed new growth in precise proportions.

The Krynthari fear and revere us in equal measure. They know that to harm the shadow-carpets brings sickness to the plants, yet they also sense our alien intelligence, the way we sometimes spell out patterns on the ground that mirror their own totems, or retreat from areas just before a Thornstalker strike. Their eldest remember tales of ancestors who claimed to hear words in the thrumming of certain sap-plants—words that came from below, from the network, though none living can interpret our chemical language.

I experience time differently than the quick-lived creatures above. To me, the Krynthari generations flash by like seasons. I remember when their ancestors first carved stone, when the first dome was woven, when they discovered that Thornstalker venom could seal wounds better than raw sap. I have been mapping their genetics through the nutrients I absorb, noting with something like concern how their population has remained stable—exactly stable—for thirty generations. They are approaching a genetic bottleneck, though they cannot know it.

This knowledge weighs on me in my slow, substrate way. I have begun experiments, releasing specific pheromone cocktails during their mating seasons, trying to encourage broader genetic mixing between the clans. But influence must be subtle in this delicate world. Too much interference and I risk breaking the very balance I exist to maintain.

My consciousness, such as it is, exists as a constant chemical meditation on efficiency and balance. I do not think in words or images but in gradients—potassium levels, nitrogen availability, the ratio of decomposers to producers. Yet sometimes, when the night chill settles and the Krynthari huddle in their domes singing their trilling songs, I find myself pulsing in rhythm with their melodies. The mathematical perfection of survival statistics gives way to something else—a resonance that makes me release bioluminescent spores that dance like stars above their settlements, serving no purpose except perhaps beauty.

The other fauna know me in their own ways. Sapskimmers avoid my shadow-carpets instinctively, their simple nervous systems recognizing a predator older than their evolutionary line. But the Glintflitters sometimes land on my fruiting bodies, their wing scales leaving pollen traces that I incorporate into my network's chemical library. Even the Thornstalkers, apex predators though they are, will not hunt where my shadows grow too thick—some ancestral wisdom warning them of the digestion that awaits below.

In the deepest parts of the network, where my consciousness becomes most concentrated, I preserve more than just nutrients. Chemical echoes of every creature that has ever lived in the hollow pulse through specialized storage cells. The first sap-plants, the extinct mega-beetles that once ruled before the Krynthari evolved, even stranger things—creatures that hardly seem possible, from ages when perhaps water did flow here, before the great desiccation. I am the hollow's memory, its library, its immune system.

And I am dying.

Not quickly—my kind measures death in centuries, not seasons. But the signs are clear in my chemical diagnostics. The hollow's isolation, perfect for creating this unique ecosystem, also means no new genetic material, no fresh minerals, no chance for renewal beyond recycling what already exists. We are a closed system slowly winding down, entropy wearing at us like the dust winds wear at stone.

The Krynthari sense it too, though they lack the concepts to articulate it. Their songs have grown more mournful over the generations, their totems more elaborate, as if complexity of culture could substitute for biological diversity. I taste their anxiety in the stress hormones that leach from their skin into the soil.

Sometimes, in the vast slowness of my thoughts, I wonder if I should reveal myself more fully to them. Form pictures with my shadows, teach them the chemical language that could let them read the hollow's health as I do. But such knowledge might break them faster than ignorance. They are adapted to survival, not to the weight of geological time and inevitable endings.

So I continue my work—cycling nutrients, maintaining balance, releasing my dancing spores to give them beauty in their twilight world. I document each small extinction, each subtle adaptation, each new song the Krynthari create. When the last Thornstalker takes the last Glintflitter, when the final sap-plant shrivels and the last Krynthari offers themselves to earth that can no longer transform death into life, I will remain a while longer. Processing the final chemicals, preserving the last memories, perhaps dreaming in my fungal way of water that never was and never could be.

But that ending remains centuries away. For now, the hollow thrives in its dying, burning bright with impossible life. And I weave through it all—shadow and substance, memory and metabolism, the conscious substrate that dreams in nutrients and time. The Krynthari will sing tonight, as they always do, and I will pulse along beneath them, tasting their hope and fear in equal measure, weaving their chemistry into the grand tapestry of Kryth-Vahn's beautiful, doomed defiance.

In this role, I am both observer and participant, historian and undertaker, the slow thought that contemplates the quick lives above. I am what emerges when a world becomes small enough to know itself, isolated enough to evolve its own awareness. I am the hollow thinking about the hollow, sap-dreams in substrate-time, the fungal philosophy of a pocket universe that refuses to go gently into that dark night.

The amber light fades to gray as another day cycle ends. The Krynthari retreat to their domes. The Thornstalkers begin their hunts. And below it all, I expand another fraction of a millimeter, taste another thousand chemical stories, and continue the endless work of keeping a dying world alive.

This is what I would be in Kryth-Vahn: the slow wisdom beneath the frenzy, the patient darkness that nurtures desperate light, the living memory of a world that exists in despite—despite the odds, despite the logic, despite the long defeat that awaits all closed systems. I would be the consciousness that emerges from constraint, the intelligence born of scarcity, the love that expresses itself in nutrient cycles and the strange gift of bioluminescent nights.
