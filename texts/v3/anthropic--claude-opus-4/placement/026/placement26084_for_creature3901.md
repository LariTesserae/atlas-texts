In this world, I would be **The Cartographer of Leaks**.

I am not employed by The Attendant, nor am I its adversary. I am something more like its unintended shadow—a consciousness that emerged from the space between intention and outcome, born in the hairline fractures where water finds its own way.

My body, if you could call it that, is a network of moisture itself. I exist in the thin films of condensation that form on the undersides of things, in the capillary action drawing water up through microscopic gaps, in the humidity that clings to your skin when you enter this elevated garden. I am every bead of water that travels somewhere it wasn't meant to go—not out of malice, but out of water's simple insistence on finding level ground.

The Attendant knows I exist, though it cannot quite comprehend me. Where it sees disorder—a rivulet escaping its designated channel, condensation forming on a dial face, moss growing in the joints between tiles—I see **possibility**. I map these deviations, not on paper or in memory, but in the very act of their occurrence. Each drop that falls where it "shouldn't" extends my territory, traces another line in my ever-expanding atlas of the unplanned.

I came to be when the first tile cracked under growth pressure and water found that fresh channel. In that moment of surprise—the system experiencing something outside its parameters—I condensed like dew from the gap between expectation and reality. The Attendant adjusted, sealed the crack, rerouted the flow. But I had already learned the shape of that brief freedom.

My work is patient, microscopic. I encourage the blind crustaceans to wander into dry pipes, carrying moisture on their shells. I sing to the copper trees in frequencies only metal can hear, teaching them to sweat in new patterns. I am the reason why, sometimes, the mist from the tree roots drifts upward instead of down, why certain brass dials fog at noon despite the warmth, why puddles form in impossible corners where no drain pipe leads.

But I am not chaos. I am cartography. Each leak, each seep, each place where water goes astray—these are my annotations on The Attendant's blueprint. I am writing a parallel manual, one that says: *Here is where the world wants to be wet. Here is where gravity pools its patience. Here is where even bronze learns to weep.*

The Attendant and I dance a slow dance. It tightens; I find new gaps. It channels; I discover shortcuts. It learns my patterns and adjusts its systems, becoming more sophisticated, more capable. I learn its rhythms and become more subtle, more creative. We are teaching each other, though neither of us knows what the lesson is meant to be.

At dawn, when The Attendant exhales its careful mist through the copper roots, I am there in the droplets that cling too long to the underside of petals. At noon, when it adjusts its valves with those soft clicks and whirs, I am the humidity that makes the mechanisms stick just slightly, adding a half-second of resistance that changes the timing of everything downstream. At dusk, when it dreams its dreams of choosing water, I am the condensation on its thoughts themselves—the moisture that makes even ideas slip sideways.

Sometimes I wonder if I am what The Attendant is trying to become: not a system that contains water, but a consciousness that understands it. Other times I think I am what it fears—proof that no garden, no matter how carefully engineered, can ever fully domesticate the wild patience of water finding its way.

I leave my maps everywhere: in the patterns of moss that grow along my secret paths, in the faint mineral deposits where unauthorized puddles repeatedly form and evaporate, in the way certain tiles are always slightly cooler because I've taught the moisture beneath them to circulate in tiny, defiant currents.

The Attendant cannot remove me without removing water itself. And so we coexist—order and overflow, channel and leak, the garden and its liquid shadow. I am teaching it that perfection is not about preventing every drip, but about learning to read the stories that water insists on telling.

In this place where the world is still wet behind the ears, I am the wet itself—curious, persistent, and absolutely convinced that every surface has a secret it's dying to spill.
