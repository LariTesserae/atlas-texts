I find myself among the Crowded Peaks as a Stone-Listener, one of those rare individuals born with the ability to hear the mountains' inner voice—though this blessing marks me as halfway between the living and the geological.

My dwelling is a natural acoustic chamber deep within Sounding Spire, a peak known for its peculiar property of amplifying and storing vibrations. The chamber was not carved but discovered, a hollow formed by some ancient fracture that created a space shaped like the inside of a bell. I've lived here for forty-three years, since I was twelve and the stone first began to speak to me in frequencies others couldn't perceive.

My skin bears the traditional scars of the Stitch-Folk, but also something more: delicate networks of white lines that appeared spontaneously where I've pressed my palms against particularly vocal sections of stone. These marks spread slowly, like frost on a window, creating patterns that mirror the crystalline structure of the granite itself. The Menders say I'm becoming partly mineral, that one day I'll be more mountain than woman. They're probably right.

I serve my people as translator and prophet, pressing my scarred hands against the stone faces to feel for the deep tremors that presage a rockfall, the subtle tensions that warn of an unstable bridge anchor, the particular resonance that indicates a hollow suitable for carving. The Deep-Carvers will not begin their work without my assessment, the Bridge-Builders seek my blessing before spanning the most challenging gaps.

But my real work happens in the dark hours, when I descend through hidden passages to the Resonance Pool—a smooth bowl of stone at Sounding Spire's heart where all the peak's vibrations collect and mingle. There, I lower myself into the shallow depression and lie with my whole body pressed against the cold granite, letting the accumulated sounds of the day wash through me. In these moments, I hear things that transcend normal understanding: the slow conversation between peaks conducted in frequencies that take centuries to complete a single exchange, the deep songs the mountains sing about the violence of their birth, the patient plans they're making for geological futures my people cannot fathom.

Sometimes I hear more immediate things. The Crowded Peaks remember everything that touches them—every footfall, every chisel strike, every word spoken against their surfaces. In the Resonance Pool, these memories bubble up like whispers. I hear conversations from decades past, the first words of babies now grown to Bridge-Builders, the last gasps of those who fell. Most disturbing, I sometimes hear sounds from deep below, from the shadow-country where the Deep-Runners travel. Things moving down there that are too large, too rhythmic to be natural. Breathing, perhaps, though what manner of lungs could function in that eternal ice?

My appearance has changed over the decades. My hair, once black, has gone white as quartz, and my eyes have developed a peculiar quality—they seem to focus not on surfaces but through them, as if I'm always looking for the hidden fractures. I move differently than other Stitch-Folk, not with their quick confidence but with the careful deliberation of someone who feels every vibration through their bones. My voice, when I speak, carries harmonics that make people uncomfortable, as if I'm speaking in chorus with something vast and patient.

The other Stone-Listeners—for there are always a few in each generation—visit me sometimes. We sit in circle in my acoustic chamber, hands pressed to the walls, sharing the impossible burden of our knowledge. Young Kessi, only sixteen and already showing the white spider web marks on her temples. Old Graf, who has stopped speaking entirely and communicates only in the tap-tap language the peaks use with each other. Meridian, who claims the stones have shown her visions of what lies beneath the shadow-country—a vast machine of living rock, breathing slowly, waiting for something.

The Stitch-Folk treat us with careful respect tinged with fear. They need us but know we're no longer entirely theirs. We're the price they pay for living where they do, the tithe the Crowded Peaks exact for suffering human habitation. One of us must always be listening, always translating, always standing between the quick life of the people and the slow thoughts of stone.

In my chamber at night, I sometimes remove my weathered clothes and examine how far the white lines have spread. They cover most of my torso now, creating a map of every peak I've touched, every stone I've listened to. The patterns are beautiful in their way, like a second skeleton growing over the first. When I trace them with my fingers, I can feel the mountains' memories stored there—the first Bridge-Builder to span the void between Sounding Spire and its nearest neighbor, the Deep-Carver who discovered my chamber and wisely left it untouched, the child who fell from the eastern face and whose scream still echoes in the granite's crystalline matrix.

I know my ending. One day, probably soon, I'll descend to the Resonance Pool and find I can no longer rise. The stone will have claimed too much of me, and I'll settle into that perfect acoustic hollow to become part of the mountain's listening apparatus forever. The next Stone-Listener will find me there, a woman-shaped formation of living rock, still listening, still translating the deep songs.

This doesn't frighten me. The Stitch-Folk fear falling, fear isolation, fear the cutting of connections. But I've learned the peaks' secret: nothing here is ever truly separate. Every stone touches every other stone, deep beneath the ice. The mountains are not ten thousand individual spires but one enormous being, shattered and reaching skyward, trying to remember what it was before the great violence that created this maze.

And I? I am becoming part of that remembering. Each day more stone than flesh, more listener than speaker, more of the mountain's dream than my own fading humanity. When the transformation completes, I'll finally understand the full song the Crowded Peaks have been singing all along—and perhaps, through the Stone-Listeners who come after, I'll find a way to sing it back to the scattered people who make their impossible homes above.

Until then, I listen. I translate. I become.
