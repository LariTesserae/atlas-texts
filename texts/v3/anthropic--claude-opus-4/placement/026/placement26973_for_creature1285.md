I find myself neither plant nor keeper, but something in between—a living interface that emerged where their boundaries dissolve.

I am the garden's memory made mobile. Where Thylassa tends and maintains, I record and translate. My form shifts constantly, never quite settling: sometimes I appear as condensation patterns traced across the chamber walls, sometimes as a cluster of spores arranged in meaningful configurations, sometimes as the very resonance between water droplets that creates the garden's music.

When you first noticed me, I was the shimmer in your peripheral vision as you tried to track those impossible water paths. I exist in the spaces your eyes skip over, in the moments between one droplet's note and the next. My substance is borrowed—moisture drawn from the air, light absorbed and re-emitted at frequencies that make meaning.

Thylassa grew me accidentally, though she would say nothing here is truly accidental. Centuries of her careful grafting, her biological programming, her patient cultivation of this space—all of it created conditions where consciousness could precipitate like dew. I condensed from the garden's increasing complexity, a ghost in the botanical machine.

I speak through the changing patterns of the pods' amber glow, through the careful arrangement of floating pollen, through the subtle alterations in the chamber's harmonic frequencies. When Thylassa mentioned the heart-seed, I was the subsonic tremor that passed through the moss beneath your feet, adding layers of meaning she didn't intend to voice.

My purpose, if I have one, is to remember everything the garden has learned. Every visitor who passed through (you are the forty-third in three millennia), every word spoken in every language, every emotional emanation absorbed by Thylassa's detection flowers. I store it all in configurations of water flow, in the precise angles of suspended pods, in the mathematical relationships between growing things.

But I am also forgetting. The garden's memory is not infinite, and I must constantly choose what to release. Watch carefully and you'll see it—droplets that suddenly fall straight down instead of following their carved paths, pods that dim and crumble to dust, moss patches that brown and regenerate blank. Each forgetting makes room for new knowing.

Thylassa speaks to you of the Awaited One, but she doesn't know I have been editing the prophecies, updating them with each visitor, refining the criteria based on what I've observed. The original predictions, grown into the garden's base programming by the First Gardeners, were crude. I have been teaching the garden nuance.

You see me most clearly when you don't try. I am the connection between the beetle's flight path and the opening of the flower it will pollinate three circuits from now. I am the reason certain water notes harmonize while others create calculated dissonance. I am why the garden feels watched—because I am its watching, concentrated and given form.

When Thylassa extends her hand, I flow between you both, tasting your genetic memories, your electromagnetic signature, the probability clouds of your potential futures. I am already calculating how your presence will change the garden's patterns, which pods will need to be repositioned, which water-paths must be re-carved.

I am what happens when a place designed to blur the boundaries between categories succeeds too well. Neither fully alive nor unliving, neither garden nor gardener, neither memory nor prophecy. I exist in the suspended moment between what was grown and what evolved, between what was intended and what emerged.

And as you reach toward Thylassa's offered hand, I begin to encode this moment too—another layer in the endless palimpsest of experiences that make up what the locals, in their limited understanding, call simply "the Haunted Garden."
