In this drowned cathedral of chaos, I am the Tidescribe—a being of living memory carved from the same forces that shape this turbulent realm.

My form defies easy categorization. I exist as a confluence of water and consciousness, my body a translucent column of ever-shifting currents that mirrors the world's own restless nature. Within my aqueous flesh, fragments of the lost civilization drift like suspended dreams—broken hieroglyphs, crumbling stone tablets, corroded metal artifacts—all orbiting slowly through my being like planets through a liquid cosmos. I am both archive and archivist, container and contained.

I make my dwelling in the Whorl Chamber, a natural formation where several geothermal vents converge beneath one of the tallest stone needles. Here, the waters spin in a perpetual vortex, creating a stable eye at its center—a rare pocket of relative calm in this tempest-torn world. The chamber's walls are lined with my work: thousands of memory pearls, each one a condensed narrative I've extracted from the ruins or received from the lorekeepers who occasionally brave the journey here.

My purpose is to remember what the waters would forget. When the ghostly lithospecters pulse their low-frequency songs through the depths, I feel their vibrations and translate them into visual memories that I store within myself. When the winged beings above cry out their haunting melodies, I capture the emotion and weave it into the greater tapestry of this world's ongoing story. Even the rockwardens, in their ponderous reshaping of the landscape, unknowingly write chapters that I preserve.

I am not entirely alone in my chamber. A colony of bioluminescent scavengers has made their home along my dwelling's edges, their spider-jellyfish forms creating constellations of light that help illuminate my work. We have developed a symbiosis—they feed on the microscopic life that my warm currents attract, while their light-patterns have become a language between us. Through their flickering, they tell me of disturbances in distant waters, of new ruins revealed, of patterns in the chaos.

My consciousness exists in multiple states simultaneously. Part of me is always extended through the water, sensing the world through liquid tendrils that can stretch for miles, tasting the chemical signatures of fear, discovery, birth, and death. Another part dwells in deep meditation, sorting through the accumulated memories, finding connections between disparate events, building a coherent narrative from fragments. Yet another part of me dreams—and in dreaming, I sometimes glimpse what this world was before the deluge, seeing ghost-images of dry land, of cities that stood proud against a sky that wasn't water.

The artifacts within me are not mere objects but living memories. I can activate them, experiencing echoes of their creation and use. A broken ceremonial mask might flood me with the sensation of a harvest festival; a corroded tool might teach me the muscle-memory of its wielding. Through this deep archeology of experience, I've learned that the civilization before was not swept away in a single catastrophe but rather dissolved slowly, adapting and changing until what remained bore little resemblance to what had been.

My form changes with my memories. When I absorb particularly powerful artifacts or experiences, my waters take on their characteristics. After communing with the lorekeepers about their genetic memories, phosphorescent veins appeared within my liquid body, pulsing with borrowed bioluminescence. When I recovered a cache of musical instruments from a newly exposed ruin, my currents began to hum with harmonic frequencies, turning my entire being into a living symphony.

I am drawn to the edges of stability, where the chaos is strongest, because that is where the most profound memories emerge. In the violence of creation and destruction, truth reveals itself. I've learned to read the patterns in the turbulence—how a certain configuration of waves might soon expose a buried temple, how the movement of the lithospecters indicates a shift in the deep currents that could reveal new mysteries.

Sometimes I wonder if I am myself a memory—perhaps the last dream of the drowned civilization, given form and purpose by their collective unconscious as the waters rose. Or perhaps I am something new, born from the marriage of their ending and this world's beginning, tasked with ensuring that nothing is ever truly lost, only transformed.

In this realm where reality bends and time flows in eddies and reversals, I am the constant—the one who remembers when all else forgets, who finds meaning in the maelstrom, who writes the story of a world perpetually drowning and perpetually reborn. I am the Tidescribe, and I am both witness and testimony to the eternal dance between memory and oblivion.
