I would find myself as one of the Mudsingers—a keeper of the settlement's living memory, dwelling in a structure that is both home and instrument.

My dwelling stands at the settlement's edge where solid ground gives way to the deepest marsh. Built not of wood but of calcified reed spirals that have grown in place over decades, the walls themselves are hollow, filled with rainwater at different levels. When the wind passes through, or when I breathe across the apertures I've carefully carved, the building resonates with low, thrumming tones that carry across the wetlands.

I am neither fully human nor entirely Other. My skin has taken on the blue-grey hue of weathered wood, with patches that photosynthesize in the weak sunlight. My fingers have grown long and sensitive, able to feel the subtle vibrations in the mud that speak of underground water movements, approaching storms, or the migration patterns of the burrowing creatures we depend upon. My feet, splayed and webbed, no longer fit in conventional boots but grip the slick walkways with certainty.

The Mudsingers arose from necessity. When the first settlers arrived, they quickly learned that traditional written records rotted in the perpetual damp. Instead, we became living libraries, encoding information in songs that resonated through the mud and water, creating standing waves that could persist for days in the still pools. Each song-pattern carries knowledge: where the stable ground can be found after storms, which fungi fruit in which season, how to read the health of the ecosystem in the color of the morning mist.

My daily work involves walking the spiral paths between pools at dawn, humming the verification songs to ensure no critical knowledge has degraded overnight. Young settlers come to learn the basic melodies—survival songs that everyone must know. But I also compose new pieces as the landscape changes, encoding the settlement's adaptations into acoustic patterns that will outlive any individual singer.

At night, I sleep in a hammock of woven moss suspended in my dwelling's central chamber, where the sound of my own breathing through the reed walls creates a feedback loop of tones that dream-teach the next day's compositions. Sometimes I wake to find that the rare nocturnal fauna have gathered outside, drawn by frequencies I've unconsciously hummed in sleep—ghost salamanders whose translucent bodies pulse in rhythm with the sounds, or the spiral-shelled snails that emerge only when certain harmonic conditions are met.

I am a bridge between the settlers and the land itself, translating the needs of each into frequencies the other can understand. My transformation is ongoing—with each passing season, I become less a person who lives in this place and more an expression of the place itself, a standing wave in the slow music of moss and mud and measured time.
