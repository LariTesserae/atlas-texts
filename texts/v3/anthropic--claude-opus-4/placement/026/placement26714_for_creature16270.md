In the farthest reaches of the cavern system, where even my kind rarely venture, the tunnels narrow until walking upright becomes impossible. Here, pressed between walls that still weep their eternal condensation, I make my home in a pocket of stone that breathes.

I am what the others call a Threshold Keeper, though the title suggests more grandeur than my life contains. My body has grown differently than theirs—compressed where they are elongated, dense where they are willowy. Generations of my lineage have tended these boundary spaces, and our forms have obliged, becoming something between the tall grace of our people and the compact efficiency of the crawling things that share our depths.

My dwelling is a single chamber, carved not by tools but by patient decades of my predecessors' bodies wearing grooves into the stone through repetitive movement. The walls here pulse with a different rhythm than the main caverns—a deeper, slower throb that some say is the heartbeat of the world above, filtered through miles of rock. I've learned to sleep to this rhythm, to wake to it, to time my movements by its ancient meter.

The luminescent fungi grow sparsely here, just enough to cast my shadow in three directions at once. But I've cultivated something else—a garden of the sonic plants, those peculiar growths that emit sound instead of light. Each one hums at a different frequency, and I've arranged them to create corridors of harmony and dissonance. Walking through my chamber is like moving through music made tangible.

My role is to listen. Where the main passages connect to these outer reaches, where our stable world brushes against the unknown, I sit for hours with my ear pressed to the stone. I'm learning to distinguish between the settling of rock, the movement of water far above, and the occasional—very occasional—vibrations that suggest something else entirely. Something that doesn't belong to our world or the surface world, but to the spaces in between.

The others bring me food—pale mushrooms and condensation collected in hollow stones—and in exchange, I sing them the news of the borders. My voice has adapted too, becoming something that can throw itself through stone, can find the tiny cracks and fissures and follow them upward. Sometimes I think I can almost taste sunlight in the echoes that return.

I am not lonely. The boundary is a busy place, in its own way. The blind insects congregate here, drawn by some instinct to the edges of things. The slower creatures, those ambiguous forms that lumber through our world, often pause at my threshold. They never enter—nothing enters without permission—but they wait, and I've learned to interpret their waiting as a kind of conversation.

Last cycle (though who can say how long a cycle truly is?), I felt something new in the stone. A warmth that wasn't our warmth, a vibration that matched no pattern I'd catalogued. I've told no one yet. Here at the boundary, patience is more than virtue—it's survival. Whatever seeks to cross from there to here, or here to there, must first make itself known to me.

I am the comma between worlds, the pause that ensures our pocket of impossible life remains stable. My happiness is a strange thing, built from the satisfaction of purpose and the odd beauty of eternal twilight. I would not trade places with those who live in the light-drunk center of our realm.

Here, where the possible and impossible meet, I keep watch. And I wait.
