# The Tidal Flats of Forgetting

You arrive at low tide, when the world has pulled back to reveal what usually hides. The expanse before you is neither land nor sea but something that refuses the distinction—a geography of exposed mud that stretches until it meets a horizon made uncertain by heat shimmer and salt haze. The ground is a network of shallow channels, some still holding water, others merely damp impressions where water recently was. The pattern from above would resemble veins, or neural pathways, or the root systems of plants that do not grow here.

The mud is not uniform. Near what might be called the shore—though the boundary shifts with each tide—it is soft, almost liquid, a substance that receives footprints deeply and releases them slowly. Further out, where the twice-daily submersion has compressed it over decades, the surface develops a skin, cracked in polygonal patterns like a puzzle whose pieces have begun to separate. These cracks fill with water at high tide, drain at low, fill again, drain again, in a rhythm so regular it has ceased to be rhythm and become simply the way things are.

The color is difficult to name. Grey, certainly, but a grey that contains multitudes—purple where certain bacteria bloom in the anaerobic depths, ochre where iron oxidizes near the surface, black in the deepest channels where organic matter accumulates. When wet, the surface reflects the sky imperfectly, creating a doubled world of clouds and haze. When dry, it turns pale, almost white in places, as salt crystallizes in delicate, fractal patterns.

The smell is profound—not unpleasant, exactly, but ancient. It is the smell of decomposition slowed to near-stillness, of sulfur compounds released by bacteria that learned to breathe without oxygen, of salt and marine decay and the particular sweetness of algae drying in sun. It is the smell of processes that began before humans had words for them and will continue long after words are forgotten.

Sound here is muffled by the soft ground and the absence of vertical surfaces. Your footsteps make a distinctive sound—a wet suction when lifting, a soft compression when landing. Water moves in the channels with barely a whisper. The loudest sound is often your own breathing, which seems intrusive in this quiet. Sometimes, from far off, comes the cry of a bird, but the distance robs it of urgency. It arrives already faded, already part of the past.

The tide is the only timekeeper that matters here. High tide transforms this place into a shallow sea, the channels becoming part of a continuous sheet of water that moves with currents too subtle to perceive directly. Low tide reveals the underlying architecture—the permanent channels, the temporary pools, the vast flats where nothing seems to happen but where everything is happening at scales too small to observe.

Between the tides, in that liminal time when the water has retreated but the mud remains saturated, the flats become a mirror for weather. Rain pocks the surface with tiny craters that fill immediately. Wind pushes ripples through the shallow films of water. Sun bakes the upper layer while the depths remain cool and wet. Each weather event writes itself temporarily on this surface before the next tide erases it, returning the flats to their blank state.

Distance becomes strange here. With no vegetation taller than your ankle, no rocks or logs to provide scale, the eye has nothing to measure by. A channel that seems nearby might be a ten-minute walk. A bird that appears large might be small and close, or large and impossibly distant. The horizon wavers, unreliable. You walk for what feels like hours and seem to remain in the same place, surrounded by the same patterns of mud and water and salt.

The life here has learned to be invisible until it cannot be. At certain times—usually dawn, usually in specific seasons—the mud erupts with activity. Millions of tiny holes appear as creatures emerge to feed in the brief window between tides. The surface writhes with movement too small and numerous to track individual organisms. Then, as if responding to some inaudible signal, they disappear again, leaving only the evidence of their passage—tiny balls of processed sediment, minute tracks that crisscross the mud like cuneiform writing, holes that spiral down into darkness.

But I am getting ahead of myself, describing the inhabitants before the inhabitation. This place resists narrative. It exists in a perpetual present tense, each tide erasing history, each cycle beginning anew. Human artifacts—a boot lost years ago, a piece of rope, the metal frame of something unidentifiable—appear and disappear with the sand's shifting. They are not monuments. They are temporary interruptions in an ongoing process that acknowledges no past, promises no future.

The tidal flats are a place of exposure and concealment in equal measure. What the tide reveals, it will hide again. What seems permanent—the channels, the slight elevations, the patches where different types of mud meet—shifts subtly with each season. The geography is reliable only in its unreliability. You could map this place, but your map would be obsolete within days.

Light behaves strangely here. With nothing to cast shadows except your own body and the occasional bird, illumination becomes uniform, directionless. On overcast days, the light seems to come from the mud itself, a dim glow that makes everything appear staged, artificial. On clear days, the sun reflects off wet surfaces in ways that destroy depth perception. You squint constantly, though the light is not exactly bright.

This is a place that exists to be traversed by water, not by anything that walks. Your presence here feels like trespass, though there is nothing to trespass against. The flats accept you the way they accept driftwood or dead birds—as temporary phenomena that will eventually be processed, absorbed, forgotten. You leave tracks, but you have seen what happens to tracks here. You disturb the surface, but disturbance is so common as to be unremarkable.

Time moves differently on the tidal flats. Not slower—that would imply time moving at all. Instead, time accumulates like sediment, layer upon layer, until the present moment contains all moments, compressed into this endless, cycling now. You could stay here for hours or days, and when you left, you would not be sure how long you had been gone. Your watch would tell you, but you would not believe it. Either too much time has passed, or not enough. The tides know, but the tides aren't telling.

# The Keeper of Forgotten Things

She has been here long enough that the distinction between her arrival and her presence has dissolved. The locals—in the small fishing town miles inland where the marsh gives way to solid ground—sometimes speak of her, though never directly. "The woman in the flats," they might say, or simply gesture in that direction when children ask about the thin column of smoke that occasionally rises from somewhere out in the maze of channels and mud.

She lives in what might charitably be called a dwelling—a structure assembled from driftwood and salvage, built on the only elevation in miles of flatness that stays reliably above the highest tides. It is less architecture than accumulation: boards of different sizes and origins, pieces of metal roofing, nets repurposed as walls, all bound together with rope and necessity. The structure has grown organically over the years, adding rooms or alcoves as materials presented themselves, following no plan except the dual imperatives of shelter and survival.

Her appearance suggests someone who has moved beyond concerns of appearance. Hair that might once have had a color has gone grey-white, kept short with irregular cuts she administers herself. Skin darkened and textured by sun and salt until it resembles the leather of her boots—boots that have been repaired so many times they are more thread and patch than original material. Her clothes are practical to the point of invisibility: layers that can be added or removed as the weather demands, all in shades that began as different colors but have been bleached and stained into the same indeterminate brown-grey as everything else here.

She moves through the tidal flats with the efficiency of long practice. She knows where the mud will support weight and where it won't, where the channels run deepest, where certain organisms congregate at specific times. Her knowledge is encyclopedic but wordless. She doesn't name things; she knows them.

Her work—if it can be called work—is collection. Not of specimens or samples in any scientific sense, but of what the tides bring and leave behind. Objects accumulate in and around her dwelling: bones bleached white by salt and sun, arranged by size and species; shells sorted by some system apparent only to her; pieces of glass worn smooth by water, organized by color in jars salvaged from the same source that provided their contents. Feathers, dried carefully and stored in bundles. Teeth—whale, seal, shark—strung on lengths of fishing line.

But the collection extends beyond the biological. She gathers human artifacts with the same patient attention: coins corroded beyond recognition, pieces of ceramic from dinner plates and teacups, buttons, keys to locks that no longer exist, jewelry turned black by oxidation. These she cleans carefully with tools made from other salvaged items, revealing what details remain. She arranges them on shelves built into the walls of her dwelling, each object placed with deliberation, though the organizing principle remains opaque.

She speaks rarely, and when she does, her voice carries the hoarseness of disuse. The fishermen who occasionally encounter her at the edge of the flats—she comes to trade sometimes, offering dried fish or salt in exchange for supplies she cannot salvage—report that she seems to listen to something they cannot hear. She will stop mid-sentence, head tilted, attending to some sound or absence of sound that escapes others.

Her routine is dictated by the tides. At low tide, she walks the exposed flats with a collecting bag woven from strips of plastic and fabric. She moves methodically, following patterns invisible to others, stopping to examine things that look identical to the untrained eye. A particular shell. A fragment of metal. A bone still partially buried. She extracts these with tools adapted for the purpose—modified spoons, pieces of wire bent into specific shapes, her hands themselves, which have developed a sensitivity that can distinguish between textures others wouldn't notice.

At high tide, when the flats disappear beneath water, she retreats to her dwelling. This is when she does her sorting, her cleaning, her careful preservation. She has developed techniques through trial and error: ways to arrest decay, to prevent salt damage, to maintain the integrity of materials that want to dissolve or crumble. She works by lamplight—oil lamps filled with rendered fat from seals that occasionally wash up dead—creating a warm circle in the darkness.

She maintains a garden of sorts, though it bears little resemblance to conventional cultivation. In containers salvaged from the tide, she grows the salt-tolerant plants that can survive here: samphire, sea beans, certain succulent species that store water in thick leaves. She knows which seaweeds are edible, which can be dried for preservation, which contain the nutrients needed to survive on a diet mainly of what the sea provides. She harvests these at specific times, following cycles she keeps in her head.

Her relationship with the creatures of the flats is one of mutual acknowledgment. The crabs know her footsteps and don't flee. The birds have learned she poses no threat. She takes only what has already died, disturbs as little as possible. She has become, in a sense, another species adapted to this environment—a large, slow-moving organism that has found its niche.

Weather is not something that happens to her; it is something she exists within. She can read the approach of storms in the behavior of the mud, the quality of the light, the way certain organisms burrow deeper or emerge unexpectedly. Her dwelling, ramshackle as it appears, has weathered decades of coastal storms. She knows where to place additional supports, how to angle surfaces to shed water, where to create drainage channels that prevent flooding. The structure breathes with the wind rather than resisting it.

She documents nothing in any conventional sense. No journals, no labels on her collections, no attempt to create a record that others could interpret. Yet there is clearly a system at work, a methodology to her collecting and arranging that suggests deep purpose. Visitors—rare though they are—report feeling they are in the presence of something between a museum and a shrine, though to what or whom remains unclear.

The objects she collects seem to fall into categories that shift over time. For months, she might focus on a particular type of shell, gathering hundreds of specimens that differ in ways too subtle for casual observation. Then her attention will shift—to metal fragments, perhaps, or to the delicate skeletons of fish. She arranges and rearranges, creating taxonomies that follow no scientific principle but clearly mean something to her.

Her health is a matter of adaptation. She has learned to treat the various ailments that arise from this life using what the environment provides: certain seaweeds for their minerals, rendered fats for waterproofing skin made fragile by constant exposure, teas brewed from the few medicinal plants that grow at the marsh's edge. She moves with the careful efficiency of someone who cannot afford injury, who has learned that prevention is the only reliable medicine.

She marks time not by dates but by events: the spring when the unusual shells appeared after a distant storm, the autumn when the tide brought in pieces of a ship's cabin, still partially intact, the winter when ice formed patterns in the mud that persisted for days. Her memory is organized around these markers, creating a chronology that maps onto the physical arrangement of objects in her dwelling.

There are nights when she lights additional lamps and spends hours looking at particular objects, turning them in the light, running her fingers over surfaces worn smooth by water and time. She seems to be reading something in them, extracting information through touch and careful observation. Sometimes she holds shells to her ear—not listening for the sea, which is everywhere here, but for something else, something contained within the spiral chambers.

She is not lonely, or if she is, she has transformed loneliness into something else—a quality of attention, perhaps, or a way of being present that requires no witness. She talks sometimes, not to herself exactly, but to the objects she collects, acknowledging them as she cleans and places them. The words are often inaudible, more rhythm than meaning, a kind of verbal maintenance that keeps her voice from disappearing entirely.

Her presence has become part of the ecosystem. The paths she walks daily have developed their own character, slightly firmer than the surrounding mud, colonized by different organisms that have learned to take advantage of the disturbance. Her midden—the pile of truly unsalvageable material that accumulates despite her thoroughness—has become habitat for creatures that need slightly higher ground. She is both apart from and integral to the life of the flats.

When storms drive unusual objects onto the flats—pieces of boats from far away, containers with foreign writing, sometimes things that resist identification entirely—she is always the first to find them. It's as if she can sense disturbance in the normal pattern of deposition, drawn to anomalies by an instinct developed over years of patient observation. She collects these special finds with particular care, often creating entire sections of her dwelling to house single objects that seem, to her, to warrant such attention.

There is something in her manner that suggests waiting, though for what remains unclear. Not anticipation exactly—that would imply expectation of change, and change is not what governs here. Rather, it's the waiting of someone who understands that important things happen slowly, that patterns reveal themselves only to those patient enough to perceive them. She has that patience. She has become that patience.

She will die here, eventually. She knows this without drama or fear. Her body will join the collection, become part of the substrate, processed by the same organisms she has spent decades observing. The dwelling will collapse in stages, its materials returning to the cycle of flotsam and jetsam. Her collections will disperse, rejoining the general circulation of objects moved by water and time.

But not yet. For now, she continues her work, walking the flats at low tide, sorting and arranging at high tide, maintaining her strange museum at the edge of the habitable world. She is keeper and kept, collector and collection, as adapted to this place as the crabs and the specialized bacteria. She has found a way to be human here, in a place that seems to resist human presence. Or perhaps she has found a way to be something else—something the tidal flats can recognize as belonging.

The town's children, when they grow old enough to venture this far, sometimes catch glimpses of her in the distance—a figure moving slowly across the vast expanse, bending occasionally to examine something invisible from their vantage. They invent stories about her: she's a witch, a ghost, a woman who loved a fisherman who drowned and now collects pieces of his boat. All the stories are wrong. The truth is both simpler and stranger: she is someone who listened to what the tidal flats were saying and decided to stay long enough to understand the language.
