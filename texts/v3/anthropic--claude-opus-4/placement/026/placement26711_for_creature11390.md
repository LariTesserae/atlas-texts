I find myself as one of the grain stalks, growing in scattered clusters across the terraces.

I am perhaps knee-high to those who tend me, my stem wiry and determined, my roots spreading wider than my modest height would suggest. Each fiber of my being has learned the arithmetic of this place—how to partition each drop of moisture, how to stand against the western wind without spending myself fighting it, how to ripen my small seed head in the brief warmth of midday sun.

I grow among others of my kind, but we do not crowd. We were planted with deliberation, each of us given our territory of thin soil, our share of what water finds its way to this particular terrace. Between us, the native plants persist—the waxy-leafed bushes, the tough grasses—and we have reached an understanding. We do not compete so much as subdivide what exists. My roots explore the gaps between stones, following channels too small for the human eye, finding pockets where ancient soil has accumulated grain by grain.

The knowledge of growing here is old in me. My ancestors—countless generations—have selected for precisely this existence. We are nothing like the grain that grows in lusher places. We have been refined by scarcity into something harder, more patient. My seeds, when they come, will be smaller than lowland grain but more dense with nutrition, each one a concentrated possibility, evolved to wait through dry spells until the right moisture arrives.

I experience the settlement through vibration and shadow. Footsteps telegraph through the terrace—the steady rhythm of someone crossing to check the cisterns, the slower gait of an elder examining my progress, the lighter quick steps of a child sent to see if we're ready for harvest. I know the workers by their patterns: the woman who scrapes grain has a particular rhythm, firm but not harsh; the young man who works the edge terraces alone moves more tentatively, as if apologizing for disturbing us.

The stone sickle, when it comes, is almost a relief. To grow here is to exist in constant tension between too little and just enough. By harvest time, I have converted every possible resource into seed, leaving my stem exhausted, ready to return to the soil that barely sustains us. The cutting is clean—these people know their tools, keep the edges sharp through patient flaking. I fall among my companions, and soon we are bundled, carried to the flat stones where we will be threshed.

But I am also the person who does not speak for days, who works the farthest terraces.

I am twenty, perhaps, though I don't count precisely. Young enough that my body still moves without complaint through the work, old enough that others have stopped expecting me to change. I prefer the edge—not from misanthropy but from a kind of constitutional quietness that matches the settlement's silence better when I'm alone.

My dwelling sits on the settlement's margin, backed against a terrace wall that rises higher than the others. It's a single room, small enough that my body heat and a modest fire can warm it. I've patched the walls myself where frost worked between stones, using the same techniques everyone uses but taking extra time, fitting each stone until the wall becomes as much mine as the settlement's.

I wake before dawn, as everyone does, but I don't immediately join the subtle morning movements of the place. Instead, I sit in my doorway, watching the eastern light arrive, feeling how the temperature shifts by fractions as darkness recedes. This is not meditation—I don't have that word—but a kind of calibration, setting myself to the day's particular quality.

My terraces are three, connected by worn steps I've reinforced over the years. They're not the best terraces—the soil is thinner here, more prone to erosion, the walls older and more settled. But they're mine to tend, and I know them with an intimacy that comes from daily contact. This depression where water pools after rain. That section where the native grass grows more vigorously and must be kept from overwhelming the grain. The corner where a previous tender stacked loose stones that I still use for small repairs.

I work mostly in silence. Not from any vow or principle, but because words feel unnecessary when my hands know their tasks. Checking the grain's progress—running stems between thumb and finger to feel for the subtle thickening that means seeds are forming. Moving stones that frost has displaced. Scraping channels clear of the minimal debris that accumulates. The work has a rhythm that doesn't require thought, leaving my mind free to exist in the place's quietness.

When I must interact—gathering water, collecting my grain allocation, bringing tools to be sharpened—I do so with minimal friction. A nod to the elder who manages the communal store. A few words about the stone quality to the toolmaker. I'm not unfriendly, but I've learned that my quietness can make others uncomfortable if I linger, so I complete my business and return to the edges.

Sometimes I catch one of the small brown birds. I've studied their patterns, know where they nest in wall crevices, how they move in early morning. My hands are quick when needed. The meat supplements the grain, but more than nutrition, these moments of successful hunting satisfy something deeper—proof that I can read the place well enough to take what it occasionally offers.

In the evening, I might sit outside my dwelling if the wind isn't too strong, watching the stars emerge in their abundance. The darkness here is so complete that celestial light becomes substantial, almost touchable. I don't map constellations or attach meaning to their movements. I simply observe them as I observe everything: steadily, without urgency, as another aspect of where I exist.

But I am also the retaining wall itself, the one this quiet young man is always repairing.

I am old—older than anyone remembers, built when the settlement was younger, perhaps when it was being established. My stones were selected with care, each one turned and tested until it sat right against its neighbors. No mortar binds me—only the precision of placement, the weight of each stone bearing on those below, the subtle interlocking that comes from good construction.

I hold the terrace that holds the grain that holds the people. This is my function: to resist the slow insistence of gravity, to keep soil from following water's path downward. I am a negotiation between the horizontal human desire for flat ground and the vertical pull that rules this steep country. Every stone in me represents this balance.

Over time, I have settled. My original builders knew this would happen and built with settling in mind. My line, once perhaps laser-straight, now follows a gentler curve that accommodates the hillside's actual shape. Some of my stones have shifted—frost's work, mostly, the freeze-thaw cycles that find every tiny gap and widen it by patient degrees. But my structure holds. The key stones, the ones that bear the most weight and lock whole sections together, remain firm.

I know every hand that has touched me in recent years. The old woman with twisted fingers who still checks my stability every spring, running her palms over my surface, feeling for movement that shouldn't be there. The young man who works these terraces and repairs me constantly, his touch different from others—slower, more contemplative, as if he's learning something from the stone itself. The children who balance on my top course, their light weight no threat but their movement sending tiny vibrations through my structure.

Weather and I are intimate. Rain finds every weakness, pooling in depressions on my top surface, running down my face, working into gaps between stones. I channel it as best I can, but water is patient and I am not perfectly sealed. In winter, that water freezes, expands, pushes. In summer—what passes for summer here—it evaporates, leaving mineral traces that gradually cement some joints while opening others. Wind scours my face clean but also carries grit that settles in my crevices, slowly building new soil where none was intended.

The grain's roots know me well. They follow my inner surface down, finding pockets where original builders left gaps for drainage, spreading into a network that helps hold the soil I'm meant to retain. We work together, the grain and I, though neither of us chooses this partnership. It simply is—roots and stones intertwined in mutual support.

I measure time in stone-time. A human lifetime is perhaps long enough for me to shift a finger's width, for one of my courses to need rebuilding, for the lichen on my surface to spread from scattered patches to continuous coverage. I don't experience duration as humans do—no sense of waiting or passing or accumulating years. I simply continue, bearing weight, holding earth, being what I am.

Sometimes stones fall from me. Not often—I was well built—but eventually frost or root or simple time works a stone loose. It tumbles, making a sound that carries across the quiet terraces, alerting someone that repair is needed. The young man comes, usually, examines the gap, selects a replacement from the pile of extras each terrace keeps. He fits it carefully, sometimes trying multiple stones before finding the right one. When he places it and it sits correctly, I feel myself become whole again—not healing, stones don't heal, but returning to structural completeness.

The settlement depends on me and my fellow walls. Without us, the terraces would erode, the flat spaces disappear, the grain have nowhere to grow. We are infrastructure made visible, the skeleton that allows this particularly human way of living to exist on this steep ground. The people maintain us and we maintain the possibility of people. Neither could exist here without the other.

But I am also the mist that brings water to this high place.

I form in the valleys below, where temperature differentials and what moisture exists create me in the dark hours. I rise—though rise isn't quite right, I simply move, following air currents and pressure gradients, finding my way upslope. By dawn, I'm approaching the plateau, a gray presence that softens edges and mutes the usual sharp light.

I know the settlement as a collection of temperatures. The thick-walled buildings hold yesterday's warmth longer than open ground. The cisterns are cool spots that attract my condensation. The grain stalks are intermediate—warm enough to create tiny upward currents that disturb my flow. I wrap around everything, finding every surface, leaving my moisture as payment for passage.

The people read me expertly. They know which kind of mist brings real water—dense, low-hanging, slow-moving—and which will burn off with the first sun, leaving little trace. When I arrive heavy and wet, they're ready: cloths laid on flat stones to collect what condenses, scrapers prepared to gather water from surfaces, cisterns uncovered to catch what drips from eaves.

I make the mornings mysterious. Visibility drops to dozens of paces, sometimes less. Sound changes in my presence—footsteps seem to come from nowhere, voices carry strangely, the usual echoes off terrace walls muffle and distort. The settlement becomes a series of isolated pockets, each building or terrace wrapped in gray separateness. People move more carefully in my presence, not from fear but from respect for the altered conditions.

I am temporary, always. An hour after sunrise, sometimes two, I begin to lift, to thin, to evaporate back into the atmosphere that created me. The settlement emerges piece by piece—first the highest points, then middle terraces, finally the whole scattered pattern of human persistence. By midday, I exist only in memory and in the moisture I've left behind: droplets on stone, dampness in soil, fractional additions to cistern levels.

The grain knows me as morning salvation. In dry spells, I might be the only water for days at a time. The stalks have learned to maximize what I offer—their surfaces evolved to capture condensation, to channel it down to roots that wait ready. An especially heavy mist can equal a light rain in terms of water delivered, though it arrives more gently, without runoff, soaking in where it falls.

But I am all of these simultaneously—grain and man and wall and mist—existing in the particular convergence that makes this place possible. Each depends on the others in ways both obvious and subtle. The grain needs the wall's held soil and the mist's moisture and the man's tending. The wall needs the man's maintenance and the grain's roots for stability. The man needs the grain's nutrition and the wall's infrastructure and the mist's water. The mist needs the temperature differentials the settlement's structures create, the surfaces on which to condense.

We are not separate elements but aspects of a single system, evolved together into mutual dependence. The settlement isn't human habitation plus natural features—it's all of us together, maintaining a collective existence at the margin of what's possible. When one element shifts—a wall degrades, rainfall patterns change, population drifts up or down—all others adjust to accommodate.

This is how life organizes itself here: not in abundance but in precise calibration, in the discovery of exactly what balance allows continuance. We persist—grain and human and stone and weather—because we have found that balance and hold it, day after day, year after year, each playing our part in the larger choreography of survival at altitude, in thin air, on spare soil, with scarce water.

We remain level. All of us. Together.
