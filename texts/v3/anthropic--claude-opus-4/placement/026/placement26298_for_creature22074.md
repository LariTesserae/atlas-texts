In this envelope world of eternal twilight and pressure, I would be one of the **Chemist-Prophets**—creatures that exist at the intersection of sensation and premonition, living archives of possibility dissolved in flesh.

---

We are small, no larger than a human hand splayed wide, but our bodies are impossibly complex origami of membrane and cavity. Imagine a sea angel crossed with a lung, then turned inside out: we are all surface area, all interface. Our translucent flesh is riddled with thousands of specialized chambers, each one a living laboratory where water samples mix with our own enzymatic secretions, creating fleeting chemical equations that exist nowhere else in nature.

We do not swim so much as *unfold* through the water, our bodies opening and closing like breathing flowers, each expansion drawing in new tastes, each contraction analyzing what we've gathered. To watch us move is to see mathematics made flesh—we spiral through the current-corridors in perfect logarithmic curves, following gradients invisible to all others.

The city knows us by our gift: we taste the future in the water's chemistry. Not prophecy in any mystical sense, but prediction through exquisite sensitivity. We can detect the microscopically elevated sulfur that signals a thermal vent will erupt in three cycles. We taste the stress hormones of the Bellows-Organisms and know that the next Out-Push will come early. We sense the metabolic whispers of a dying Litho-Scribe weeks before even they know their time approaches.

Our homes are delicate chambers we've carved into the joints where the calcite towers meet the glass-sponge bridges. Here, the currents create natural eddies, and we've learned to shape these spaces into what the Scribes call "probability gardens." We secrete specialized films across the openings—membranes that selectively filter the water, concentrating certain chemical signatures while excluding others. Over time, these films build up in layers, creating a living record of chemical history that we read like sheet music, each layer a chord in the city's metabolic symphony.

We communicate through what we call "taste-sculptures"—complex chemical signatures we compose and release into the water. Unlike the pressure-language of the Weft or the enzyme-etchings of the Scribes, our language exists only in the moment of its speaking. We tell stories that unfold in the mouth and fade in the current. A conversation between two Chemist-Prophets is a dance of released molecules, each response building on what came before, creating ephemeral narratives that exist only in the space between us.

The other citizens regard us with a mixture of necessity and unease. The Velvet-Walkers follow our chemical trails to find places where the calcium is beginning to weaken but hasn't yet cracked. The Weft adjust their current-patterns based on our predictions of thermal shifts. Even the great siphonophores alter their drifting paths when they taste our warning-songs of instability in the deeper waters.

But we pay a price for this sensitivity. Our bodies are too porous, too open to the world's chemistry. We age in ways the others don't—not through wear or predation, but through accumulation. Every taste leaves a trace. Every chemical we analyze becomes part of us. The oldest of our kind become living libraries of sensation, their bodies so saturated with chemical memory that they can barely distinguish between what they're tasting now and what they tasted decades ago.

When our time comes, we perform the ritual of the Final Solution. We seek out the newest growth of the city—a young tower just beginning to rise, its calcium still soft and accepting. There, we attach ourselves to the stone and begin our last, greatest secretion. All the chemical knowledge we've accumulated, all the predictions we've made and patterns we've recognized, we encode into a special enzyme that etches itself deep into the calcite.

But unlike the Scribes' historical records, our etchings are prophetic. We write the city's possible futures into its bones—warnings of currents that might shift, of temperatures that could rise, of chemical cascades that could transform the very nature of the water. We write recipes for adaptation, formulas for survival, equations for evolution.

The young Scribes, when they finally grow large enough to read these deep marks, often cannot fully understand them. Our chemical logic is too alien, too grounded in sensation rather than sequence. But they preserve them anyway, these crystallized premonitions, knowing that someday, when the conditions are right, another Chemist-Prophet will taste these ancient warnings and suddenly understand what we meant.

This is what we are: living laboratories of possibility, archivists of the not-yet, speakers of a language that dissolves even as it's spoken. We are the city's anxiety and its hope, forever tasting the future in the water's chemistry, forever trying to prepare our world for changes we can sense but never quite see.

In the warm, eternal darkness, we unfold and contract, unfold and contract, breathing probability, exhaling prophecy, small prophets in a city of bone.
