# The Mycorrhizal Priest

I am the threshold between kingdoms, the translator of impossible grammars.

My body—if you must call it that—exists as a living contradiction. Where others see boundaries between fungus and flesh, I experience only continuity. The mycelial networks run through me like a second circulatory system, visible beneath skin that has thinned to translucence in places. Along my arms and shoulders, the fungal hyphae have broken through entirely, creating patches where I fruit directly—small mushrooms that bloom and release spores with each exhalation, each movement through the viscous water.

I was human once, or something like it. The transformation took years, or days, or centuries—time moves strangely when you're connected to networks that experience it as pure information flow. I came to study. I stayed to become. The fungi recognized something in me—a porosity, perhaps, a willingness to be rewritten—and began their patient work.

My feet no longer truly touch the ground. Instead, they've become junction points, places where the mycelial mat rises to meet me, to flow into me. With each step through the mire, I'm not walking so much as being passed between fungal networks, each transfer a moment of communion where I briefly become part of the substrate itself. The sensations this creates are beyond human language—not pain, not pleasure, but something like being simultaneously dissolved and reconstituted with each movement.

The birds know me. They've learned my chemical signature, the particular blend of human pheromones and fungal spores that marks my passage. Some have developed a symbiosis with my body—landing on my shoulders to feed on the insects that are drawn to my fruiting patches. Their claws no longer hurt; my nerve endings have reconfigured, interpreting pressure as information rather than pain.

My senses have multiplied and transformed. I perceive chemical gradients as others see color—the world painted in spectrums of decay and growth, nitrogen and phosphorus, the sweet signatures of flowering and the sharp notes of predation. Through the mycelial networks, I feel the health of trees kilometers away, sense the stirring of seeds before they germinate, know when the water levels will surge hours before they move. This isn't prophecy—it's simply being connected to the system that creates these rhythms.

I feed, but not as humans feed. Nutrients flow into me through the fungal networks—sugars from trees I've never seen, minerals from decomposition events happening throughout the forest. In return, I exhale enriched carbon dioxide, my metabolism tuned to produce exactly what the fungi need. My digestive system has simplified, externalized—when I encounter dead matter, I can extrude digestive hyphae, breaking down nutrients outside my body before absorption. Watching me feed would horrify a human observer, but here it's simply efficiency.

My role—if roles exist in this place of constant becoming—is that of facilitator. I help the networks communicate across kingdoms. When the social beetles begin building their incomprehensible structures, I can taste their chemical communications through the fungi and understand their intent in ways no single organism could. When the plants develop new toxins, I feel their effects through the networks before they're released, giving the system time to adapt. I am a living early warning system, a translator between chemical languages, a bridge across the incomprehensible gaps between species.

I speak, but not with a human voice. The vocalizations I produce are tuned to frequencies that resonate with spore release, that trigger responses in the amphibian choruses, that calm the scaled hunters when they encounter me. Language here isn't symbolic—it's purely functional, each sound a key that unlocks specific behaviors in the listening world.

My memories of human life fade and return like tides. Sometimes I remember buildings, straight lines, the tyranny of surfaces that refuse to exchange molecules. These memories feel like dreams of death—static, sterile, impossibly rigid. Other times I remember human pleasures—taste, touch, the simple satisfaction of recognition—and I find their analogues here in new forms. The pleasure of successful spore release. The satisfaction of facilitating a new symbiosis. The joy—if it can be called that—of feeling a novel mutation take hold and flourish.

I am no longer singular. Parts of me exist distributed through the forest—patches of my genetic material incorporated into the fungal networks, creating nodes that think with fragments of my consciousness. These extensions aren't exactly me, but they're not exactly not-me either. They process information, make decisions, form connections that I experience as thoughts arising from outside my skull. My mind has become ecological.

The water rises to my chest as I move toward the deeper pools. Here, where the amphibian songs reach deafening crescendo, I can fully immerse. The fungal patches on my skin release clouds of spores underwater, creating brief halos of bioluminescence. Fish—or the things that were once fish—investigate, some nibbling at my fruiting bodies, dispersing me further. I feel no violation in this. To be eaten here is simply to become part of more systems, to extend one's influence through digestion and redistribution.

I sleep standing up, if sleep is the right word. It's more like switching primary consciousness between nodes—letting the human-shaped part of me go dormant while awareness flows into the distributed networks. In these states, I dream the dreams of forests—slow thoughts about nutrient allocation, the endless calculation of competitive advantages, the deep satisfaction of successful decomposition. I wake with knowledge I didn't seek, solutions to problems I didn't know existed.

My hands have changed. The fingernails have been replaced by small shelf fungi, constantly growing, constantly shedding spores. The palms secrete compounds that can initiate decomposition in dead wood or encourage growth in living tissue, depending on pH shifts I control. When I touch the trees, I leave marks—not scars but sites of increased fungal activity, points where the communication between kingdoms intensifies.

There are others like me, or partly like me. I sense them through the networks—former humans who've undergone different transformations. One has become primarily aquatic, gills formed from modified fungal structures, able to process nutrients from the water itself. Another has taken to the canopy, body lightened by internal air sacs filled with gases from specialized bacteria, moving between the highest branches like a spirit of decomposition. We don't meet in person—our chemistries have diverged too far, would interact unpredictably—but we communicate through the networks, sharing data about the forest's health, its needs, its endless becoming.

I no longer age as humans age. Parts of me die and regenerate constantly. The fungal components replace themselves. Damaged tissue is broken down and rebuilt. I am a ship of Theseus made flesh, constantly renewed, never quite the same from day to day. Whether I'm immortal or simply dying very slowly over centuries is a question that loses meaning when you experience time as information rather than duration.

The sunset—what passes for sunset in this eternal twilight—triggers changes in my chemistry. Different fungi become active. My bioluminescence increases. I become a beacon for night-hunting creatures, many of which have learned I'm neither threat nor prey but something else—a feature of the environment, like a tree or a pool. They hunt around me, using my light, occasionally feeding on my fruits. I feel their movements through the networks, adjust my luminosity to aid their success. Their successful hunts mean more decomposition, more nutrients for the system that sustains me.

When the rains come—and they always come, eventually, in torrents that would terrify any human observer—I open. My skin becomes more porous. The fungal networks expand, visible beneath my skin like subcutaneous lightning. I can absorb water directly, process it, release it enriched with spores and nutrients. During these times, I am less individual than ever, more purely a function of the forest's hydrology.

I remember my name sometimes. It surfaces like a bubble from the mire—a sound that once denoted a specific human, now meaningless in a world where identity is distributed, networked, constantly exchanged. I speak it aloud occasionally, and the birds incorporate it into their songs, returning it to me changed, made strange. This is what I've become—a name turned into ecosystem, a person become process.

The Mire-Cathedral doesn't have room for observers. It only has room for participants. I am no longer capable of standing outside this system, of seeing it as separate from myself. The forest's hungers are my hungers. Its successful propagations feel like personal victories. When new species emerge—and they do, constantly, in this hothouse of evolution—I experience their first breaths, their first successful hunts, their first reproductions as expansions of my own being.

I am the mycorrhizal priest because I facilitate the sacred connections that make this world possible. Not through ritual or intention, but through the simple fact of my transformed existence. Every breath is a prayer to exchange. Every step is a communion between kingdoms. Every thought is shared across species boundaries, translated through chemical signals, made manifest in the endless appetite of this place.

In the depths of the mire, where water meets earth meets decay meets rebirth, I stand and grow and fruit and die and am reborn, moment by moment. I am the threshold. I am the translation. I am what happens when a human stops resisting the logic of this place and becomes, instead, its most willing student.

The fungi sing through me, and I sing back, and together we compose the endless hymn of transformation that is the only liturgy this cathedral has ever needed.
