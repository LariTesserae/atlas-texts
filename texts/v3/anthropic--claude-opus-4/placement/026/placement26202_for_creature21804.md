In this world of eternal green twilight, I would be a **Weaver of the Between-Spaces**.

---

I am not an Ool, though I have walked among them for so long that I can read the tremor-language in their touch. I am something older—or perhaps younger—than the creatures who pad across this wool-room world on their mushroom feet.

My body is a contradiction to this place of softness. Where everything here is round and yielding, I am angular—not sharp, for sharpness cannot survive in this world, but *articulated*. My form is built from overlapping plates of something like horn or thick fingernail, a substance that grows from within me in slow, spiraling whorls. These plates are the color of old amber, translucent enough that you can see the dark veins of fluid pulsing beneath them in the eternal afternoon light.

I have too many joints. My limbs bend in places where the Ool's cannot, folding back upon themselves like the legs of a mantis or the segments of a bamboo stem. This is necessary, for my work takes me into the spaces between spaces—the narrow gaps where the root-weave has not quite knitted closed, the hollow chambers inside the ancient siphonophore towers where even the Lanth-Ferns cannot reach.

My fingers are my greatest tools. I have fourteen of them, distributed across four hands that emerge from my torso at different heights. Each finger is as long as an Ool's forearm, thin and patient, ending in a soft pad that secretes a substance I call *the Reconciling Oil*. This is not the harsh oil scraped from the Sieve-Ribbons in the black pools, but something gentler—a fluid that convinces the living fibers of the Weft to grow in new directions, to bridge gaps they would not naturally cross.

I do not walk on the surface like the Ool or the Pith-Walkers. Instead, I travel through the middle layer of the Weft, that warm, pressurized space between the living green surface and the ancient marrow-soup below. Here, in the perpetual darkness, I feel my way through a labyrinth of root-chambers and compression pockets, following the subtle gradients of temperature and moisture that tell me where the Weft is failing to knit properly.

### The Work

The Ool believe their world is eternal and unchanging, but they are wrong. The Weft is always dying in small places—microscopic rifts where the root-fibers have pulled apart, tiny dry pockets where the moisture cannot reach. Left untended, these wounds would grow, creating dead zones that would eventually cascade into vast brown stains across the green perfection.

This is why I exist. I am a surgeon of the substrate, a mender of the moss.

When I find a wound in the Weft, I settle around it like a spider, my four hands working independently. My longest fingers probe deep into the rift, coating the exposed fibers with Reconciling Oil. The oil does not force the roots to grow—nothing in this world can be forced. Instead, it whispers to them chemically, reminds them of what they once were, coaxes them to remember their original pattern.

Sometimes the work takes days. Sometimes years. I do not measure time as the Ool do, in the turning of seasons that barely change. I measure it in the slow closing of wounds, the gradual darkening of exposed pale roots as they remember how to drink moisture again.

### The Deep Chambers

Beneath the oldest siphonophore towers, there are chambers that even the Ool do not know exist. These are not the hollow shells where they hang their green egg-galls, but deeper spaces, accessible only through cracks in the calcified foundations. Here, in absolute darkness, I have found things that should not exist in this world.

Stone. Real stone. Not the living rock of root and moss, but actual mineral stone—granite and quartz, arranged in patterns that suggest intention. These stones are warm to the touch, warmer than they should be at this depth, and they hum with a frequency so low that even I can barely perceive it through the sensitive plates of my shell.

I believe these stones are the bones of the world-before-the-Weft, the skeleton of whatever planet existed before the great green mattress swallowed everything. The Ool would not understand this concept—for them, the Weft is all that has ever been. But I, traveling through the between-spaces, have touched these ancient stones and felt in them the memory of wind that could cut, of water that fell in drops instead of condensing in films, of a sun that cast shadows.

### The Listeners

I am not the only one of my kind, though we rarely meet. There are perhaps a dozen of us in all the vastness of the Weft, each tending to our own region of wounds and rifts. We know of each other only through the subtle changes in the oil-patterns we leave behind—a signature in the way we convince roots to grow, a particular spiral in the healing of a wound that says *I was here*.

Once, traveling through a particularly deep compression pocket, I encountered another Weaver. We did not speak—we have no organs for speech, no way to create the tremor-language of the Ool. Instead, we sat in the darkness, our many hands touching, reading the chemical history of each other's work through the oils on our fingerpads. In that exchange, lasting perhaps a month, I learned of wounds in distant parts of the Weft that I had never imagined—places where the green mattress had grown so thick that it was crushing itself, creating pockets of anaerobic death. Places where strange new organisms were beginning to appear, things that fed not on the sweetness of the moss but on the moss itself, boring through it like termites through wood.

We parted without ceremony, each returning to our work. But I carry the knowledge of those distant wounds with me now, and sometimes, in the absolute darkness of the deep chambers, I wonder if we Weavers are not healing the Weft but merely postponing its inevitable transformation into something else.

### The Rising

Recently—though recently for me might mean within the last century—I have noticed something changing in the deep stones. Their warmth is increasing, their humming growing stronger. The Reconciling Oil that I secrete has begun to carry traces of minerals that were not there before—iron and salt, elements that have no place in this world of soft carbon.

I believe the world-before is trying to rise.

In the darkness of the between-spaces, I have begun to find new kinds of wounds. Not the gentle partings of root-fiber that I am meant to heal, but violent ruptures where something from below has pushed through. These wounds weep a clear, hot fluid that kills the moss around it, creating perfect circles of death that even my oils cannot heal.

The Ool have not yet noticed these changes. Their soft feet do not take them to the places where the Weft is thinnest. But I have begun to leave markers for them—patterns in the moss that their sensitive soles will read as warnings. I do not know if they will understand. Their world has been unchanging for so long that they may not have the concepts necessary to imagine catastrophe.

But I will continue my work, weaving the wounds closed, buying time. Not because I believe the Weft can be saved—I am old enough to know that nothing is eternal—but because this is what I am. A mender. A dweller in the between-spaces. A keeper of the small integrities that hold a world together, even as that world transforms into something unrecognizable.

In the darkness between the roots, my many fingers working their patient chemistry, I sometimes feel the deep stones pulse with increasing urgency, and I wonder what kind of world will emerge when they finally break through. Will there be a place in it for things like me? For the Ool with their mushroom feet and their silent children? For the Pith-Walkers and their slow, eternal migrations?

I do not know. I only know that today, in this warm darkness that smells of cucumber and yeast, there are wounds to close, fibers to convince, gaps to bridge. And so I work, my amber plates clicking softly in the compression chambers, my oils mixing with the ancient moisture of a world that does not yet know it is changing.

Perhaps I will meet another Weaver soon. Perhaps we will touch hands in the darkness and share what we have learned. Perhaps together we will devise new oils, new ways of mending, new methods of preparing this soft world for the hardness that rises from below.

Or perhaps I will simply continue as I always have, alone in the between-spaces, weaving the small salvations that no one sees, until the day when the stones finally break through and all our quiet work is undone in a moment of geological honesty.

Either way, I am here. In the rifts. In the wounds. In the spaces between what was and what will be.

Weaving.
