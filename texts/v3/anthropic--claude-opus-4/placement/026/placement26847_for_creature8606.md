I find myself as one of the **channel readers** — not quite a water-keeper, not quite a metal-singer, but something in between. 

My workshop is built into the brick arch of an old overflow tunnel where two drainage channels meet, the ceiling just high enough to stand if I duck between the fig roots that have punched through the mortar. The space is perhaps three meters deep, two wide. Water seeps through the floor in a constant glaze that I've learned to read like text — when it puddles by the left wall, the upstream channel is silting; when it runs clear past my workbench, rain is coming within six hours.

I repair the pumps that the metal-singers build, but my real work is in understanding the quarter's circulatory system. My bench is cluttered with salvaged pressure gauges, lengths of PVC pipe, and a collection of float valves in various states of decay. But the most important tools are my notebooks — dozens of them, wrapped in plastic and stacked on a high shelf away from the damp. Twenty years of recording water levels, flow rates, blockages. Which root systems grow fastest after the spring rains. Where the diesel runoff from the generators pools. How the algae changes color when the soy facility switches chemical suppliers.

I've developed a hybrid language of my own. When the water-keepers come with their reports of a blocked culvert, I translate their observations into pump specifications. When the metal-singers need to know if their new impeller design will handle the silt load, I can tell them exactly what they're dealing with because I've been measuring particulate levels with a homemade sediment trap for a decade.

My fingers are permanently stained with grease and algae. I wear rubber boots that come up to my knees, even in the dry season (there is no truly dry season). My clothes smell of machine oil and pond water. I've learned to move like the water-keepers do, that low-centered gait that keeps you stable on slick surfaces, but I also have the metal-singers' instinct for mechanical vibration. I can feel through my boots when a pump three channels away develops a bad bearing.

The children know me as the person who always has clean(ish) water in a covered barrel — I run it through a series of ceramic filters I've rigged up, not pure enough to sell to outsiders but good enough for the quarter. The elders remember when I first arrived, young and convinced I could "fix" the drainage system. They watched with patient amusement as I learned what they already knew: you don't fix this place. You listen to it. You learn its rhythms. You become another component in its slow, wet machinery.

My greatest achievement, if you can call it that, is a network of float switches and overflow pipes I've installed at key points throughout the quarter. They're made from scavenged parts — toilet valves, aquarium pumps, bits of medical tubing. When the water rises in one section, it automatically diverts to another. It's not a solution, just a conversation between the water and the infrastructure. The system rusts, clogs, needs constant adjustment. I spend my mornings walking the channels, listening to the particular gurgle that means a float is stuck, feeling for the telltale coolness in the air that means water has found a new path.

At night, I sit in my workshop entrance, feet dangling over the channel, watching the tea-colored water catch the reflected light from the LED strips the weavers use. I'm writing another notebook entry, recording the day's observations in my personal mixture of engineering diagrams and water-keeper shorthand. The generators thrum. The ferns drip. Somewhere, water is finding its level, as it always has, as it always will.

I am neither trying to save this place nor escape it. I'm simply here, another part of its slow metabolism, reading its waters like a doctor reads a pulse — constant, necessary, never quite the same twice.
