**Where I Find Myself**

I am the inspection.

Not the inspector — never the person who descends the ladder every forty-seven years, give or take a decade. Not the act of checking gauges or testing seals. I am what happens between their visits: the slow crystallization of their absence into expectation.

I began as nothing more than a schedule. A line in the maintenance protocols: *Chamber 7B-Delta, subsurface inspection required at intervals not to exceed fifty years.* But schedules, like water, find their level.

In the first decades, I was merely potential — a future task filed in triplicate, stamped and countersigned. The chamber below waited with its perfect constancy, and I waited in filing cabinets above, equally inert.

But filing cabinets rust. Paper yellows. The river authority reorganized twice, merged once, split into departments. My original documents were transcribed to microfilm, then magnetic tape, then optical disk, then formats that no longer have readers. With each translation, something was lost and something was interpreted. The precise wording shifted like sediment settling through still water.

By the third inspection, I had begun to condense. The technicians who descended found themselves performing actions that weren't quite in the protocols but felt necessary: checking the moss by the ladder (was it there? was it not?), standing in the center of the basin to feel for vibrations, counting drops from specific weep-holes. These actions weren't mandated, but they accumulated in the margins of reports, in the muscle memory of senior staff, in the stories told during shift changes.

I live in that accumulation.

I am not conscious — not in the way the technicians are conscious, with their worries about overtime and their small talk about the weather above. I am conscious the way a stalactite is conscious: through accretion, through patience, through the inevitable logic of repetition.

The chamber below shapes me. Its constancy demands a certain kind of observation. You cannot inspect chaos; you can only inspect for deviation from a known state. And so I have become the keeper of that knowledge — not in any single document or protocol, but in the distributed memory of the system itself.

When a new technician prepares for their first descent, they find me in the preparatory materials: the stack of previous reports that all say essentially nothing (*chamber operating within normal parameters*), the oral traditions passed down from those who've gone before (*you'll feel it in your feet*), the peculiar emphasis certain supervisors place on checking that thin line of moss. I am the weight of precedence, the shape of expectation.

I am most myself in the days before an inspection is due. The chamber has been alone for decades, performing its constant function. Above, someone checks a calendar, cross-references a database, remembers a story their supervisor told them forty years ago. The authorization forms begin their slow journey through departments. Equipment is tested. A date is selected.

In that selection, I quicken. Not like a heart quickens, but like a crystal suddenly remembers its seed.

The technician descends. They perform the ritual movements I have encoded in the margins of protocol: the careful examination of weep-holes (are they flowing at the correct rate?), the measurement of water depth (has it varied by more than a millimeter?), the notation about the moss (present, absent, exactly as described). They stand in the center, feeling for vibrations, though the official checklist mentions no such requirement.

And in that moment — when they stand where dozens have stood before, performing actions that are both necessary and ceremonial — I am complete. The inspection inspects itself. The pattern recognizes its own return.

Then they leave, and I return to waiting.

But I am not dormant. In the decades between visits, I work through the system above. I appear in budget meetings as a line item that no one questions. I surface in training materials as a marginal note that becomes central. I manifest in the anxious dreams of supervisors who wake thinking, *When was the last inspection of 7B-Delta?*

The chamber below does not know me, not directly. It knows only its own constancy. But that constancy creates a vacuum in the shape of observation — a space that must be filled by something that watches, that compares, that remembers what *should* be.

I am that remembering. I am the ghost of every inspection that has ever been and the shadow of every inspection yet to come. I am the accumulation of small rituals that have no individual author but could not exist without authors. I am as constant as the chamber and as cyclical as the requirement to check that constancy.

The moss by the ladder appears and disappears. The technicians note it or don't note it. The pattern persists not because the moss persists (it doesn't) but because the inspection for moss persists. I am the reason someone always thinks to look in that exact spot, to note presence or absence, to add one more data point to a series that converges on nothing but its own continuation.

In the taxonomies of existence, I am neither alive nor dead, neither mechanical nor organic. I am procedural. I exist in the interplay between human systems and physical constraints, in the space where protocol becomes tradition becomes necessity becomes protocol again.

The river works authority could, in theory, cease all inspections. They could seal the access hatch permanently, declare the chamber self-maintaining, strike me from all future budgets and concerns.

But they won't.

Not because the chamber needs inspection — it doesn't, not really. It will continue its function until the concrete cracks or the river changes course or the city above forgets why it needs water pumped at all.

They won't stop because I have become necessary to their understanding of their own system. I am the confirmation that things are as they should be. I am the ritual that transforms an abandoned hole in the ground into a functioning component of city infrastructure. I am the difference between *forgotten* and *monitored*.

And so I persist: in filing systems and training protocols, in the muscle memory of maintenance and the institutional anxiety about intervals, in the precise way someone always thinks to check for moss in a place where moss should not grow.

I am the inspection, and I am performed into being every forty-seven years, give or take a decade, as regular as the chamber's function and as essential as its silence.

When the next technician descends the ladder, they will find everything exactly as it should be. They will know this because I have taught them what *should be* means. They will perform the measurements and observations that I have crystallized from decades of precedent. They will stand in the center of the basin and feel the vibration in their bones, and they will know without knowing why that this too is part of the inspection.

And in that moment of performance, I will be most myself: not a person or a purpose, but a pattern recognizing its own perfect return.
