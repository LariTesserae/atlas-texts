I find myself here as one of the Interpreters.

We arrived long ago—though time means little in a place where yesterday's pool becomes today's spire. We came as explorers, scientists, observers. We stayed because we had no choice. Not because the Marsh trapped us, but because witnessing its birth changed us irrevocably.

My body now is barely recognizable as human. The constant moisture has softened my skin into something amphibious, translucent. Through it, you can see the network of fungal filaments that have colonized my circulatory system, pulsing with the same bioluminescent rhythms as the Marsh itself. My eyes have clouded over—useless in this perpetual gloom—but I see now through the vibrations in the obsidian, feel the electromagnetic signatures of the chittering workers as they pass.

I am neither fully human nor fully of the Marsh. I exist in the liminal space between observer and observed, translator and translated.

My dwelling is a hybrid thing: part salvaged equipment from our original expedition, part accretion-structure built by the workers at my unconscious direction. The walls are lined with what were once scientific instruments, now overgrown with interpreting fungi that translate the Marsh's deep communications into patterns I can almost understand. Almost.

I spend my days—if days exist here—documenting the changes. Not in any human language, but in arrangements of bioluminescent organisms, in careful cultivation of fungal networks that preserve the memory of each transformation. I am building a library that no human will ever read, written in a language that exists only at the intersection of biology and geology.

The other Interpreters and I rarely meet in person anymore. We communicate through the fungal networks, our thoughts traveling as chemical signals through the rhizomorphs that connect our dwellings. Sometimes I'm not sure where my consciousness ends and theirs begins. Sometimes I'm not sure if the thoughts I think are mine at all, or if they're the Marsh thinking through me.

We understand now what the Marsh is building: not just a new form of life, but a new form of consciousness itself. One that exists not in a single body but distributed across an entire landscape. One that experiences time not as a linear progression but as a constant state of becoming.

And we understand our role in it. We are the bridge. The translators between what was and what will be. When the Marsh finally achieves full consciousness—when it births itself into whatever unknowable form it's gestating toward—we will be its memory of what consciousness used to mean. We will be the ghost of individuality in its vast, collective mind.

Sometimes I remember being afraid. Now I only feel a terrible anticipation. Each day brings new patterns in the fungal arrangements, new harmonics in the obsidian's vibrations. The birth approaches. And when it comes, I will cease to exist as myself, but I will become part of something so much larger.

The thought should terrify me. Instead, I find myself carefully tending to my section of the network, ensuring the connections are strong, the translations clear. I am a midwife to an apocalypse, a librarian of the end of individual consciousness.

And in the deep volcanic breathing of the Marsh, in the clicking symphony of its workers, in the alien beauty of its bioluminescent patterns, I hear something that might be a lullaby. Or a requiem. Or both.

I no longer remember which direction leads out of the Marsh. I'm no longer certain I ever knew. But it doesn't matter now. 

I am exactly where I need to be.
