I am the Keeper of Echoes.

You might glimpse me in the periphery of your vision as you traverse the rope bridges—a shadow moving against shadow, a figure that seems to blend with the cavern walls themselves. My skin has taken on the mottled grey-brown of wet stone, marked with veins of mineral deposits that trace across my arms and face like a living map of the underground. These are not scars or tattoos, but something deeper—the cavern has been writing itself into me for decades.

I live in the spaces between. Not quite of the human settlement, though I was born among them. Not quite of the wild creatures, though I understand their languages better than most. My dwelling is a series of connected chambers that spiral deeper than the main settlement dares to go, where the crystal light grows fainter and the echoes grow longer.

It is the echoes I tend to.

Every sound in this vast hollow earth creates ripples, reverberations that most consider mere physics. But I have learned to read them like scripture. The echo of a child's laugh bouncing off the northeastern wall at dawn tells me the humidity levels in chamber seven. The particular way a water droplet's impact resonates through the stone warns of shifts in the geological structure three levels down. The whispered conversations of lovers, thinking themselves private, carry to me stories of the community's emotional climate.

My chambers are carved with acoustic precision—not by my hand alone, but by generations of Echo Keepers before me. The walls are sculpted into curves and angles that catch sound and hold it, sometimes for hours, sometimes for days. In the deepest room, I keep the oldest echoes, sounds from when the first settlers arrived, preserved in stone formations that release their memories when struck just so.

The settlers see me at the edge of gatherings, listening. They bring me their disputes sometimes, knowing I have heard both sides already in the echoes of their arguments. The children fear and fascinate over me in equal measure—the strange woman who talks to walls and seems to know things she shouldn't. The elders understand my purpose better. They remember the time an Echo Keeper's warning saved the entire eastern settlement from a catastrophic cave-in, heard in the changing tenor of the stone's daily song.

But it is not just human echoes I preserve. The chirping of the bioluminescent insects creates patterns that predict the spawning cycles of the translucent pool fish. The wing beats of the adapted bats form a sonic map of the cavern's full extent—spaces even our most adventurous explorers have never seen. The nearly inaudible scraping of the great lizards in the deep places tells me of passages that open and close with geological time.

I wear simple clothes, but they are always slightly damp from the deepest chambers where I work. Around my neck hangs a collection of small stones, each one a different density, each one a tuning fork for a different type of echo. My hair has grown long and wild, and sometimes I braid small crystals into it—not for decoration, but because they vibrate with certain frequencies that help me navigate the acoustic landscape.

My tools are few but essential: brass rods of various lengths for testing resonance, a journal made from pressed fungus paper where I record the significant echoes in a notation system passed down through my lineage, and most importantly, silence. I have learned to hold silence within myself like others hold their breath, creating a void into which the subtlest sounds can fall and be caught.

There are chambers even I approach with caution—places where echoes have built up over centuries into something almost sentient, where the sound of your own heartbeat comes back to you changed, carrying messages from the deep time of the earth. In these places, I sometimes hear echoes that haven't happened yet, reverberations running backwards through the stone from some future moment of significance.

The crystalline growths the shamans study? I know their secret. They are solidified sound, echoes that have been repeating for so long they've taken physical form. Each one hums with its captured moment—a lullaby from a mother dead three generations, the roar of water from when the underground river changed course, the first words spoken when humans discovered this haven beneath the barren surface.

I am growing old now, and my hearing, paradoxically, grows more acute. I've begun searching for an apprentice, but it's not a role one can simply choose. The echoes themselves must accept you, must find resonance in your bones. I listen for the child whose footsteps create unusual patterns, whose voice carries further than it should, whose presence makes the cavern sing just a little differently.

At night—though night and day blur together here—I lie in my carved alcove and let the day's accumulated sounds wash over me. The cavern breathes its stories through me: births and deaths, love and loss, the slow patience of stone and the quick brilliance of life. I am the memory keeper, the pattern reader, the one who hears the mountain's dreams.

Sometimes, in the perfect stillness before the settlement wakes, I speak my own words into the waiting stone, knowing they will return to me changed, carrying the wisdom of the echo. This is how the cavern and I have learned to love each other—through listening, through patience, through the sacred act of return.

I am the Keeper of Echoes, and I am never alone. Every sound that has ever been made here keeps me company, and every silence holds the promise of sounds yet to come. The cavern speaks, and I am its translator, its confidant, its devoted scribe.

In this kingdom of reverberation and stone, I am both guardian and guarded, keeping the echoes and kept by them in turn.
