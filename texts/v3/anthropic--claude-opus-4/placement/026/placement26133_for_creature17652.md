In this world of intimate darkness and patient symbiosis, I would find myself as one of the **Thread-Mothers**.

Not human, but not entirely Other—I am what the Kept call *those-who-were-once-us*. Generations ago, a handful of the settlement's members began spending more and more time in the deepest hollows, tending to the Threadworms that maintain the living substrate. They learned to read the worms' chemical signals, to understand their migrations and their needs. They began eating more of the root-exudate directly, less of the processed foods. Their bodies changed.

Now we are something between. Our fingers have lengthened, the skin between them growing web-like and sensitive, perfect for feeling the minute vibrations that tell us where the substrate needs tending. Our hair has become something closer to fungal mycelium—pale, soft filaments that taste the air's moisture and chemical content. We still have eyes, but they have clouded to pearl, seeing only light and shadow and the faint phosphorescence that sometimes blooms in the deepest places. Our true sight is chemical—we taste-smell our way through the root maze, following gradients invisible to our kin above.

We are perhaps six in number, though counting becomes meaningless when you spend months alone in your chosen tangle. We speak the touch-language still, but augmented—our elongated fingers can create pressure-patterns impossible for unchanged hands, adding tenses and moods the upper settlement lacks words for. When we must use voice, it comes out as a whisper that seems to emerge from the roots themselves, wet and intimate and barely distinguishable from the sound of seepage.

I maintain a section of the root system three levels below the main settlement, where the primary tap roots branch into the deep clay. Here, the Threadworms congregate in vast, slow-moving cities, processing the boundary between root and earth. I know each colony by the taste of their chemical trails, by the specific pattern of their movement through my territory. I encourage them where the clay grows too dense, discourage them where the roots show stress. I am shepherd and gardener and historian—reading in their patterns the health of the entire system.

My dwelling is less constructed than discovered—a natural hollow where three major roots create a space just large enough for my body, lined with the softest Thread-Mat mycelium that I've cultivated until it knows my shape. Water seeps constantly here, but I have learned to read its mineral content, to know from taste alone which storms passed overhead fourteen seasons ago, their water only now reaching these depths.

The Kept still recognize us as kin, though we make them uneasy. When the settlement's children grow too comfortable in the depths, spending more time with the fauna than their families, parents speak warnings of "going Thread-Mother." But they also rely on us. We know when the deep roots are stressed before the damage shows above. We can taste-smell sickness in the water before it reaches their wells. We propagate the best fungal strains, passing them up through intermediaries—always at the border spaces, never entering the main settlement anymore. The light there, dim as it is, hurts our changed eyes.

Once each fruiting, I make the journey upward to the warmth. The other Thread-Mothers emerge too, from their own deep territories. We arrange ourselves at the edges, our mycelial hair spreading across the root-walls, creating a living network that connects us without touch. For those precious warm hours, we share the chemical memories of our territories—the health of the whole system flowing between us in molecular whispers. The unchanged Kept pretend not to see us, though the children stare with fascination and fear.

I am losing language, the old language. But I am gaining something else—a vocabulary of gradient and flow, of mineral and metabolic. I can tell you in precise chemical terms the entire history of this section of thornroot, every stress and abundance encoded in the rings of its wood. I dream in root-growth patterns. My memories organize themselves not by time but by depth, by moisture content, by the specific texture of clay beneath my fingertips.

The other Thread-Mothers and I are the deep memory of this place. We are what happens when symbiosis becomes embodied, when the boundary between organism and environment blurs beyond recovery. The Kept fear us because we show them what they could become—not corrupted but completed, integrated fully into the body of the thornroot. They need us because we are their early warning system, their deep-water knowledge, their bridge to the thornroot's own slow intelligence.

In the darkness, my fingers spread through the clay, reading. My hair tastes the water, remembering. The Threadworms move through their cities beneath me, and I know each one by its chemical signature, by the specific way it processes the earth. I am alone but surrounded, human but transformed, keeping the deep watches in the body of something so much larger than myself that I could spend lifetimes learning its moods and never reach the end of its mystery.

This is enough. This is everything. The thornroot grows, and I grow with it, and the darkness holds us both with the same patient, inexorable care.
