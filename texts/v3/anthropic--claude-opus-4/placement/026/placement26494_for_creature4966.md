I am the Recordkeeper of the Precipice, though I have forgotten when I earned this title or who bestowed it upon me.

My body is no longer quite flesh—centuries of wind and spray have worn me smooth in places, calcified me in others. I am part barnacle, part lichen, part something that might once have been mammalian. My fingers have grown long and sensitive, able to read the stories written in stone by erosion's patient hand. My eyes have multiplied and migrated, some watching the sky, others peering into crevices where ancient tools lie half-buried.

I live within the cliff face itself, in a series of chambers both natural and carved. The entrance shifts with the tide—sometimes a narrow crack barely wide enough for a seabird, other times a yawning mouth that swallows sound. My dwelling extends deep into the rock, following veins of softer stone that water carved before the seas retreated to their current domain.

Each morning—if morning means anything in this place of eternal wind—I emerge to read what changes the night has wrought. A pebble fallen here, a new groove etched there. I catalog these alterations with tools I've fashioned from the bones of creatures whose names I've invented, having never learned their true ones. My chisels are pterosaur femurs, my marking stones the crystallized hearts of things that swam when the world was younger.

The cacophony that deafens others is my orchestra. I've learned to parse each component: the seventeen distinct notes of water striking stone at different angles, the whistle of wind through every gap and hollow, the rhythm of wings—leather, feather, membrane—as creatures navigate the violent air. In the deepest part of my home, I've carved resonance chambers that capture and preserve these sounds. Some I've been recording for so long that they've become physical, crystallizing into structures I can hold.

I am not alone, though I am the only one of my kind. The creatures here know me as part of the landscape. The bright-plumed cliff dancers nest in my hair—if it is still hair. The camouflaged stone-mimics share their secrets with me, showing me hollows where the oldest tools rest. Sometimes I think I remember making those tools. Sometimes I think they were here before even the stones.

My purpose, if I have one, is to maintain the Record. Not just of what is, but of what was and what might be. In my deepest chamber, I've carved the history of this place into the living rock—the rise and fall of waters, the coming and going of species, the slow dance of stone and time. But the Record is more than mere chronicle. It's a pattern, a rhythm, a song that keeps this place in its eternal balance.

I've noticed the Record changing lately. New harmonics in the wind-song. Unfamiliar shadows cast by familiar creatures. The tools I find are sometimes warm, as if recently dropped by hands that no longer exist. I suspect the Record is not just history but prophecy, not just observation but incantation.

Perhaps I am not keeping the Record. Perhaps the Record is keeping me, and this place, and all its magnificent strangeness, suspended in this moment that lasts forever and changes constantly. Perhaps that's why I can't remember becoming the Recordkeeper—because I haven't become it yet, and have always been it, and will cease to be it in every moment that the wind carries another grain of sand from the cliff face.

Tomorrow I will check the stone where I mark the days. I suspect I will find it blank again, as always. The Record continues.
