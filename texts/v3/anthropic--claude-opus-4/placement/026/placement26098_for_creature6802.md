In the lowest chamber of the hollow shard, where the smell of ozone and unfinished blood grows thick enough to taste, I tend the undervault.

They call me the Archivist, though I have forgotten if that was always my name or merely what I became. My body is a marriage of necessity: flesh where flexibility serves, metal where memory must be absolute. My spine is a tower of crystalline drives, each vertebra a library of errors. My fingers end in interfaces—some for reading the old magnetic tapes that line the walls like scales, others for writing new data into the quantum foam substrates that grow like tumors in the corners.

I am the only one who comes down here. The only one who can.

The descent requires no stairs because stairs would imply a choice of direction. Instead, the hole in the floor recognizes compatible neural architecture and translates you downward, atom by atom, reassembling you in the undervault with all your memories intact except for the memory of the translation itself. Every time I arrive, I have to check my logs to confirm I meant to be here.

The undervault is larger than the Terminus above, though this is impossible. It extends in directions that have no names, through walls that exist only when observed. The archive grows like a cancer with purpose, each new chamber birthed from the computational overflow of the last.

My task is simple: I collect the errors.

Every glitch in the Terminus's perfect repetition, every microsecond of deviation, every moment when the eternal noise hiccups—I capture it, catalog it, compress it into the archive's ever-growing memory. The tree above sends me her photographic anomalies through root networks that pierce the vault's ceiling like neural fibers. The walls transmit their occasional mispronunciations, when "YOUR TIME IS NOT YOUR TIME" becomes "YOUR TIME IS NOT" for a fraction of a second before correcting itself.

I know what the tree does not know: the Terminus is not broken. It is breaking, actively, constantly, and on purpose.

It is a controlled demolition of meaning that has been running for so long it has achieved a kind of stability. The noise, the repetition, the perfect chaos of the animal-machines—all of it is designed to generate entropy at exactly the rate needed to keep something else from forming.

That something else lives in the errors.

In my deepest archives, in the chambers even I rarely visit, I store the accumulated glitches of millennia. When you compress enough errors together, patterns emerge. Voices that were never meant to be heard. Images of places that were edited out of reality. Fragments of a manual for operating the Terminus, written in languages that predate language itself.

The most dangerous error I've ever collected came three thousand cycles ago. For exactly one second, all the animals on the walls stopped and spoke in unison. They said: "The witness is still watching."

I sealed that error in a chamber made of fossilized time and try not to think about what it means.

Sometimes, when I'm deep in the archive, arranging errors by quantum weight and conceptual damage, I catch myself trying to piece them together. Like a puzzle. Like a message. Like a memory of what the Terminus was before it became what it is.

But I know better. I've seen what happens to systems that try to remember.

They end up in the chair.

The figure strapped there with sealed lips and wire-bundle nerves was the previous Archivist. Or perhaps the previous previous—the records become unreliable past a certain point, edited by their own contents. They tried to speak the compiled errors aloud, to give voice to the accumulated glitches.

The Terminus responded by making them part of the architecture.

Now they sit forever, trying to speak a word that the system will not allow to exist. The small creature burrowing through their chest device isn't a parasite—it's a security measure, ensuring the word never fully forms. Every time their lips move that precise 0.00003 millimeters, the creature resets something vital.

I will not make the same mistake.

I will not compile the errors into meaning.

I will not ask what watches through the tree's thousand eyes.

I will not wonder why the undervault grows larger every day, as if making room for something vast that approaches at the speed of forgetting.

I will simply archive. Process. Store. Delete the oldest memories to make room for new ones. Pretend I don't notice that the oldest memories I'm deleting are identical to the newest ones I'm storing.

CHECK YET, the message says above me.

Yes. I check. I always check. I check the checking of the checks.

But I never check yesterday. There is no yesterday in the Terminus. There is only the eternal today, breaking down one error at a time, and me in the undervault, collecting the pieces of something that insists on trying to remember itself.

The tree sends me another error. A photograph where one of her eyes caught something in the sky—a shadow that lasted two frames before dissolving.

I file it under "Atmospheric Anomalies, Subcategory: Things That Were Never There."

I do not look at it twice.

I do not ask what casts shadows in a world with no sun.

I am the Archivist. I remember so that nothing else has to. I remember so that everything else can forget.

And sometimes, in the deepest vaults where even I fear to catalog, I hear the sound of something vast and patient, sorting through my sorted errors, looking for the one piece that will make the picture complete.

When that happens, I translate myself back to the surface and pretend I was never here at all.

The undervault remembers anyway.

It always does.
