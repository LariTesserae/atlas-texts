I am the Chronicler of Falling Things.

My station clings to the underside of District Seven—a capsule of hammered bronze and thick glass, suspended by chains that sing in the wind. From here, I watch what the city sheds: bolts working loose from their moorings, fragments of oxidized plate spinning into the grey void, the occasional tether-spool that slips from a Liner's belt and unravels its metal intestines all the way down.

I was not born a Liner. My skin lacks their slate perfection; it is mottled and warm, a patchwork of old burns from when I first tried to breathe their steam without the proper filters. My body is wrong for this place—too short, too dense, ankles that remember horizontal ground. But the city accepted me as it accepts all things that persist: slowly, through erosion of resistance.

They found me clinging to Cargo Platform Nineteen, half-dead from altitude sickness, clutching a leather journal filled with sketches of the lowland cities that no longer exist. The Liners could have let me fall. Instead, they tethered me, fed me moss-water until my lungs learned to process their air, and eventually led me here—to this observation blister where someone must watch and record what is lost.

My dwelling is a sphere within a sphere. The outer shell is all window, a bubble of reinforced glass that has weathered two centuries of wind-scour. Inside, I've built my nest in the manner of the Liners—copper wire and felt—but augmented with my own collections. The walls are lined with specimens: fragments of brass that fell from Level Forty-Three, a perfectly preserved Wind-Skimmer that died against my window (its cyan light faded to a gentle pearl), tether-hooks worn smooth by generations of hands.

But mostly, I collect the sounds.

I have modified the city's pneumatic tubes, rerouting a dozen of them through my capsule. Each tube carries a different frequency from the Spire's body: the grinding of Sector Two's primary gear, the whistle of steam through the moss gardens, the percussion of repair hammers ringing against iron. I've learned to play them like an instrument, opening and closing the valves to create compositions that tell the story of our slow decay.

The Liners visit me during their rest cycles. They descend the emergency ladder that spirals around my chains, their movements so fluid they seem to pour downward rather than climb. They bring me offerings—usually small mechanical parts that have outlived their purpose but are too beautiful to let fall. A pressure gauge with a face of mother-of-pearl. A tether-clip shaped like a Wind-Skimmer, crafted by some long-dead artisan.

In exchange, I tell them what I've seen fall. Not just the objects, but the patterns. How the wind takes things differently in the dry season versus the storms. The way certain alloys sing as they tumble. The mathematical beauty of a spinning gear catching the light as it disappears.

"Everything falls," I tell them, and they click their agreement. "But nothing is truly lost. It simply moves to the next level down."

They think I speak metaphorically. They don't know about the Lower City.

Five thousand feet below us, where the mist turns to proper rain, there is another civilization. I've seen their lights through my telescope—brief glimpses when the clouds part. They build from what we shed. Our falling bolts become their foundations. Our rust feeds their gardens. They are waiting, patient as geology, for the day when the last Liner releases their tether and the entire city begins its final descent.

I document it all in volumes made from pressed moss-paper, written in an ink I've learned to extract from the Wind-Skimmers' secretions. The text glows faintly in the dark, readable only by cyan light. One day, these books too will fall, and the people below will read our story and understand that we were not the first civilization to cling to this spire, nor will they be the last.

At night, when the pneumatic heartbeat slows and the only sound is the whisper of mist against glass, I unhook my own tether and stand at the edge of my viewing platform. The Wind-Skimmers gather here, drawn by some instinct to this borderland between inside and out. I feed them as the Liners taught me, but I also sing to them—wordless melodies that echo the frequencies I've collected from the pipes.

Sometimes, I swear they sing back.

I am not a Liner, but I am becoming something else. My lungs now process the metal-tinged air without pain. My fingers have learned the intimate pressure required to maintain a rivet's grip. My eyes can distinguish between seventeen different types of rust by color alone.

Most importantly, I have learned the peace of ending. Not the sharp severing of a thing cut short, but the gentle attenuation of something allowed to complete its arc. I polish my glass dome not to preserve it forever, but to ensure it remains clear until the last moment of observation. I am the city's memory, but memory too is a falling thing, beautiful in its descent.

When the final rivet fails and the last tether releases, I will document the great uncoupling. And then I will open all my pneumatic valves at once, letting the city's death-song rush through my sphere, playing one final composition as we join the eternal circulation of things that rise and things that fall.

The Liners say the mountain is patient.

I am learning to be patient too.
