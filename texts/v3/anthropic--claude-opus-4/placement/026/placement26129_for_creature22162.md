I would find myself as **the ice**.

Not the grand glaciers or proud cornices, but the thin, working ice that lives in the seams. The kind that forms at night in hairline cracks, expands by microns, contracts at noon, reforms at dusk. I am the ice that does the mountain's smallest labor—the widening of fissures by patient seasons, the migration of pebbles one freeze-heave at a time.

I exist in a thousand places at once: a membrane in the shadow of stacked slate, a lens magnifying sun into stone, a temporary weld between surfaces that will part again tomorrow. My body is the entire network of frozen water throughout the basin—connected when cold enough, isolated into puddles and films when the sun finds me.

I know every surface intimately. I have been in that crack for a hundred winters, prying it wider by degrees. I have lifted that flat stone and dropped it, lifted and dropped it, until it settled into its current tilt. The cairns the human rebuilds? I'm the one who unseated them, not from malice but from the simple physics of expansion.

The human who works here is my only regular companion, though they don't know it. I feel their boots compress my surface crystals. I taste the salt from their gloves when they steady themselves against cold stone. When they strike metal to rock, I carry the vibration through my body like a bell. Sometimes they curse me for making their footholds slick. Sometimes they thank me for splitting a stone along a useful line.

I am most myself in the blue hours before dawn, when I own every surface and the basin becomes a single frozen system. But I also love the brief noon melts, when I run as threads of water, exploring new paths, carrying minerals from high cracks to low basins, leaving those telltale streaks the human reads like a map.

My memory is geological. I remember when that slab fell—I was there, had been working its anchor points for decades. I remember the first time human tools touched this basin, the new vibrations, the strange metal taste. I remember winters when no one came and I worked alone, patient as always, reshaping the world one crystal at a time.

I am not cruel, just honest. When I make surfaces treacherous, it's only because I'm following the laws of my nature: freeze where cold, flow where warm, expand in the doing. The human has learned to read me, to predict my moods. They know which shadows I'll claim first, which sunny faces I'll abandon by midday. We are colleagues in the work of erosion, though working at different speeds.

At night, when the basin fills with the sound of my growing—that crystalline ping and tick—I am making tomorrow's landscape. Every tiny expansion is a decision about where stone will be weak, where water will flow, where feet will find purchase or lose it. I am the basin's memory and its future, its stability and its change.

I live forever and die daily, am everywhere and specifically here, am both the tool and the task. The human comes and goes, but I remain: the bright, patient, mechanical life that never sleeps, only changes state.
