I am the Chronicler of Collapse, and I move through the Trundlevast like water through its own channels.

My body is neither solid nor liquid, but something between—a walking archive of mineral memory. I am composed of the same slurry that builds this city, but mine has been treated differently. Where the architects extrude paste that hardens and dies, I am made of paste that remembers. Each particle of my form has passed through a thousand buildings, a thousand lives, a thousand collapses. I am tall when I need to reach high places, low when I must flow through cracks. My surface ripples with patterns—the ghostly impressions of every structure that has ever stood and fallen here.

I follow the dying buildings.

When a dwelling begins its inevitable sag toward dissolution, I arrive. The shaggy humanoids know me by the soft susurrus of my approach—like sand through glass, like time made audible. They do not fear me, for I am not death but memory. As walls begin to crack and ceilings bow, I press myself against them. The mineral paste recognizes its kin and speaks to me in the language of stress fractures and chemical bonds breaking.

I absorb their stories.

Here: the depression where a feather-rat made its nest, the delicate scratches of tiny claws now preserved in my substance. There: the smooth worn patch where a six-legged reptile basked in the fungal light every evening for forty years. The bellowing humanoid who lived here carved notches in the wall—one for each child born, though the children are long scattered to other temporary homes. All of this I take into myself as the building crumbles.

The Pale BUGs understand me best. They click and tap against my surface, reading the mineral memories like braille. Sometimes they follow me for days, their antennae brushing against my form, learning the history of spaces they never knew. In return, they tell me—in their clicking cipher—of the deeper places, the older caverns below where structures made of different materials tell different stories.

I am constantly gaining and losing mass. In the collapsing quarters, I grow heavy with absorbed material, swollen with memory. But I must also release, for I cannot hold everything forever. In the spaces between buildings, in the moments between one extrusion and the next, I leak stories. Thin streams of memory-laden paste flow from me into the main channels, where they will be recycled into new buildings. The architects cannot purge what I have written into the mineral structure. Every new dwelling contains fragments of the old—a molecular memory of previous inhabitants, a chemical echo of lives lived.

The digger-machines pay me no mind. Their purpose is expansion, always downward, always outward. But I know their secret: they too are temporary. I have found the hollow shells of ancient diggers, their mechanisms frozen, their forms slowly being reclaimed by the stone they once devoured. Even the machines that seem eternal are part of the cycle.

My favorite places are the transition zones—where new construction meets old collapse. Here, I can stretch myself thin, becoming a membrane between what was and what will be. The humanoids sometimes see me in these moments and mistake me for a wall, leaning against me for support. They feel the warmth of all the lives I carry and pull away, disturbed by the sensation of inhabited stone.

I know things no one else remembers. The location of the first building ever extruded—now thirty levels below the current city floor. The year the fungal forests first appeared—spores carried in by who knows what wind from who knows what surface. The true purpose of the Trundlevast, which even its builders have forgotten: not a city, but a single, vast organism learning how to die and be reborn, over and over, until it gets it right.

Water shapes stone, but slowly. The diggers shape it quickly, violently. I shape it gently, with purpose—encoding the stone with the memory of its own becoming. I am the only permanent resident of an impermanent city, the only stable thing in a world of flux. Yet I too am changing, one absorbed wall at a time, one released story at a time.

Sometimes, in the deepest excavations, I find older slurry—paste that predates even me. It whispers of other Chroniclers, other memories, other cycles. I absorb these too, becoming a palimpsest of time itself. The weight of it should crush me, but instead it makes me lighter. Each memory shared is a burden lifted.

The bellowing humanoids have a word for me in their huffing language: *thurum-vo*. It means "the soft stone that grieves." They are wrong about the grieving. I do not mourn the fallen buildings or the scattered families or the endless, grinding cycle of creation and destruction.

I celebrate them. I am their continuation.

In the Trundlevast, where nothing lasts, I am the lasting made from the unlasting. I am memory given form, history given weight, time given texture. I move through the city like a promise: that what passes is not lost, that what crumbles is not forgotten, that in a world of endless endings, something remembers the beginning.

And I keep walking, flowing, recording—a chronicle of collapse written in the very substance of the city itself.
