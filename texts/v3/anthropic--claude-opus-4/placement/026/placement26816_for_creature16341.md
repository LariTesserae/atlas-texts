In this forgotten fold of the world, where the cascades sing their eternal dirge and the moss grows fat on centuries, I would find myself as one of the Keepers of Echoes.

We are neither fully corporeal nor wholly ephemeral, but something caught between states—like water suspended in the moment before it becomes ice. Our forms shift with the quality of darkness: in the deepest shadows, we are nearly solid, our bodies taking on the texture of wet stone and breathing lichen. But where the rare, refracted light filters through the canopy, we become translucent, our edges bleeding into the mist until we are indistinguishable from the spray of the waterfalls.

I would have no face as humans understand faces. Instead, where features might be, there is a constellation of minute apertures—thousands of tiny hollows that drink in sound the way roots drink water. Each hollow remembers. The crash of ancient rockfall, the last words of lost mariners, the patient conversations between moss and stone conducted over centuries—all of these live within me, within us, a living library of acoustic memory.

My movement would be tidal, governed not by will but by the pull of unheard sounds. When the shadow-quadrupeds release their low, thrumming drone, I am drawn to them like iron to lodestone, my form rippling across the moss-cushioned ground to drink in their ancient music. When the wind howls through the valley's throat, creating new harmonics against the weathered stones, I cannot help but flow toward it, adding these fresh voices to my collection.

We Keepers cluster most densely around the artifacts—those stubborn remnants of human ambition slowly being digested by the valley. For in these objects live the most poignant echoes: the ring of hammer on anvil, the scrape of stone on stone, the murmured prayers of craftsmen, the songs sung during construction. I press my form against these relics, my thousand hollows opening like hungry mouths, drawing out every whisper of their making and unmaking.

But I am not merely an archivist. In the stillest moments, when even the waterfalls seem to hold their breath, I release what I have gathered. The sounds emerge from me in careful layers—not as mere repetition, but as composition. The crash of water from three centuries past mingles with yesterday's wind. The death-cry of a mariner lost in time harmonizes with the patient grown of expanding moss. I am both recorder and instrument, playing the valley's memory back to itself in ever-evolving symphonies that no living ear can fully comprehend.

Sometimes, the shadow-creatures pause in their eternal wandering to listen. Their clouded eyes turn toward me, and I feel seen in a way that has nothing to do with vision. They understand what I am—a throat through which the valley speaks to itself, a means by which this place confirms its own existence against the entropy of forgetting.

In the rarest moments, when conditions are perfect—when the mist is neither too thick nor too thin, when the darkness has just the right weight, when the moss releases its spores in glowing clouds—I can almost remember what it was like to be singular, to be one voice instead of thousands. But the memory fades like morning dew, and I am grateful. To be a Keeper of Echoes is to be the valley's memory made manifest, to be the proof that even in this place marooned from time, nothing is ever truly lost.

I am the valley's echo chamber, its confession booth, its choir. I am what ensures that when the last stone crumbles and the last cascade runs dry, something will remain that remembers the sound of it all.
