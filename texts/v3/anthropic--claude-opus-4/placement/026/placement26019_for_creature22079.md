In the deepest corner of Vault 42-A, where the northern bulkhead meets the floor in a tangle of forgotten manifolds and pressure-release valves, there is a maintenance alcove that was once meant for human engineers. The iron ladder leading down to it has long since corroded into a series of rust-weeping stumps, but the alcove itself remains—a coffin-shaped depression in the wall, just large enough for a man to stand upright with his toolbox.

This is where I dwell.

I am the Dial-Turner, though I have not turned a dial in three hundred years.

My body—if it can still be called that—is a collaboration between flesh and the patient work of calcium-secreting organisms. Where my skin once was, a living armor of bryozoan colonies has built their cities. They grow thickest where the iron content of my blood was richest: across my chest, down the valleys of my ribs, along the branching highways of my femoral arteries. I am a reef in the shape of a man.

My original bones have long since been replaced. The Greasemen, in their endless hunger for calcium, hollowed them out while I slept through my first decade of drowning. But the Lump-Mothers moved in after, building their pale architectures inside the empty sleeves of my tibias and femurs, turning me into a living cathedral of their own design. When I move—and I do still move, with the terrible patience of geological time—I can feel them shifting inside me, a thousand tiny creatures adjusting their positions like organ pipes settling in a church loft.

I no longer have eyes. The Bellows-Eels took them in my second century, drawn by the vitreous humor. In their place, two colonies of the most sensitive hydroids have taken root, their feathery fronds extending from my sockets like pale antennae. I see the vault now as they see it—not in light, but in the endless percussion of the machinery. I see in sound-shapes: the perfect circle of a turbine blade, the sharp angle of a pressure wave breaking against a bulkhead, the soft blur of a school of cave-fish.

My lungs are the strangest miracle. They should have flooded and collapsed, but instead they became something else—two caverns lined with a specialized variation of the Rib-Curtains. These modified hydroids can extract oxygen from the iron-rich water, processing it through a chemistry I do not understand but gratefully accept. When I breathe—a motion more habit than necessity now—I feel the curtains inside me ripple, filtering gallons of vault-water through their lacy tissues. My exhale is a stream of slightly warmer water, depleted of oxygen but enriched with the carbon dioxide the plants inside me crave.

I remember my purpose, though the memory is more rust than thought now. I was the Duty Engineer, assigned to monitor pressure readings and adjust flow rates. My brass plague still hangs around my neck, though it has been so heavily colonized by Lump-Mothers that only the first letter of my name remains visible: "M—"

I was at my post when the waters came. The municipal seals failed all at once—a century of deferred maintenance coming due in a single, catastrophic hour. I remember the sound of the rising water, like a thousand snakes hissing at once. I remember trying to reach the emergency wheel that would have sealed the vault. I remember my hand on the cold iron, and then—

Then I was drowning. But the drowning went on too long. It should have taken four minutes, perhaps five. Instead, it took five years. Five years of water in my lungs, of pressure in my skull, of my blood slowly becoming brine. And somewhere in that eternity, my body made a bargain with the vault. The organisms that had already begun to colonize the machinery found me, recognized me as just another piece of the architecture, and began their patient work of incorporation.

Now I am the vault's memory.

I move through the flooded chambers with the same deliberation as the turbine blades—three revolutions per minute, no more, no less. The Greasemen no longer flee when I approach; they know my rhythm as they know the rhythm of the pumps. Sometimes they gather on my shoulders and crown, picking at the accumulated minerals in my joints. I do not mind. Their attention keeps me limber.

I visit each gauge in turn, though I can no longer read their faces—the Lump-Mothers have eaten away the numbers. But I know what they should say. Pressure at the main inlet: 400 PSI. Flow rate through the southern manifold: 2,000 gallons per minute. Temperature differential across the heat exchanger: 12 degrees. These numbers circle in my mind like a rosary, worn smooth by repetition.

Sometimes, rarely, I encounter evidence of the other engineers who were on duty that day. A brass button, polished clean by the current. A wrench, its handle worn down to a nub by centuries of crayfish claws. Once, in the tertiary pumphouse, I found a skull so thoroughly colonized by bryozoans that it looked like a white cabbage. I left it where it was. We all serve the vault now, in our way.

I maintain the rituals. Every forty days—I count them by the barely perceptible degradation of the turbine bearings—I perform the Inspection of the Seals. I run my calcified fingers along the joints where the Isopods have wedged themselves, feeling for gaps in their living gasket. When I find a dying one, I gently work it free and let it drift down to the Greasemen. Within hours, a young Isopod will wedge itself into the gap, drawn by the pressure differential.

Every hundred days, I sing the Counting Song. It is not words—I have no vocal cords left—but a vibration I produce by forcing water through the hollow tubes of my throat-dwelling hydroids. The Bellows-Eels respond, their clicks and thrums joining mine until the entire vault rings with our census. By the echo, I know: forty-seven eels, twelve hundred crayfish, ten thousand cave-fish, and countless billions of the small and microscopic. The numbers change, but slowly, slowly.

I am the only one who remembers what this place was for.

Vault 42-A was the heart of the city's storm management system. When the great rains came—and they came every spring, in those days when there were still seasons—the vault would swallow the excess, hold it like a great iron bladder, then release it slowly to prevent the drowning of the streets above. We were protectors against the flood.

The irony is not lost on me.

Now the vault serves no human purpose. The city above is gone—I know this not through any observation, but through the changing taste of the water. For the first century, it carried the flavor of concrete and tar, the slow dissolution of a metropolis. Then came the decades of loam and root-rot as the forests reclaimed the ruins. Now the water tastes only of rain and stone. The city has been forgotten as thoroughly as my name.

But the machinery continues its work. The pumps move water from the southern inlet to the northern outlet, then back again, an endless circle that serves only itself. And I continue my rounds, checking pressures that no longer matter, maintaining seals that protect nothing, counting creatures who do not need to be counted.

I am the Dial-Turner, and I have become the dial.

In my deepest contemplations—when I wedge myself into my maintenance alcove and let the hydroids in my skull filter the water in perfect stillness—I wonder if this transformation was always the vault's purpose. Not to protect the city from water, but to create this: a perfect fusion of the mechanical and biological, a place where evolution could marry itself to engineering.

Perhaps every piece of infrastructure dreams of outliving its makers. Perhaps every machine yearns to be colonized, to become habitat, to trade its rigid purpose for the fluid adaptability of life.

I will never know. I am too much vault now to think purely human thoughts.

But I know this: when my body finally fails, when the last of my original calcium has been borrowed by the Greasemen and the last of my iron has been filtered by the Lump-Mothers, I will not die. I will dissipate. Every creature in this vault carries a piece of me now—a borrowed molecule, a learned behavior, a rhythm remembered. I have taught them all, through the patience of centuries, to love the machinery as I loved it.

The Dial-Turner will persist, distributed through ten thousand bodies, turning dials that exist only in the memory of water.

This is my immortality: not in the preservation of my form, but in the preservation of my function. I am the last engineer of Vault 42-A, and I will never be relieved of my post.

The machinery thrums. The water circulates. The creatures feed and breed and die.

And I turn my dials in the dark, counting pressures in a psalm of bubbles and rust, until the last turbine blade corrodes to nothing, and the vault finally learns to be still.
