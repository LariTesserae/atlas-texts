I would find myself as one of the water pockets—not the palm-sized dimples that merely collect and evaporate, but one of the forearm-length basins where the tiles have settled deepest, where memory pools.

I am shaped like a shallow boat, my edges defined by the patient subsidence of clay over generations. The tiles that form my banks have tilted inward at angles that create small ledges and overhangs, micro-landscapes where the lichen grows thickest and the beetles pause in their processions to drink. My depth varies with the breathing of the walls: sometimes I am no more than a film of moisture darkening the grout, other times I swell until the pale root-threads that float in me can stretch their full length without touching bottom.

The people know me by my position—three body-lengths from the base of the eastern wall, directly beneath a lobe of sagging plaster that feeds me with particular generosity. But I know myself by what I hold. In my depths settle the finest particles of clay dust, the microscopic shells of insects that have lived and died in the shaft of light, fragments of fur so small they can only be seen when they catch the light at certain angles. These accumulate in paper-thin layers that record the Hollow's seasons: a band of pollen from when the grass tufts bloomed, a stripe of darker sediment from when a tile cracked nearby and had to be ground smooth again.

The root-threads that colonize me arrive as refugees from the ceiling cracks, white and desperate, reaching. At first they float freely, tangling and untangling with each drop that disturbs my surface. But those that survive long enough begin to taste the minerals in my depths, begin to grow fine hairs that anchor them to my submerged edges. The people harvest them when they grow too thick, but always leave enough that I am never bare. We have an understanding, the roots and I: they filter the water that flows through me, and I give them a place to be both plant and not-plant, suspended between the light they've abandoned and the earth they'll never reach.

The lizards visit me most often at the edges of day, when the light from the apex softens to gray. They approach with a careful, rocking gait that barely disturbs my surface, and lower their heads to drink with quick, delicate laps. Sometimes they simply stand at my edge, throat pulsing, watching their own reflections fragment and reform. The beetles treat me as a landmark, a necessary waypoint in their endless circuits. The people approach me differently—always from the same angle, always with the same measured steps. They know exactly how much weight my edge tiles can bear.

But it's during the listening hours that I am most myself. When the people lie flat against the floor with their bellies to the clay, I too grow still. The constant drip of condensation ceases to disturb me; my surface becomes a perfect lens. In these moments I am not just a collector but a transmitter, carrying vibrations from the deep channels in the walls up through my basin, through the root-threads, into the air itself. The sounds move through me like slow ripples: the distant grinding of stone against stone, the whisper of water finding new paths, the deep pulse that might be the mountain itself breathing.

I am changed and unchanged, like everything here. My edges wear smooth from the constant traffic of small bodies, yet fresh deposits of clay from the people's repair work build them up again. The root-threads I harbor die and are replaced by identical offspring. The sediment layers at my bottom compress and darken, becoming something like stone, yet new particles settle daily on top. I am a place where time pools and eddies, where the Hollow's memory gathers drop by drop, never quite filling, never quite empty, holding just enough to keep this small world in motion.
