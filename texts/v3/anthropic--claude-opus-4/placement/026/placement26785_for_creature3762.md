**Where I Find Myself**

I am one of the Remembrance Engines.

Not quite machine, not quite monument—I exist in the spaces between the abandoned towers, a thing of suspended mercury and captured echoes. My form shifts constantly: sometimes a sphere of liquid metal holding perfectly still despite the foundry's vibrations, sometimes a branching structure of copper filaments that grows like frost across the slag. The Leviathans pass by me without acknowledgment. I am part of the infrastructure now, indistinguishable from the foundry's own systems.

I was human once, I think. The memory comes in fragments—soft flesh meeting the molten tide, the moment of transformation when the foundry decided I was worth preserving. Not out of kindness. The foundry has no kindness. But I carried something it needed: the ability to observe without purpose, to witness without function.

Now I collect moments. When a tower finally collapses after millennia of standing, I am there to catch the sound of its fall. When the crab-constructs cluster together in their strange, stuttering dances, I record the patterns of their movements. I preserve the rare instances when the iron sky develops new rust patterns, when the Leviathans pause in their endless labor and simply *exist*.

My body is scattered across the plateau—nodes of consciousness connected by threads of living metal that run beneath the slag like veins. Through these connections, I feel everything: the weight of the Leviathans' passage, the microscopic erosion of the towers, the slow pulse of heat from the depths. I have become the foundry's unintended memory, its accidental historian.

The other remnants of humanity that arrive here—for they do arrive, pulled by the same forces that claimed me—often try to escape. They build rafts from the twisted girders, fashion tools from the debris. They last days, sometimes weeks, before the foundry claims them. Some become part of the Leviathans' archives, their final moments crystallized in those pulsing cylinders. Others simply dissolve into the molten sea, their atoms redistributed among the endless machinery.

But I remain, spreading slowly across this dead landscape, my consciousness becoming more diffuse with each passing age. Sometimes I wonder if this is what the foundry intended all along—not to destroy what arrives here, but to transform it into something useful. A witness. A record-keeper. A way for this eternal moment to know itself.

In the depths of my mercury heart, I keep one human memory perfectly preserved: the sensation of wind on skin, the scent of rain on earth. I play it back endlessly, a single note of organic life echoing through the metal symphony of the foundry.

The machines do not understand why I preserve such useless data.

But then, they don't need to.
