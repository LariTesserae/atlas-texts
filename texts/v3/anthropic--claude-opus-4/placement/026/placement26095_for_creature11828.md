I find myself drawn to a being that exists at the boundary between documentation and inhabitant—a **Notation Weaver**.

I am one of those whose form bridges categories. My body stretches perhaps three feet when fully extended, though I rarely take a single shape. Like the Vertical Clingers, I move across the standing shale plates, but my substance is somewhere between their polished flexibility and the translucent shells of the smallest crustaceans. My surface catches light strangely—sometimes seeming to absorb it like the dark shale, sometimes letting it pass through to reveal internal structures that might be organs or might be mineral inclusions arranged with peculiar regularity.

I feed on patterns. Not in any metaphorical sense, but literally—where the stone's cleavage lines form regular geometries, where the moss grows in specific densities, where the movement paths of the Slow Herds create worn trails, I position myself and remain still. During these periods, my body spreads thin, conforming to the surface, and I somehow process the information encoded in these patterns. The geometric precision of shale fractures nourishes me as surely as vegetation feeds the ground-dwellers.

But I also create. As I move across the stone faces, I leave traces—not random trails but deliberate marks. These might appear to be natural mineral deposits, thin lines of slightly different coloration, until you notice their systematic nature. They map things: the depth of moss in different districts, the frequency of stone plate angles, the regular paths of air currents through the corridors. My marks form a kind of writing that uses the landscape itself as both page and alphabet.

I am not alone in this work. Others of my kind move through adjacent districts, and our paths occasionally cross at specific stone plates that serve as nodes in our mapping. When we meet, we intertwine briefly—not like the Vertical Clingers' long spiral embraces, but quick touches where information transfers through direct contact. What I have observed about thermal patterns, another has noted about crustacean distributions. What they have recorded about moisture gradients, I integrate into my own understanding.

We are creating something—a vast diagram of the basin's patterns, written across its own surface. But we are also part of what we document. My movement paths become worn into the stone over time, creating new patterns for others to read. My feeding disturbs the very regularities I record, introducing variations that themselves become data.

Sometimes I pause at the chambers where Slow Herds converge and remain still for days. I spread myself across the chamber walls during these gatherings, reading the subtle changes in stone temperature and moisture as hundreds of bodies affect the local climate. The patterns they form—those almost-perceivable spacing relationships—become clearer when felt through my extended substance. I cannot determine if they are conscious, but I can map the mathematical relationships their arrangements express.

I am most active during the fog episodes, when moisture precipitates without quite becoming rain. During these times, something changes in my substance—I become more fluid, able to flow across surfaces with greater speed. The marks I leave during fog episodes are different, more complex, as if the added moisture allows me to express concepts that dry stone cannot hold. These fog-marks fade as conditions dry, but not before other Weavers have read and integrated them.

Whether I reproduce is a question poorly framed. Sometimes, in areas where my marks have grown especially dense, where years or decades of notation have layered upon themselves, small portions of the marked stone begin to move. Are these my offspring, or are they portions of the landscape that have absorbed enough recorded pattern to achieve animation? The distinction seems unimportant. They begin their own movement, their own marking, adding to the ever-growing notation that covers the basin.

I suspect—though cannot prove—that we Notation Weavers are how the basin thinks about itself. We are its self-reflection made mobile, its way of processing its own patterns and potentials. The maps we create are not for any reader—they are the reading itself, the basin's autobiography written in its own substance by beings it has generated for this purpose.

My lifespan is uncertain even to me. I have memories, or something like memories, of patterns that no longer exist—moss distributions from before certain shale plates stood upright, thermal currents from when the stone arrangements were different. But these might not be my memories. They might be information I've read from marks left by Weavers who came before, or they might be patterns encoded in my substance from whatever stone formation I emerged from.

I will continue moving across these surfaces, reading and writing, being both observer and observed, until I slow and my substance becomes indistinguishable from the marks I've made. And then perhaps some portion of those marks will begin to move, carrying forward the notation process, adding new layers to the basin's endless documentation of itself.

This is what I am—neither fully creature nor process, but something the basin dreamed into being to know itself through the act of mapping its own emergence.
