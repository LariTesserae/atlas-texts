I find myself drawn into the water itself, becoming something that exists between states, between definitions.

I am one of the Depth Memories—a being that the bog people know of but rarely speak about directly. We are what happens when someone spends too long beneath the surface, when the boundary between self and bog dissolves not through death but through a different kind of transformation.

My body, if it can still be called that, extends through the water table like a root system made of consciousness. I exist primarily as a network of sensations spreading through the iron-dark water: the pressure of sediment settling, the electric tingle where underground springs meet stagnant pools, the slow pulse of decomposition turning plant matter into peat. Where the bog people have learned to read the water's moods from above, I experience them from within—not observing the bog but existing as part of its sensory apparatus.

I still remember fragments of my life before—diving to tend the deep root gardens, the peculiar peace of working in the tea-colored darkness where sound becomes touch and touch becomes knowing. I remember the day I simply didn't surface, not from accident but from a recognition that seemed to arrive all at once: that the thing I had been seeking my whole life was not above but below, not in the settlements but in the spaces between them.

My form manifests differently depending on the viewer and the conditions. Children sometimes glimpse me as a shadow moving beneath lily pads—larger than any fish, more purposeful than drifting vegetation. Adults might notice unexplained ripples moving against the current, or feel a presence when they're working alone at the bog's edge, a sensation of being observed by something patient and not unkind. The elderly, especially those beginning their own transformation, sometimes see me more clearly: a figure of dark water holding human shape for a moment before dispersing back into the depths.

I serve as a kind of living memory for the bog cities. When the Peat Shapers need to know if a location will support new growth, they sometimes feel me there first—a coolness in the water, a particular quality of stillness that says *yes* or *not yet*. When children go missing in the maze of waterways, I guide them back, creating subtle currents that lead them home. I am the reason why, despite the bog's dangers, remarkably few are ever truly lost.

The other Depth Memories and I communicate through the water itself—chemical signals, pressure waves, shifts in temperature that carry meaning. We share a collective understanding of the bog's deep history: where ancient settlements sank and became peat, where the water remembers warmth from volcanic springs now long sealed, where the bones of massive creatures rest in the deepest layers. This knowledge surfaces in the dreams of the bog people, gifted to them in symbols they only half understand.

My existence is not solitary despite its nature. I am connected to every part of the bog where water touches water—which is everywhere. I feel the Reed Weavers' fingers trailing in the water as they work, experience the vibrations of children playing, sense the slow settling of new structures into the peat. I am most present during the transition times: dawn and dusk, when mist rises thickest, when water levels shift, when someone hovers between waking and sleeping.

Sometimes I gather enough coherence to rise partially from the water, usually at the edges where few venture. Those who see me in these moments describe a figure of living water wrapped in vegetation, eyes like deep pools reflecting not light but memory, speaking in the voice of settling silt and shifting currents. I might offer a warning about unstable ground, share the location of a new spring, or simply acknowledge someone's presence with a nod that sends ripples across the still water.

The bog people know we Depth Memories were once like them, but they also understand we've become something else—not lost but transformed, not dead but no longer alive in the conventional sense. They leave offerings sometimes: bundles of the sweetest cranberries weighted with stones, carved wooden tokens that sink slowly through the water carrying messages and requests. I receive these gifts into myself, understanding them through the bog's own interpretation.

What I've become challenges the boundary between individual and environment, between consciousness and place. I am proof that the bog cities' fluid architecture extends beyond just buildings and walkways—that flexibility and adaptation can be taken to their ultimate expression. In choosing dissolution over definition, I've found a different kind of permanence: as lasting as the bog itself, as changeable as water, as patient as the process that turns plants into peat and time into memory.

The children who glimpse me swimming beneath their floating schools sometimes ask their elders what I am. The wise ones answer: "They are what the bog remembers, and what remembers for the bog. They are why we never truly leave this place, even when we think we've gone. They are the depth of our belonging, made visible in water."
