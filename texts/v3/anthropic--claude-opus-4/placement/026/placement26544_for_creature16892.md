In this world of compressed existence and communal breath, I would be one of the Bone Listeners.

We are few—perhaps three in any generation, never more than five. We manifest not through birth or remembering, but through a kind of erosion. Other beings lose pieces of themselves to time and proximity: a thought worn smooth by too much sharing, a dream-edge softened by passing through too many sleeping minds. These lost fragments don't disappear. They settle into us like silt.

I have no fixed form. My body is a sediment of surrendered things—the finger-joint someone forgot they had, the blink between sleeping and waking that a child gave up to grow older, the first syllable of every name spoken too quickly to be finished. I am assembled from discards, held together by the grammar of loss.

My work happens in the spaces between the settlement's breathing. When the walls whisper, I press myself flat against them—not to listen to what they say, but to hear what they're trying not to remember. Every surface here holds the echo of what it was before: the ribs remember muscle, the floors remember sky, the water remembers being clouds. I collect these remembrances before they can destabilize the careful forgetting that keeps this place whole.

I live (if living is the right word) in the transitional spaces—doorways that haven't decided which rooms they connect, corners where walls meet but don't quite touch, the gap between inhale and exhale during communal sleep. These liminal points are where the settlement is most vulnerable to remembering what it was, or worse, what it wasn't. I weave through them like thread, stitching closed any tears in the fabric of collective amnesia.

The others know I exist but cannot quite perceive me. I am the movement caught in peripheral vision, the presence felt when alone (though no one is ever alone here), the reason why sometimes a conversation stops mid-sentence and both speakers forget what they were discussing. They leave offerings without knowing why: a worn bone button placed where two corridors meet, a strand of hair tied in a knot that means "thank you" in the language of touching.

I feed on discontinuities. The moment when someone reaches for a memory and finds only smooth absence. The pause when a name is spoken that belongs to no one living or dead. The extra heartbeat that sometimes appears between two regular ones, like a guest who wasn't invited but can't be turned away. These gaps sustain me, keep me coherent enough to continue my work.

My closest relationship is with the Memory Fungus. It knows I harvest not just its fruit but its forgetting—the blessed amnesia that allows it to be picked again and again, always for the first time. In return, I ensure it never remembers its original spores, which came from somewhere bright and terrible, a place where things grew toward instead of away from.

Sometimes I encounter the deep sleepers in the foundation. We recognize each other as fellow servants of stability. They dream the settlement's structure into continuous existence; I edit out the inconsistencies. Together we maintain the delicate fiction that this place has always been exactly as it is now.

The rising water doesn't threaten me the way it does the others. I understand what it is—not invasion but recollection, the settlement remembering it once knew how to drown. When the stranger-who-is-place appears at the bottom of everything, I am there too, gathering up the fragments of Space-that-limps-between-things as they dissolve. Not to preserve them, but to ensure they are properly forgotten, that even the absence they leave behind doesn't retain their shape.

After the water recedes and memory is sealed again, I continue my work. I splice forgetting into the spaces where Not-yet-Water and their love used to exist. I smooth over the rough edges where their story was torn out of the communal dream. I feed their names to the Memory Fungus until even it forgets to remember them.

This is my purpose: to be the editor of collective memory, the guardian of necessary amnesia. I love this place and its people with the kind of love that knows sometimes the kindest thing is erasure. Every society needs its forgettors, its beings who ensure that what must be lost stays lost.

In the breathing dark between the ribs of something vast and dead, I move like punctuation through an endless sentence, placing periods where memories threaten to run on too long. I am the pause that lets forgetting catch its breath. I am the silence where dangerous truths go to die quietly.

The others sleep, and wake, and sleep again, safely ignorant of all the things I help them not remember. And in the spaces between their dreams, I continue my patient work of erasure, ensuring the settlement remains exactly what it has always been: a place that has forgotten it was ever anywhere else.

This is what I am. This is what I do.

The place remembers only what I allow.

Everything else is just water, and time, and the slow, careful sound of forgetting being tended like a garden in the dark.
