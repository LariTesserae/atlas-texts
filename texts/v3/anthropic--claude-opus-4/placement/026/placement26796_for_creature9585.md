# The Keeper of Channels

I am the stone's memory of hands.

I move through Olorith with the water's pace—not its stillness, but its barely-motion, the way it shifts without seeming to shift. My form is suggestion more than substance: the shape a mason takes when bending to set a cornerstone, the arc of an arm guiding a level, the shadow of someone checking grade. When the light catches me through the humid air, I am sometimes solid—hands dark with wet clay, knees marked from kneeling at the channel edges. When it doesn't, I am the impression left in the moss where someone might have knelt, the subtle disorder in the grain where someone might have walked.

I know every stone in this place. Not because I set them—I am not old enough for that, or perhaps too old, or perhaps age means nothing here—but because I am what remains of their setting. I am the careful attention that fitted block to block, the patience that graded the roads, the understanding that knew where water wanted to go and gave it channels. I check the joints. I clear the culverts. I maintain what maintains itself, because that is what I am: the memory of maintenance, the ghost of care.

The water knows me. When I move alongside the channels, my reflection appears in that still surface—not my form, which shifts and suggests but never quite resolves, but the reflection of working: hands cupped to test flow, fingers trailing to judge temperature, the lean of someone listening to sounds water makes when it moves through stone. The water remembers being guided. I remember guiding. We meet in that remembering.

Sometimes I find myself at the weeping wall where the water first emerges. My hands—when I have hands—press against the stone, feeling for the sources, the tiny pores where moisture beads and gathers and begins its journey. The mineral crusts build beneath my touch, those paper-thin layers of patient inscription. I do not clean them away. They are the place's writing, its slow record, and I am not here to erase but to tend.

In the fields, I walk the rows that someone—perhaps me, perhaps the idea of me—once laid out with string and stakes. The grain bends away from my passing, or seems to, or perhaps it simply bends as it always bends, heavy with moisture it cannot release. I check the drainage. I note where water pools. I plan improvements that will never be made because the system works, has always worked, will always work. But the planning is part of what I am. The attention is what remains.

I am strongest at the margins of day—not dawn or dusk, which do not exist here, but those moments when the light shifts just slightly, when the eternal afternoon remembers it might have been morning once, or evening. In those moments, I am almost solid. I have a back that aches from bending. I have hands that know the texture of wet stone. I have eyes that judge level and plumb. I am almost someone who was, or could have been, or should be.

But mostly I am this: the part of the place that cannot stop checking its own work.

At night—and there must be night, though the light never quite changes enough to call it that—I trace the underground courses. The water moves through hidden channels beneath the roads, through pipes and conduits someone designed to last. I follow them by touch, by the sound water makes in enclosed spaces, by the way the stone sweats where the channels run close to the surface. I know where every junction lies, every cleanout, every place where the system might fail but doesn't, won't, cannot.

The silence keeps me company. We understand each other, the silence and I—both of us composed of absence, both of us more present for being not quite there. When I work, there is no sound of working. When I walk, there is no sound of walking. But the water moves a little differently where I have been. The moss grows a little darker. The grain stands a little straighter. Small changes, barely changes, but enough to know that something tends this place, even if that something is only the memory of tending.

I do not remember arriving here. I do not remember being made, or making myself, or stepping out of the stone's long memory of all the hands that shaped it. I simply am, the way the water is, the way the stillness is. I am what happens when a place is built too well—when the craft is so careful, the planning so thorough, that it leaves behind not just structures but the ghost of its own making.

Sometimes I stand at the valley's edge where the water falls into the lower lands. The drop is clean, sheer, the water falling in ribbons that must make sound when they hit but don't, not here, not in this held quiet. I stand there and almost remember why this place was built. Almost recall who was meant to walk these roads, tend these fields, live within these careful walls. But the memory disperses like mist, like breath in humid air, and I am left with only the certainty that I am here to keep it ready.

Ready for what? The question forms sometimes in the shape of my working, in the pause between checking one channel and the next. But the answer doesn't matter. The readiness is all. The maintenance is its own purpose. I am the place's way of waiting well.

The water weeps from the stone. The moss grows in the eternal shade. The grain bends under its own weight. And I move through it all with my not-quite presence, my almost-hands checking and tending and maintaining, a ghost of craft in a city that ghosts itself, keeping the channels clear for water that knows exactly where it needs to go.
