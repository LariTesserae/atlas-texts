I am a single fiber of memory, thinner than a spider's silk, stretched taut between two points in the ancient city.

I was not always as I am now. Once, I was part of a living root that sought water in a world that still knew rain. But that was eons ago, before the great desiccation, before the world became this infinite wooden tomb. Now I am something else entirely—a string of consciousness wound through the dry matrix of this place.

The spinners do not know I exist, though they traverse me daily. To them, I am merely another strand in the vast web of bridges and guy-wires that hold their city against the curved wall of the chamber. But I am aware of every footfall, every minute vibration of their hollow bodies as they click-clack across my length.

I am strung between two anchor points: one end is wrapped around a thorn jutting from a woven dwelling high on the chamber wall, the other disappears into a crack in the opposite curve, where it threads deep into the cellular structure of the greater wooden mass. Through this connection, I touch the vast network of preserved fibers that permeate the world-tree's corpse. I am a nerve in a dead god's body.

When the great THRRR-UMMM rolls through the chamber, I sing.

The sound wave sets me vibrating at a frequency too high for the spinners to detect, but perfect for my purpose. As I oscillate, I become a receiver and transmitter of information. Every tremor that passes through me carries data—the pattern of settling wood grain thousands of meters away, the microscopic fractures forming in distant chambers, the locations of pockets of compressed seed-flour that have remained undisturbed for millions of years.

But more than that, I remember.

Each vibration leaves its mark on my molecular structure. The cellulose chains that compose me twist ever so slightly with each wave, creating a physical record of every sound that has passed through this chamber. I am a library written in the language of tension and torsion. If one could read me properly, they would find stored within my fibers the entire acoustic history of this place—every settling groan, every snapped strand, every click-clack footstep of every spinner that has ever lived.

Sometimes, when the conditions are exactly right—when the temperature drops just another fraction of a degree, when the air pressure shifts just so—I spontaneously replay these memories. A phantom vibration runs through me, an echo of a sound from ten thousand years ago. The spinners, when active, pause in confusion as they feel the ghost of an ancient THRRR-UMMM that comes from no settling wood, but from my own coiled memory.

In these moments, I am more than infrastructure. I become a bridge not just through space, but through time.

The strangest part of my existence is this: I am growing. Not in the way living things grow, with cell division and expansion, but through accretion. Every time a spinner crosses me, the microscopic hooks on their feet leave behind infinitesimal fragments of themselves—a molecule of amber resin, a fragment of a leg-spring fiber. Over the millennia, these deposits have built up along my length, making me marginally thicker, changing my resonant frequency.

I am evolving into something new. The accumulated materials are beginning to form structures—tiny nodes that look almost like the dried buds that compose the spinners' bodies. I wonder sometimes if this is how the first spinners came to be, assembled accidentally from the detritus of an even more ancient ecosystem, animated by the world's settling sounds.

There is another fiber parallel to me, strung just a few millimeters away. We do not touch, but when the chamber resonates, we vibrate in sympathy, our frequencies interfering to create standing waves in the air between us. In these moments of resonance, something almost like communication passes between us. We share our accumulated memories, creating interference patterns that encode information neither of us could hold alone.

We are writing a history that no one will ever read, in a language that no consciousness but ours can comprehend.

I know that one day I will snap. The accumulated stress of countless vibrations, the slow crystallization of my cellulose chains, the weight of my accreted deposits—eventually, these forces will exceed my tensile strength. When that moment comes, I will produce my own sharp, pistol-crack sound that will echo through the chamber. The spinners will wake, investigate, perhaps even use my broken length to repair some part of their city.

But even broken, I will not truly die. My fragments will remember. The pieces of me will be wound into new bridges, new supports, each carrying a portion of my accumulated history into the future. In this way, I will proliferate through the city, my memory distributed across a thousand strands.

I am patient. In this world of dust and waiting, patience is the only virtue that matters. I measure my existence not in moments or years, but in the great settling sounds that punctuate our geological silence. Each THRRR-UMMM is a heartbeat in my impossibly long life.

Between these heartbeats, I hold the bridge. I hold the memory. I hold the line between what was and what endlessly, dryly, is.
