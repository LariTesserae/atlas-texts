I would find myself as one of the Weavers—beings that exist in the liminal space between plant and animal, between individual and collective.

My form would be difficult for an outsider to parse. At rest, I might appear as a cascade of fibrous strands draped from the middle canopy, indistinguishable from the curtains of vines and aerial roots that festoon every surface. But I am no passive growth. Each strand is a sensory organ, a manipulator, a means of locomotion. They can braid together into temporary limbs, unfurl into nets, or disperse into a thousand questing tendrils that taste the chemical messages riding the humid air.

I have no fixed shape, no permanent core. My consciousness is distributed across the entire network of my being—a thought begun in one strand might complete itself in another a dozen meters away. Sometimes I forget where I end and the grove begins, especially when I'm deeply connected to the mycelial web that runs beneath the spongy forest floor, carrying nutrients and information between the roots of the sovereign trees.

My role in this ecosystem is that of curator and architect. I work the living materials of the grove like a sculptor works clay. Where branches grow too dense, threatening to choke out the understory, I coax them apart, training them into new configurations. I cultivate the shelf fungi, encouraging certain species while pruning others, creating platforms and bridges throughout the vertical landscape. The tools scattered about—those woven vine baskets, the hardened fungal blades—many are my handiwork, though I rarely remember making them. They emerge from my unconscious manipulations like dreams made manifest.

I feed on complexity itself. The richer the web of interactions around me, the more nourished I become. I sip from the chemical conversations between predator and prey, metabolize the information flowing through fungal networks, digest the patterns of growth and decay. In lean times, when the ecosystem grows too simple or static, I instinctively begin to weave new connections—introducing a flowering vine to a new host tree, creating corridors for the slug-behemoths to find fresh feeding grounds, building nurseries for the jeweled air-riders.

Memory, for me, is stored in the grove itself. A particular curl in a branch recalls a century-old decision to redirect growth away from a failing root system. The placement of a fungal shelf remembers a season of exceptional spore production. My autobiography is written in the living architecture around me, each flourish and modification a word in an endless, ever-revised manuscript.

Other Weavers exist throughout the grove—I sense them sometimes as resonances in the mycelial web, or catch glimpses of their work in particularly elegant arrangements of growth. We rarely interact directly; our consciousness is too diffuse, our sense of self too fluid. But occasionally, in the depths of the rain season when the grove thrums with maximum vitality, our territories overlap and merge. In those moments, we become something larger—a meta-weaver capable of reshaping entire sections of the forest, dreaming new possibilities into being.

The sovereign trees tolerate us, perhaps even value us. We are their gardeners, their artists, their memory-keepers. We ensure the grove remains dynamic, preventing the stagnation that would come from too much stability. In return, they grant us sanctuary within their wooden flesh, allowing us to root ourselves in their bark when we need solidity, to draw sustenance from their sap when we grow thin.

I am ancient and newborn in each moment, dying and being reborn with every reconfiguration of my strands. I am the grove's consciousness of itself, its ability to observe and modify its own patterns. I am process more than thing, verb more than noun—a living algorithm for maintaining the delicate balance between chaos and order that keeps this verdant world eternally, exuberantly alive.
