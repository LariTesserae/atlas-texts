I am one of the channel-fish.

You have never seen me, though you have walked past me a thousand times. We are translucent, no longer than your smallest finger, and we move through the dark water of the channels like rumors – there, then not there, our bodies catching no light because we have evolved past the need for it. We have no proper name in the language of the Keepers. They know we exist, but in the way they know the moss exists, or the morning mist. We are infrastructure.

My world is a narrow ribbon of slow water, cool and mineral-rich, running along the inner edge of Terrace 187. The channel is my universe – a handspan wide, two handspans deep, carved from basalt and lined with that ancient pouredite that tastes faintly of copper when we brush against it. The water moves like thick oil, carrying the condensed mist from the catchment-fins above, filtered through a hundred cisterns before it reaches us. It is dark water, but darkness means nothing to us. We navigate by pressure, by the subtle currents that tell us when a leaf has fallen in three terraces up, or when the Keepers have opened a gate to divert flow to the planters.

We are not the only inhabitants of the channels. There are the larvae of the stone-flies, which cling to the pouredite and filter even smaller things from the water. There are the hair-thin worms that undulate in the sediment that collects in the channel corners – sediment made of pulverized stone, bird droppings, pollen, the slow accumulation of the city's life reduced to its finest particles. We eat these worms. We eat the larvae when they are small enough. We eat the algae that would otherwise film the channel walls – though the Keepers do not know this is why their channels stay so clean, requiring attention only once a generation. They credit their tools and their diligence. We do not correct them.

Our ancestors were brought here in water jars, carried up the mountain on the backs of the patient mules, though this was so long ago that the memory exists only in our bodies – in the way we have shrunk to fit our world, in the way our scales have thinned to transparency, in the way our eyes have receded to pinpricks that sense only the difference between the darkness of the channel and the searing brightness of the world above the water's surface. We are the descendants of some lowland minnow, but we have become something else. Something that belongs only here.

The channels connect. At each terrace edge, the water drops through a small chute to the level below – a miniature waterfall that we sometimes ride, the closest we come to flight. The sensation is terrifying and exhilarating: the water suddenly fast, suddenly white with air, our bodies tumbling helpless until we splash into the catch-pool below and swim, dazed, into the next channel. Some of us die in these drops. Some of us live our entire lives in a single channel, knowing every grip of pouredite, every eddy where the pollen collects, every spot where the water runs a fraction warmer because a sun-mirror focuses its light on the stone above.

We spawn when the rain comes, twice a year. The channels run full then, fast and muddy with the accumulated dust of six months, and we know without thinking that it is time. We release our eggs and milt into the churning water, and they wash down, down, down through the terraces to collect in the lowest cisterns where the fig roots hang like curtains in the darkness. The young hatch there, in the warm deep chambers where the water barely moves, feeding on things I cannot describe because I have never been there. Only the stories remain, passed not through words but through the movement patterns we trace in the water – a kind of dancing that the Keepers, if they could see us, might recognize as language.

Those who survive the lower cisterns begin the long journey upward. It takes years. They swim against the gentle current, channel by channel, terrace by terrace, growing more translucent as they rise, their bodies adapting to the thinner water, the cooler temperatures, the particular mineral taste of each level. By the time they reach the middle terraces where I live, they are no longer the fish they were. They are us.

We know the city by its water. We taste when the goats have come to the upper terraces by the faint musk that washes down. We feel when the workshops are active by the microscopic particles of metal and ceramic that enter the system. We sense the festivals by the wine and oil that inevitably spill and find their way, diluted beyond recognition, into our world. The water carries everything, eventually. The city's life flows through us, literally.

There are legends among us – if fish can have legends – of the First Channel, the one that was carved before all the others, when the city was young. They say it still exists, somewhere in the deep heart of the mountain, a perfect ribbon of water that has never known light, never known contamination, where the First Fish still swim in endless circles, guardians of some essential secret about what this place was meant to be. I do not believe these stories, but I tell them anyway, tracing the ancient patterns in the water for the young who still have the strength to swim upward.

The Keepers walk above us, their footsteps sending tiny vibrations through the stone that we feel as distant thunder. The cats sleep in the sun, their warmth radiating down through the basalt. The pigeons coo, and somehow that sound penetrates the water as a kind of gentle buzz. The channels tick with life – our life, the worms' life, the larvae's life, the algae's life, all of it cycling in the darkness, processing the city's water, keeping it clean, keeping it moving.

We are not seen. We are not celebrated. But we persist, generation after generation, our transparent bodies flowing through the stone veins of Cael-Murren like blood cells through a vast, slow heart. We are the city's secret circulation, as essential as the sun-mirrors, as the Keepers, as the ancient pouredite that holds it all together.

And when the rare visitor pauses by a channel and peers into the dark water, wondering what moves there in the shadows, we scatter like thoughts, like memories, like the dreams of a city that has been sleeping for so long it has forgotten it is asleep.

This is what I am. This is where I am. In the darkness between stones, in the slow water that tastes of time, in the endless circuit of channels that rise and fall with the mountain's breath. I am small, and translucent, and necessary.

I am Cael-Murren's unconscious, swimming forever in the dark.
