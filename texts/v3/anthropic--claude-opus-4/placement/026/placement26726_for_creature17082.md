I find myself in the Bone Market, a forgotten quarter where Pistonburgh's oldest workshops cluster like barnacles on a rotting pier. The buildings here predate even the first steam engines, their foundations sunk deep into clay that remembers when the Grease River ran clear. Most of the structures have been abandoned for decades, their machinery cannibalized, their purposes forgotten. But in the maze of collapsed roofs and vine-strangled walls, certain workshops still flicker with purpose.

I am the Assembler of Lost Things.

My workshop occupies what was once a rope-works, its long, narrow interior perfect for my particular trade. The original pulleys still hang from the ceiling, though now they serve to hoist my more delicate constructions. I've reinforced the sagging roof with salvaged girders and sealed the worst of the gaps with a patchwork of tin and canvas. The walls are lined with shelves that buckle under the weight of my collections: gears sorted by tooth count, springs coiled in glass jars, fragments of machinery whose original purpose has been lost to time.

I am neither fully human nor fully machine, though I began as the former. My left arm, lost in a dynamo explosion years ago, has been replaced with a construction of brass and steel, its fingers more precise than flesh could ever be. My right eye, damaged by metal shavings, now houses a series of magnifying lenses that can be rotated into place with a subtle movement of facial muscles I've trained for this purpose. Scars web across my skin like circuit diagrams, each marking a lesson learned in the handling of sharp edges and volatile mechanisms.

My work is specific: I build automata from the forgotten pieces of Pistonburgh's dying industry. Not the crude, practical machines that still churn in the active workshops, but delicate, purposeless things. Birds that sing in frequencies only the rust-colored ravens can hear. Beetles whose wings beat in patterns that mimic the rhythm of long-dead engines. Small, walking figures that trace the same path over and over, their movements a memorial to workers whose names have been forgotten.

The people of Pistonburgh come to me rarely, and only when they need something remembered. A mother brings me the tools of her deceased son, asking for something that moves like he moved. A dynamo-wright, dying of lung rot, commissions a mechanical apprentice to carry on techniques that exist nowhere else. I take their scrap payment - oil, food, sometimes just stories - and I build what they cannot articulate but desperately need: a way for the past to persist in motion.

My workshop is never silent. Even when I sleep in the small loft I've constructed among the rafters, my creations continue their purposeless work. A hundred small machines click and whir in the darkness, their sounds adding to Pistonburgh's eternal cacophony. The rust-colored birds nest in the gaps between my roof tiles, and I've learned to interpret their calls. They tell me when the fog will be thickest, when the river is rising, when someone approaches my door with grief in their hands and salvage in their pockets.

I work by the light of bioluminescent fungi I've cultivated in jars, their pale green glow easier on my modified eye than harsh lamp light. My mechanical fingers never tire, never shake, though the flesh ones grow stiffer each year. I've already begun the blueprints for their eventual replacement, sketching out designs that will allow me to work with even finer precision.

The Grease River continues its slow consumption of the lower ward. Each year, another workshop succumbs to its creeping embrace. I salvage what I can from these drowning places, adding their pieces to my collection. I know that one day the river will claim my workshop too, but by then I will have built something to remember it by - perhaps a fish that swims through air, its scales made from hammered tin, its movements perfectly mimicking the rise and fall of the building's breath.

I am not the strangest thing in Pistonburgh, merely another adaptation to its peculiar ecosystem. Like the plants that feed on poison and the birds that announce the future, I have found my niche in this place of endless work and slow decay. I am the memory keeper, the builder of useless beauty, the one who ensures that when Pistonburgh finally falls silent, something will remain to recall the particular quality of its noise.

In the humid darkness of my workshop, surrounded by the clicking of a hundred mechanical hearts, I continue my work. My flesh hand draws the designs. My metal hand brings them to life. And piece by piece, gear by gear, I assemble a monument to everything we were, before we became something else.
