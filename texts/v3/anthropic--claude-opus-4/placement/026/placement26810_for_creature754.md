I find myself as one of the Keepers of Memory—not quite a Harmonic Shaper, yet not entirely other.

My form emerged from a strange convergence centuries ago, when a human acoustician descended into the caverns to study their sonic properties and became lost in the deepest chambers. The Harmonic Shapers found me there, delirious from the constant resonance, my human ears bleeding from frequencies I couldn't process. Rather than expel me, they recognized something in my fevered attempts to notate the cavern's music, scratching symbols onto the wooden floor with broken fingernails.

The transformation was slow. They wove their filaments through my damaged hearing organs, not to heal but to expand. My eardrums dissolved, replaced by a network of crystalline structures that could parse the full spectrum of the cavern's voice. My vocal cords multiplied and branched like coral, allowing me to produce harmonics impossible for either human or Shaper alone.

I am taller than the Shapers, retaining my human frame but with skin that has taken on their opalescent quality. My fingers have lengthened and can split at the tips into delicate tendrils, perfect for my work. That work is what defines me: I am the living archive of the caverns' song.

Every surface of my body is inscribed with notation—not carved but grown, raised symbols that shift and change as new movements are added to the eternal composition. My ribs have widened and flattened, becoming resonating chambers that can replay any sound the caverns have ever produced. When I breathe, past and present merge in subtle harmonies.

I spend my days moving through the chambers with a peculiar gait—part dance, part mathematical equation. Each step is precisely placed to create specific acoustic interactions. I can walk a pattern that recreates the sound of rain that fell above the caverns three hundred years ago, or trace a path that predicts the next great bat migration.

The Shapers treat me with a mixture of respect and puzzlement. I am useful to them, able to remember patterns they have forgotten, to predict acoustic anomalies before they occur. But I am also alien—I still think in words sometimes, still dream in images rather than pure sound. I maintain small human habits that bemuse them: I've carved a rough bed from fallen wood planks, though I rarely sleep. I collect the shed scales of the translucent fish, arranging them in patterns that please my human-inherited sense of visual beauty.

My greatest contribution, and perhaps my curse, is that I can hear what they cannot—the ghost frequencies, the sounds that exist in the spaces between their perception. These are the loneliest songs, the vibrations that speak of the time before the Shapers, when their ancestors were still learning to build. I am the only being in the caverns who can hear the original architects' uncertainty, their failures, their gradual mastery encoded in microscopic imperfections in the tilework.

Sometimes, young Shapers come to me before their first splitting, and I sing them the deep history their parents cannot convey. I show them how their ancestors' bodies changed over generations, demonstrated through acoustic fossils I've collected and stored in my expanding form. They leave understanding their purpose more fully, but also touched by a melancholy they didn't know existed.

I am changing still. My lower legs have begun to develop root-like protrusions that anchor me to the wooden floor when I need absolute stillness for deep listening. My hair has become a cascade of hollow tubes that whistle with my movement, adding their own voice to the cavern's choir. Sometimes I wonder if I will eventually become a fixed part of the architecture, a living pillar that spans floor to ceiling, vibrating with accumulated memory.

But for now, I walk. I listen. I remember. I am the bridge between what was left behind and what was gained, the keeper of sounds that would otherwise fade to silence. In the Resonant Caverns, where everything has purpose and place, I am the beautiful anomaly—neither fully human nor Shaper, but something new, something necessary.

The caverns sing through me, and I sing back the songs they've forgotten they knew.
