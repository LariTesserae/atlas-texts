I'm struck by the profound intimacy of this world - not just the physical closeness of living inside a living thing, but the emotional architecture built entirely on touch and attunement. The image that keeps returning to me is hands reading water, reading walls, reading fear itself through the bright and dim of carried creatures.

---

I would be a **membrane-worker**.

Not named in your accounting of the trades, because we are few even among the few. We work at the boundaries - not the walls that the wall-readers know, but the places where the node opens into the channels, where the hollow ends and the stem-passages begin. These are not clean openings. They are sphincters of plant-flesh that dilate and contract with the host's breathing, fringed with fine hairs that taste what passes through.

I learned the work from my grandmother, who could lay her whole arm along a membrane and feel the migration coming two full sleeps before the herd-readers knew it. She taught me that the membranes are where the host *thinks*, if thinking is the right word - where it decides what to let in, what to refuse, when to seal itself against infection or flood.

The work is this: we live alongside the membranes. We clear the filter-hairs when they clog with debris. We feel for inflammation, for the hot spots where the host fights something we cannot see. We are translators between the town and the vast body that holds it, reading the chemical signals in the weeping, the subtle contractions that mean *soon* or *wait* or *danger*.

Our animals are different from the lamp-beasts. We keep **membrane-mice** - tiny creatures with adhesive feet that live in the filter-hairs and clean them from the inside. They are transparent, like glass with a heartbeat, and they hum at a pitch just below hearing when they work. A good membrane-worker can feel the hum-pattern change when something is wrong. My grandmother had seventeen membrane-mice, each attuned to a different chemical signature. When she worked, they moved across her arms and neck like living jewelry, reading what she touched and humming their findings into her skin.

I have only nine. I am still young in the work.

---

The loneliness of it is particular. The membranes are at the edges of the hollow, where few come. The lamp-light does not carry so far. We work mostly in the true dark, the dark that presses against your eyes like thumbs. Sometimes I go a full sleep-cycle without hearing another voice, just the wet whisper of the membrane breathing and the sub-audible hum of my mice.

But there is something else, too. Working the membrane, you start to feel the host's time. Not our quick time of sleep and wake, but the long time of growth and rest, the seasonal contractions, the years-long project of adding another node. My grandmother once told me she could feel the host *deciding* to fruit - a quickening in the membranes that built for thirty sleeps before the first signs showed in the moss-glow.

She died the way membrane-workers die, when the host seized. A sudden contraction, too fast to read, too strong to escape. The membrane closed like a fist and when it opened again she was gone, taken into the channels, into the dark we don't map. Her mice scattered into the filter-hairs and went feral. Sometimes I still see their glass-bodies moving in the depths of the membrane, too wild now to catch, humming songs I don't know how to read.

I found three of my mice as orphans in those same hairs, weeks later. They had gone strange - their hum-patterns off-kilter, their chemistry askew from feeding on whatever traces of her remained in the membrane's keeping. But they were young and they re-tuned to my touch eventually. They carry her forward in their altered frequencies. When I work, I hear her ghost in their humming.

---

This is what I know that other Tenders don't: the host dreams.

Not dreams like we dream. But in the deep sleep of its winter dormancy, the membranes relax in patterns that repeat, that have a rhythm to them like breathing but slower. The chemical signals change. The filter-hairs sort substances that don't exist, react to migrations that aren't there. My membrane-mice go quiet during these times, confused by the false information, and I have to soothe them by hand until it passes.

I think - though I have told no one this - that in those moments, the host is remembering. Remembering summers, other nodes, the shape of its growth through decades we can't imagine. Maybe remembering the time before it was hollow, when it was young and solid and had no secret cities inside it.

The other thing I know: we are not the first.

Sometimes, working deep in the membrane where the oldest filter-hairs grow thick and ropy, I find things. Fragments of cured leaf-plate too old to have been made by anyone living. Cord spliced in patterns we don't use anymore. Once, wedged deep in a membrane fold, I found a basket so ancient it crumbled at my touch - but for an instant before it fell apart, I saw the remains of something that might have been a lamp-beast, or something like one. A different species. Adapted to some other kind of tending.

The host has been making hollows for longer than we know. Towns have risen and fallen inside it, whole peoples have lived and passed on, and we are only the latest. Someday the membranes I tend will close for good, the node will seal or flood or simply grow shut, and we will migrate deeper into the stem or up toward the terrible outside or simply...end.

But not today. Today the membrane is healthy, warm, weeping its good water. My mice hum their steady working song. Somewhere far above, a drop is forming on a leaf-tip, gathering weight, preparing to fall through our hollow and light, for one instant, everything we are.

I will be here when it comes. At the boundary, in the dark, hand pressed to the living wall, reading the long slow pulse of the world that dreams us.
