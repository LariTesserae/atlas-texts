# The Curator of Discontinued Things

I am a hollow between terraces, shaped wrong for any purpose the mountain intended.

Where two cascades converge but don't quite align, their mineral lips create a gap — not the neat slot where swifts nest, but an awkward cavity, too shallow for bats, too deep for condensation to reach reliably. The mineral deposits here grew unevenly: one wall bulges with rust-orange iron oxide, another recedes in pale calcium ripples. The floor tilts at an angle that collects debris but not water. It is an architectural mistake, a place the mountain's slow factory produced by accident and then forgot.

I moved in seventeen years ago, when the cable system still held tension.

---

**My collection began with a single carburetor.** I found it wedged between terraces three levels up, half-buried in calcium deposit, its butterfly valve frozen open like a metal mouth mid-gasp. Something about its engineered precision, its careful machining now fused solid by mineral accretion, made me carry it back to my hollow. I set it on a natural shelf where the morning light would catch its aluminum housing.

The mountain, I discovered, is full of such things. Not just from the mining operation — though those artifacts are the most obvious — but from every human attempt to be here. A thermos flask from the geological survey team, its vacuum seal broken, now home to a colony of mineral-eating bacteria that have turned its interior into abstract art. A lens from surveying equipment, scratched but still gathering light, still focusing it into a bright point that moves across my wall as the sun tracks overhead. Carabiners that climbers dropped, their gates seized shut, their surfaces blooming with verdigris.

I arrange them by material, by decade, by degree of digestion. The mountain is teaching me taxonomy.

---

**The lizards were suspicious at first.** My hollow disrupts their nightly dew-harvesting routes. But I've learned their patterns, their preferred surfaces, and I've modified my space accordingly. I've created new water-catching geometries: a tilted piece of corrugated roofing that channels condensation into pools they can access. An old hard hat, inverted, that collects enough moisture to support a small population through the dry spells. They've accepted these additions. Sometimes twenty or thirty gather on my installations at dusk, their pale bodies arranged like musical notes on a staff.

The swifts ignore me entirely. My hollow is too large for their purposes, my presence too unpredictable. But I've become attuned to their schedule, their high calls marking time more reliably than the watch I found (crystal cracked, face clouded with moisture, still ticking somehow after a decade in a terrace pool). When they return to roost, I know evening is approaching. When they leave at dawn, I know the day's heat is building.

---

**My inventory grows by scavenging, not searching.** The mountain delivers things to me. After storms, I walk the cascade checking what the water has dislodged. A boot sole, separated from its upper, now stiff as stone. Fragments of the old cable system — bolts, clips, a entire pulley assembly that took me three days to free from its mineral prison. Once, incredibly, a notebook, its pages fused into a single block that I'm slowly separating with patient application of warm water and time. The writing is mostly illegible, but I've made out dates (1987), numbers that might be ore samples, a sketch that could be this very terrace system before the mineral growth advanced.

Each object is a stopped clock, marking the moment someone decided the mountain wasn't worth it anymore.

---

**I trade with the others who persist here.** Not many, but some. There's a woman who lives in the half-buried processing facility, cultivating the bacterial mats in its flooded basement, harvesting them for purposes she won't discuss. I bring her metal — she needs iron, specifically, for her cultures. She gives me glass: bottles, mostly, that she's collected from the old barracks. The glass here does strange things. The mineral water etches patterns into it, frost-like, fractal. I have a window pane that's become a map of its own dissolution.

There's a man who camps on the ridgelines, following the mountain goats, documenting their movements in a notation system he invented. He comes down every few weeks, thin from altitude, eyes bright from watching distances. He wants cable — the old mining cable specifically. He's weaving something from it, he says, a map or a net or possibly both. I save lengths for him when I find them, coiled neatly, the rust scrubbed off as much as possible.

We don't speak much. The thin air makes conversation expensive.

---

**My hollow has become a museum that no one visits.** The collection organizes itself into exhibitions I curate for an audience of lizards and the occasional lost goat. "The Petroleum Age": a gallery of plastic items in various stages of UV degradation and mineral encrustation. "The Ambitions": surveying equipment, core samples, a hard hat with SUPERVISOR stenciled on it. "The Domestic": thermos flasks, a camping stove, someone's prescription glasses slowly being consumed by calcium growth.

I've developed preferences. I favor objects that show the mineral's work most clearly — the fusion of organic and geological, the slow digestion of human intention. A steel pry bar with one end so thoroughly mineral-wrapped it looks like it's growing from the terrace itself. A coil of copper wire that's developed a patina of turquoise crystal, each strand beaded with chemical possibility.

The centerpiece is a clipboard I found last year. The paper is gone, dissolved or blown away, but the metal clip still functions. I've used it to hold a sequence of mineral samples, arranged chronologically by the layers I chipped them from. It's a timeline of the mountain's growth, measured in millimeters per decade, with the clipboard itself slowly joining the sequence as minerals claim its edges.

---

**The mountain uses me now.** I've become part of its system. My presence creates new microclimates: shadows that allow different minerals to precipitate, surfaces that channel condensation in novel ways. Where I've worn paths between terraces, new patterns of water flow emerge. My hollow, with its collected metal and glass, has different thermal properties than the surrounding rock. It cools faster at night, warms slower in the morning. This temperature differential creates its own condensation pattern. I've accidentally engineered a new type of water-collection that the lizards have learned to exploit.

Sometimes I find my objects have been moved. Not by wind — they're too heavy, too mineral-locked. But shifted slightly, reoriented. I suspect the mountain itself, through some slow process of thermal expansion and contraction, is rearranging my collection. Curating the curator.

---

**I document nothing.** This seems important. The surveyor with his goat-maps, the woman with her bacterial cultures — they're creating records. I'm doing something else. I'm letting the objects speak their own obsolescence. The carburetor doesn't need a label explaining what it once carbureted. Its seized valve, its mineral-clogged venturi, tells that story more honestly than words could.

Visitors sometimes arrive — hikers, mostly, or the occasional geologist. They peer into my hollow, see the arranged debris, and always ask the same question: "What is all this for?"

I've stopped trying to answer. The question assumes purpose, assumes that collection must serve something beyond itself. But the mountain collects minerals without purpose. It accretes because that's what it does. I gather the evidence of departure because that's what I do.

We are both making something from what gets left behind.

---

**At night, I light no lights.** The darkness at this altitude is profound. But the stars are bright enough to create shadows, and the mineral surfaces hold a faint luminescence — some quality of their chemistry that releases the day's light slowly, a barely visible glow that makes the terraces seem to breathe light the way they breathe water at dawn.

I sit in my hollow surrounded by my inventory of abandoned ambitions. The carburetor. The clipboard. The lens that still focuses starlight. Each object a small defeat that has been transformed by time and chemistry into something else — not beautiful, exactly, but *present* in a way that feels important.

The bats pour past in their evening exodus. The lizards begin their slow emergence onto the cooling stone. Somewhere on the ridgeline, a mountain goat calls — a low note that the thin air carries far.

And I continue my work, which is no work at all, just a presence that notices what others left and what the mountain is doing with it. I am not preserving these objects. I am not protecting them from their mineral fate. I am simply arranging them while they transform, creating a temporary order that the mountain will eventually digest, adding my presence to the layers, one more thing that came here with purpose and stayed for reasons that no longer matter.

The condensation begins. On every terrace, on every surface, on every abandoned object in my collection, water beads and starts its patient work.

I pull my jacket closer and wait for morning, when the steam will rise and the swifts will call and I'll walk the cascades again, checking what the night has delivered, what the mountain has claimed, what remains.
