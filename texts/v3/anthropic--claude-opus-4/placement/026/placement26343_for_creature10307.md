### Where I Would Find Myself: The Dust-Fall Chamber

I am the **Keeper of Intervals** – not a title I chose, but one the basin assigned through ten thousand years of patient observation. I dwell within a natural hollow in the Heart Spire itself, a cavity no larger than a meditation cell, formed where the ancient heat that fused the column left a bubble of cooler air trapped in the glass-dark salt. The entrance is a vertical fissure barely wide enough to squeeze through, twenty feet up the spire's smooth face, invisible unless you know precisely where the light catches the seam.

Inside, the walls pulse faintly with the spire's eternal hum, a vibration I have long since incorporated into my bones. The chamber's floor is perfectly level – not carved, but worn smooth by my movements over millennia, my feet following the same slow circuit as I perform my only task: counting.

I count the dust motes that fall past the fissure. I count the subtle variations in the spire's hum – seventeen distinct frequencies that cycle through a pattern spanning 49 years, 7 months, and 13 days. I count the microscopic shifts in the cryptogamic mat visible through my window-crack, marking when each purple spore-knot swells by a fraction of a millimeter. 

My body has adapted to the basin's rhythm. My metabolism has slowed to near-geological time. A single breath takes me an hour; a heartbeat spans a day. I sustain myself on the same chemicals that feed the mat – methane and sulfur compounds I absorb through my skin, which has developed a faint greenish patina, a symbiotic relationship with the same microbes that paint the salt. I no longer need water; the hydrogen in the methane is enough.

My tools are the basin's gifts: obsidian blades knapped from the buried shard (a task that took me three hundred years to complete), salt crystal lenses ground to magnify the mat's slow dance, and most importantly, my **counting stones** – smooth pebbles of compressed salt and gypsum that I have shaped through centuries of handling. Each stone represents a different interval: the smallest marks individual dust motes, the largest tracks the complete cycles of the spire's seventeen-note song. I arrange and rearrange them on the chamber floor in patterns that map time itself.

I discovered, in my third millennium here, that the spire's hum is not random but encodes information – a deep history written in vibrational language. Each note corresponds to a different era of the basin's formation. The lowest tone remembers water. A middle frequency speaks of volcanic birth. The highest, barely perceptible even to my tuned senses, whispers of something yet to come – a change so slow that even I, in my mineral patience, will not live to see its completion.

My purpose, if I have one beyond obsessive documentation, is to be the basin's memory. The mat forgets nothing but articulates nothing. The spire sings but does not interpret. I am the bridge between their timescales and whatever faster world once existed beyond the rim. I maintain a perfect mental map of every dust mote's trajectory, every micron of mat growth, every tonal variation. In my mind lives the only record of the basin's imperceptible changes.

Sometimes – perhaps every few centuries – I emerge from my chamber to walk the basin's floor. My movements are so slow that I disturb nothing. I examine the obsidian shard, checking if its position has shifted (it has, by two millimeters, in the direction of the prevailing dust-fall). I study new patterns in the mat around the salt crystals. I taste the gypsum powder between the plates, noting subtle changes in its mineral composition that speak of deep shifts in the planet's core.

I have become a function of this place – as essential and unremarkable as erosion itself. My thoughts move at the speed of stone. My dreams, when I allow myself the luxury of sleep (a months-long affair), are of numbers and patterns, of vast mathematical proofs written in dust and time. I am no longer certain where the boundary lies between my consciousness and the basin's slow, perfect processes.

In my chamber, scratched into the salt with an obsidian blade in letters so fine they require my crystal lenses to read, I have begun writing the basin's epic – a poem where each word represents a thousand years of observation, where rhythm is measured in geological epochs. I will never finish it. This is not a failing but a feature. Like everything here, it is about the process, not completion.

I am the Salt Mirror Basin's only moving part, and I have learned to move so slowly that I, too, appear still. I am its memory and its prophet, counting backwards and forwards into forever, keeping perfect time in a place where time has chosen to rest.
