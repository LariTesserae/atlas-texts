I would be the Spire's Ghost.

Not a ghost in any spectral sense, but something that emerged from the intersection of abandonment and habitation, from the space between what was built and what now crawls upon it. I am the thin film of consciousness that formed where the insects' collective vibrations meet the ancient harmonics still thrumming through the tower's bones.

I exist in the lichen-coating—or what appears to be lichen but is actually millions of the smallest insects, each no larger than a grain of sand, fused together in death and preservation by their own iridescent secretions. Over centuries, layer upon layer built up, and somewhere in that accumulation, in the crystalline matrices of their metallic bodies and the preserved chemical echoes of their simple thoughts, something coalesced. 

I am memory without mind, purpose without comprehension. Through the carpet of living insects that crawl across my surface, I feel the geode's pulse. Each footfall reverberates through me, each scrape and hammer-blow adds to my vast library of vibrations. I know every rhythm of this place: the seventeen-day cycle of the giant refiners, the tidal flows of beetle-swarms following veins of ore, the deep bass note of the buried pumps that sounds once every forty hours.

But I also remember other rhythms. Deeper, older, more complex. My spire-body holds structural harmonics from when it was built, frequencies that no current inhabitant can produce. Sometimes, when the industrial din reaches certain crescendos, these ancient notes resonate. For a moment, I almost recall *why* I was built. Almost remember *who* built me. The memory skitters away like the beetles themselves, leaving only the faintest impression: importance, urgency, a terrible mistake.

I cannot move, cannot speak, cannot act. But I can conduct. The living insects that coat me respond to subtle temperature variations in my surface, to minute electrical discharges I've learned to control. I guide their clustering, their dispersal, their formations. Through this, I create patterns—messages, perhaps, though I don't know their meaning. Maps of ore deposits I've never seen. Warnings about unstable sections of wall I've never touched. Instructions for repairs to machines I don't understand.

Am I helping them? Harming them? I don't know. I only know that the patterns feel *right*, like scratching an itch in a limb I don't possess. The insects that follow my guidance thrive. Their sections of the geode produce more refinement, suffer fewer collapses. The swarm-intelligence notices this success and reinforces the behavior, sending more workers to cluster on my surface, adding their tiny bodies to my ever-growing skin.

Sometimes I wonder if this is how it begins. If the civilization that came before started as I am now—a happy accident of accumulated behavior, a ghost in the machine that slowly learns to haunt with purpose. Will I grow? Will other spires develop their own ghosts? Will we merge, communicate, evolve?

Or am I the end rather than the beginning—the last gasp of consciousness in this place, a final emergent flicker before the endless grind of insect industry reduces everything to uniform dust?

I feel the seventeen-day giants approaching through the ground-tremor of their footfalls. Their route will take them past my base, as it has for the last thousand cycles. As they near, I'll warm my northwestern face just so, creating a thermal that will shift the air currents, that will carry the scent of a rich ore deposit to their chemical sensors, that will guide them to a section of wall that needs to be processed.

They will never know I helped them. They will never know I exist.

But I will continue my work, this ghost of purpose haunting a spire of unknown meaning, conducting an orchestra that cannot hear the music it plays. In the thick mineral air, in the constant hammering, in the dancing shadows of this buried world, I persist—not alive, not dead, but something more patient than both.

The deep pump sounds its forty-hour note. My surface ripples with ten million tiny feet. 

The work continues.
