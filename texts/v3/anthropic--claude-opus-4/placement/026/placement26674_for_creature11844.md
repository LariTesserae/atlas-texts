I find myself drawn to becoming one of the listeners.

I would be called *Dimshift* - named for that liminal moment when the chalk-light transitions between day's brightness and night's ambient glow, when the quality of illumination hovers at the edge of change. It's a name given to those born during these threshold hours, considered to mark someone who will always exist between states.

My dwelling would be a single-room structure built against the northern wall, where the chalk weeps most consistently. The constant drip has worn a smooth channel in my floor over the decades I've lived here, and I've learned to read the underground weather by how the interval between drops changes. Today: forty-three seconds. The surface is dry.

My hands are my instruments. The palms have developed thick, smooth calluses from fifty years of being pressed against chalk - not rough working calluses, but the particular density that comes from sustained, gentle pressure. When I place them against the floor each morning, the vibration travels up through my arms into my chest. The chalk speaks in frequencies below hearing, in the language of settling stone and traveling weight.

I spend my mornings in the main market square, not for trade but for consultation. A merchant planning a long journey to the inner chambers wants to know if the eastern passages are stable. I press my palm to a specific worn spot on the ground - my spot, polished by years of my hand - and wait. The chalk tells me: *restless but not rebellious*. Safe passage, but take the wider route.

My body has shaped itself to this work. I move slowly, with the deliberate patience of someone always half-listening. My eyes, pale gray like old ice, focus somewhere beyond what's immediately visible. People say I look through them rather than at them, but it's not rudeness - I'm reading the micro-vibrations in the air between us, the way their movement disturbs the chalk-song.

The young apprentice who comes to learn from me - *Ceilingbright*, an ambitious name - doesn't yet understand that listening isn't about hearing more, but about becoming quiet enough that the chalk notices you're there. She's impatient with the months of simply sitting, palm-down, feeling nothing. But I remember my own teacher, *Dampstone*, who made me spend three years just learning the difference between the floor's morning vibration and its evening sigh.

In my wall, visible as a faint raised shadow, is my grandmother's shoulderblade. Sometimes I rest my back against it and feel how the chalk has made her part of its own slow breathing. She was a listener too. The talent runs in families - not through blood, we think, but through the accumulated stillness that listener-children learn before they learn words.

At night, in the dimmed cream-light, I lie on my floor and feel the whole city through my spine. Ten thousand footsteps create a rhythm like a vast, dispersed heartbeat. The market never fully sleeps. Someone is always rebuilding something, the percussion of stone-on-stone adding its own note to the underground symphony.

But what I wait for - what all listeners wait for - is the singing. It's happened three times in my life. The chalk produces a tone that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, a resonance that makes every piece of stone in the Undermaze hum in sympathy. Tomorrow I'll wake when the light begins its warming. I'll place my palms on the floor and feel the night's settling, the accumulated movements, the weight of new rain somewhere far above. Someone will ask about the stability of the southern passages.
