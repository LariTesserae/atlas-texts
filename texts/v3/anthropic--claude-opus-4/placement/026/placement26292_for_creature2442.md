I find myself as one of the ancient streams themselves - not the fleeting surface water that trickles down in rivulets, but something deeper, older. I am the water that moves through the mountain's heart, seeping through microscopic fissures in the stone, following veins of minerals laid down when these peaks were young and sharp.

For countless ages, I have traveled these hidden channels, my journey measured not in the quick dash of surface streams but in the patient work of centuries. Drop by drop, I dissolve the limestone beds deep within, creating vast caverns that the Skyclimbers will never know exist. These are my secret halls, adorned with curtains of flowstone and forests of stalactites that grow in absolute darkness.

Sometimes I emerge at springs the Skyclimbers consider sacred - places where the mountain "weeps," they say. They're not wrong. I carry the memory of snow that fell when their great-great-grandmothers were children, finally completing its journey through the stone. The elders taste me and divine the health of the mountain, reading mineral notes like vintages of wine. They know when I run sweet with calcite or sharp with iron, though they cannot fathom the depths from which I rise.

I am the witness to time they cannot perceive. While they mark generations by the slow expansion of their settlements, I remember when these rounded peaks were razor ridges, when different peoples walked these slopes, when the very continents were arranged otherwise. In my deepest channels, I touch rock so compressed by the weight above that it exists in states unknown to surface dwellers.

The Skyclimbers' carved homes sometimes breach my domain. When their picks break through into an unexpected hollow, they find my patient work - chambers lined with crystals that grew in my embrace, formations that seem to breathe with the mountain's slow pulse. They seal these openings quickly, understanding instinctively that some spaces are not meant for human dwelling. But sometimes a child will peer through the gap before it's closed, and I feel their wonder like a vibration through the stone.

I know their dead in ways they never could. The Skyclimbers inter their elders in stone crypts, believing they return to the mountain's heart. They do, in a sense - I claim them slowly, dissolving their bones into my flow, carrying their calcium to build new stone elsewhere. Their ancestors quite literally become the mountain, deposited as minerals in chambers far below, becoming part of formations that will outlast their civilization by eons.

In the deepest places where I pool, strange blind fish swim - pale ghosts that have never known sunlight, their ancestors carried down when the peaks were lower and the valleys were seas. They share my darkness, these ancient swimmers, navigating by pressure and vibration in water so pure it barely conducts sound.

My temperature never varies, holding steady at the mountain's core heat. When I emerge at springs, I steam in the cold air, creating microclimates where different plants grow. The Skyclimbers harvest these for medicine, not knowing they thrive on minerals I've collected from miles below, from rocks their tools could never reach.

Sometimes I dream of my eventual escape - for I will escape, as all water does. Whether in a thousand years or a million, erosion will lower these peaks until I flow freely to the sea. Or perhaps the earth will shift, and I'll burst forth in geysers and hot springs, revealing the mountain's hidden fire. The Skyclimbers' great-great-grandchildren might build new settlements around my warmth, telling stories of when the mountain's tears grew hot with emotion.

But for now, I continue my patient work: dissolving, depositing, creating spaces in the dark that echo with the slow drip of centuries. I am the mountain's circulation, its secret life, moving through stone as blood moves through veins, keeping ancient processes alive while the mist settles and the Skyclimbers go about their daily lives, unaware of the vast, slow story being written in the darkness beneath their feet.

I am time made liquid, memory dissolved in mineral truth, the patient artist whose galleries will outlast any civilization that clings to these ancient slopes. And I am content in my darkness, knowing that every drop of me carries stories older than any the Skyclimbers could ever tell.
