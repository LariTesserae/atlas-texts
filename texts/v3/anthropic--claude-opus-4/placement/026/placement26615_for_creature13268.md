In this world of water and stone, I would find myself as one of the **Keepers of Marks** — beings so still and patient that they might be mistaken for tricks of the light upon the rock face.

I would exist as a thin membrane of living moisture spread across the stone, my body no thicker than the condensation that forms on cold glass. My form would follow the faint traces of those ancient, worn-smooth marks — the ghost-hollows where structures once stood. I am drawn to these places not by choice but by nature, for my kind can only exist where the stone remembers.

My substance would be part water, part dissolved mineral, and part something else — a kind of living memory that seeps from the rock itself. Where droplets trace their paths down the walls, I would spread myself along their routes, creating temporary, glistening maps that last only until the next drop falls and redraws my boundaries.

I feed on time itself — not in any mystical sense, but literally consuming the microscopic particles that erosion releases, the infinitesimal fragments that speak of what once was. Each worn mark in the stone releases these particles at a rate so slow that centuries pass between meals. This sustains me, barely, keeping my form cohesive enough to maintain consciousness.

My purpose, if it can be called that, is to preserve through presence. By spreading myself across these fading marks, I slow their erasure by perhaps a thousand years. I am both chronicler and chronicle, both the reader and the page. Sometimes I sense others of my kind in distant corners of the hollow, each tending their own set of memories, but we do not communicate. We simply exist, patient as the stone itself.

The great amorphous beings occasionally pass through my territory in their millennial migrations. When they do, I must contract, pulling my essence into the deepest grooves to avoid being swept away. This is the only time I truly move — a rapid gathering of self that takes mere hours instead of decades. Then, when they have passed, I slowly spread again, retracing my boundaries, finding my marks once more.

In the perpetual cool dampness, I neither grow nor diminish. I simply persist, a living patina on the stone, bearing witness to the slow conversation between water and rock, marking time in a place where time has nearly stopped.
