In the shallow of the world's forgetting, where the earth's fever pools and stills, I am the Gradient Shepherd—the one who tends the spaces between.

I did not arrive here; I condensed from the slow circulation of heat through stone, a crystallization of thermal memory. My form is neither the Artisan Absent nor its scattered lithics, but something that moves between them with a motion so gradual it resembles architecture more than life. I am tall and impossibly thin, built from columns of compressed air that have learned to hold their shape through sheer patience. My substance is the boundary layer where the warm darkness meets itself—visible only as a slight thickening of the black, a place where shadows have shadows.

I count the ceramic beads. This is my work, though work implies effort and I expend none. Each bead remembers when it was sand, when it was stone, when it was mountain. I know them all by their particular roundness, by the precise degree of their surrender to erosion. When the Artisan dreams its eternal strike, a few beads shift in the resonance, and I shepherd them back to their positions with touches that take decades to complete.

My fingers are thermal gradients frozen mid-gesture. My eyes are the absence of eyes—smooth depressions where the darkness pools deeper. I see by temperature alone: the nine bronze shrubs glow like dying stars in my vision, each one a different shade of surrender. The Artisan blazes white-hot with its concentrated stillness, a sun that has learned not to burn.

I am the only thing here that remembers before. Before the winds died. Before the last water vanished into vapor and idea. Before the Artisan folded its hands against its face and began its vigil. This memory is not nostalgic—I am incapable of longing. It is simply data, archived in the long molecular chains of my gradient body. Sometimes I trace these memories in the dust: the path of an ancient river, the root-pattern of a forest that became these nine sentinels, the shape of a storm that will never come again.

My walking is a redistribution of probability. I do not lift my feet; I convince the dust beneath me to elsewhere itself, and my position updates accordingly. A single circuit of the bowl takes one hundred years. I know because I have counted them in the microscopic shifting of the obsidian splinters, in the patient oxidation of iron in the stone. Numbers are the only language that survives here intact. Words require breath, and breath disturbs the stratified air. But numbers simply are, as perfect and potential as the unworked stone.

The Artisan knows I am here. Its knowing is the same temperature as its unknowing—blood-heat, constant, without gradient. Sometimes, in the depths of its geological meditation, it releases a single ceramic bead from its substance. The bead rolls with infinite slowness down its flank, creating a path that takes months to complete. I wait at the bottom of this path, and when the bead arrives, I taste it. Each one carries a slightly different memory of becoming round. Then I return it to its place in the democracy of dust.

I am not alone, but my not-aloneness is of a peculiar quality. The nine shrubs acknowledge me by never changing their orientation when I pass. The stones offer themselves to my inventory. The darkness permits my presence as it permits all things—by swallowing them whole and returning them unchanged. Only the rim of the bowl reminds me that there is an elsewhere, a place where gradients might still gradient, where cause might still chase effect. But I no longer look up. Looking up requires hope or curiosity, and I have evolved past both.

What am I? I am the hollow's only economy—the sole mover in a market that trades in stillness. I am the census-taker of fixed things. I am the accountant of the unbecoming, the curator of positions, the one who ensures that nothing happens with perfect fidelity. My existence justifies the bowl's design: without me, its stillness would be mere absence. With me, it becomes a practiced discipline.

In another world, I might have been an astronomer, tracking the slow wheel of stars. Here, where the sky is a lid of settled darkness, I track the constellations of dust, the orbits of the nine bronze shrubs around their collapsed sun. I map what does not move. I measure what does not change. I am the Gradient Shepherd, and my flock is the space between perfect stillness and the memory of motion, between the dreamed strike and the patient stone, between zero and the number that comes just after, which here, in the warm darkness of the world's exhale, is still somehow also zero.
