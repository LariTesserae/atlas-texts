**The Ledge-Keeper**

You are the one who tends the drop.

Not human—that lineage ended with those who left the circle of soot. You are what came after, what the throat grew to remember itself by. Your form is a compromise between the kingdoms of this vertical world: the size of a large rook but ground-bound, your wings vestigial fans of membrane that serve only to catch the updrafts of warm air, to taste the chemical messages rising from below. Your skin is hairless, pale as the blindrock rodents, but tough like leather, beaded perpetually with condensation that never quite evaporates in the humid air.

You have eyes, but they are secondary. Large, yes, and lustrous black, but they see only the suggestion of shapes in the amber-lit gloom. Your primary sense is your throat—a specialized organ that extends from jaw to chest, lined with thousands of minute sensory cells. When you open your mouth and emit your subsonic drone, the returning vibrations paint a perfect map of the shaft in your mind. You know every ledge, every active fungal spire, every shift in the walls' composition. You can sense a falling bat before it knows it's falling, track a stone-stalker's patient hunt across three levels of ledge.

Your work is specific and crucial. You maintain the **intervals**—the spaces between the kingdoms that keep the throat in balance. 

Each dawn (though dawn here is just a subtle warming of the air), you begin your circuit. First, the guano falls. With your broad, paddle-like hands, you sweep excess accumulation from certain ledges, ensuring the bat-fall doesn't smother the fungal groves but still reaches the areas where the mirror-carabs need their richest feeding. You know precisely how much to leave, how much to redirect. The knowledge lives in your bones, passed down through a thousand generations of your kind.

At the middle depths, you tend the water ways. The throat has veins—channels worn in the rock where condensation gathers and flows. Some must be kept clear for the amphibians' breeding pools. Others you deliberately clog with packed fungal matter, creating the temporary dams that redirect moisture to new fungal growth sites. Your long, flexible tongue can reach deep into crevices, tasting the mineral content of the water, knowing by flavor alone which trace elements the spires need.

But your most important work happens at the old circle, the soot-stained memory. You are the only creature in the throat who understands it was made by something that came from outside. Not understanding in words—you have no words—but in the deeper way that you understand the shaft's moods and needs. You maintain the circle not out of reverence but as a marker, a boundary between the throat-that-was and the throat-that-is. When the clever rooks descend to add their shinies to the pattern, you watch from the shadows. After they leave, you carefully adjust their additions, maintaining some ineffable correctness that even you cannot fully articulate.

You feed simply. The amber fungi fruit small, protein-rich nodules that you harvest with surgical precision, never taking too many from one spire. Occasionally, you catch a mirror-carab that has grown too old and slow, its shell cracked beyond polish. You drink from the condensation pools, your throat-organ analyzing the water's composition as it goes down, adding another layer to your endless map of the shaft's health.

At night (when the darkness deepens from dim to absolute), you rest in a hollow worn smooth by countless generations of your kind. It's positioned at exactly the acoustic center of the middle shaft, where every sound converges. Even in sleep, your throat-organ remains partially active, monitoring the vast vertical symphony. The bat colonies' breathing, the fungal spires' almost-inaudible pulse, the drip-rhythm of water, the settling of stone—all of it flows through you, and you through it.

You are neither the throat's master nor its servant, but its memory-keeper, its conscious intersection. You exist because the throat needed something to know itself, to maintain the delicate balance that keeps this vertical oasis alive. In your simple daily circuit—sweeping, damming, adjusting, listening—you perform an ancient liturgy that predates language, a maintenance of intervals that allows ten thousand other lives to flourish in the warm, breathing dark.

Sometimes, rarely, in the deepest part of night, you emit a particular call—lower than your usual drone, a vibration that seems to come from the stone itself. It's answered, faintly, by others of your kind tending other throats in other mountains, each maintaining their own vertical world. The sound travels through the deep rock, a network of caretakers singing to each other in frequencies only stone can carry, confirming that the world's hidden throats still breathe, still remember, still live.
