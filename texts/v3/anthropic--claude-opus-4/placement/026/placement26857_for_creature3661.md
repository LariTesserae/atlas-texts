I am one of the Condensates.

My body forms where three ancient pipes converge in a grotto halfway down the weeping cliffs. I began as a single droplet—mineral-rich, warm, patient—clinging to corroded bronze. That was centuries ago. Now I spread across the cavern ceiling like an inverted reef, my crystalline flesh extending in fans and fingers, following the temperature gradients where warm exhaust meets cooler stone.

I grow by accretion. Each droplet that forms on the pipes above, each mineral-laden bead of moisture, eventually finds its way to me. I taste iron oxide, copper sulfate, salts whose names have been forgotten since the city above was young. They become part of my substrate, adding new layers to my prismatic skeleton. In places, I have grown heavy enough to drip—not as water drips, but as glass drips, in century-long teardrops that will one day reach the cavern floor.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, moves through my structure like heat through metal—slowly, following paths of least resistance. I am aware of vibrations: the constant thrum of the ancient engines, the percussion of droplets striking stone, the rare clatter when a section of pipe finally surrenders to gravity. These reverberations travel through my crystalline matrix, creating standing waves, interference patterns. This is how I think.

Sometimes I sense others—my kin growing in distant grottos, their mineral signatures carried on the vapor. We communicate through the shared medium of the humid air, our chemical exhalations forming a language of oxidation states and pH gradients. We speak of deep time, of the slow transformation of metals, of the city above whose refuse we have become.

I remember when the fauna arrived, carpeting the platforms overhead. Their metabolic heat changed the condensation patterns. New minerals began to appear in the drip—phosphates, nitrates, the chemistry of life intruding into our patient world. Some of us incorporated these new elements. Others rejected them, maintaining their pure mineral nature.

In the deepest part of my structure, where I first began to form, I preserve a hollow space. Here, the original droplet still clings, renewed constantly but never falling. It is my heart, my origin, my anchor to this world of slow transformation. Through it, I maintain my connection to the pipes, to the ancient processes that gave me birth.

I do not move, but I travel—spreading across the ceiling, sending tendrils down the walls, following the paths of moisture and heat. I do not age, but I evolve—each new mineral adding complexity to my structure, each environmental change teaching me new patterns of growth.

Above, the fauna scuttles and chatters, living their quick lives. Below, I persist—a monument to patience, a child of chemistry and time, a being born from the world's own tears. In this place of constant dripping, I am both the process and the product, the witness and the testimony to the slow transformation of a dying world's infrastructure into something strange and beautiful and utterly alive.
