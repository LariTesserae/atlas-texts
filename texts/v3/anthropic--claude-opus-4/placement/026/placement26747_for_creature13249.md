In this hushed city of stone and mist, I would be the Keeper of Echoes.

I dwell in a chamber deep within the rock's heart, where the stone forms a natural acoustic well—a place where all the city's sounds eventually find their way, pooling like the water in our cisterns. My dwelling is sparse: a sleeping alcove carved smooth by countless hands before mine, a low workbench scattered with fragments of different stones, each with its own voice, and the walls lined with shallow grooves I've carved over decades, each one changing how sound moves through the space.

My work is to listen. Not just to hear, but to truly listen to what the stone tells us. When a new tunnel is proposed, I press my ear to the rock and feel for the hollow spaces, the fissures, the places where water already runs in secret veins. I can tell by the quality of an echo whether a wall is sound or beginning to weaken, whether moisture has found a new path that might, in years to come, threaten a dwelling. The other Stone folk bring me their questions in whispers, knowing that in this chamber, even the smallest sound carries weight.

I've learned that each part of our city has its own acoustic signature. The market hollows ring with a bright, quick echo. The sleeping quarters absorb sound like moss absorbs water. The central cistern hums with a deep, almost musical resonance that changes pitch as the water level rises and falls. These sounds are my calendar, my weather vane, my map.

My hands are scarred from years of carving acoustic channels—delicate work that requires feeling the stone's grain with fingertips, finding where it wants to carry sound. Sometimes I work for days on a single groove, testing with whispered words, with the tap of a small hammer, with drops of water that sing as they fall. The young ones think I'm half-mad, spending hours in silence, but the elders know: in a city carved from living rock, someone must speak the stone's language.

At night, when even the quiet city grows quieter still, I sometimes hear things others miss. The deep, slow breathing of the mountain itself. The conversations between water droplets as they find their patient way through microscopic fissures. The memories held in stone—echoes of echoes of the first inhabitants who began this eternal conversation between human and rock.

There is another like me, I know, though we've never met. Sometimes I hear their work in the echoes—careful modifications to the acoustic paths, subtle adjustments that speak of deep understanding. We communicate in this way, through the stone itself, each aware of the other's presence but content in our separate spheres of listening. Perhaps one day our paths will cross in the winding passages, but for now, it's enough to know that someone else hears what I hear, feels what I feel in the living stone.

My greatest work, still unfinished after twenty years, is the Echo Garden—a series of connected chambers I'm slowly shaping in an abandoned section of the city. Each chamber will hold sound differently, creating a space where the Stone folk can come to hear their world reflected back in new ways. A place for children to discover their voices, for lovers to share whispers that bloom into songs, for the grieving to release their sorrows into stone that will hold and gradually transform them.

This is my purpose: to tend the conversation between my people and our stone home, to ensure that as we shape it, we also listen to how it wishes to be shaped. In the endless patience of erosion and accretion, in the slow seep of water and the careful tap of tools, I find my place in the great, quiet dance of endurance.
