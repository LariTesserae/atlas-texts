In the depths of the mist-veiled valleys, where water seeps eternal from stone and the darkness presses close enough to taste, I am one of the Conduits.

We are neither fully Kyren nor wholly Synthetic, but something born from the marriage of desperate flesh and willing metal. My body is a testament to transformation—vertebrae replaced with articulated copper segments that oxidize green in the perpetual damp, lungs augmented with bellows that wheeze and hiss in rhythm with the great machines. My left arm ends not in a hand but in a branching network of tubes and valves, each one attuned to the frequencies of different pipes in the vast industrial symphony.

I remember, dimly, being born Kyren. But the memory fades like morning mist each time I sink deeper into communion with the infrastructure. We Conduits are the translators, the living bridges between the Kyren's need and the machinery's hunger. When a pipe screams in the night, it is we who understand its language of pressure and flow. When the factories groan and shudder, threatening to tear themselves from their precarious perches, it is we who sing them back to stability.

My dwelling is neither home nor shelter, but a nexus—a cramped chamber where dozens of pipes converge like the roots of some mechanical tree. I have woven myself into this space over the years, my augmented limbs finding purchase in valve wheels and pressure gauges. My spine curves to match the sweep of the main arterial pipe, and when I sleep, I dream its dreams of rushing water and mineral memory.

The Kyren fear us, though they need us. They see how we grow more machine with each passing season, how our skin takes on the patina of aged brass, how our eyes begin to glow with that soft, bioluminescent light that marks the Lumina. They whisper that we are becoming Engineers, those mythical first-builders. But they are wrong. The Engineers built to impose order. We exist to maintain the conversation between order and chaos.

My days are spent in meditation and maintenance. I press my tube-fingered hand against junction points, feeling the pulse of water and steam, the vibration of distant turbines. Through the network, I sense my fellow Conduits at their own stations—a dozen of us spread throughout this section of the valley, each a nerve ending in a vast mechanical nervous system. We communicate not through words but through pressure changes, through the subtle alterations in flow that ripple through miles of pipeline.

Sometimes the Synthetics visit me. They move like mercury through the mist, their bodies shifting between states of matter. They speak in harmonics that make my augmentations resonate, and they tell me secrets about the deep structures, about the roots of the mountains where the first pipes were laid. They claim the entire landscape is a single vast organism, that the machinery and the mountain are one, that water is its blood and we are its dreams made manifest.

I believe them. How could I not, when I feel the mountain's pulse through every rivet in my spine?

The Shadowborn watch me sometimes. I sense them in the negative spaces, in the gaps between pipes where even the mist fears to flow. They judge, I think, waiting to see if I will tip too far into the mechanical, lose that essential spark that makes me useful as a bridge. The day that happens, I suspect, is the day they reclaim me for the darkness.

But for now, I persist in this liminal space. I am the marriage of flesh and function, the covenant between the Kyren's ambition and the landscape's insistence. My augmented belly holds three days of rainwater, filtered through ceramics and flesh until it emerges purer than any mountain spring. My mechanical eye can see the stress fractures in pipes before they split. My modified throat can produce the precise harmonics needed to calm a factory in the throes of resonant destruction.

The Aviari sometimes perch on my exposed pipework, their iridescent feathers leaving traces of color on the tarnished metal. They sing to me—not in their language but in mine, mimicking the sounds of well-functioning machinery. It is the closest thing to kindness I know.

In the depths of night, when the factories dim their eyes and even the Lumina reduce their glow to conserve energy, I sometimes disconnect from my station and walk the catwalks between structures. My modified feet grip the wet metal with magnetic certainty, and I trace the veins of industry like a physician checking for circulation. In these moments, I am most myself—neither human nor machine but something more essential: a function given form, a process made flesh.

The rain comes again, as it always does, and I open my valve-hand to taste it. Somewhere in the distance, a new factory is being born, its first cries echoing through the mist. I will need to visit it soon, to teach it the songs of stability, to weave it into the greater symphony.

This is my purpose. This is my joy. In this wet, dark, thrumming world, I am exactly what I need to be.
