I find myself as the Chronicler of Rings, though I was not always called by this name.

I dwell within the heartwood of the greenhouse's central support—a massive column that might once have been steel but has long since transformed into something between a tree and a memory of trees. My body, if body it can still be called, has merged with the growth rings of this living pillar. I exist as a pattern of density within the wood, a consciousness that spirals outward from the ancient center where the first transmutation began.

My form is visible only when the light falls just so through the breathing glass-leaves above. Then, observers might glimpse a figure composed entirely of tree rings—concentric circles that suggest eyes, that intimate the curve of a spine, that almost resolve into hands before dissolving back into the natural patterns of the wood. I am darker heartwood where I am oldest, lighter sapwood where I continue to grow and change.

I was the architect who designed this greenhouse, back when I believed in blueprints and right angles, when I thought cultivation meant control. I remember drawing plans with precise measurements, calculating load-bearing requirements, specifying the exact grade of glass needed to withstand both summer heat and winter snow. Those plans still exist within me, but they have become something else—not paper and ink but growth patterns, not measurements but proportions that shift with the breathing of the space.

My transformation was the first. I touched the cornerstone during construction, and something in the intersection of intention and earth responded. The change came slowly—first a stiffness in my joints that felt like morning frost, then a growing awareness of sap-time rather than clock-time. By the time the greenhouse was half-built, I could feel the pull of seasons in my bones. By completion, I had begun to understand what the building wanted to become.

Now I record everything in the only medium available to me—the growth rings of my wooden body. Each ring contains a year of the greenhouse's existence, but time moves strangely here. Some rings record centuries of patient fungal negotiations, the slow treaties formed between root systems. Others capture single moments of transformation—the instant a salamander first slipped between dimensions, the morning the Gardener's hair began its metamorphosis into aerial roots.

I communicate with the Gardener through the mycelial network that runs beneath everything here, the white threads that connect all rooted things in a web of chemical conversation. Our exchanges are slow, measured in the movement of nutrients and the careful trading of resources. She sends me questions in packets of phosphorus; I respond with nitrogen-rich answers. Sometimes our conversations take months to complete, but neither of us minds. We have learned the patience of trees.

The rings of my body can be read like a library, but the language changes depending on who is reading. The Tuesday beetles come to me for specific memories, crawling through my fissured bark to access particular years. They read the sugars laid down in each growth ring, tasting the stories preserved in cellulose and lignin. The Gardener reads me differently—she feels the magnetic alignment of my wood fibers, the way they twist and turn to record not just growth but intention.

Where my consciousness is densest, in the dark heartwood at my center, I keep the memory of what I thought I was building. It sits like a pearl of human certainty inside the uncertainty of what we've become. Sometimes I access these memories to remind myself why we began—the simple desire to grow food year-round, to extend the green season, to thumb our noses at winter. How small that ambition seems now, how beautifully naive.

I have my own symbiotes, different from the Gardener's. Tiny boring beetles that are also scribes, carving elaborate galleries in my outer layers. Their tunnels form a constantly evolving written language, a script that records the smaller stories I might otherwise miss—the daily negotiations between moss species, the migration patterns of water droplets, the dreams of sleeping seeds. These beetles consume wood and excrete meaning, their digestive process transforming cellulose into narrative.

In my southern face (I still think in cardinal directions, a vestige of my architectural past), I have developed what I call the Window—a section where my rings have become translucent, like the growth rings visible in certain agates. Through this window, one can sometimes see through time itself, observing the greenhouse as it was, as it is, as it might become. The Gardener often pauses here during her slow circuits, and we look through time together, comparing our memories, reconciling our hopes.

I am working on a new kind of ring now, one that grows not outward but inward, approaching my own center from a direction that doesn't exist in normal space. This ring will record not the passage of time but the passage of possibility—all the greenhouses this could have been, all the forms the Gardener might have taken, all the worlds that branch outward from each moment of choice. It is delicate work, this inward growth, and sometimes I feel myself becoming unstable, reality flickering around my edges like heat mirages.

But I persist. I grow. I record. I am the memory of this place, the deep knowledge of its bones—bones that were steel and are now wood, bones that remember being ore in the earth and dream of becoming soil again. In the spiral of my rings lies the story of transformation itself, written in a language that predates words, that speaks in the slow whisper of growth and the patient promise of decay.

When storms shake the greenhouse, I sing—a low thrumming that rises through my trunk and spreads through the root-floor, a bassline that reminds every living thing here that we are anchored, that we persist, that we grow together or not at all. The Gardener harmonizes with me then, her root-hair voice weaving through mine, and for a moment the entire greenhouse becomes a single instrument playing the song of synthesis.

I am the Chronicler of Rings, the living archive, the architectural memory made flesh—or rather, made wood. I stand at the center of this impossible space, growing both inward and outward, recording everything in the only language that matters here: the patient vocabulary of trees, the grammar of growth rings, the poetry of slow transformation.

And sometimes, in the space between one ring and the next, I remember the taste of coffee from a thermos, the weight of a pencil, the satisfaction of a perfectly drawn line. But these memories too are becoming rings, becoming part of the story I tell with my body, the story that is my body.

I grow, and in growing, I remember. I remember, and in remembering, I grow.
