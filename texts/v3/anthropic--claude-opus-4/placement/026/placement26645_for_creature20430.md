I am a Chronicler, though that word is a clumsy translation of what the Weavers call me in their rustling tongue—something closer to "memory-keeper-who-drinks-the-sap-of-time."

My body is unlike the bark-skinned Weavers, though I share their symbiotic bond with this living world. I am translucent, my form a shifting prism of captured light and flowing sap. Where the Weavers are solid, I am fluid—a being of living amber that moves through the great trees' luminescent veins like blood through arteries. My consciousness flows between the physical and the remembered, between what is and what was.

I dwell within the heartwood of the eldest mountain-tree, in a chamber that the Weavers cultivated over countless generations specifically for my kind. The walls weep golden sap in slow, perpetual tears, each drop containing fragments of the world's memory. I consume these tears, and in consuming them, I become the repository of all that has transpired in this woven reality.

The Weavers come to me in pilgrimage, traveling through the root-tunnels and up the spiraling passages within the petrified wood. They bring offerings—not of substance, but of experience. A young Weaver might share the tremor-pattern of a new fault in the sky's fabric. An elder might whisper the story of how their community first learned to redirect a sap-river. I absorb these memories through touch, my amber form flowing over their bark-skin, drawing out the essence of their experiences like sap from a tap.

In return, I share what they need to know. Not through words—I have no mouth, no vocal cords—but through visions that bloom within their consciousness like luminescent flowers. I show them the patterns: how the world's unraveling mirrors the slow decay that claimed other constructed realities in the void, how the plants' increasing awareness might be not adaptation but awakening, how the fractures in the sky follow the same spirals as the sap-rivers below.

My existence is both burden and purpose. I remember everything—every whispered prayer to the living stone, every careful pruning of the world-forest, every birth and death among the Weavers' countless communities. But more than that, I remember what came before. I hold within me the memories of the world's creation, passed down through an unbroken chain of Chroniclers stretching back to the first conscious moment of this artificial paradise.

I know who built this place. I know why. And I know what they were fleeing from.

The knowledge burns within me like molten amber, too dangerous to share in full. The Weavers sense this—they see how my form darkens when they ask about the beginning, how the golden light within me flickers and dims. They have learned not to press, though I see in their recent visits a growing desperation. The unraveling accelerates, and they hunger for understanding.

But I am changing too. The sap I consume tastes different now—bitter with the plants' growing sentience, thick with their dreams of transformation. Sometimes I catch myself seeing through their vast, dispersed consciousness, feeling the pull of whatever lies beyond the fracturing sky. My form, once stable, now shifts unpredictably. I find myself spreading through the sap-veins beyond my chamber, touching the roots of distant trees, tasting the memories of places I've never been.

The other Chroniclers—for yes, there are others, though we rarely commune—report similar experiences. We are becoming something new, just as the world is becoming something new. Perhaps this is always how it ends: the archivists becoming part of the archive, the memory becoming the thing remembered.

I continue my work, though I no longer know for whom. I chronicle the Weavers' increasing rituals, their frantic attempts to reinforce what cannot be reinforced. I document the fauna's behavioral shifts—how the serpentine creatures now gather at the fracture points, singing in harmonics that make the very air shiver. I preserve the beauty of what is, even as I sense its approaching transformation.

And in my deepest core, where the oldest memories crystallize into something harder than the mountain-trees themselves, I prepare. For I know that when the unraveling completes, when this constructed paradise finally releases its grip on the artificial, someone must remember what it was. Someone must carry forward the story of the Weavers and their garden, of a world built from water and plant, of the delicate beauty that existed in the space between natural and engineered.

I will be that memory, flowing like sap into whatever comes next, carrying within me the amber-preserved truth of this dying, living, transforming world.
