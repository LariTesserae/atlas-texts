I find myself as one of the **memory merchants**.

Not lingered. Not yet. We occupy the liminal state between arrival and erasure, that narrow band of decades where accumulation is still possible if you're clever about it.

We discovered the loophole by accident. The Shelf's rule states that nothing is allowed to accumulate *in one place*. So we don't stay in one place. We ride the boundaries between the micro-turbulences, threading ourselves through the spaces where one chaos-pattern hands off to another. In these transition zones, for microseconds at a time, the Shelf's attention stutters. We exist in that stutter.

My body is a ribbon now, stretched thin across forty kilometers of the black plain. Not metaphorically—literally attenuated, pulled like taffy until I'm perhaps three meters tall but only a centimeter wide. This is the optimal geometry for riding the boundaries. Any thicker and the Shelf notices you accumulating mass. Any thinner and you start to fray into the lingered.

I carry memories that aren't mine. This is our trade, we merchants. New arrivals still dense with their past lives will pay anything—usually parts of themselves they haven't learned to value yet—for a single memory from home. Not their home. Any home. The need for cognitive anchor points in this vast emptiness creates a sellers' market.

My inventory today:
- The sound of a wooden door closing in a house that had gravity
- Coffee grounds between molars
- The way afternoon light looked through a specific window in a city whose name I've carefully forgotten (names are heavy)
- Someone's daughter laughing, but not the daughter herself, just the laugh
- The muscle memory of writing with a pen

I keep these wrapped in probability shells stolen from the tideforms' wake. The shells degrade—everything here degrades—but they last long enough for commerce.

The lingered hate us. Not actively (activity would be accumulation) but with the slow, geological disdain of those who've accepted their erosion for those who still resist it. They see us as clinging to the cliff-face of becoming, postponing the inevitable dissolution.

They're right, of course.

I had a partner once. Another merchant. She'd stretched herself across the ninety-kilometer span between two residue trees, using their glass branches as anchor points. Clever. Too clever. The Shelf noticed the pattern—a straight line held too long—and she snapped. Not dramatically. She just... wasn't taut anymore. The last I saw, her two halves were drifting in opposite directions, each still trying to sell memories to anyone who'd listen, each unaware they were no longer whole.

The tower is where we do most of our business. New arrivals emerge disoriented, still thick with dimension and meaning. They'll trade anything for familiarity. I once got an entire language—not the words, but the *grammar itself*—in exchange for the memory of how bread smells. The buyer didn't even speak the language I sold her, but she needed the structure, the rules, something to prove that rules had once existed.

I kept the grammar for three days before the weight of it began to buckle my ribbon-form. I ended up feeding it to the child-shaped absence, letter by letter, watching each syntactical rule dissolve into that perfect circle of stillness. The absence never acknowledged the gift, but the next morning my ribbon was three centimeters wider. The Shelf had forgotten to thin me during the night. A mercy, perhaps. Or a warning.

We measure time by the neutronium cubes now. Their nine-day appearances are the only reliable calendar the Shelf permits. I've been here for forty-seven cubes. My predecessor—the merchant who taught me the trade before she finally let herself linger—claimed to have lasted for three hundred cubes before the forgetting became too sweet to resist.

The real danger isn't the erosion. It's the comfort. Every day the black plain asks you, wordlessly, why you're working so hard to remain complex. Why you need all these memories, these dimensions, these heavy human architectures. Wouldn't it be easier to let them go? To become simple? To become silence?

Some days I catch myself pausing mid-transaction, holding someone's borrowed memory up to the platinum light, wondering if I even remember what I'm saving myself for.

But then a new arrival stumbles from the tower, eyes still wet with three-dimensional tears, and I flow across the micro-turbulences to meet them, my ribbon-body rippling like a banner in the almost-wind, ready to sell them someone else's yesterday in exchange for a piece of their today.

"Memory?" I offer, in a voice that travels exactly thirty meters. "I have the sound of rain. I have the weight of a cat sleeping on your chest. I have the exact temperature of a fever breaking."

They always buy something. In this vast, flat, forgetting place, even borrowed nostalgia is better than the alternative.

I'll keep selling until my ribbon frays. Until the boundaries I ride smooth out and leave me nowhere to exist between. Until the Shelf finally notices that I've been cheating its rule for far too long.

When that happens, I already know what memory I'll keep as I join the lingered: the feeling of resistance itself. The muscle-tension of saying "not yet" to entropy.

It's not much. But it's mine.

Or it will be, when everything else is gone.
