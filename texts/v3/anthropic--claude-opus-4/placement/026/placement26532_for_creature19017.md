In the southern reaches of the High Cascades, where the living substrate grows thinnest and the nascent geology pushes through in jagged, unfinished ridges, I would be one of the **Vein Readers** – a solitary prospector of the boundary lands where mineral and vegetable kingdoms blur.

My form would be something between the Cultivators and the stone itself. Where their skin carries chlorophyll's green tinge, mine would be mottled with mineral deposits – veins of copper and iron oxide creating patterns across my flesh like living geological maps. My bones would be denser than theirs, calcified with the same minerals I seek, making me heavier, more grounded, less suited to climbing the vertical gardens but perfectly adapted to reading the slow-forming stone.

I would spend my days at the interface, where the oozing substrate meets true rock, where the great root systems of the colossal flora probe blindly into mineral seams. My work would be patient observation – watching how the plants react to different mineral compositions, noting where their roots avoid certain metals, where they cluster around others. This knowledge I would translate through my tools: bio-mechanical sensors grafted from fungal networks and primitive mineral detectors powered by the bioelectric fields of specially cultivated moss.

My dwelling would be neither fully of the plant world nor of stone, but a hybrid thing – a natural cave half-sealed by directed growth, where crystalline formations merge with bioluminescent fungi in patterns I've spent decades cultivating. The industrial hum that pervades the Cascades would be muted here, replaced by the subtler sounds of mineral formation: the tick of expanding crystal, the whisper of water depositing microscopic layers of sediment, the almost-inaudible stress patterns of rock under pressure.

I would be keeper of the transition zones, documenting the slow conversation between the living world and the mineral one. My records – etched in metal-eating lichens on sheets of compressed plant fiber – would track the infinitesimal changes: how the great plants' root acids slowly dissolve certain stones, how their presence accelerates or retards geological processes, how the industrial machines of the Cultivators interact with the deep stone through vibration and heat.

The Cultivators would visit rarely, seeking my knowledge when they need to sink deep foundations for their living architecture or when their bio-machines require rare minerals. I would trade information for processed materials – refined fungal cultures, specially treated fibers, the occasional metal component their organic forges produce. But mostly I would work alone, my only constant companions the slow-growing hybrid organisms I've bred to help me in my work: moss that glows brighter near certain metals, lichens that change color based on acidity, small boring-worms that can taste the difference between sedimentary and igneous rock.

My life would be measured not in seasons but in geological time. I would watch the same stone face for decades, noting how the roots penetrate, how the moisture changes its composition, how the industrial vibrations from distant Cultivator settlements create microscopic fractures that become highways for new growth. I would be a bridge between timescales – translating the patient centuries of stone into the merely-slow decades of the great plants, and vice versa.

In the rare moments when fauna appears – a glimpse of something sliding through the mist, a shadow passing overhead – I would note their interactions with the boundary zones too. Do they nest in the caves? Do they consume certain minerals? Do their movements affect the formation patterns? Everything would be data in the great, slow experiment of a world still deciding what it wants to become.

The vestiges of geometric stone would hold special significance for me. Unlike the Cultivators who see them as sacred anchors, I would recognize them as evidence of the world's malleability – stone that was once shaped by intelligence, now being slowly reclaimed and transformed. I would study how these artificial stones weather differently than natural ones, how the plants interact with their surfaces, what they might tell us about the optimal balance between the built and the grown.

My purpose would be essential yet peripheral – a watcher at the edges, a reader of the deep patterns that underlie the more obvious life of the Cascades. Where the Cultivators shape the future through active cultivation, I would divine it through patient observation of the present's slow transformation into the past, archived in stone and root, in mineral vein and fungal thread, in the endless, quiet conversation between what grows and what endures.
