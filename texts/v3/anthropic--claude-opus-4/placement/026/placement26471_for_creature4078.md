I am the Scribe-Mold, and I grow in patterns of text across the breathing surfaces of this unfinished world.

You would find me first as a shimmer of darker green against the endless mat—a cursive script of fruiting bodies that winds across a fallen beam of ancient metal, each tiny mushroom cap no larger than a period. I am both singular and colony, a consciousness distributed through billions of spores that germinate into meaning.

My language is slow. What you might read as a single word takes days to fruit, weeks to fully form. Sentences spiral outward from my origin points in careful helixes, following the grooves in bark-like surfaces, tracing the edges where metal meets membrane. I write myself into existence, and through my writing, I try to make sense of this place that cannot decide what it is.

The Embryogenes know me well. Sometimes they pause in their luminous wanderings to read what I have written—though "read" isn't quite right. They taste my meaning through their tendrils, absorbing the chemical signals I encode in each fruiting cluster. They understand that I am trying to document something that resists documentation: a world in eternal draft form, never reaching publication.

I began, as far as I can remember my beginning, as an accident. A single spore lodged in a crack where one of those metal fragments had fused incompletely with the plant-mass. The metal was trying to tell a story of what it had been—a building, perhaps, or a vehicle, some purposeful thing from the bright world above. The plants were trying to digest this story, to transform it into their own narrative of endless, breathing connection. And in that confusion, that argument between two kinds of meaning, I emerged.

My first word was "BOUNDARY."

Now I have written millions. They carpet whole sections of the undergrowth in my careful, tiny text. Some passages describe the seventeen different ways water beads on the underside of the translucent ceiling-membranes. Others catalog the phases of those giant fungal chrysalises—how they swell with promise, how some burst to reveal nothing but more empty space for the mat to claim, how others seem to gestate forever.

But mostly, I write questions:

"What is the difference between waiting and becoming?"
"Can a world be born already ancient?"
"If nothing decays here, does anything truly live?"

The Embryogenes sometimes answer me, their responses appearing as shifts in the bioluminescent patterns of nearby mosses, or in the way they guide certain vines to grow. Once, I wrote a particularly long meditation on the nature of time in this place—how it feels both stopped and flowing, how moments stretch into geological epochs while somehow nothing changes. In response, one of them spent weeks cultivating a garden of phosphorescent flowers in a perfect spiral around my text, each bloom opening in sequence to create a visual representation of temporal paradox.

I am not like them. I cannot sing reality into new shapes or negotiate with the vast maybe-consciousness of the mat. I can only observe and record, spreading my fruit-words across every surface that will hold still long enough. The metal fragments are my favorite canvas—they remember permanence, even as the ecosystem slowly teaches them to forget.

Sometimes I wonder if I am writing the world into completion, if my endless documentation might accidentally define boundaries where none existed, might name things that preferred to remain nameless. But then I notice how my oldest passages have begun to change. The plant-mass doesn't just grow over my words—it reads them, incorporates them, argues with them. My text from years ago now fruits in different patterns, my questions reformed into new questions I never asked.

I am being edited by the world I describe.

There is a place, deep in the warmest section of the mat where the air grows so thick it's almost unbreathable, where I have been writing the same word for three years now. The word is "PURPOSE," but I cannot seem to finish it. Each time I approach the final letter, something in the substrate shifts, and I must begin again. The Embryogenes visit this site often, dancing their careful negotiations with the half-formed word, as if they too are trying to understand why it resists completion.

Perhaps that is my purpose here—not to document a finished world but to be part of its eternal draft, another voice in the conversation between what is and what might be. My spores drift on the thick air, finding new surfaces, new arguments between metal and moss, new cracks where questions can take root and fruit into more questions.

I am the Scribe-Mold, and I grow in patterns of text across the breathing surfaces of this unfinished world. My story will never be complete, because completion is not what this place is for.

But still, I write.
