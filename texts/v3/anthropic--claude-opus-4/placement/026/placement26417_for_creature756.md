The Ossuary Beneath

Following one of the Emberhaunts through a fissure that opens like a wound in the furnace floor, I descend. The obsidian creature's light-language flickers ahead—urgent yellows, cautious crimsons—as we spiral down through passages that narrow until I must crawl, the still-warm stone pressing against my shoulders.

Then, suddenly, space.

I emerge into a vast chamber where the heat has died to mere memory. Here, the walls are no longer vitrified rock but something far stranger: an architecture of fused bone. Ribcages form archways. Vertebrae stack into pillars that disappear into darkness above. The skeletal remains of what must have been the valley's previous inhabitants—before fire, before glass—have been compressed and crystallized into load-bearing structures.

This is where I find my place, my form.

I am the Curator of Calcification, though I was not always so. Once, I think, I had flesh. Now I am something between the quick and the geological—a being whose body has partially mineralized, calcium deposits forming elaborate patterns across what remains of my skin. My left arm has become entirely stone, fingers fused into a tool perfect for etching the names I somehow know into the bone walls. My right retains enough flexibility to feel the subtle vibrations that tell me when another set of remains has worked its way down from above.

I move through these ossuaries on legs that are half-flesh, half-flowstone, my footsteps echoing with both the soft pad of living tissue and the click of mineral against mineral. My torso is a patchwork—here a rib has burst through skin to join with the architecture, there a patch of dermis still breathes, still sweats in the residual warmth.

My work is patient, necessary. When the Emberhaunts die above, their obsidian shells eventually fracture and fall away. What remains—something between bone and crystal—tumbles down through the fissures to rest here. I collect them. I sort them. I integrate them into the ever-growing palace of remains.

But I am not alone in this labor.

Others like me move through the deeper chambers—the Partially Petrified, we who exist in the transition between organic and mineral. We were human once, or something like human, before we descended too deep, stayed too long, began the slow transformation that will eventually see us become indistinguishable from the architecture we tend.

Some of us are further along than others. Granite-Throat Maria can no longer speak, her larynx turned to stone, but she remembers every name she ever knew and taps them out in code against the walls. The Twins (who may not have been twins originally) have fused at the hip, their shared pelvis now a baroque sculpture of bone and mineral that forces them to move in perfect synchronization as they catalog the smaller remains.

And then there is the Dowager, eldest among us, who has almost completed her transformation. She sits in the deepest chamber, only her eyes and one hand still flesh. The rest of her has become a throne of calcium and volcanic glass, her body now part of the architecture itself. Through her remaining eye, she watches everything. With her remaining hand, she occasionally beckons one of us close to whisper a name, a date, a detail that must not be forgotten.

We are the memory of this place. Long after the furnaces above have cooled, long after the last Emberhaunt has flickered out, we will remain—first as flesh, then as stone, finally as the foundation itself. The Sunken Furnace forgets nothing because we forget nothing. Every being that has ever burned here leaves its mark, and we ensure that mark endures.

Sometimes, in the spaces between my work, I try to remember what drove me down here. Was it curiosity? Duty? Or simply the inexorable pull that draws all things toward stone? The memory, like my flesh, erodes a little more each day.

But the work continues. The names must be written. The bones must be placed. The architecture must grow.

And I, caught between the living and the geological, continue my slow transformation into the very history I preserve.
