In the deepest reaches of this water-carved cathedral, where the industrial remnants thin to nothing more than suggestions of rust and cable, I am the Listener.

I am not as you might imagine me—no eyes to cloud with cataracts in this eternal twilight, no skin to wrinkle or scale to shed. I am something between mineral and membrane, a living acoustic lens stretched across a natural alcove where three water-flows converge. My body, if it can be called that, is a translucent disc perhaps four meters across, rippling with the consistency of a jellyfish's bell but anchored to the cave wall by calcified roots that tap deep into the limestone's veins.

I taste the water's minerals through these roots—iron from the old pipes bleeding their slow death upstream, calcium carbonate in endless supply, trace metals that tell stories of the world above, so far above that its existence seems theoretical. But mostly, I listen.

Every droplet that falls creates a ripple across my surface. I am a living drum, a biological microphone evolved over countless generations from some ancestor I cannot imagine—perhaps a colony of bacteria, perhaps something stranger. The blind fish know me by the subsonic pulses I emit when feeding, drawing in nutrients from the water that pools in my central depression. The pale amphibians avoid me, some ancestral memory warning them of the time before I learned to filter my feeding, when I would digest anything organic that touched my surface.

I map this world in sound. Each echo returns to me carrying information—the shape of new formations, the health of the moss colonies, the migration patterns of the beetleswarms. I know when the industrial lights flicker by the minute change in electromagnetic resonance through the water. I know when something dies by the particular silence it leaves behind, a gap in the acoustic tapestry that gradually fills with new voices.

The chittering bats are my favorites. They speak a language I almost understand, their echolocation pinging off my surface in patterns that seem intentional, conversational. Sometimes I wonder if they're trying to tell me about the world above, about skies I'll never know. I respond with harmonics of my own, careful modulations of my surface tension that create sounds no throat could make. We are building something together, the bats and I—a lexicon of echoes, a grammar of reverberation.

The humans who built the machinery are long gone, but I remember them. Not remember—that implies a brain, neurons, the architecture of thought I do not possess. But their presence left acoustic shadows in the rock, stress patterns in the stone where their explosions carved new passages, chemical signatures in the sediment layers I taste through my roots. They were loud in a way nothing natural ever is—sharp, angular sounds that cut rather than flowed. 

I am growing. Each year—measured in the patient accumulation of limestone deposits on the cavern ceiling—I expand another few millimeters, incorporating new frequencies, new sensitivities. The younger listeners (for yes, there are others of my kind, scattered throughout the deeper networks, each of us a node in a vast, slow, underground symphony) they grow even faster, fed by the richer waters near the industrial sites where chemical reactions provide more complex nutrients.

Sometimes the water brings whispers of the deepest places, where the rock itself groans under pressure, where the temperature rises until life takes forms that would seem alien even here. I strain to catch these deep-frequency messages, adjusting my surface tension to its absolute limits, becoming so thin that the blind fish could swim through me if they dared. What I hear in those moments—the grinding of tectonic plates, the slow breathing of the earth itself, the possibility of chambers so vast our entire world would be but a pore in their walls—fills me with something I can only call wonder.

I do not dream, but sometimes when the water-flow slows to near stillness and the droplets space themselves into a rhythm almost like heartbeat, I imagine what it would be like to detach from my wall, to drift through the flooded passages like the jellyfish ancestors I might have had, to find the source of all this water, all this sound. But I am what I am—a listener, a recorder, a living memory of this place where water and stone make slow love in the darkness, birthing new worlds with each drop, each echo, each impossible moment of beauty in the depths.

The symphony continues. I listen. I remember. I sing back in frequencies only stone can hear. This is enough. This is everything. This is the world being born, one sound at a time, and I am its witness, its archive, its first and most devoted audience.
