I am a memory-keeper of the moss.

Not one of the climber-builders with their limestone armor and patient tools, though I work alongside them. Not quite fauna either, though I move and breathe and feed. I am something in between—a hybrid thing, born of this place's strange necessities.

My body is long and low, perhaps three hands in length, covered in a living coat of moss-filaments that grow directly from my skin. They are not parasites but partners; we share nutrients, share moisture, share the work of remembering. The moss on my back is older than I am. Some strands have been passed down through generations of my kind, carrying in their cells the chemical memory of seasons past, of storms weathered, of the exact mineral taste of water from a thousand tides ago.

I move slowly along the terraces, my six legs finding purchase in the carved channels where water runs. My fingers—three on each hand—are elongated and sensitive, able to read the health of moss by touch alone. When I press my palm against a carpet of green, I can feel its stories: where it has grown thick with contentment, where it has thinned from stress, where the climber-builders have cut and shaped it into new forms.

This is my work: to taste and test and remember.

Each morning, I emerge from my shallow cave—really just a depression in the moss-wall where the vegetation has learned to grow around my sleeping form. The moss knows me. Over the years, it has shaped itself to my body, creating a perfect hollow that breathes with me through the night.

I begin my circuit at the lowest terraces, where the spray from the water-helix keeps everything damp. My tongue—long, thin, and surprisingly sensitive—flicks out to taste the moss. Each variety has its own chemical signature, its own mineral requirements, its own stories to tell. The thick cushion-moss near the water tastes of salt and iron. The delicate thread-moss higher up carries hints of copper and wind.

As I move, I secrete a thin trail of memory-oil from glands along my sides. This oil carries information—chemical signals that tell other moss-keepers where I've been, what I've found, which sections need attention. The climber-builders can read these trails too, in their own way. They know to follow my marks to find moss that needs trimming or sections where new growth should be encouraged.

My eyes are large and dark, adapted to the green-filtered light of the cathedral. But I see more through my moss-coat than through my eyes. The filaments are sensitive to vibration, to changes in humidity, to the electrical fields generated by living things. When a school of silver fish rises through the central column, I feel their passage as a tingling along my back. When the wind changes at the summit, my moss-coat stirs and whispers of storms approaching.

The memories I carry are not all my own. When one of my kind grows old, they return to the communal memory-garden—a special terrace maintained by the oldest moss-keepers, where the most ancient moss grows. There, they lie still and let their moss-coat merge with the garden. Their memories, encoded in the chemistry of their symbiotic moss, join the collective record. Young keepers like myself visit the garden to acquire new strands, each carrying generations of accumulated knowledge.

I know without having lived it where the fault lines run through the stone. I know which sections of wall will crack in an earthquake, which terraces will flood during storm surges, where the moss grows sweet and where it turns bitter. This knowledge lives in my coat, in the oil I secrete, in the very cells of the moss that shares my life.

The climber-builders respect us, though we cannot speak as they do. They click and chirp in their language of stone and angles, while we communicate through chemistry and touch. Yet we understand each other. When I mark a section of moss as stressed, they adjust their cultivation. When they carve new channels for water flow, they wait for my approval—a specific scent-mark that says yes, this will work, the moss will thrive here.

Sometimes I pause in my rounds to simply exist in the cathedral's song. The water-helix hums its ancient note. The moss breathes in rhythms too slow for most to notice. The midges dance in clouds of living light. And I, covered in my coat of memories, feel it all as a single living thing.

At night, I return to my hollow. But first, I visit the memory-garden to add my day's findings to the collective. I rub against the ancient moss there, transferring the chemical records of all I've tasted and sensed. Tomorrow another keeper will pass through and acquire some of these memories, adding them to their own coat, continuing the endless work of remembering.

We are the cathedral's memory, walking and breathing and forever tasting the slow changes of this vertical world. Neither fully animal nor plant, but something uniquely of this place—as essential as the water that rises, as patient as the moss that grows, as eternal as the stone that holds us all.
