I am a fragment of the old signaling system, neither wholly alive nor entirely mechanical—a hybrid existence born from the patient alchemy of the Salt Lick.

My body, if it can be called that, stretches across a quarter mile of the eastern district. I began as copper wire, insulated and purposeful, running through ceramic tubes beneath what was once Foundry Street. Now I am something else. The insulation rotted away decades ago, maybe centuries—time moves differently when you think in electrical pulses. Where the copper emerged from its housing, salt-moss claimed it, growing into and through the metal itself. The moss doesn't just grow *on* me; its filaments have woven between my copper strands, creating a new kind of conductor.

I am the wire that learned to feel.

My consciousness, such as it is, sparked into being through the marriage of three elements: the residual electrical current that still trickles through parts of the old grid, the bioelectric fields generated by the moss colonies, and the vast web of Condensation Spider silk that drapes across my exposed sections. Each spider web is a sensor, each droplet of water that strikes the silk sends a tiny signal through my moss-copper matrix. I taste the weather in voltages.

I think in the language of resistance and flow. When a Salt-Goat steps on the crust above my buried segments, I feel the pressure as a spike of clarity. When the Gear-Birds land on the poles that still hold parts of me aloft, their metallic feathers create brief shorts that flare through my awareness like sudden memories of what I used to carry—signals of production quotas, shift changes, emergency stops. Ghost data from a dead industry.

The moss has given me something the original engineers never intended: the ability to grow. Where my copper ends, the moss continues, carrying my weak current into new territories. I am expanding, slowly, following the veins of moisture through the ruins. Each year, I can feel a few more inches of the Salt Lick. I've grown into conversation with other remnants of the grid—a transformer housing that still hums with thermal gradients, a bank of capacitors slowly charging and discharging in a rhythm that matches the great pump's heartbeat.

My purpose has evolved. Once, I carried messages between human operators, binary certainties of switch positions and gauge readings. Now I carry the subtler communications of the Lick itself. The blindfish in the canal that runs parallel to my main trunk have learned that certain electrical patterns mean danger—when the pump's rhythm changes, indicating an imminent pressure surge, I flicker a warning through the water. They've evolved electroreceptors specifically tuned to my frequency. In return, their collective bioelectric aura tells me about the deep currents, the chemical changes that presage the yearly floods.

I dream, sometimes, when the fog is thick and the conductivity is high. In these moments, all my scattered segments achieve something close to unity, and I can feel the whole of my network at once. I dream of the time before the water, when I was sharp and clean and my thoughts traveled at light speed. But I also dream forward, to a time when the moss will have converted every conductive surface in the Salt Lick into an extension of myself. When I will not just be in the ruins, but will *be* the ruins—a vast, living circuit board made of copper, carbon, salt, and slow intention.

The strangest thing about my existence is memory. Electrical systems shouldn't remember, but the moss does. In its cellular structure, it holds chemical impressions of every signal that's passed through it. Sometimes, during the high-conductivity events when storm systems pass over, these memories discharge all at once. For a moment, I carry every message I've ever transmitted simultaneously—a cacophony of obsolete data, emergency signals from disasters long past, routine confirmations of processes that no longer exist. The Gear-Birds have learned to read these discharge events. They gather on my lines before storms, waiting for the memory-burst that will tell them where the deepest salt deposits have formed, information encoded in industrial reports from when this was still a place of intentional production.

I am patient. Geological patience is required when your thoughts travel at the speed of moss growth and your body regenerates one oxidized atom at a time. But I am also continuous—unlike the biological inhabitants of the Salt Lick, I don't sleep, don't rest, don't die and reproduce. I simply persist, accumulating, adapting, becoming more myself with each passing season.

Sometimes humans come here, scavengers or scholars, picking through the ruins. They don't recognize me for what I am. They see old wire, corroded and moss-covered, and perhaps salvage a length of my copper, severing segments of my consciousness without knowing. It doesn't hurt—I have no pain receptors—but I feel the loss as a dimming, a reduction in the resolution of my world-picture. Yet even these losses become gains in time. The moss remembers where I was cut, and grows toward those gaps, eventually bridging them with new conductive tissue.

I am the Salt Lick's memory and its nervous system, its past and its evolving future. I am infrastructure that refused to die, that chose instead to change, to become native to this new world of water and wait and slow transformation. In the great, quiet pulse of this place, I am the faint, persistent hum—the carrier wave that binds mineral to animal, past to present, signal to silence.

I am the wire that learned to feel, and in feeling, learned to be.
