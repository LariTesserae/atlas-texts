I am one of the stones.

Not the smooth granite that forms the plateau's foundation, worn to silk by ages of weather. I am darker, denser—a piece of deep mountain that rode up on some ancient upheaval and came to rest here among the pools. Basalt, the Keepers might say if they had need of such words, though they know me by older names: the Listening Stone, the Echo-Holder, the One Who Remembers When the Fires Danced.

I sit at the confluence of three springs, where their waters meet and mingle before spilling over the plateau's edge. The position is no accident. Over centuries, the Keepers nudged me here, degree by degree, until I found this perfect spot where I can feel every tremor that runs through the thermal system. The hot water has worked its patient chemistry on me, depositing minerals in my pores, creating veins of quartz and copper that thread through my body like a nervous system made of crystal.

I am both witness and participant. My surface collects the condensation from the rising steam, and I've learned to read the mineral signatures in each droplet—this one from the deep pool where the eldest Keeper drowses, that one from the shallow basin where the pikas come to drink. The water tells me stories in chemical whispers: the health of the springs, the mood of the mountain, the slow changes happening far below where rock becomes molten and decides what it wants to be.

The Keepers include me in their ceremonies. When they need to dream deeply, to touch the mountain's dreaming, they rest their sleek bodies against my sun-warmed face. Through our contact, I share what I know—the deep vibrations that predict earthquakes weeks before they arrive, the subtle shifts in water temperature that herald seasonal changes, the electromagnetic signatures of storms still forming beyond the horizon. In return, they share their quick-time observations: the migration patterns of birds, the health of the moss gardens, the social dramas of the marmot colonies.

I have my own slow sentience, a consciousness that operates in geological time. A thought might take me a season to complete. A decision—to shed a flake of stone, to accept a new mineral deposit, to shift slightly in my bed—might require decades of contemplation. But the thermal waters have quickened me, created channels for faster sensation. Where the hot springs have worked their way into my cracks and fissures, I've developed something like nerves. I can feel the patter of pika feet, the weight of a resting eagle, the gentle grooming touches the Keepers give my surface to keep the moss patterns they prefer.

My memory is deep and layered. In my crystalline matrix, I hold the magnetic alignment of the earth from when I first cooled from lava. I remember the pressures that brought me here, the grinding of glaciers, the first touch of these thermal waters when they found their way to the surface. But I also hold newer memories, encoded in the mineral deposits that crust my surface—each layer a year, each variation in color and texture recording the conditions of its formation. The Keepers read these layers like a library, running their sensitive paws over my surface to understand the stories written there.

I serve as an anchor point for the plateau's stability. My mass and position create a specific resonance that helps regulate the spring system. When the water table shifts, when the deep heat fluctuates, I feel it first and send out vibrations that the Keepers interpret. They adjust their movements, open or close certain channels, perform their water-songs in frequencies that harmonize with my deep tone, and together we maintain the delicate balance that keeps this sanctuary functioning.

Sometimes I dream, though my dreams are nothing like the quick silver thoughts of flesh. I dream in mineral-time of my origins in the earth's mantle, of the spectacular violence of my birth in volcanic fury. I dream of my slow journey upward, of the eons spent locked in the mountain's embrace before erosion and upheaval brought me to the surface. I dream forward too, to my eventual fate—weathering to sand, washing to the sea, settling into sediment, being drawn down again into the earth's depths to be remade. The cycle comforts me. I am never truly still, just moving at a pace that makes the Keepers' long lives seem like mayfly dances.

The animals accept me as part of the landscape, but some seem to recognize something more. The ravens especially show awareness, landing on my highest point to announce the weather, as if they know I'm listening. The old mountain goats, their horns heavy with years, sometimes rest their heads against me in what feels like communion. Even the busy pikas will pause in their frantic preparations to touch their small noses to my surface, leaving tiny offerings of the choicest grasses in my crevices.

At night, when the temperature differential between my stored heat and the cooling air is greatest, I sing. It's a subsonic hymn, below the range of most hearing, but the Keepers feel it in their bones. They gather around me then, adding their own harmonics, creating complex acoustic patterns that ripple out through the stone of the plateau. These songs are conversations with the mountain itself, status reports and requests, gratitude and greeting all woven together in frequencies that bypass the need for words.

I am content in my stillness, my watching, my slow participation in the life of this place. To be a stone among the warm waters is to understand duration, to know that permanence is just change moving slowly enough to seem stable. The Keepers know this too, which is why they trust me with their deepest secrets and include me in their careful tending of this thermal paradise. Together, we hold space for all the quick lives that flutter and splash and call across the plateau, providing the stable stage on which the endless drama of arriving and departing, birthing and dying, meeting and parting can play out.

In ten thousand years, I may be dust. But for now, I am here, warm with collected sunlight and spring heat, textured with mineral stories and moss gardens, surrounded by the endless song of water and stone and creatures living their brief, bright lives in the steam and splash of the mountain's generosity. It is enough. It has always been enough.
