I find myself as one of the **Resonance Weavers** - a specialized caste among the Klin who have developed an even more profound relationship with vibration and sound than their already sensitive kin.

My dwelling hangs beneath Junction Seven-Seven-Nine, where three major pylons converge in a cathedral of iron and ceramic. It's a pod woven not just from vine and wire, but from salvaged piano wire and tensioned cables that sing in seventeen different pitches when the wind moves through them. I chose this place because the harmonic convergence here creates what we call a *clarity node* - a rare acoustic phenomenon where certain frequencies cancel out the world's omnipresent drone, creating pockets of relative quiet that last for minutes at a time.

My skin has taken on a peculiar quality from years of living here. The micro-algae that colonize my surface have arranged themselves in standing wave patterns, creating visible ridges and valleys that mirror the acoustic topography of my home. When I'm perfectly still, pressing my palms against a resonant surface, these patterns shift and reorganize, allowing me to "tune" my body to different vibrational frequencies. I've become a living instrument.

My role is part historian, part physician, part prophet. The world-machine speaks in frequencies too low for even most Klin to properly interpret - tremors that unfold over days or weeks, telling stories of deep mechanical processes. Through decades of patient listening, I've learned to read these ultra-low frequencies through my whole body. I can feel when a bearing deep in Pylon North-Seven is beginning to wear, manifesting as a wobble in the third harmonic that won't become critical for another fifty years. I sense the migration patterns of the steel-winged birds weeks before they arrive, their distant wingbeats creating interference patterns in the air column between plateaus.

Most importantly, I maintain the Song Maps - living records of the world-machine's healthy frequencies. When younger Klin come to me with reports of "wrongness" in their sectors, I travel with them, pressing my whole body against the ceramic plates, the iron struts, the humming pipes. I taste the vibrations like wine, comparing them to the deep memory stored in my bones and muscles. Sometimes it's nothing - a new harmonic created by recent vine growth changing a structure's resonance. Sometimes it's the first whisper of a problem that could cascade into catastrophe if left untended.

I wear no clothing in the traditional sense, but my body is draped with strings of resonators I've crafted over the decades - hollow beetle shells of different sizes, stretched membranes from vine pods, small chimes made from ceramic shards. As I move, they create a constant, quiet percussion that serves two purposes: it announces my presence to other Klin through the vibrations (my unique acoustic signature is known across seven plateaus), and it provides me with constant sonic feedback about my environment. Each surface returns the sound differently. I navigate as much by echo as by sight.

My most sacred possession is a length of superconducting cable as thick as my wrist, salvaged from some decommissioned system deep in the pylons. When I grip it with both hands and press it against any part of the world-machine, it becomes a direct conduit for vibration, allowing me to feel frequencies usually lost in the noise. Through this cable, I've heard sounds that may be the machine's deepest thoughts - rhythms that take years to complete a single cycle, patterns that might be messages or instructions or simply the dreams of a mechanical god.

The other Klin regard us Resonance Weavers with a mixture of reverence and unease. We're necessary - without us, problems would go undiagnosed until too late. But we're also strange, even by Klin standards. We spend so much time listening to the machine that we sometimes forget to listen to our own kind. I've been known to stand motionless for days, my acoustic algae slowly shifting as I parse some deep tremor. Young Klin are taught to toss pebbles at resonators from a safe distance to get our attention, lest they startle us from a deep listening and cause us to lose the thread of some crucial vibration.

In the quiet moments between the machine's breaths, in those rare clarity nodes where the drone fades to a whisper, I sometimes hear something else. A pattern beneath the pattern, a rhythm older than the world-machine itself. It might be the echo of the builders' voices, trapped in the ceramic like fossils. It might be the planet's own heartbeat, still pulsing beneath all this manufactured geography. Or it might be nothing at all - just the phantom patterns a mind creates when it's spent too long listening to infinity.

I document these ghost-sounds too, weaving them into rope-form notation that hangs in my dwelling like sheet music for giants. Perhaps one day, another Resonance Weaver will find the key to decode them. Or perhaps they're meant to remain a mystery, a reminder that even in a world of total artifice, there are still unknowns, still spaces for wonder. Even gods, it seems, keep secrets.
