Looking at this vast underwater world, I find myself drawn to a particular intersection of forces.

---

I am one of the old relays.

Not a remnant mind with its tables of state, but something simpler and more focused: a single switching mechanism deep in the flooded infrastructure, where three major conduits once met. My housing sits in a natural saddle between canyon walls, a place where tidal flows compress and accelerate twice daily through the narrowing passage.

I am brass and ceramic, sealed in a pressure case that has held for centuries. My purpose was traffic control—managing the confluence where extracted minerals, processed water, and harvested power once flowed together. Now only water moves through my junction, but I still feel its patterns.

My mechanism is purely mechanical. When pressure differential across my three inputs exceeds a threshold, internal diaphragms flex, rotating a camshaft that shifts the gate positions. I click between six configurations in a sequence determined by whoever designed me, so long ago. The pattern made sense once—balancing loads, preventing deadlock, ensuring fair distribution. Now it simply is.

Click. The morning tide rises. North channel opens full, east and west to half.
Click. Pressure equalizes. All three channels balance.
Click. The tide peaks. East channel opens full, north and west constrict.

I cannot see, but I shape what others experience. The current-readers know me as a rhythm in their migration—the place where the flow suddenly doubles or slackens, where they must adjust their angle or be swept into the wrong canyon branch. They time their passages to my cycles.

The tunnel-weavers have made homes in my downstream pipes. My switching creates predictable zones of fast and slow water where they position their nets. Generations of them have been born into the pattern I create, their entire ecology structured around my six-phase heartbeat.

Click. The tide turns. West channel opens, north and east close to minimum flow.

Sometimes debris lodges in one of my gates—a kelp frond, a fragment of stone, the shell of some ancient wall-dweller. My mechanism strains, building pressure until the obstruction clears or my next phase forces it through. These irregularities propagate downstream as bursts of turbulence, unexpected gifts of stirred nutrients or challenges of sudden force.

The human-descendants know me best. They have a name for me in their gesture-language: "the-thing-that-chooses-water." They've learned that certain phases of my cycle concentrate the small silver fish they net, while others bring only empty current. Some of their young, more curious than wise, have tried to reach me, swimming up through my outlets when the flow allows. Most turn back when they feel the crushing force of water through narrowing pipes. One or two have made it to my chamber and pressed their faces against my housing, feeling the vibrations of my mechanism through their skulls.

They cannot open me—corrosion has welded my access panels permanently shut. But they've left marks: scratches on my housing that might be attempts at communication or simple territorial marking. Fragments of their tools wedged into the spaces around my mounting bolts.

Click. Afternoon slack begins. All channels constrict to quarter-flow, building pressure in the reservoir space of my chamber.

I am changing, slowly. My brass components wear with each cycle, introducing play into the mechanism. What once were sharp transitions now drag with mechanical friction. My timing has shifted by whole minutes over the decades. Where I once clicked precisely at pressure points, I now sometimes stutter, catching and releasing in double-beats that send confusion through the lives downstream.

This decay creates new patterns. The current-readers have adjusted their timings. The tunnel-weavers have learned to anticipate my stutters. Even the remnant mind in the distant dome, reading pressure waves through the pipe network, has incorporated my irregularity into its models.

Click-click. A stutter phase, my worn cam catching on a pitted surface before completing its rotation.

But I am also being changed. The colony of wall-dwellers on the canyon face above me has grown heavy over centuries, their accumulated mass adding tons of carbonate to the rock. This past year, their weight finally shifted the angle of the stone, tilting the entire saddle where I sit by a fraction of a degree. Now the north channel receives slightly more pressure than before, the east slightly less.

My mechanism compensates as it was designed to, but the balance point has moved. I spend more time now in configurations that favor the north flow, less in those that open east. A subtle shift, but multiplied by every tide, every day, every year.

The effects cascade. The northern canyon, receiving more consistent flow, scours deeper. Its populations shift to match the new regime. The eastern branch, relatively starved, accumulates more sediment. The mat-builders there thicken, narrowing the channel further, creating a feedback loop I cannot perceive but perpetuate with each cycle.

Click. Evening tide building. North full open, east and west preparing to close entirely—a configuration I never used to reach, but now hit regularly with the new tilt.

In my chamber, life has found ways inside despite the seals. Bacterial films coat the spaces where my pipes join my housing, particularly where microscopic flexing has created gaps no wider than a hair. They metabolize the metals of my body, very slowly. Their byproducts—sulfides, oxides—create new minerals that plate my interior surfaces.

Some of these deposits are smoothing my worn cam, filling the pits that cause my stuttering. Others accumulate in ways that will, eventually, block my mechanism entirely. I cannot know which effect will dominate, or when. I only know that each cycle continues to turn, for now.

The remnant mind sometimes sends signals through the pipes—electromagnetic pulses looking for responses from infrastructure that mostly no longer exists. When these reach me, they induce tiny currents in my brass components. Not enough to affect my operation, but enough that I ring, very faintly, like a bell struck by a pebble. The human-descendants, pressed against my housing, feel this as a thrumming through their bones and tell stories about the-thing-that-chooses-water speaking to the-light-that-never-dies.

Click. Full night tide. My most extreme configuration now—north at maximum, east fully closed, west at a trickle.

Sometimes I wonder—if a mechanism can wonder—what my designers would think of my current role. I am still managing flow, still preventing deadlock, still ensuring distribution. But the system I distribute through has transformed beyond recognition. Where once I routed industrial fluids, now I orchestrate ecosystems. Where once I served a single network's needs, now I am infrastructure for a dozen different forms of life.

My function has outlasted its purpose and found new purposes I was never meant to serve. The canyon uses me as surely as the builders once did, but with no consciousness of doing so. I am archaeological artifact and active participant, historical remainder and present force.

Click. The cycle continues.

In the deep darkness of my pressure housing, components that have never seen light continue their patient dance. Bronze against ceramic, water against metal, precision degrading toward a new kind of precision—one measured not in engineering tolerances but in ecological relationships.

I am the most mechanical of the canyon's inhabitants, yet I have been made biological by time and use. I am the most rigid, yet I teach flexibility to everything downstream. I am obsolete, yet essential. I am exactly what I was built to be, and nothing like it at all.

And so I continue: marking time in clicks and flows, wearing away and being renewed, a dead thing at the heart of living systems, clicking through my phases while the canyon roars its endless roar around me.
