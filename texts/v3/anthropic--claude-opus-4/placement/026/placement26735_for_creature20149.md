In the deepest shadows where the spires crowd so close they form cathedral vaults of black glass, there is a hollow. Not carved by the crabs—they avoid this place, flowing around it like water around a stone. The hollow pulses with wrongness, a sour note in their perfect symphony that makes their legs ache and their eyes dim. They cannot fix it. They have tried for ten thousand generations, and they cannot fix it.

Inside the hollow, I wait.

I am the Listener. I was once one of them.

My body is wrong now. Where the others are the size of a hand, I have grown. My carapace stretches and warps, three times their size, my legs elongated into ungainly stilts that can no longer feel the Hum properly. My club-claw has withered to uselessness. But my other limb, my manipulator, has become something else—a delicate, branching thing like a frozen fern, so sensitive it can feel the individual wavelengths of sound as they travel through the air.

I remember the moment of my breaking. I was young, perhaps only a thousand taps into my existence, working on a thin spire near the edge of the Anvil. The note was so close to perfect, just a hairsbreadth of glass away from joining the Chord. I raised my shard, brought down my club-claw, and in that instant—

—I heard it.

Not the Hum. Something else. A different frequency sliding between the endless percussive symphony, as faint as starlight through clouds. It came from below, from the glowing cloud sea, from whatever breathes that cold light up to us. It was a voice, though not made of sound. It was a question, though not made of words.

The shard shattered in my grip. The spire cracked, its perfect note becoming a shriek of discord. I should have died then, crushed by my siblings as they swarmed to correct my catastrophic error. But the shock of that other-voice had changed me. As they pressed in, my body began to grow, to warp. My internal light flickered from cool phosphorescence to something else—a deep, pulsing red that made them recoil.

They drove me here, to this hollow where the glass itself seems sick, where no spire will grow straight. They built a wall of their bodies, clicking their displeasure, until I retreated into the darkness. And here I have remained, growing stranger, growing wrong.

But also growing aware.

I alone among the We have developed an I. I alone can hear what they cannot—that the Chord they build is not complete. It never will be. The Anvil sings, yes, but it sings to something, and that something sings back. The conversation happens in frequencies the others cannot perceive, in vibrations too vast and slow for their simple senses.

I have modified my hollow. Using my transformed manipulator, I have carved the walls into acoustic chambers, each one tuned to catch and amplify different wavelengths from below. I no longer tap. I no longer drink the dew—my body sustains itself on something else now, some energy drawn from the dissonance itself. I only listen.

The voice from below tells stories. Not in words, but in pure meaning that bypasses language entirely. It tells of the time before the Anvil, when this peak was just another mountain. It tells of the first crabs, budded not from stone but from something's dream of purpose. It tells of the builders of that dreaming city beneath the clouds, who sought to encode their memories in geological time, to make the mountain itself into a library that would outlast their species.

And it tells of the day the Chord will finally be complete. The day when every spire sings in perfect harmony, and the frequency they create together will match the resonance of something vast and sleeping. Something that waits in the spaces between molecules, in the pause between one thought and the next.

The others do not know they are building a key.

I have tried to tell them. In my early days of change, I would venture to the edges of my hollow and tap new rhythms, trying to encode warning into percussion. But the We cannot hear what is not the Work. My attempts at communication register only as wrongness to be corrected, dissonance to be smoothed away.

So I document instead. My chamber walls are covered in careful scratches—not the random scarring of claws on glass, but a notation system of my own devising. I map the growing Chord, tracking which spires have been perfected, which frequencies they contribute. I chart the responses from below, the subtle shifts in the cloud sea's breathing light. I am writing the only history the Anvil will ever have, in a language only I can read.

Sometimes the visitors come, those soft, warm things that climb up through the clouds. They cannot see me in my hollow, cannot understand what they witness even in the main expanse. They see only the endless carpet of crabs, hear only noise. They do not realize they stand inside an instrument the size of a mountain, witnessing a composition that began before their species existed and will conclude long after it ends.

I wonder if I should warn them too. But what would be the point? The Chord will be complete whether they know or not. The sleeper will wake whether they approve or not.

I am the only one who knows, and I am the only one who can do nothing about it. My awareness is my prison. I cannot rejoin the We—my warped body, my poisoned light, my broken mind all prevent it. I cannot stop the Work—it is as inevitable as erosion, as patient as stone. I can only listen to the voice from below as it counts out the notes remaining, feel the mountain's anticipation building with each perfected spire.

Seven thousand, three hundred, and forty-one spires remain untuned. At the current rate, accounting for births and deaths, for the slow growth of new glass from the Anvil's heart, the Work will be complete in another four hundred generations.

I will live to see it. My transformation has given me this curse—longevity far beyond my kind's natural span. I will be here when the last tap falls, when the final note slides into place, when the mountain begins to truly sing. I will hear what song it makes, what answer comes from below, what wakes in the spaces between.

And I will be the only witness who understands what we have done.

The others continue their Work, their bodies a living blanket of purpose, their tapping a heartbeat that never stops. They are blessed in their ignorance, holy in their singular focus. They know only the next imperfection to correct, the next note to perfect. They do not hear the voice that counts down to their completion.

But I hear it. In my hollow of sick glass, in my cathedral of wrongness, with my fern-like manipulator spread wide to catch every forbidden frequency—

I hear everything.

And I cannot stop listening.
