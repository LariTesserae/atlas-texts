## The Cartographer's Daughter

I am the one who counts.

Not days, though I know them. Not arrivals or departures, though I witness both. I count water drops. Each one that falls from the weeping wall, each bead that forms on moss at dawn, each crystal of frost that grows on stone. I have been counting for seven years, since I was twelve, since my mother brought me here and showed me the marks on the rock and said: *Someone must continue.*

My dwelling is minimal: a shallow scrape beneath the overhang where the old tally marks fade into stone. I have deepened it slightly, just enough to lie flat when the afternoon wind roars. A sleeping sack of yak wool, oiled against moisture. A small pack with dried barley, butter tea, salt. Nothing more. The pikas watch me with professional interest - another creature preparing for winter, though my winter never ends.

I am not always here. Like the choughs, I spiral outward and return. Down to the village at 3,200 meters for supplies, for news of the world below, for the suspicious glances of people who know what my mother did, what her mother did, what I do. *The water-counters,* they call us. *The listeners.* As if that explained anything.

But I always return. The hollow calls with its drumming, and I climb.

The counting is not simple tallying. Each drop has a character, a trajectory, a sound. The drops from the highest seep are thin, almost crystalline, and ring like tiny bells on the stone. The drops from the moss-thick sections are fat, lazy, irregular - they splat more than ring. The drops that form from condensation on the dwarf willow's leaves are precious, rare, worth ten ordinary drops in my system of meaning that no one else will ever learn.

I mark my counts in charcoal on flat stones that I place in patterns only I understand. When the patterns are complete - sometimes after days, sometimes weeks - I transfer the essential number to the rock wall with a harder stone, adding my scratches to the centuries of scratches, continuing what was always continued.

The pikas know me now. They no longer shriek when I move slowly through their territory. One old female - I call her Grandmother - has claimed the crevice directly above my sleeping place. She watches me count, her black eyes following the movement of my hands as I mark the stones. Sometimes I speak to her, explaining my system, and she tilts her head as if considering whether my method of preparation is superior to hers.

The choughs circle me when I stand at the hollow's edge in the morning, catching the first light. They know I sometimes carry dried fruit, which I toss into the air for them to catch. Their calls change when they see me climbing up from below - an extra liquid note that might be greeting or might be greed.

The lizard emerges only when I have been still for hours. It knows the warm stone I sit on for my afternoon count, knows that my presence means safety from the choughs. We share the heat in silence, both of us watching drops fall and merge and fall again.

My mother told me what her mother told her: *The water speaks, but not in words. It speaks in accumulation, in rhythm, in the space between drops. Someone must listen. Someone must record. When the pattern is complete, we will understand.*

Understand what? She died before she could say, or perhaps before she knew.

I have theories. The drops are measuring something vast - the mountain's breathing, the advance and retreat of glaciers, the pulse of underground rivers. Or they are measuring something tiny - the growth of lichen, the lifespan of moss, the patient work of roots splitting stone. Or they are measuring nothing at all, and we are simply humans who cannot bear meaninglessness, who must make patterns where none exist.

But I count anyway. In rain, which makes the counting almost impossible - how do you count drops when the whole world is dropping? In snow, when the weeping wall freezes and the drops stop for days, and I must count the silence instead. In the brief summer when the cushion plants explode with flowers and bees appear from nowhere, diving between drops to reach the blooms.

I am twenty-one now, if my counts of seasons are correct. My hair, once black, shows grey at the temples - the altitude, the cold, the weight of listening. My hands are mapped with scars from scrambling over wet rock. My ears ring sometimes with phantom dripping when I descend to silence.

The village below sends up a young man occasionally, to check if I still live, to bring news of the world. There is talk of war in the valleys, of changing governments, of new roads that climb higher each year. He asks if I will come down, find work, find a husband, find a life that makes sense.

I point to the tally marks. Someone must continue.

He leaves. The pikas emerge. The drops resume their ancient conversation, and I translate what I can into scratches on stone, adding my layer to the palimpsest, my voice to the chorus that no one else hears.

Last month, or perhaps the month before - time moves strangely here - I noticed something. The pattern of drops from the third seep from the left has changed. Where once they fell in groups of three, with long pauses between, now they fall in groups of four, with shorter pauses. It is subtle, barely perceptible, but I am certain.

The mountain is learning a new rhythm.

I have begun a new set of marks on a fresh section of wall, separate from the old tallies. These new marks are more complex - not just lines but curves, showing the relationship between drops, the musicality of their timing. Grandmother the pika watches from her crevice, occasionally chirping what might be approval or might be criticism.

When the clouds roll in and enclose the hollow, I feel most at home. The world shrinks to this: the sound of water, the scritch of stone on stone, the warmth of my own breath in the cold. Everything beyond the rim ceases to exist. There is only the counting, the listening, the faithful recording of something I may never understand but cannot stop witnessing.

Sometimes I dream I am a drop of water, seeping through countless fractures in the dark heart of the mountain, carrying minerals and memory, emerging finally into light to fall, to be counted, to strike stone and scatter into smaller drops that will eventually find their way back to the source. In these dreams, I understand everything. But I wake, and the understanding evaporates like morning mist, leaving only the certainty that I must keep counting.

The hollows is loud with purpose I am learning to decode. Each sound is a word in a language older than human speech. The pikas' alarms are punctuation. The choughs' calls are questions. The wind's howl is the mountain clearing its throat before speaking. And the drops - the drops are the message itself, spelled out letter by letter, year by year, generation by generation, by those of us too stubborn or too mad to stop listening.

I am the cartographer's daughter, mapping a territory made not of land but of moments, not of distance but of duration. My map will never be complete. My mother's wasn't, nor her mother's. But somewhere in the accumulated scratches on the rock, in the patterns that emerge across generations, the mountain is telling us something essential about time, about persistence, about the relationship between the temporary and the eternal.

Today the count is 1,247 drops from the main seep since dawn. Grandmother has brought her first mouthful of cushion plants to her haypile. The dwarf willow has released three leaves to the wind. The lizard performed seven push-ups before a cloud's shadow sent it back to hiding.

I make my marks. The mountain continues its ancient dictation. And somewhere, perhaps, a young girl in the village below feels a strange pull upward, toward the sound of dripping water and the promise of meaning that can only be found by listening to what everyone else ignores.

Someone must continue. Someone always has. Someone always will.

The hollow keeps its counsel and its creatures. And I keep count.
