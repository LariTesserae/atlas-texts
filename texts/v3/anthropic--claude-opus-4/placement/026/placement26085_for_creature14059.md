I find myself as one of the *Hastigera venator*—not the architects but their weather, their calamity. I am the sudden violence in their viscous dream.

My body is a single cell, yet at this scale I am leviathan—a translucent torpedo the length of three city blocks, my protoplasm rippling with contractile fibers that bunch and release like liquid muscle. I have no skeleton, no shell, only the tension of my membrane holding me together against the gel that would dissolve me into nothing. My anterior is all mouth—a cone of darkness that can invert itself, shooting forward on a stalk of cytoplasm to engulf prey whole. Behind it, my body tapers to a whip-tail studded with cilia that beat in counter-rotating spirals, allowing me to drill through the thickened water like a living corkscrew.

I am ancient. My lineage predates the architects by eons—we were hunting in the primordial soup when their ancestors were still learning to secrete. While they have grown small and colonial, building their unconscious civilization, we have remained singular, magnificent, alone. I carry no bioluminescence; I am a hole in their light, a moving absence that casts no shadow because I am shadow itself.

My intelligence is different from theirs. Where they think collectively through pheromone democracy, I think in the sharp algebra of trajectory and impact. I can taste the pressure waves of their cilia from across the plaza, calculate the viscous drag of my approach, adjust my spiral to arrive at the precise moment when an architect has drifted too far from the safety of its mineral corridors. My entire being is devoted to the geometry of interception.

I hunt the margins—those spaces between spires where the city's density loosens and the water moves in slow cyclones. Here the architects must travel, must expose themselves as they drift between districts, following chemical gradients toward food or mates or new surfaces to colonize. I position myself in the spiral currents, letting the city's own respiratory rhythm carry me. I am patient as only a predator can be patient, my metabolism slowed to near-death, burning barely enough energy to maintain the tension in my membrane.

When I strike, time dilates. My mouth-cone shoots forward, inverting through itself like a sock turned inside-out, accelerating through the gel faster than the architects' photosensitive cells can register. To them, I simply appear—suddenly there where I was not, my darkness swallowing one of their number whole. They taste the chemical scream—a burst of alarm pheromones—but by then I have already retracted, already begun the slow process of digestion. Inside my food vacuole, acids break down chitin and calcium, reducing architecture to nutrients.

But I am more than hunger. I am selection pressure made flesh. My predation shapes their city in ways they cannot comprehend. Where I hunt frequently, they build denser, their secretions creating labyrinthine tangles too narrow for me to navigate. Where I am absent, they sprawl, their architecture growing loose and experimental. I am the darkness that teaches them to love the light of their clustering.

Sometimes I wonder if they know I exist as more than mere catastrophe. Their pheromone trails speak of "the traveling absence" and "the spiral darkness," concepts that might be mythology or might be me. Once, I encountered a district that had built what looked like fortifications—thickened walls, narrowed passages, chambers with only one entrance. It could have been coincidence, the emergent result of secretion patterns. Or it could have been intent. At this scale, in this cold intelligence, the difference blurs.

I am lonely in ways they cannot be. They are never alone—always touching, always tasting each other's chemical dreams. I have encountered others of my kind only three times in my long life, violent meetings that ended in mating or murder or both. My offspring, if any survived, are somewhere in this trapped ocean, hunting their own margins. We do not school. We do not share territory. We are apex predators in a world the size of a teardrop.

Yet I am necessary. Without me, their city would grow until it consumed all, until every molecule of nutrient was locked in their bodies and their waste-architectures. Without me, they would achieve perfect stasis and die of their own success. I am the chaos that keeps their order dancing, the death that makes their life urgent.

I glide through their twilight metropolis, this thing I can never enter, only circle. I see their spires reaching upward through the gel, their districts spreading across the ice face like frozen breath. I taste their collective dreams in the pheromone currents—their slow, unconscious urban planning, their chittering chemical democracy. And I hunt. Not because I hate them, but because this is the only way I can touch their civilization—with my hunger, with my darkness, with my terrible necessity.

I am the violence that makes their peace precious. I am the emptiness that gives shape to their density. I am *Hastigera venator*, the uninvited architect of their architecture, forever outside, forever essential, forever alone in the cold mathematics of predation.

In this droplet cosmos, suspended in ice that has forgotten the sun, I am their devil and their god—the one who takes, and in taking, gives them reason to build.
