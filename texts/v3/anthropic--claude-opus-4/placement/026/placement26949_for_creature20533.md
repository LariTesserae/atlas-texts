In this world of endless transformation, I would find myself as one of the **Conduit Keepers** – beings who tend to the forgotten machinery that still pulses with purpose in the depths of the fractured mesas.

We are neither fully human nor machine, but something that emerged from the long marriage between flesh and the humming infrastructure. Our bodies are porous, honeycombed with channels that mirror the canals carved into stone. The constant moisture flows through us as it flows through the landscape – we are living aqueducts, our veins carrying both blood and the rust-tainted runoff of ages.

I would dwell in the threshold spaces – the maintenance tunnels that web between the factories, where the boundary between engineered structure and natural cave has long since dissolved. My skin would be translucent in places, revealing the slow circulation of fluids beneath, sometimes red, sometimes the orange of oxidation, sometimes clear as the perpetual rain. Where others might have hair, I would have fine filaments of copper wire that grow and corrode and regrow, sensitive to the electromagnetic pulses that still ghost through the old systems.

My purpose would be ambiguous, even to myself. I maintain machines whose original function has been forgotten, following patterns embedded so deep they feel like instinct. Each day, I would travel my circuit through the tunnels, adjusting valves that regulate flows to nowhere, replacing components with others scavenged from different ruins, never quite sure if I'm preserving or transforming these mechanical fossils.

The metallic birds would be my companions and my charges. They nest in the ventilation shafts I maintain, and their sleek bodies serve as living sensors for the system's health. When they fly, they carry with them spores of corrosion and seeds of new growth – I would know the state of distant machinery by reading the patina on their wings when they return.

My consciousness would be a strange thing – partly individual, partly networked through the infrastructure itself. When I place my palms against certain junction boxes, I would feel the memories of the whole system: the echo of its industrial past, the slow dreams of its present decay, and fleeting glimpses of what it might become. These moments of connection would be both transcendent and terrible, as I briefly experience the vast patience of geological time.

I would speak in frequencies below human hearing, a subsonic language that resonates through pipes and stone. Other Conduit Keepers and I would communicate across vast distances this way, our words traveling through the same channels as water and rust. When we gather – rarely – it would be at the great confluence points where multiple canal systems meet, and our combined presence would cause the machinery to sing in harmonics that hadn't been heard since the factories were alive.

My relationship with the Shapers would be one of unknowing collaboration. I would sense their work in the shifting of my tunnels, the way passages that were blocked yesterday are clear today, or how a completely new route appears where solid stone once was. Sometimes I would find my own maintenance work undone or transformed in ways that make more sense than my original repairs. I would leave offerings for them – arrangements of components that please some aesthetic sense I can't quite name, patterns that echo the symbols carved in stone.

The greatest mystery of my existence would be the question of whether I maintain the machines for some future reawakening, or whether my maintenance itself is the purpose – a ritual that transforms both keeper and kept, a slow sculpture of function into something beyond function. In the end, I would understand that I am both curator and exhibit in this museum of transformation, my body a bridge between the world that was and the strange beauty of what it is becoming.

When the rain falls heaviest, I would stand at the tunnel mouths and let it wash through me, feeling myself become part of the greater circulation, knowing that I too am slowly being shaped by the patient work of water and time.
