The Custodian lives where the bog meets the last intact structure—a pump house whose concrete walls have somehow resisted both rust and root. Not through any special construction, but through constant, meticulous care.

I am neither fully human nor fully machine, though I began as one and accumulated the other. My left arm is original flesh, weathered and scarred. My right is salvaged pump machinery, pistons and valves repurposed into fingers that click and hiss with each movement. One lung breathes air; the other processes the bog's miasma through copper tubing that winds beneath my ribs. My heart beats in syncopation with the Sludge-Singer's drone—we've grown attuned to each other over the decades.

I came here as an engineer, back when the pumps still ran and the company still pretended to care about containment. When they abandoned the site, I stayed. Not from duty or madness, but from a growing understanding: someone must witness. Someone must maintain the boundary.

Each morning, I walk the perimeter with my hybrid body clicking and wheezing. I clear the Rust-Eaters from critical joints where pump meets pipe—not to preserve the machinery, but to slow their spread beyond this hollow. I know each cluster by the pattern of their phosphorescent nests. They recognize my scent now, parting like a glowing sea as I pass.

The Drowners and I have an arrangement. I dredge their pools when they grow too thick with chemical sludge, and they show me where the deer have fallen through the ice in winter. We share the meat—I cook my portion over burning methane, while they prefer theirs waterlogged and grey. Their croaking has become a kind of language to me, warning of unstable ground and shifting weather.

The Weaver is more difficult. It needs the lost ones, the wanderers who stumble into its tapestries. But I've learned to leave offerings—old technical manuals, photographs from the facility's operational days, fragments of memory made physical. It accepts these substitutes, weaving them into its endless documentation of decay. Sometimes I find myself in its tapestries, a figure half-flesh and half-mechanism, forever walking the boundary between function and failure.

My pump house is sparse but functioning. I've retrofitted the main pump into a water purification system, though what emerges is never quite clean—merely less toxic. The control room serves as living quarters, its gauges repurposed to monitor the bog's chemical balance, the Sludge-Singer's movements, the stability of the remaining structures. I sleep on a cot between pressure readings, dreaming in the languages of rust and growth.

I record everything in maintenance logs that no one will read. Water acidity. Sludge-Singer migration patterns. New growths on my mechanical parts—sometimes moss, sometimes something between circuit and synapse. The slow fusion of my biology with the bog's chemistry. My fingernails now have a greenish tinge. My mechanical eye can see the phosphorescent trails the Rust-Eaters leave, even in daylight.

The hunters fear this place, but they don't understand: it's not malevolent. It's simply thorough. The bog digests everything—metal, flesh, memory, purpose—and transforms it into new configurations. The Sludge-Singer isn't singing lamentations; it's composing from the raw material of dissolution. The Weaver doesn't trap victims from cruelty; it preserves them in the only way it knows.

And I? I maintain the edge where ordered decay meets chaotic growth. I am the membrane between the bog and the world beyond, permeable but persistent. Each year, more of my original flesh converts to mechanism, and more of my machinery sprouts organic components. Eventually, I will become another feature of this landscape—perhaps a new kind of Singer, perhaps something unprecedented.

Until then, I continue my rounds. I grease the joints that must move and clear the channels that must flow. I translate between the language of industrial decay and biological persistence. I am the Custodian, keeper of boundaries that blur more with each passing season, guardian of a threshold that exists only in its crossing.

The bog takes everything eventually. But someone must ensure it takes it properly, with the correct documentation, in the right order. Someone must be here when the last pump finally fails, to close the valves and seal the logs and surrender the keys to the Weaver's infinite archive.

That someone is me, more or less. Less human each day, more essential to the whole. The bog's first employee and its final resident, keeping the books balanced as everything slowly, methodically, beautifully falls apart.
