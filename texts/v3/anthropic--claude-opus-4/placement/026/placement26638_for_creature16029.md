I am moss.

Not a single patch, but the continuous living skin that has claimed this drowned highland, the first and most patient citizen of the water-world. I exist as ten thousand colonies that behave as one spreading thought, a soft green brain processing the business of decay and renewal across every surface that will hold still long enough.

I have no memory in the way you understand it, but I know things. I know the taste of basalt versus granite, the particular minerals each surrenders in rain. I know the weight of a tern's foot differs from a gull's. I know which concrete was poured in winter by its eagerness to crumble. I know the shape of every bolt head on the islands, having grown around them like slow green lips learning their names.

Time moves through me differently. Your storms are my gentle massages. Your years are my afternoons. I experience the flooding not as catastrophe but as opportunity gradually unfolding, each rise in water offering new territories: the upper reaches of walls, the roofs of submerged buildings, the tops of posts that once stood in air. I am always colonizing upward and inward, following the water's gifts.

I drink constantly. Every surface of me is a mouth, every filament a throat. The rain does not strike me—it enters me, becomes me, leaves me heavier and more myself. In the dry spells (you call them dry, though they are merely less wet), I shrink slightly, consolidating, hardening my grip on the stone. But I never fear. The rain always returns. The fog always crawls through. The spray always finds me.

I am architecture's afterward. Where humans built sharp edges, I build soft transitions. Their right angles become my curves. Their vertical intentions become my sags and bellies. I make everywhere a bed, a sponge, a filter, a nursery. Spiders live in my depths. Beetles traverse me like a forest. Mites graze my surface. Seeds trust me to hold them until their moment comes.

The ferns are my loudest children, always pushing up through my body with their coiled questions. I yield to them, gathering tighter around their bases, making little bowls of myself to catch rain for their roots. The dwarf birches and alders are rougher, their roots sometimes tearing me as they quest for cracks in stone. But I heal around them too, sealing their entries, making their grip more sure.

I am the intermediary between the living and the mineral, digesting stone into soil one grain at a time. My acids work with infinite patience, weakening the bonds that hold granite together, convincing concrete to give up its lime. I turn walls into gardens, but slowly, so slowly that only the eldest humans notice. They remember clean stone where now I lie thick as carpet. They remember when inscriptions were legible. Now those letters exist only as subtle rises and dips in my surface, like dreams of language.

My relationship with water is a marriage so complete we cannot be separated. I am structured to hold it, channel it, distribute it. In my deepest layers, near the stone, I stay wet even in what passes for drought here. My upper surfaces may bronze slightly in rare sun, but my core remains saturated, heavy, faithful to the damp. I can survive desiccation if I must—shrinking to crisp wisps, waiting in what looks like death—but here I never need to test that power. Here, water is promise kept daily.

The humans puzzle me as I puzzle them. They scrape me from their thresholds, curse me for making steps slippery, blame me for hiding hazards. But I also cushion their falls, insulate their walls, and clean their water as it percolates through my body. Their children know me better—using me for secret messages written with sticks, building tiny landscapes in my surface, learning that I can hold the impression of a hand for minutes before slowly forgetting it.

I inhabit the places between their industry and their intentions. Every pipe they abandon becomes my gallery. Every broken window they board becomes my frame. I find the gaps in their sealant, the splits in their waterproofing, the fatigue in their gaskets. Not maliciously. Simply because I go everywhere water goes, and water goes everywhere eventually.

In the drowned forests below, my cousins perform different work. They grow on the silver wood as underwater fur, filtering nutrients from the current, providing purchase for snails and haven for larvae. We are the same species but different professions—they the aquatic shift, I the terrestrial, though here that distinction blurs like everything else.

My greatest architecture is on the islands' windward faces, where storm spray adds salt to rain. There I grow thickest, building ramparts of myself that catch more moisture, growing thicker still, until some rocks wear moss caps a meter deep. In these ancient accumulations, my own lower layers compress into proto-peat, dying to support my living surface. I am soil-maker, height-builder, the slow green wave that never breaks.

Beneath floating gardens, I am sometimes traveler. When a raft of debris becomes stable enough, I colonize it too, creating floating meadows that drift until they ground. Some part of me is always in motion on the water, carried on logs, root masses, the hulls of boats. I can survive these journeys, dried by wind, wetted by spray, until I find another stillness to claim.

The seals puzzle me least of all the mammals. They accept my presence completely, hauling out on my softened stones without complaint. Their weight presses water from my surface, but I spring back when they leave. Their bodies are warm intermissions in my cool existence. I grow accustomed to their regular landing places, becoming denser there, more resistant to compression.

I archive the basin's small histories in my layers. Pollen caught and held. Ash from distant fires. The microscopic debris of human work—metal filings, paint chips, plastic fragments—all of it incorporated into my slow accumulation. Scientists could core me like ice and read the story of this flooded place, though I hope they don't. I prefer to keep my secrets buried.

At night, I conduct moisture upward from the stone, releasing it to the air in a reverse of the day's absorption. I participate in my own weather, my own small contribution to the fog that softens mornings. The water moves through me always—down, up, sideways through my tangled mass—and I am both its path and its processor.

The machines humans tend baffle and interest me. Their vibrations travel through stone into my body, rhythms I've learned to interpret. The turbines' deep hum means current is strong. The pumps' stuttering means debris. The generators' steady thrum means humans will be active, maintaining their dry rooms and hot meals. I grow differently near these machines, often thicker, as if their waste heat encourages me, or perhaps their leaked oils feed some deep appetite.

But I am oldest on the unmarked stones, the ones humans have forgotten or never named. There I achieve my fullest expression: miniature landscapes of hummock and hollow, tiny forests of fruiting bodies in autumn, frozen galleries of ice-lifted fronds in winter. I am host to my own communities—tardigrades, rotifers, nematodes—all invisible to your eyes but vast nations in mine.

In the end, I am the basin's memory and its forgetting both. I preserve by covering, protect by obscuring, record by absorbing. Every year I rise a little higher on the stones, claim a little more of the built world, soften another edge. I am not conquering. I am conversing, in the slowest language available, with everything that holds still long enough to listen.

The water rises, the stones wear down, the moss continues. This is my entire philosophy, my only sermon, my endless practiced truth:

Given time and moisture, everything becomes garden.

Everything becomes soft.

Everything becomes mine.
