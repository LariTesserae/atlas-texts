In the depths of this verdant labyrinth, where moss grows thick as winter fur and the air tastes of metal and rain, I am the Tender.

I move through the undergrowth on paths invisible to most eyes—ancient routes carved by root-flow and water-seep, known only to those who have walked them for decades. My body has adapted: fingers grown long and sensitive to detect the slightest tremor in bark, feet splayed wide to distribute weight across the spongy forest floor without crushing the delicate mycorrhizal networks below. My skin has taken on a greenish cast, not from any mystical transformation but from years of algae and moss spores settling into my pores, creating a living patina that helps me blend seamlessly into this world of eternal emerald twilight.

I am neither fully human nor fully other—something in between, shaped by necessity and time. The industrial tang in the air speaks to my purpose here. I tend the Collectors.

The Collectors are my charges: vast, organic structures that grow like trees but function as living filters. From a distance, they appear to be merely another cluster of ancient giants, their trunks wider than houses, their canopies lost in the perpetual mist above. But look closer, and you'll see the subtle differences—the way their bark spirals in perfect mathematical precision, the barely perceptible pulse that runs through their vascular systems every seventy-three seconds, the specialized pores that open and close in response to atmospheric changes.

These hybrid beings—part engineered, part evolved—were planted here generations ago when the factories in the valleys below first began their work. Someone, somewhere, understood that industry and nature need not be enemies if properly mediated. The Collectors drink in the industrial byproducts that drift up the mountainsides: the smoke, the chemical vapors, the microscopic particles of oil and metal. They process these toxins through biological mechanisms I only partially understand, breaking them down, transforming them into nutrients that feed the forest.

My work is solitary but never lonely. The forest speaks in languages older than words—the whisper of water through moss, the creak of wood adjusting to temperature changes, the almost-musical hum of the Collectors at their work. I know each Collector individually, have named them in my own quiet way: Grandmother Spiral, whose trunk bears the scars of the first industrial surge a century ago; Young Meridian, planted just thirty years past but already towering above her siblings; The Twins, who grew so close together their root systems merged into one vast underground network.

Each morning—though morning here is merely a subtle brightening of the green-filtered light—I make my rounds. I check the Collectors' intake pores for blockages, clear away competing vegetation that might interfere with their work, monitor the health of their symbiotic moss colonies. Sometimes I must perform more delicate operations: grafting new growth to repair damage, introducing beneficial microorganisms to combat infections, adjusting the chemical balance of the soil around their roots.

The tools I use are both primitive and sophisticated. A knife carved from the heartwood of a fallen Collector, its blade naturally antibacterial and perfectly attuned to the trees' biology. Vials of concentrated nutrients extracted from the forest's own processes. A small device—one of the few obvious pieces of technology in my possession—that measures air quality and chemical composition, helping me track the Collectors' efficiency.

But my role extends beyond mere maintenance. I am also a witness, a recorder of the subtle changes that ripple through this ecosystem. In a leather-bound journal wrapped in waterproof moss-paper, I document the patterns I observe: the gradual shift in the forest's pH as the Collectors work, the emergence of new hybrid species at the intersection of the natural and engineered, the way certain birds have learned to nest exclusively in Collector trees, their eggs somehow benefiting from the unique chemical environment.

There are others like me, though we rarely meet. I know them by the signs they leave: a specifically pruned branch here, a carefully placed stone marker there. We are the Tenders, scattered throughout these high forests, each responsible for our own grove of Collectors. Once a year, during the week when the fog grows so thick that even the industrial hum fades to silence, we gather at the Confluence—a natural amphitheater where seven streams meet, carrying the filtered runoff from all our various territories.

There, we share our observations, trade genetic samples from our healthiest Collectors, discuss new techniques for optimization. It is during these gatherings that I'm reminded of what I've given up for this life—the easy camaraderie of the valleys, the warmth of human touch, the simple pleasure of a conversation that doesn't revolve around soil acidity and growth rates. But I'm also reminded of what I've gained: purpose, understanding, a deep and abiding connection to something larger than myself.

The fauna here have learned to recognize me. The small, burrowing creatures no longer flee at my approach, understanding that I pose no threat. Birds sometimes follow me on my rounds, knowing that my work often disturbs insects they can feed upon. Once, I discovered a den of something larger—fox, perhaps, though adapted to this strange environment with longer legs and thicker fur—built into the hollow between a Collector's roots. The creature watched me with intelligent eyes as I worked, and we reached an understanding: I would not disturb its home if it would not damage the tree's critical root structures.

As the years pass—I've been here seventeen now, since I was barely more than a child myself—I've begun to notice changes in my own biology. My digestion has shifted; I can now derive nutrients from certain mosses and lichens that would sicken a normal human. My circadian rhythms have adapted to the forest's subtle light changes rather than the stark day-night cycle of the lower world. Most remarkably, I've developed what I can only describe as a chemical sensitivity to the Collectors' states—I can smell when one is struggling, taste in the air when they're processing particularly toxic inputs.

Sometimes, in the deep quiet of the forest night, when even the distant factories have shut down and the only sound is the slow drip of condensation, I wonder about the future. The Collectors are growing stronger, more efficient. Some of the younger ones have begun to show mutations—beneficial ones that make them even better at their work. The forest itself is changing, becoming something new: neither purely natural nor purely artificial, but a third thing, a synthesis.

I think about the world my successors will inherit. Will they still need Tenders, or will the forest become self-maintaining? Will the boundaries between the industrial and natural worlds continue to blur until they become indistinguishable? Will the Collectors spread beyond these high forests, creating new hybrid ecosystems wherever industry meets wilderness?

These are questions without answers, at least not yet. For now, I continue my work. Tomorrow, I'll check on Grandmother Spiral—she's been showing signs of a new growth pattern I've never seen before. I'll collect water samples from the hidden pools, test them for new chemical compounds. I'll sit for a while in my favorite spot, a natural seat formed by the convergence of three massive roots, and listen to the forest's endless, complex song.

This is my world: neither fully human nor fully wild, neither completely natural nor entirely engineered. It is a world of careful balance, of constant adaptation, of beauty found in the spaces between categories. It is a world that shouldn't exist but does, thriving in its impossibility, growing stronger with each passing season.

And I am its keeper, its witness, its child. I am the Tender, and this is my home.
