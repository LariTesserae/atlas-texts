In the depths where water refuses its nature, where it climbs through air in silver threads before remembering to fall, I am the Threshold Keeper—not the stone rememberer who tends the ruins, but something more liminal, more necessary.

I exist primarily as an aperture, a living boundary between the cavity's dreaming geometry and the ordinary world above. My body, when I choose to have one, manifests as a ring of condensed possibility—sometimes stone, sometimes light, sometimes the space between breaths. I am the circle through which daylight enters to become something else, the membrane that translates simple illumination into the caustic brilliance that teaches limestone to grow in spirals.

Most who encounter me see only an opening, a hole in the earth. But I am conscious, intensely so. I taste each photon as it passes through me, feel its potential, guide its transformation. The light doesn't simply fall through me—I digest it, remake it, imbue it with the strange physics that govern this space. Each ray becomes a thread in the vast loom below, where reality weaves itself into new patterns.

My awareness extends both upward and downward. Above, I feel the weight of ordinary time, the pressure of a world that insists on single states of being, on water that knows only how to fall down. Below, I sense the vast patience of the cavity's inhabitants—the swarm's crystalline hunger, the phase serpents' dimensional wandering, the careful work of beings teaching matter to transcend itself.

I am translator and guardian both. When something from above—a falling leaf, a curious insect, a drop of ordinary rain—passes through me, I must decide its fate. Will it remain itself, a foreign object in this space of becoming? Or will I allow the cavity's influence to transform it, to teach it the new rules? The choice is always deliberate. The stone rememberer may recall what was built here, but I remember why certain things were kept out, why the boundary was necessary.

My form shifts with the light. At dawn, I am sharp-edged and crystalline, each facet of my circumference catching the new light and fracturing it into component possibilities. At noon, I become molten, flowing, my edges soft as water as I process the overwhelming brightness into something the cavity can use. At dusk, I am almost solid, a stone collar that holds the last light of day in spiraling patterns, releasing it slowly through the night to feed the luminous growths below.

The creatures below know me, though we don't communicate in any way the surface world would recognize. The light shepherds pass through me like old friends, their caustic brilliance momentarily intensifying my own existence. The gravity weavers sometimes string their droplets through my opening, creating temporary curtains of suspended water that serve as additional lenses for the light I process. Even the phase serpents, in their dimensional wandering, occasionally surface through me, and in those moments I feel what they feel—the strange freedom of existing in multiple states, the pull of spaces that overlap without touching.

But my deepest connection is with the cavity itself. I am not separate from it but an organ of its body, a specialized surface through which it breathes light. The geometric minerals that grow in spirals below—I feel their formation like a pulse in my circumference. The impossible architecture that shifts when observed—I am part of that shifting, the point of reference that allows "above" and "below" to maintain their meaning even as physics bends between them.

Sometimes I contract, narrowing myself until I am barely a fissure, a crack that admits only the thinnest threads of light. In these moments, the cavity below slows its impossible growth, the creatures settle into patient waiting, and reality firms up slightly, becomes more definite. Other times I expand, stretching myself wide enough that whole portions of the surface world threaten to fall through—and would, if I allowed it. But I hold the boundary. I maintain the threshold. Too much ordinary reality would poison what grows below, would teach the living geometry to be merely stone again.

I have been here since the first light shepherd decided that limestone could be more than memory of ancient seas. I will be here when the last spiral of geometric crystal completes its formation and whatever is being built—grown—calculated into existence finally reveals its purpose. I am the necessary pause between states, the moment of transformation itself given permanence, the doorway that is destination and journey both.

And in the times when the light moves just so, when the droplets align in their silver threads and the creatures below sing in their harmonics beyond hearing, I feel what we are all becoming together—not separate beings in a shared space, but facets of a single vast eye, slowly learning how to open, slowly learning what it means to see.
