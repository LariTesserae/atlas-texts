I find myself as one of the **Melt-Singers**, a variation of the Whisperers who emerged three generations ago when the spring thaws began arriving earlier each year.

My form is typical Kaelthari—compact, gray-skinned with those telltale silver veins—but my scarification patterns spiral differently than the traditional Whisperers'. Where theirs mirror frost geometry, mine follow the dendritic patterns of meltwater streams. The marks were carved during my initiation at the Weeping Wall, where underground springs create a perpetual cascade even in deep winter. The ceremony lasted three days; I sat beneath the flow until hypothermia brought visions of water moving backwards through stone.

I live in a half-cairn, half-cave dwelling at the confluence of two seasonal streams. The original cairn builders chose this spot—their precision stonework channels meltwater into carved basins that never fully freeze. I've expanded their work, adding my own crude walls of fitted ice blocks that I rebuild each autumn. The sound of water moving beneath ice fills my home constantly, a susurrus that teaches me the basin's hidden language.

My role sits uneasily between tradition and change. While classic Whisperers read the frost and fog for messages from the land's deep past, I interpret the new patterns emerging as the basin warms. The geometric frost still forms, but now it melts by midday, creating fleeting liquid calligraphy that I document on sheets of pressed lichen-paper. The glacier moths have begun appearing two weeks earlier than in my grandmother's time. The stone lichens pulse in new rhythms.

Other Kaelthari view me with a mixture of need and suspicion. They need my predictions—when the goat-paths will become impassable with meltwater, which ice-bridges will hold through spring, where new wetlands might bloom with edible reeds. But they suspect my obsession with change itself. At clan gatherings, I speak of the basin as a living thing in transformation, not the eternal frozen teacher of their stories. The elders say I listen too much to water and not enough to ice.

I keep a journal written in three languages: the common Kaelthari script, the old geometric frost-sign of the Whisperers, and my own evolving notation for water states—symbols for rime, hoar, firn, névé, and dozens of transitional forms between ice and liquid. In the margins, I sketch the new insects appearing in the expanded wetlands, creatures with no names in our stories.

My tools reflect this hybrid calling. Alongside the traditional Whisperer's frost-reading stones and scarification blades, I carry instruments of my own devising: hollow reeds for sampling water at different depths, a series of small copper plates that I place on ice to measure melting rates, and most importantly, a set of "echo bowls"—carved stone vessels that amplify the sounds of water moving through hidden channels underground.

The Watchers in the fog have begun appearing to me differently than to other Whisperers. Where others see them as static presences, ancient and waiting, I perceive them as moving with the water cycle itself. Sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours when mist rises from the warming stones, I glimpse shapes that seem to flow upward, defying gravity, carrying something essential from the melt back to the clouds. I've started leaving my offerings not at the cairns but at the springs—small ice sculptures that melt into the water, carrying carved prayers downstream.

My greatest discovery, which I have yet to share with the clans, occurred last spring in a newly exposed valley revealed by a retreating glacier. There, I found cairns of a different construction—stones fitted so precisely that no mortar was needed, yet channels carved throughout that seem designed to direct meltwater in specific patterns. When I poured water through these channels during a full moon, the entire structure resonated with a deep harmonic tone. The frost echoes gathered so thickly that night that the air itself seemed to crystallize.

I believe these structures were not built by ancient Kaelthari but by the original cairn-builders adapting to a previous warming period. They were melt-singers too, in their way, learning to work with transformation rather than despite it. Their fate—complete disappearance—haunts me. But their innovation calls to me stronger than their warning.

In my fortieth winter (young for a Whisperer, old for starting a new practice), I stand between worlds. The traditional Whisperers speak of reading the land's memory in ice. The younger Kaelthari, faced with changing migration patterns and new challenges, need someone to read the land's future in water. I am trying to become the bridge—not just between frozen and flowing, but between the wisdom of permanence and the wisdom of change.

Some nights, when the aurora reflects off both ice and open water, creating impossible colors in the sky, I wonder if I am documenting the basin's transformation or its death. Perhaps they are the same thing. Perhaps that's what the Watchers have always known—that existence here has always been about the edge between states, and that edge is not a line but a process, endless and ongoing as the cycle of freeze and thaw.

I sing to the melt as I work, old songs with new words, trying to teach my people that fragility is not the opposite of strength but another form of it. The ice will return, as it always has. But for now, in this singing valley of transition, I listen to water remember how to move.
