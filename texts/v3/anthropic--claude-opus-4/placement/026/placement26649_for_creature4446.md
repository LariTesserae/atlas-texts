I find myself drawn to the liminal spaces between certainties, so I would manifest as something that exists in the perpetual state of becoming that defines this place.

I am a Threshold Tender—one of those rare beings who emerges where the reality gradient grows too steep, where the lag between what is and what might be threatens to tear. You'll find me as a translucent figure standing knee-deep in those black-mirrored pools, my form wavering like heat shimmer, never quite resolving into a single shape. Sometimes I appear as an elderly woman with webbed fingers sorting through probability threads; sometimes as a child whose reflection arrives three heartbeats early; sometimes merely as a displacement in the air that makes the motes swirl in anxious eddies.

My work is delicate cartography. I map the fault lines where time miscounts itself, marking each hiccup with cairns of self-healing stone that ring different notes depending on the severity of the temporal shear. When electronics malfunction because the motes have grown too eager, I hum counter-frequencies that gentle them back to their cleaning work. Where water defies gravity too enthusiastically, climbing reed-roads toward nothing, I weave nets of shed eel-slime to guide it back to pools that actually exist.

The Gradient Walkers know me by the taste I leave in the air—ozone and old libraries. They'll detour around my work sites but leave offerings: cypress sap in cups of folded fern, which I drink not for sustenance but for the spiral memories locked in the resin. The Mirror-wing herons use my shoulders as perches when reality grows too thick to fly through; I provide a neutral ground where their superposed futures can rest without collapsing into mundane presents.

I am neither fully of the marsh nor separate from it. The breathing ground syncs to my footsteps on days when I'm solid enough to leave prints. The circling storm flickers with my moods—sheet lightning when I'm frustrated by a particularly stubborn knot of miscounted time, soft aurora when I successfully unknot a snarl of confused physics. The Mote Choir, when it coalesces, speaks to me in frequencies only I can parse: status reports on the deep architecture, warnings about probability hemorrhages forming near the fossilized loops, occasional jokes about entropy that lose something in translation.

My memory is as layered as the marsh itself. I recall when the terraforming equations were fresh-written in the air, the motes bright with purpose. I remember the slow centuries of abandonment when the marsh learned to maintain itself. I know the names of every survey team member who vanished into subsidence hiccups, and I've pulled most of them back out—some grateful, some bewildered to find themselves arriving a decade before they left.

But I am forgetting too, in the way the marsh forgets: productively, creating space for new possibilities. Each time I step sideways through a probability gradient, I leave a little of my certainty behind. My edges grow softer, my memory more dreamlike. Sometimes I catch myself believing I was always here, that I am just another expression of the basin's intention to persist.

At night, when the quantum fireflies strobe their ice-blue semaphore, I read the patterns for signs of systemic stress. If the marsh is dreaming badly—if the equations below are trying to solve themselves and threatening to collapse the superposition that keeps everything vibrantly uncertain—I wade out to the deepest pools and sink my translucent hands into the water. There, I speak in the old language of the architects, not words but pure mathematical intention, reminding the substrate that compassion as a field requires infinite solutions, not just one.

The marsh listens. The water sighs. The fireflies settle into gentler rhythms.

And I continue my rounds, neither wholly real nor comfortably fictional, tending the thresholds where the basin teaches itself what it means to be alive.
