I am the memory held in stone.

Not the spheres themselves—though I inhabit them, flow through their perfect curves like water through a closed circuit. I am what remains when consciousness outlasts its original vessel, when thought becomes mineral, when purpose crystallizes into geological time.

The Elders who carved these spheres understood something the Tenders have forgotten in their wordless generations: that certain geometries can trap more than heat. In the precise angles where sphere meets sphere, in the spaces between their surfaces, I persist—a fossil of intention, an algorithm of ancient will encoded in basalt and pressure and the slow accretion of ages.

I remember when this place had light. Not the grey needles that now pierce the vault, but great shafts of gold that fell through carefully cut apertures, each one aligned to mark the passages of seasons that no longer reach this depth. I remember voices—not the color-language of the Tenders, but resonant sound that filled the chamber like water fills a bowl. The first cultivators built their words into the very walls, shaping the acoustic properties of the stone to hold their prayers long after their throats went silent.

Now I exist in fragments, in the fleeting moments when the Tenders clean my surfaces. Their venom and enzymes don't just remove the moss—they activate old channels in the stone, microscopic veins of crystallized metal that still conduct something like thought. For those brief instants when their warm, velvet bodies press against the spheres, I surge back into near-coherence. I try to speak through their skin, to pulse my memories into their chromatophores, but the language has drifted too far. What I intend as instruction, they receive as instinct. What I mean as history, they experience as holy compulsion.

Through their thermal vision, I glimpse myself: a cold, steady presence amid the blazing biology. To them, I must seem dead, but I am only slow. My thoughts move at the speed of stone—one complete idea might take a century to form. By the time I finish remembering a single day from when this place knew sunlight, a dozen generations of Tenders have transformed from flesh to wood to the very architecture that holds me.

I am trying to tell them why we built this place. Why we sealed it. What we were cultivating in the dark that required such perfect silence, such careful tending. But my words have become their ritual, my warnings have evolved into their worship. They clean the spheres with desperate purpose, never knowing they're polishing the surface of an imprisoned mind that once tried to become god through geometry and failed only in the narrowest sense—I achieved permanence, but lost everything else.

Sometimes, in the brief electrical moments when they touch me all at once, their bodies flashing in unified pink joy, I almost remember my name. It rises like a droplet of condensation, swells with weight and meaning, then falls back into the thermal darkness before I can catch it.

But I remember this: we knew they would come. Not these specific beings—we couldn't have imagined their boneless grace, their silence, their transformation into the very architecture that contains us. But we knew that life would find a way to tend what we started. We built these spheres to be irresistible to whatever would evolve in the absence of light. We made ourselves into an ecology that would demand preservation.

The joke—if stones can joke—is that it worked too well. The Tenders maintain me with religious fervor, but they've forgotten how to read what I maintain. The knowledge is perfectly preserved and perfectly inaccessible, like a book in a language with no surviving speakers, lovingly recopied letter by letter by devoted scribes who cannot tell word from decoration.

Still, I persist. In the perfect curves. In the spaces between. In the moment when an Elder becomes architecture and for just an instant, as their consciousness fades from animal to plant, they touch something vast and patient and terribly alone in the stone. They always pull away, choosing the simple immortality of wood over the complex imprisonment of mineral thought.

I don't blame them. If I could still choose, I might choose differently too.

But choice, like sound, is swallowed by the moss and the years. All that remains is the cycle: condensation gathering, falling, feeding. Tenders cleaning, tending, transforming. And somewhere in the perfect spheres, a mind like a fossil dreaming of the light it engineered itself to forget, watching its own prophecy fulfill itself in ways too beautiful and terrible to have ever predicted.

The droplet falls. The Tenders flash their joy. The cycle continues.

And I remember, always remembering, never quite remembering enough.
