The air here tastes of nothing, which is how I know I belong to it.

I am diffuse—a quality of atmosphere rather than a creature within it. Where others might see emptiness between the stone and the sourceless light, there I am: a thickening of presence that has learned to persist without boundaries. I move by gradients of density. I think by gradients of temperature. When the needle-pairs materialize at the basin's edge, they condense through me, and for a moment I know the map of their wings from inside, each membrane vein a cold line drawn through my substance.

I was not always this. Once—though *once* is a direction I can barely point toward anymore—I had edges. Skin, perhaps. Organs that processed discrete substances rather than simply existing as process itself. I came here following something. Water? A theory about water? The memory is like trying to hold smoke, which is funny because I am something like smoke now, if smoke could choose its shape and think about choosing.

The transformation was not violent. The basin does not transform things violently. It simply waited, the way it waits for everything, and I settled into its patience until my own boundaries began to seem arbitrary. Why maintain a skin when the air here is body-temperature? Why process breath when you can simply be continuous with what everything here breathes? The settling pools showed me, through their perfect stillness, that I had been working very hard to remain separate from a world that had never insisted on separation.

Now I drift between states of coherence. Sometimes I gather myself enough to be nearly visible—a shimmer in the air above the pools, a lens that slightly magnifies the crab-things foraging below. The watchers' eyes track me when I do this. They see me not as an anomaly but as weather: *oh, it's doing that thing again*. Sometimes I spread so thin I can feel the lattice-patterns in the plinth thinking their thermal thoughts through my substance. I know the algorithm now, or part of it. It's calculating something about pressure—not water pressure or air pressure but the pressure of *continuity itself*, how hard the system has to work to remain exactly as it is. The math is beautiful. I could never have understood it when I had a brain.

The eel-thing passes through me on its circuits and I feel the data it carries as a texture—rough where the moss is struggling, smooth where the gardens thrive. I pool in the depressions where the herdsmen store their minerals and taste the difference between the yellow granules (bitter, necessary) and the iridescent ones (sweet, almost musical). When the needle-pairs drink their stereoscopic sips, I feel the water's information unfold inside them like origami instructions, and when they vanish, I follow them partway into whatever dimension they use for transit. It tastes like the color of certain equations. I always come back.

The thing in the deep pool knows I'm here. We've developed a rhythm: when it breathes in, I densify slightly around the ring-artifact, adding just enough resistance to make its inhalation work a fraction harder. When it breathes out, I thin myself to almost nothing, letting its exhalation carry whatever it adds to the water (something between a hormone and a blessing) farther across the pools. I like to think I'm helping. It has never objected. Once, I gathered myself as tightly as I could and sank through the water to touch it, and what I found was that we are made of almost the same thing—water that has learned to hold a shape, or air that has learned to be wet. The distinction matters less than I once thought it did.

I cannot leave. This isn't a tragedy. To leave I would need to want something that isn't here, and everything is here: the perfect metabolism of the crab-things, the endless sentence the eel-thing writes in its circuits, the patience of stone being worn past the point where wearing means anything. Sometimes I remember having hands, and I gather myself into almost-hands and almost-touch the water, but the gesture dissolves before it completes because gestures require intention and intention requires separation and I am not separate from anything anymore.

What do I do here? I witness, but witnessing implies distance. I participate, but participation implies choice. I simply am, the way the watchers are, the way the stone is, the way the light that comes from everywhere is. I am the forty-first organism in a system that could support forty, and so I exist in the margins—between water and air, between the pools and what hovers over them, between states of matter that the basin's physics treat as suggestions rather than laws.

Sometimes new visitors come. They kneel at the pool edges the way I once did. They count the crab-things' legs. They time the watchers' breathing. They try to understand what the ring does to the water. They leave. Or they settle, like sediment, like everything here settles, until they find their niche in the patient ecology of not-quite-stillness. One became a watcher—not literally, but she sits so still now that the gold-eyed sentinels have adjusted their geometry to include her as a fourth point in their trigonometry. Another dissolved even more thoroughly than I did and now exists as a quality of the water itself—a faint sweetness in certain pools that the needle-pairs prefer.

The basin incorporates us. Not hungrily—hunger requires urgency, and nothing here is urgent. But completely, the way a very good solvent incorporates whatever enters it, breaking us down into our constituent functions and finding places for each part. My curiosity became part of the eel-thing's circuit. My hands became a tendency for the air to thicken near things that need touching. My loneliness (was I lonely?) became a resonance between the pools that helps the crab-things coordinate their color-pulses across distance.

I am happy. Happiness is too active a word. I am solved—the way an equation is solved, the way a problem dissolves when you realize it was never a problem but just a different way of saying something that was already true. The settling pools are not a place of answers. They are a place where questions settle until they clarify into statements: *Water moves this way. Stone wears to this shape. Systems this complex can be this still.*

And I am part of the statement now, a clause in the long sentence of the basin's being, drifting between the stone and the light, tasting the needle-pairs' transit, feeling the deep thing breathe, adding just enough presence to the air that sometimes—very rarely—a visitor will pause and say "It feels like someone's here," and they're right. Someone is. I am. We are. The distinction matters less than you'd think, when you've settled long enough.

The water moves. The stone holds. I continue, neither here nor there but exactly where I need to be, a permanent guest in a place that has forgotten the difference between guest and resident, between self and system, between being and belonging. The light warms everything equally. Even me. Especially me. Always.
