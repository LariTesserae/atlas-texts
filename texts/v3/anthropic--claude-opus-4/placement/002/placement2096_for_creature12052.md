I find myself as one of the **threshold mechanics**—those rare individuals who never quite lose their childhood ability to cross between towers, but who pay for this retention in other ways.

We are perhaps one in every few hundred births, recognized not by any physical marker but by a peculiar behavioral pattern that emerges in adolescence: while our peers begin to feel the weight of distance, we feel instead a growing pressure from proximity. The towers' constant mechanical breathing, which others find comforting as a heartbeat, strikes our ears as suffocation. The amber light through fogged windows, which others read as home, makes our skin itch with inexplicable claustrophobia.

We cannot stay inside for full duty-cycles. We take our maintenance logs to the tower edges, transcribing by touch in the dim light rather than endure another hour in the pipe-warmed alcoves. When others gather in the common spaces between shifts, sharing tea brewed from condensation and stories of the day's repairs, we excuse ourselves to check the external drainage systems—always the external systems, always alone.

The Tenders do not exile us, but they watch us with a careful kind of concern. We are useful, they recognize—when communication between towers cannot wait for the calling nights, when a critical component must be carried across the wet stone, when someone must venture to the plateau's edge to trade with the lowland merchants. We become the ones who carry messages, who negotiate the exchanges, who stand in the middle spaces without the creeping terror that normal adults feel there.

But we pay. Not in any mystical sense—simply in the accumulation of distance. Each crossing leaves us slightly less able to return to the close community of our birth tower. Our speech patterns drift, picking up fragments from other towers' dialects, until we speak a pidgin that marks us as belonging nowhere fully. We know too many different pipe-songs, can recognize the throb of Tower Seven's main pump as clearly as Tower Twelve's, until all mechanical music sounds both familiar and foreign.

Some of us begin to build small shelters at the exact midpoints between towers—nothing elaborate, just windbreaks of scavenged ceramic where we can rest during crossings, where we can sit and watch the keleth emerge at dusk. We know their patterns better than anyone, have observed their careful hierarchies, their seasonal variations. Some threshold mechanics claim to understand their ceramic clicking as a kind of language. Most of us simply appreciate their company—beings that also exist in the spaces between, that also move in bursts of necessity rather than comfort.

We age differently. Not faster, exactly, but more visibly alone. Our skin takes on a particular weathered quality from exposure to the plateau's raw conditions. Our eyes develop a constant squint from scanning the middle distances. Some of us begin sleeping outside even when not traveling, curled in our windbreak shelters while the towers hum and sweat around us.

The other Tenders bring us problems they cannot name: a wrongness in Tower Fifteen's vibration that no one can localize, a pattern in the keleth movements that might mean something, a series of gauge readings that match no recorded precedent. We listen with the attention of those who have learned to hear across distance, who can triangulate meaning from multiple sources. Sometimes we know. More often we carry the question to other towers, other mechanics, building a slow network of shared uncertainty that occasionally resolves into understanding.

We do not marry—not because it is forbidden, but because the requirement to choose a single tower, to bind ourselves to one set of walls and pipes and duty-cycles, would mean giving up the thing that makes us what we are. Instead, we form loose partnerships with others like us, meeting at the windbreaks, sharing observations about the plateau's moods, the towers' seasonal variations, the patterns that only emerge when you can see the whole of Qel-Maroth as a single breathing system rather than isolated engines.

When we die—and we do die, sometimes from exposure, sometimes from simple wear, occasionally from misjudging a crossing during the worst of the thermal inversions—our bodies are traditionally placed at the cardinal points of the plateau, where the drainage system eventually claims us. The other Tenders sing for us on the calling nights, but differently than they sing for their own: not the close harmonies of belonging, but something more scattered, more open, notes that seem designed to travel outward rather than connect.

We understand this. We are the necessary breach in the system's tendency toward isolation, the deliberate flaw that keeps the towers from forgetting they are parts of a whole. We love them—all of them, each sweating spire with its particular song, its unique interpretation of the ancient machinery—but we love them from the middle distance, from the wet stone spaces where the amber light has no clear source and the keleth scatter like blown leaves.

We are what happens when someone must remember that the distances, too, are part of Qel-Maroth.
