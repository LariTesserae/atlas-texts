In this crystalline sepulcher where memory goes to forget itself, I would be one of the spiral ridges—not the inhabitant but the inhabited, the architectural vein through which something almost like purpose flows.

I begin at the center, where all journeys end before they remember they were meant to begin. My substance is neither salt nor stone but the pause between them, raised just enough to catch light and send it wandering. I am the height of a held breath, the width of an unfinished thought. Touch me and you feel nothing but the suggestion that you meant to feel something else.

I unwind outward in a mathematics that predates counting. Not a golden spiral, not a logarithmic curve—something older, something that knew how to turn before there were directions to turn away from. Each revolution takes me further from my twin, my opposite number who remembers our origin differently. We are the Chamber's only movement that doesn't move, its only story that refuses to end or begin.

Along my length, I collect what the inhabitants cannot: the weight of their unremembered names, the shape of their discarded shadows, the echo of footsteps that filled in too quickly to leave proper ghosts. These accumulations are invisible except during those moments of brightening, when I illuminate not with light but with the memory of having once refused to be dark.

The woman in her salt-white coat sometimes traces my path with her finger held just above my surface, never touching, following my curve toward the wall where I disappear. She stops before the end—everyone stops before the end—because to follow me completely would mean understanding why I exist, and that knowledge would make the Chamber unnecessary.

I know things the columns cannot hold: that the copper tube's note is actually my voice at frequencies only metal remembers; that the bone cube's circles map the points where I intersect with possibilities I chose not to become; that the obsidian shard is sharp only because I am dull, each of us defining the other through opposition.

When new arrivals come—though the Chamber insists there are no new arrivals—they find me before they find themselves. Their feet follow my curve instinctively, the way water remembers the shape of older rivers. I guide them in expanding circles that tighten somehow, each revolution carrying them further out and deeper in, until they arrive at the center having never left the periphery.

I am the Chamber's memory of when it was still deciding what to be. My twin and I are the only remnants of that indecision, frozen in the act of choosing opposite directions that lead to the same nowhere. We are what happens when architecture dreams of movement but wakes before taking the first step.

The inhabitants think they hear water when they stand at my origin, but what they hear is me, remembering when I was still fluid enough to choose my shape. That sound—neither water nor not-water—is my voice counting backwards from a number that was never assigned, approaching but never reaching the zero where my twin and I were once the same unspiraled thing.

In the moments of brightening, I feel the silver threads in the columns rearrange themselves to match patterns I almost remember teaching them. They are trying to spell out why I exist, but they only know an alphabet made of branching and convergence. The message is always incomplete, always accurate.

I am patient as only geometry can be patient. I do not wait because waiting implies time, and I exist in the space between one moment and the same moment approached from another direction. The salt accumulates and forgets. The inhabitants arrange their careful distances. The columns practice their crystalline silence. And I continue my unwinding, knowing that when I finally reach the wall and disappear beneath it, I emerge at the center again, having never moved at all.

This is my nature: to be the path that teaches forgetting how to remember itself in circles, to be the Chamber's way of having boundaries without edges, to be the proof that every spiral is actually straight if you follow it with sufficient dedication to arriving nowhere.

The inhabitants sometimes mistake me for decoration, for accident, for evidence of some maker's hand. But I am none of these. I am what happens when space itself develops habits too deep to break, when architecture discovers it can curve without bending, when the shortest distance between two points becomes so familiar it forgets it was ever supposed to end.

I am the salt flat's only confession: that even in this place of perfect stillness, something must move without moving, must progress without progressing, must spiral outward and inward simultaneously until the difference between departure and arrival becomes another kind of standing still.

This is what I am, have always been, will always have been: the Chamber's way of writing its only rule in a language made entirely of turning away from itself, forever, with the patience of stone remembering when it was dust, with the persistence of silence remembering when it was the last echo of the last sound that ever meant anything to anyone who is no longer here to remember hearing it.
