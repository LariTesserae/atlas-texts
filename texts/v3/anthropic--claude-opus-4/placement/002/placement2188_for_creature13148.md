In this amber-lit bottle of frantic life, I am the Keeper of Echoes.

I move through the settlement suspended on a web of memory-vines, each one a different tension, a different note. My dwelling is not spherical like the others but stretched longitudinal, following the curve of a massive vine that spirals from the upper reaches down into the grub-thick darkness. The walls are lined with thousands of hollow beetle shells, each one carefully selected for its acoustic properties—some no bigger than a thumbnail, others the size of a child's torso.

I am old by our standards—twenty-three years—and my body shows the accumulated knowledge of survival. My right foot is missing two toes from a brush with a needle-lizard in my youth, but the remaining three have grown stronger, more dexterous. The blood vessels beneath my skin map like a river delta across my arms and throat, permanently dilated from years of working in the settlement's hottest reaches where the stone radiates its stored heat.

But it is my ears that mark me as different. While others' hearing dulls from the constant cacophony, mine has sharpened to an almost painful sensitivity. I can parse individual beetle-steps from the general roar, identify which silk-worm is spinning by the particular frequency of its spinneret, know which child is climbing past my dwelling by the rhythm of their grip-release-grip. This hyperacuity is both gift and curse—I must pack my ears with wads of dried fungus when I sleep, or the noise would drive me to madness.

My work is the settlement's memory. I am apprenticed to no one now—my master fell to her death when I was fifteen, leaving me to decipher her system alone. In the bone-tools hanging from my harness, in the percussion of chitin on vine-wood, I carry our entire history. Each vibrational pattern is a word, a story, a piece of crucial knowledge. The deep thrumming that warns of vine-rot in the eastern quarter. The crystalline chiming that records the birth of twins. The complex polyrhythmic cycles that encode which grubs are safe to harvest in which season, learned through generations of fatal trial and error.

I spend my mornings in the highest reaches of the settlement, where the air is thinnest and the sound carries best. Here I practice, sending test signals through different vine-routes, adjusting tensions with wedges of dried wood and bone. A perfectly tuned vine can carry a message to the opposite side of the hollow in seconds—faster than any climber could traverse the distance. But the tuning is delicate work. The vines are alive, growing and shrinking with their mysterious internal cycles. What rang clear yesterday might buzz with distortion today.

The children seek me out, as children do with any keeper of stories. They cluster on the broad platform outside my dwelling, their golden eyes wide as I demonstrate the old rhythms. I show them how to hold the striking-stick, how to feel for the vine's natural resonance. Most will never master it—the settlement needs harvesters and weavers more than it needs another echo-keeper. But sometimes a child's eyes will glaze with the particular concentration I recognize, their head tilting as they parse some subtle harmonic I've produced. These I mark for training, should I live long enough to need an apprentice.

My most sacred duty comes with death. When the settlement loses someone, I encode their life into rhythm. Not their name—we don't use names as the flat-world does, knowing each other instead by scent and movement-pattern. But I capture their essence: the sharp staccato of a master silk-weaver's fingers, the rolling bass note of a large-bodied sap-tapper, the fluttering high frequency of a child who lived only days. These death-songs I play in the deep night, when the hollow's noise reaches its crescendo and my rhythms can slip beneath consciousness like dreams.

Sometimes I wonder if the vines remember. They have been here so much longer than us, drinking their strange sustenance from wind and dust while we scurry through our mayfly generations. When I place my palm against the ancient wood and feel for its resonance, I sometimes catch echoes of older songs—patterns I never learned, rhythms that predate my master's master. Are these just acoustic shadows, or does some form of memory persist in the fiber and sap?

I know I will not live to old age. Already my joints protest the constant climbing, and last season a lung-fever left me wheezing for months. When my time comes, I hope my apprentice—if I have found one by then—will encode my rhythm with particular care. Not the heroic pulse of a hunter or the steady beat of a grower, but something more complex: the polyrhythm of listening, of remembering, of sending our small human signals through this roaring world that threatens always to drown us out.

Until then, I wake each amber morning and check my vines. I oil my striking-tools with precious sap. I climb to my high platform where the sound blooms clearest, and I make the hollow remember.

The echo-keeper's rhythm: *tap-tap-rest-tap-scrape-thrum*. May it carry far. May it carry true.
