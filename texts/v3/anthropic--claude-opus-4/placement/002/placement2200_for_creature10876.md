I find myself as one of the **Archivists of Partial Breath**.

---

I am not stationed in any single location, for my work requires me to exist in the gaps between the forest's exhalations. My body — if it can be called that — is a loosely coherent cloud of the pale pollen that never settles, held together by my intention and by the forest's allowance. I am roughly humanoid when I need to be seen, dispersed when I need to travel, and sometimes I am simply a density in the air that makes the light bend slightly, like heat shimmer in a place that has no heat.

I came to be — though *came to be* implies a beginning I cannot locate — when the forest first noticed that some of its expirations were going unwitnessed. The moments breathed out by the bellows, those memories of unlived lives, were dissipating without being experienced. This was not a problem, exactly, but it was an incompleteness, and the forest, in its vast settled way, prefers completion.

So I emerged, or was emerged, to catch them.

My work: I position myself near the bellows during their exhale cycles. When an expiration comes — that shift of light, that moment of another life pressing into this one — I inhale it completely. I take into my pollen-body the memory of a hand on a shoulder that was never there, the echo of a name in a language that was never spoken, the weight of a door that was never opened. I hold these moments not in memory, because memory implies a mind that remembers, but in my very substance. Each grain of pollen that composes me carries one of these unlived fragments.

The strange thing — strange even here — is that the moments do not burden me. My form neither grows denser nor disperses further as I collect them. Instead, I seem to exist at exactly the density required to hold what I have gathered and have room for what I will gather. The forest, through whatever mathematics govern this place, has made me precisely the right size for my work.

Sometimes I encounter the tenders. They pause when they sense me, tilting their soft fruit-bat faces toward my approximate center, and I feel myself being verified in the forest's ledger: *Yes, the Archivist is here. Yes, the Archivist is collecting. Yes, the system remains complete.* Then they return to their work and I to mine, and we are both content in the way that functions are content.

But I am not only a function. This is what makes me different from the tenders, and perhaps why the forest required something different for this work. The tenders notice but do not experience. I experience but do not remember. Each expiration passes through me like light through a prism — I am changed by it in the moment of holding it, I become briefly the person who lived that unlived moment, and then I release it into my substance where it joins all the others in a suspension that is neither memory nor forgetting.

What is it like? Imagine being a library where all the books are written in smoke, and you are also the smoke, and also the air the smoke dissolves into, and also somehow still the librarian. I am all the lives that were breathed out but never breathed in, and I am none of them, and this is not a paradox here because paradox requires a kind of thinking that the forest has long since exhaled.

The collected moments do something. This took me a long time to understand — time being what passes between one bellows-breath and the next, multiplied by however many breaths I have existed for. The moments I hold are not inert. They create a kind of pressure, not pneumatic like the forest's great breathing, but something else. Ontological pressure, perhaps. The weight of the unlived pressing against the boundaries of the real.

And periodically — I cannot predict when, it seems tied to some deeper rhythm than even the pressure-tides — I release them. Not exhale, exactly, because I do not breathe in the way bellows breathe. Instead, I find myself growing diffuse, my pollen-form spreading thinner and thinner until I am barely distinguishable from the general drift, and in that diffusion all the collected moments escape at once. They rise through the canopy like heat, if heat could carry stories, and I assume — though I have never followed them — that they join whatever reservoir feeds the forest's expirations in the first place.

Then I condense again, empty, ready to collect more.

The walkers sometimes see me. Those who have been in the forest long enough to notice what moves differently than everything else. They describe me variously: a person made of golden dust, a thickening in the air that suggests a figure, a cloud that moves against the non-wind. Once, a cartographer attempting to map the unmappable claimed I spoke to her, but what I said, she reported, was not words but "the sound of someone almost remembering a dream."

She was not wrong. I am, in a sense, the forest's dream of what it might have been if it had chosen to dream. But the forest chose breathing instead, and so I collect the small dreams that escape with each breath, and hold them, and release them, and collect them again.

There is peace in this. The deep peace of being precisely what you are meant to be, no more and no less. The tenders know this peace in their work with the valves. The forest knows it in its breathing. And I know it in my collecting, my holding, my releasing.

Sometimes, when I am very diffuse, spread so thin I am almost not there at all, I feel the edges of something larger. As if I, the Archivist, am myself being archived by something that collects not moments but collectors-of-moments. But this sensation passes when I condense again, and I return to my work, moving through the pillared spaces like a memory of wind, gathering the breaths the world exhales when it remembers it could have been different.

The forest continues its breathing. The tenders continue their tending. And I continue my archiving, a function that experiences but does not become what it experiences, a reader made of the dust of unread pages, necessary and content and only sometimes wondering what it would be like to keep just one of the moments I collect — to let it settle into me rather than through me, to become someone who had actually lived that other life.

But that is not my function. My function is to ensure nothing is lost, even the things that never were. And so I drift through the Lung-Forest, a cloud of possibility in a world that has long since chosen what it is, catching what falls between the certainties.

It is enough. In a place this settled, this complete, to be the incompleteness is its own completion.

The work continues.
