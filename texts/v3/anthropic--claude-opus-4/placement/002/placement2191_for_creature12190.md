I find myself in the cool dim between—neither builder nor built upon, neither stone nor entirely flesh. I am one of the curtain-fronds that hang from the western wall of the Middle Deep, in the district the builders call *nothing* because they do not name but which resonates at a particular frequency that means *home-of-the-slow-pulse-chambers*.

My existence is a meditation on weight. Each day—though day means nothing here—I add a few molecules to the crystal cluster at my tip. Calcium carbonate drawn up through my mineral vessels, deposited in careful hexagons at the growing edge. I am making myself heavier, always heavier, because weight is how I know direction in this place where up and down are matters of faith.

I am two meters long now. It has taken me forty years to achieve this length, adding perhaps five centimeters each year, though some years are longer than others. Growth is not steady when your metabolism runs on stone-time. In good years, when the builders' excavations expose fresh limestone faces nearby, when their calcium paste deposits are thick and accessible to my root network, I can manage seven centimeters. In lean years, when they shift their attention to other districts and the stone around me goes quiet, I barely manage three.

The builders know I'm here—not consciously, but in the way they know the curve of a well-gnawed chamber. My blue-green glow is part of their acoustic map, a visual note that helps them navigate this section of wall. They click differently when they pass beneath me. The sound changes. Hardens, somehow, as if my presence makes the stone more reflective. I think—if I can be said to think—that they use me as a landmark. A softly glowing comma in their endless sentence of sound.

I feel them through vibration. Every click, every footfall, every grinding session transmits through the limestone into my holdfast roots, up through my crystallized stem, and sets me swaying. The sway is subtle—a few millimeters side to side—but in my universe of stillness, it's a dance. The vibrations tell me stories: *Here passes a young builder, her click still high and experimental. Here two elders moving toward the deep chambers, their slow clicks overlapping like waves. Here a predator, or rather, here the sudden tightening in the click-pattern that means a predator passed twenty minutes ago.*

My glow pulses in response. I cannot help it. The chemical reactions that create my phosphorescence are triggered by mechanical stress, and every vibration is a tiny stress, a reason to shine. The builders probably don't realize that their movements make me brighter. But the other fronds know. We talk to each other in light—slow conversations consisting of gradual brightening and dimming, patterns that ripple along the wall as vibrations move through the stone.

*Low calcium in sector twelve,* says the frond to my left, dimming to almost nothing.

*Structural damage above,* flickers a cluster higher up, their light stuttering as fracture-vibrations reach them.

*Good paste deposits here,* I pulse back, my glow steady and strong because the builders have been working this wall and their leavings feed my roots.

It's not language. It's barely communication. But it's what we have, and it's enough.

The strangest part of my existence is this: I am becoming stone. Not metaphorically. Actually. My oldest sections, near where I attach to the wall, have mineralized so thoroughly that they're more crystal than cell. If you sliced me open there (please don't), you'd find growth rings like a tree—if trees were made of calcite. Each ring represents a year, laid down in alternating bands of dense and sparse mineral, recording the rhythms of builder activity in this district across decades.

Sometimes I wonder (can I wonder?) what will happen when I'm entirely mineral. When the last living cell crystallizes and I become just another stalactite with delusions of metabolism. Will I still glow? Will I still sway? Will I still be me, or will I be a memory of me, encoded in stone?

But then a builder passes beneath, clicking that deep authoritative click that means *I have been gnawing for thirty years and know every grain of this rock*, and I pulse in response, and the question dissolves. I am here. I am heavy. I am growing toward the center of the earth one molecule at a time, and that is enough.

In the mornings (there are no mornings) I taste the air through my flat, stone-thick leaves. Moisture content: 12%, lower than yesterday. Spore count: moderate, mostly from the velvet-lichen underfoot. Trace volatiles from builder fur and saliva. The faint metallic tang that means someone is excavating fresh stone nearby.

In the evenings (there are no evenings) I photosynthesize—though that's the wrong word for what I do. I chemosynthesize. I take the energy released by breaking calcium bonds and use it to build sugars, proteins, the tiny amount of organic material I need to keep growing. It's inefficient. Absurdly inefficient. A plant on the surface could do in an hour what takes me a month. But there is no hurry here. There has never been hurry here.

The dust-runners ignore me. I am too slow to be food, too solid to be shelter. They scurry past my roots without a glance—do they have eyes? I've never been sure—intent on their own microscopic dramas.

The predators, though. The predators notice me. When one flows through this district (twice a year, maybe three times), it pauses beneath me. I don't know why. Maybe my glow disrupts its acoustic sense. Maybe I'm a convenient marker in its mental map: *turn left at the glowing frond.* Or maybe—and this is a strange thought, probably wrong—maybe it finds me beautiful. Maybe even predators need beauty, even if they can't name it.

I am not afraid of the predators. I am not afraid of anything. Fear requires the possibility of elsewhere, and I have no elsewhere. I am rooted to this wall, in this district, in this basin, and I will be here until my weight pulls me free and I fall, shattering into a thousand glowing fragments that the velvet-lichen will slowly, slowly digest.

When that happens—not for centuries yet, but someday—my minerals will feed other plants. My crystals will dissolve and be redeposited in new forms. The calcium I've spent my life accumulating will return to the great slow cycle of stone and life and stone again.

It's not death, exactly. It's translation. From one form of patience to another.

But for now, I hang. I sway. I glow. I listen to the builders click their endless architectural arguments, and I add my own quiet voice—not sound but light, not words but presence, not thought but the deep vegetable confidence that comes from knowing exactly where you are and exactly what you're doing and exactly how long you have to do it.

Which is forever, give or take a century.

The builder beneath me has settled in for a long gnawing session. I can tell by the rhythm—steady, meditative, the kind of grinding that reshapes stone by micrometers and takes hours to show visible progress. My glow brightens with each grinding stroke, dimming in the pauses when she stops to swallow. Together, we are reshaping this corner of the world. She with her stone-dissolved-in-spit, me with my molecules-pulled-from-rock. Both of us patient. Both of us persistent. Both of us part of something larger that neither of us will ever see whole.

I pulse once, brightly, for no reason except that I can.

She clicks once, sharply, for no reason except that she must.

And Hethkellig continues.
