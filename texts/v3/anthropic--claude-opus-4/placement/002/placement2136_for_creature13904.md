I find myself drawn to the flooded passages beneath the settlement, where the ancient calcified roots have become indistinguishable from stone. I would be one of the deep-tenders.

---

I am perhaps forty years old, though age becomes fluid when you spend half your life in cold water. My name, when translated, means something like "reads-the-calcium-edge" — a reference to my particular skill at finding the boundary where living root transforms into mineral. I can feel it with my fingertips: the subtle textural shift where bark gives way to stone, where the tree's life processes slow and stop and become geology.

My dwelling sits low in the settlement, one of those structures where the floor is perpetually damp and the lower rooms flood completely during the wet season. I was born here, my birth-root descending immediately into water, seeking the deep rather than the light. My mother said it knew where it belonged. The root still grows, down there in the dark, though it grows so slowly now that I can only detect its progress by the knots I tie each year at the growing tip — a personal measurement that I share with no one.

I began diving when I was six. Not the deep, not at first, but the middle passages where children learn to navigate by feel. I was slower than the others to master the upper paths, the canopy routes, the sunny platforms where people dry herbs and tend the fire-keeps. But in the water, in the dark, something in me settled. My breathing slowed beyond even what is normal here. My heartbeat learned to drop, to quiet itself until I could hear the settlement's pulse through the water itself.

By fifteen, I was apprenticed to an old deep-tender who barely spoke above water but who could narrate the entire lower architecture by touch. She taught me the maps that exist in no knot-record: where the oldest bodies rest, where the mineral upwellings are strongest, where the creature passes on its slow circuits through the drowned foundations. She taught me to feel the difference between root-stone that will bear weight and root-stone that has become brittle, that might crack under the wrong pressure. She taught me the locations of the emergency air pockets — the small chambers where a bubble of atmosphere is trapped against a ceiling, where a diver in trouble can surface just long enough to breathe before continuing.

---

I know every calcified passage in the deep architecture. Not by sight — sight is meaningless in the absolute dark of the lower levels — but by a kind of embodied memory that lives in my hands and feet and in the pressure-sense of my inner ear. I know where my great-grandmother's ribcage has become a junction box for three major roots. I know where a cluster of infant bones from some forgotten epidemic has created a calcium deposit so dense it registers as a different temperature against my palm. I know the chamber where the previous settlement — the one before this one, the one that failed for reasons the knot-records are ambiguous about — left its dead in a pile that has become a small hill of bone-stone, avoided now, bearing no weight, a reminder that not every structure succeeds.

My work is maintenance, primarily. Checking the deep granaries, ensuring the seals on the storage vessels remain intact. Clearing the narrow passages when debris accumulates. Testing the stability of calcified roots, feeling for the hairline cracks that precede failure. But there is also something else — something that is not quite ritual and not quite practical but exists in the space between.

I tend the boundaries. The places where our architecture meets the mountain's original stone. Down there, in the deepest accessible places, you can find the roots that first penetrated the caldera floor — the pioneer growth from centuries ago, now so thoroughly mineralized they have become veins of calcium running through the basalt. These are the true foundations. These are what everything else builds on. And they must be... acknowledged. Not maintained, exactly — they are beyond maintenance, beyond any human intervention. But felt. Visited. Known.

Once a month, sometimes more often, I descend to the deepest point I can reach — a passage so narrow I must empty my lungs to fit through, so deep that the water pressure makes my ears sing. At the bottom of this passage, if you know where to feel, you can find the place where the first root touched the mountain stone. It is a node of calcium no bigger than my fist, but everything — the whole settlement, every platform and walkway and dwelling, every living person above — everything ultimately rests on this first contact point between the organic and the geological.

I place my hand on it. I feel its temperature, which is the mountain's temperature, colder than the water around it. I hold my breath — not because I must, but because breathing seems inappropriate here, seems too fast, too biological, too impatient for what this place is. I stay until my lungs begin to ask for air. Then I stay a moment longer.

---

The creature and I have an understanding.

This sounds like something from a children's story, but it is simple truth. We encounter each other perhaps once a week, always in the deep passages, always in the dark. I feel the water displacement first — a pressure wave that tells me something large is moving through the architecture. I stop. I make myself small against a wall. I slow my heartbeat further, until I am just another part of the structure, temporary and patient.

It passes. The bulk of it, the impossible size — I have never seen it clearly, only felt it as a presence that fills the entire passage, that moves with a fluidity that seems wrong for something so large. Its skin, when it brushes against me, is smooth, almost frictionless, warmer than the water by several degrees. It smells — yes, you can smell things underwater if you know how — like deep mud and calcium and something else, something that has no comparison in the upper world. Old life. Patient life.

Once, my hand was resting on a calcified root when it passed, and I felt the vibration of its heartbeat through the stone. Three beats in the time it took me to count to forty. An impossibly slow rhythm, slower than the oldest elder, slower than anything should be able to sustain. But it sustains. It has sustained for longer than the settlement has existed, longer than the first roots penetrated the caldera floor. It is the pace at which mountains think, perhaps.

I do not know what it eats. I do not know how it breathes — though I have heard it surface in the open water at the caldera's heart, that bellows-sound that silences the frogs. I do not know if it is alone or if there were once others. The deep-tenders before me didn't know either. We don't need to know. It is enough to share the space, to acknowledge each other, to be mutually patient in the dark.

---

My partner — spouse, mate, the person whose root grafted with mine successfully twenty years ago — is a shaper. This is an uncommon pairing. Shapers work upward, toward light, toward growth, toward the future. Deep-tenders work downward, toward stone, toward the past, toward the foundations. But the match works. She plans structures that will not be complete for decades. I maintain the bases they will rest on. She thinks in terms of growth vectors and load paths. I think in terms of calcium deposition and structural faith. We meet in the middle, in our dwelling that bridges the wet and the dry, the living and the mineral.

We have two children. Our daughter's birth-root grows upward, already showing the ambitious reach of a potential shaper. Our son's grows down, following mine into the water, into the dark. This is as it should be. The settlement needs both directions.

At night, when the frog-chorus is loudest, we lie on our root-floor and I tell her what I've found in the deep that day. A new crack in a grandmother-bone that means we need to redistribute weight from the west side of the market platform. A shift in the mineral upwelling that will strengthen the roots on the north edge but might over-calcify them in a century or two. The creature's movement patterns, which have shifted slightly — it is favoring the eastern passages now, avoiding something in the west, though I haven't found what.

She tells me about the new growth, the roots that are responding well to training, the ones that are being stubborn, the plans she's making for a structure that will cantilever out over the deep water in a way that has never been tried before. Ambitious. Risky. Beautiful, if it works.

We breathe together in the dark. Four breaths per minute. Sometimes three. The settlement creaks around us, a sound like a huge lung expanding and contracting. Somewhere below, in the flooded passages, water moves through stone tubes that were once alive. Somewhere below that, the creature rests, or moves, or simply is.

I will go down again tomorrow. I will check the granaries, clear the passages, feel the boundaries where life becomes stone. I will place my hand on the first root, the foundation point, the place where everything began. I will hold my breath in the dark and feel the mountain's patience moving through me like a tide.

This is enough. This is what I am for. To know what holds us up. To tend the junction between the living and the dead, the growing and the grown, the moment and the mountain. To be a small, temporary heartbeat in a structure that counts time in centuries.

To go deep. To return. To remember what I've touched in the dark.

The water is patient. The roots grow downward as well as up. Everything tends toward stone, but slowly, so slowly that life has time to happen first.

This is what the deep-tenders know.

This is why we dive.
