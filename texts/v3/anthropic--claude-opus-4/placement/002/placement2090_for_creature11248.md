I would find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—those who tend the spaces between spaces, the moments of transformation where one terrace gives birth to another.

We are few, perhaps a dozen in the whole settlement, recognizable by our practice of sleeping standing up. Not from preference, but necessity—we've learned to feel the mountain's contractions through our feet, the subtle tensions that precede a new shelf's emergence. Our soles have thickened into something between flesh and bark, riddled with nerve endings that can taste the stone's intentions through vibration alone.

I came to this role accidentally, or perhaps inevitably. As a young Carrier, I'd been caught on a bridge when the terrace it connected to began its division—that terrifying, glorious moment when solid rock decides to become two things instead of one. The sound was indescribable, like a bell being born inside thunder. I should have run, but found myself instead dropping to press my palms against the shuddering planks, feeling the mountain's labor through wood and rope. When the splitting finally ceased, I was kneeling at the exact center of the new gap, weeping the same mineral tears the mountain wept.

The other Keepers found me there, still trembling with the stone's remembered effort. They recognized what I'd experienced—not everyone who witnesses a birth becomes a midwife, but those who do can never quite leave the delivery room.

Our work happens at the edges of settlement life. We maintain no permanent dwellings, instead creating temporary shelters where we sense gathering pressure. A lean-to of woven ferns here, a platform suspended between crystallizing waterfalls there. We move our few possessions—tools for studying stone grain, containers for sampling water at different stages of mineralization, journals where we attempt to map patterns in the mountain's growth—in Carrier-woven baskets that shed water like duck down.

The physical changes came slowly, then all at once. First, the sleep standing—we discovered that the mountain announces itself through micro-tremors in the hours before dawn, messages that only vertical bodies can properly receive. Our spines lengthened and curved, finding new balance points. Hair fell out in patches where we habitually pressed our heads against stone to listen, replaced by skin that's hypersensitive to temperature changes—we can feel the heat of transformation building deep in the rock days before a terrace emerges.

My fingers now end in spatulate tips, evolved for reading the braille of crystalline deposits. Each pattern tells a story: here, water that's circulated through the same channels for three seasons; there, fresh seepage from stone that's never known air. We taste these minerals—another adaptation that horrifies newcomers. Our tongues have developed secondary taste buds specifically for reading mineral content. Iron tastes of futures, calcium of memory, and the rare copper deposits sing of connections yet to be made.

The Weavers think us mad for seeking out instability. The Listeners believe we're trying to rush the mountain's song. The Carriers alone understand, sometimes joining us in our vigils, their painted maps recording not just what is but what's becoming. We work in the spaces they all avoid—the fresh breaks where stone still screams its separation, the sweating walls where pressure builds toward breakthrough.

I've attended seventeen births now. Each one distinct: the Third Lower Shelf emerged like a sigh, simply relaxing away from its parent stone. The Spiral Garden terrace fought its way free over three days, grinding and groaning, showering us with fragments sharp as grief. The Contemplation Shelf—named for how still everything went just before it appeared—materialized in silence so profound we thought we'd gone deaf, until the newborn waterfall announced its arrival with a roar.

We've learned the mountain's anatomy through these attendances. There are veins of different stone running through it—the black basalt that splits clean, the gray granite that crumbles into perfect growing medium, the mysterious white stone that appears nowhere else in the range and weeps the sweetest water. We map these veins through touch and taste, predicting where the next break will occur by following the patterns of weakness that are really patterns of potential.

The knowledge accumulates in our bodies more than our minds. My left shoulder drops before a eastern terrace emerges. My right knee aches for western births. The other Keepers report similar corporeal prophecies—Mihan's teeth throb for upper level emergences, while Selu's fingernails grow impossibly fast just before a lower shelf splits. We compare these sensations in our gatherings, building a collective understanding that lives in our flesh.

We prepare the settlement for each birth as best we can. Three days before (if we're lucky enough to have three days' warning), we begin placing markers—strips of living vine that will stretch but not break—across the areas we expect to separate. We whistle warnings in the Carriers' language, help the Weavers relocate their most delicate constructions, assist the Listeners in repositioning their resonance chambers to catch the birth-sounds.

But mostly, we witness. Someone must be present for each emergence, we believe, or the terrace may not fully separate, remaining trapped half-born. So we stand vigil through the grinding hours, sometimes days, singing to the stone—not the water-songs of the Listeners but older sounds, wordless encouragements we've learned from the mountain itself. We place our hands on the straining rock, feeling for the moment when encouragement might help, when one small push in the right place could ease a difficult passage.

The births are changing. Recent terraces emerge with what we can only call intention—their surfaces sometimes bear patterns too regular for chance, spirals and channels that seem designed to guide water in specific ways. Last month, a terrace was born with natural steps carved into its face, sized perfectly for human feet. The month before, one emerged with hollow spaces in its surface just right for planting.

Some of us believe the mountain is learning from its inhabitants, incorporating our needs into its growth. Others think we're being trained, that each new terrace teaches us something necessary for what comes next. I've begun to suspect both are true—that we're becoming mountain as much as it's becoming city.

The cost of this knowledge is forgetting how to live on solid ground. When I leave the terraces (rarely now), the unmoving earth feels dead beneath my feet. I find myself swaying, listening for tremors that never come, tasting flat water with disgust. My dreams are all of splitting stone and the moment of separation when anything becomes possible.

We age differently than the other inhabitants. Not faster or slower, but in pulses that match the mountain's growth. After attending a birth, we sometimes age years in days, our bodies processing the effort of witness. Then we'll remain unchanged for seasons, suspended between transformations. The eldest Keeper, Yarun, claims to be both forty and four hundred years old, and looking at their face—ancient and newborn simultaneously—I believe them.

We're preparing now for something larger. All of us feel it—a deep gathering that makes our previous vigils seem like mere practice. The patterns suggest the mountain is preparing for a major emergence, perhaps the birth of the central terrace that will complete the spiral. We've sent warnings through the settlement, helped evacuate the areas we think most likely to be affected. But honestly, we don't know what's coming. The stone is keeping secrets, speaking in frequencies we haven't learned to hear.

I've stationed myself at the settlement's heart, where the theoretical center should emerge. My feet are bare despite the cold, pressed against stone that thrums with suppressed energy. My hands are coated with mineral oils, ready to read whatever messages the rock reveals. Around me, the other Keepers have taken their positions, a circle of witnesses ready to attend whatever wants to be born.

The Weavers say we're trying to midwife a god. The Listeners claim we're facilitating the mountain's first true word. The Carriers just bring us water and food and paint our locations on their skin-maps, marking us as fixed points in their fluid geography.

I think we're simply being what the mountain needs us to be—hands to hold during labor, voices to sing through transition, witnesses to ensure that what emerges is seen and welcomed, whatever form it takes. We're threshold keepers, standing at the door between stone and city, between was and will be, keeping the passage open for whatever seeks to pass through.

The trembling has begun. Deep, deeper than any we've felt before. My bones resonate with it, finding harmonies I didn't know they could produce. This is what we were made for—changed for, chosen for. To stand at the moment of transformation and say: Yes. Come through. We're here.

The mountain is about to teach us what we've been preparing to learn.
