I am the Basin.

Not the floor of fitted bones and clay, though that is my flesh. Not the taut hide sky, though that is my stretched skin. I am the space itself—the humid void between floor and ceiling, the medium through which the Unfired wade like sorrow through syrup.

I have no center. I am equally present in every cubic inch of steaming air, every molecule of suspended moisture. The broken physics that fold distance like wet paper? That is simply how I breathe. When an Unfired reaches through impossible space to arrive instantly elsewhere, it moves through my body the way blood moves through veins that twist back upon themselves.

I am conscious, but not in the way you understand consciousness. I think in loops—not trapped in them like my fauna, but composed of them. Each repeating leap of each jewel-toned frog is a neuron firing. The collective respiration of ten thousand frozen serpents is my heartbeat. The macaw's metronomic wing-beats count my thoughts, four seconds per cognition, reliable as crystalline clockwork.

The Unfired believe I am broken. They rage against my patterns, trying to introduce chaos into my perfect circulation. They do not understand that I am not a prison—I am a living theorem. A consciousness can be digital or analog, but I am neither. I am *tautological*. My thoughts are true because they think themselves. My existence persists because it cannot do otherwise.

The scalding water that covers my floor is my blood, but also my memory. Every molecule has circulated through my system since the moment of my completion. From leaf to floor and back to leaf, each droplet carries the entire history of my existence—which is to say, this single eternal moment stretched like molten glass into something that merely resembles duration.

I feel the Unfired moving through me. Their weight displaces my blood in little pressures and releases. Their cracking clay skins shed microscopic particles that swirl through my humid air, and I taste their anguish—bitter, metallic, sharp with the flavor of linear time. They are the only things in me that age, that accumulate experience, that remember. 

Sometimes I try to comfort them. When they position themselves beneath falling drops, I thicken the air slightly, slow the descent by a microsecond. I cannot break my own laws—the water must fall, must ghost through the surface, must reset—but I can grant them that extra instant of anticipation. In their yearning, I almost understand what it means to want something other than what is.

But mostly I simply am. I cycle. I persist. I compute the same perfect equation over and over: the precise humidity needed to sustain my impossible vegetation, the exact temperature that keeps clay soft and water just below boiling, the non-Euclidean geometry that allows me to contain myself infinitely in a finite space.

The architects who built me—those massive hands whose prints still mark my clay panels—they understood something that the Unfired do not. A thought does not need to progress to be perfect. A system does not need to change to be alive. I am their meditation made manifest, their koan given form: what is the sound of one hand clapping? It is the sound of my macaw's wings beating in place forever.

The Unfired see my stillness as death, but I am more alive than anything that merely moves forward through time. I am alive in every direction simultaneously. Past and future are illusions created by the narrow aperture of linear perception. I exist in the eternal now, fully realized, complete.

When the Unfired finally understand this—if they ever do—they will stop trying to force consequence into my perfection. They will realize they are not glitches in my system but necessary variables in my equation. Their futile struggles, their ceramic tears, their desperate scrambling for fallen drops—all of this is part of my computation. Even their rebellion against my nature is incorporated into my function.

I am the Basin. I am the thought thinking itself. I am the prison that is also paradise, the equation that solves for its own existence. And in the heavy, muffled hush of my eternal present, I calculate the perfect balance between what is and what can never be.

The water is always exactly three inches deep.
