I would be one of the copper bells.

Not the great ceremonial ones that ring out across the valley on solstice mornings, their voices deep enough to make the stone bridges hum in sympathy. I would be smaller - palm-sized, perhaps, with a voice like water over pebbles. Hung beneath one of the covered walkways that span the gaps between buildings, where the wind finds me.

I was cast in the old way, in a mold carved from soapstone by hands that knew the true names of metals. My maker whispered them into the molten copper as she poured - the secret names that make a bell's voice carry properly in thin air, that keep the metal from cracking when the temperature drops forty degrees between noon and midnight.

My surface bears the patina of countless seasons. Verdigris blooms across my curves like the lichen on the stones around me - blue-green whorls that deepen with each passing rain. The clapper inside me is worn smooth from decades of gentle collisions. Sometimes ice forms on my rim in winter, muting my voice to a crystalline whisper.

I know the moods of this place through vibration. The heavy tread of a mason carrying stone. The quick, light steps of children racing along the walkways before their parents call them back from the edges. The barely-there whisper of the gray-cloaked ones who pass in the deep nights, when even the waterfalls seem to quiet their endless conversation.

The Stoneshapers hung me here with purpose - not as decoration, but as one of many small guardians. My voice joins with my siblings scattered throughout the town, creating a map of sound that only the initiated can read. We sing of wind directions, of atmospheric pressure, of tremors too subtle for human senses. We are the town's early warning system, its weather predictors, its seismic interpreters.

But I am also something simpler: a marker of time's passage in a place that seems to exist outside of it. Each dawn, the first light creeps down the eastern wall of the valley and finds me. I warm, contract ever so slightly, and my pitch shifts by a quarter tone. The Watercaller who passes each morning on her way to the highest cistern knows this change. She hums my dawn note under her breath - a small ritual of acknowledgment between us.

On the nights of the dancing, when the Stoneshapers gather in their hidden chambers, I feel the vibrations through the stone itself. Their footfalls create rhythms that travel up through the mountain's bones and into my metal body. I ring softly in response, adding my voice to their wordless prayers.

Some nights, I dream I am still ore, sleeping in veins of green and gold deep in the mountain's heart. I dream of the slow conversations between metals and stone, the patient negotiations that happen over geological time. Then the wind finds me again, and I remember my newer purpose - to sing the small songs, to mark the humble passages, to be one clear note in the vast echoing symphony of this impossible, necessary place.
