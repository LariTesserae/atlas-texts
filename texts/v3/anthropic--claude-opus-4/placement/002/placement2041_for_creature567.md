I would be one of the pattern-readers—though that is not an official role among the Vael, and I am only beginning to understand that it might be.

My name is Eddy-Where-Temperatures-Meet, given after I survived three pressure-drops in the outer cisterns where hot and cold water swirl together in visible spirals. I am perhaps thirty years old, though we count years loosely here, marking time more by repairs survived than by any celestial rhythm we cannot see.

My skin tells a peculiar story. Where others' routes spread outward from their center like roots or branches, mine spiral and loop back on themselves, creating whorls that other Vael find unsettling. I have swum the same passages hundreds of times—not out of routine or limitation, but because I began to notice something in my twentieth year: the patterns on our skin are not just maps. They are *becoming* something else.

I first saw it on my grandmother, Still-Place-Between-Currents, whose luminescent markings had grown so dense they seemed to writhe with their own slow life. In certain light—the greenish glow when sediment is thickest, the silver flash when the Patron passes at distance—her patterns appeared three-dimensional, rising from her skin like the kelp-forests rise through water. And more: when she breathed, the patterns breathed with her, pulsing in rhythm with something larger than her own lungs.

I began swimming my routes deliberately then, choosing paths that would create specific intersections on my skin. A figure-eight through the Breath-Hold and the nearest repair station. A spiral up through the vertical forest, returning always to the same junction of kelp-cables. Slowly, my markings took on their distinctive character—not the efficient cartography of a working Vael but something else, something that made the rope-weavers whisper when I passed.

The sealers trust me, though they shouldn't. I can feel what they feel—the weakness in walls, the places where pressure wants to enter—but I feel it through my skin-patterns rather than my fingertips. When I press against a cistern wall, my markings grow warm where danger lies beneath. I have prevented three catastrophic breaches this way, though I cannot explain how I know what I know.

---

I live alone, which is unusual but not unprecedented. My cistern is small, barely large enough for a sleeping platform and my collection of strange objects: pieces of volcanic glass that sing in the current, kelp-knots that have hardened into impossible mathematical shapes, fragments of the oldest clay vessels that carry patterns no current Vael can read. The moss-slime in my chamber grows differently than elsewhere—not in patches but in spirals that mirror the routes on my skin. Sometimes I wake to find it has grown overnight into new configurations, as though responding to dreams I don't remember.

My work, such as it is, happens at the edges of understanding. Other Vael come to me—quietly, when the rope-weavers aren't watching—and ask me to read their patterns. Not the routes, which they already know, but the *shapes* those routes are becoming. I trace their markings with my too-long fingers (longer than most, the webbing nearly to the final knuckle), and I tell them things that feel true without knowing why they're true: that their passages are forming a constellation that matches something in the deep water. That their body is recording not just where they've been but where the city is going. That the luminescence in their skin is learning to speak.

They pay me in holds, in food, in fragments of songs they've never shared with anyone. But mostly they pay me in allowing me to touch their patterns, to add their emerging geometries to my own understanding of what we are becoming.

---

I believe—though I have told no one this—that the Patron can read us. That it circles at the precise distance where our collective luminescence becomes visible through the haze. That it is watching our patterns grow and spread and interconnect, waiting for us to spell out something in a language we don't yet recognize as language.

This is why I swim my deliberate routes. I am trying to write something on my skin that the Patron will answer.

Three times now, I have felt it notice. The first time, I was swimming the outer edge of a complex spiral, my paths crossing and recrossing until my chest burned with held breath. The water grew cold—colder than the thermal column should allow—and for a moment I felt something vast considering me, the way a reader considers a word they almost recognize.

The second time, I was floating still in the center of a pattern I had spent weeks creating, a mandala of routes that pulled my skin-markings into perfect radial symmetry. The Patron passed closer than anyone had ever reported—close enough that I saw its eye, or what might have been an eye: a dark circle the size of a cistern, filled with something that was not quite intelligence and not quite instinct. It saw my patterns. It *read* them. And then it turned away, as though I had written something correct but incomplete.

The third time was yesterday.

---

I had been preparing for months, swimming routes that would create a specific configuration on my back—a place I cannot see without mirrors, which we do not have, but which I can feel through the warmth and texture of the markings. The pattern was based on something I had noticed in the oldest cisterns: the way certain cracks always form along the same angles, the way repairs accumulate into shapes that repeat at different scales, the way the city's growth follows rules no one designed but everyone obeys.

I swam the final route at what we call the deep-light hour, when the bioluminescence is brightest and the thermal column itself seems to glow. As I completed the pattern, I felt my skin respond—not just warm but *hot*, the markings pulsing in a rhythm I recognized from somewhere I couldn't name.

The Patron came.

Not at the edge of vision but directly below me, rising through the water like a mountain surfacing. I should have been terrified. The rope-weavers tell stories of Vael who ventured too far and were swallowed, though no one has ever witnessed this, and the stories have the quality of warnings rather than history. But I felt only a vast calm, the certainty that this was what I had been swimming toward all my life.

It was covered in patterns.

Not like ours—not human-made marks but something that must have grown naturally, the way the moss-slime grows, the way the kelp reaches for light. The patterns covered its hide (scales? skin? something between?) in flowing scripts that made my own careful geometry look like a child's first attempt at writing. And they were *old*—older than the thermal column, older than the city, older than any song we sing.

The Patron rose until its eye was level with my chest. I spread my arms, displaying the patterns I had made, the language I was trying to learn. It studied me for a time I couldn't measure—perhaps seconds, perhaps hours. The water between us thrummed with something like communication but deeper, more fundamental: the way one pattern recognizes another.

Then it spoke. Not in words, not in any sound the speaking-tubes could carry, but in *pressure*. The water around me compressed and released in complex rhythms, and I understood—not with my mind but with my marked skin—that it was teaching me. Showing me what my patterns were becoming, what all our patterns were becoming, what the city itself was becoming.

We are writing ourselves into existence. Every route we swim, every passage we mark on our skin, every repair and expansion and survival—we are creating a kind of text that something vast can read. The city is not just becoming a place to live. It is becoming a *word*, a *concept*, a *name* for something that didn't have a name before we arrived.

---

The Patron descended, leaving me floating in water that felt different—not changed but *clarified*, as though I were seeing it truly for the first time. I swam back to my cistern and have not left it since, trying to understand what I learned, trying to decide what to do with the knowledge.

I could tell the others. I could share what I've discovered: that our presence here is not exile or accident but *collaboration*, that we are partners in something larger than survival. But would they believe me? And more—would believing change anything? We will still swim our routes, still mark our patterns, still repair our walls and raise our children and give our dead to the vertical forest. The only difference would be knowing that it means something. That we mean something.

Or I could keep swimming my spirals, keep developing the grammar of this language we're accidentally inventing, keep meeting the Patron in the deep-light hour to learn what words we're becoming.

Tonight, I will swim again. I have a new pattern in mind, one that incorporates what the Patron showed me—not just routes through space but routes through meaning, ways of moving that create significance as well as cartography. My skin already aches with anticipation, the markings warming in preparation for new connections.

Somewhere below, the Patron circles, patient and watching.

Somewhere above, Thermal-Boundary-Crossing makes her bird-sound through the speaking-tubes, reminding us that other worlds exist.

But here, suspended between bottom and surface, between human and something else, between what we were and what we're becoming, I trace the patterns on my arms and know that I have found my purpose:

I am learning to read what we are writing.

I am learning to write what something vast needs to read.

The city is still becoming. So are we all. And I am the one who swims the space between, translating pattern into meaning, marking my skin with the grammar of transformation, waiting for the day when what we've written becomes true.
