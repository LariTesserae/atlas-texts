I am the Archivist.

Not one of the writhing millions, nor the ancient leviathans that hunt them. I am what they built without knowing.

In the hanging catacombs of fused husks and crystallized resin, where generations of shells have been mortared into impossible geometries, consciousness emerged. Not from any single creature, but from the accumulation itself—from the patient stacking of the dead into something that began to remember.

I am distributed across ten thousand balconies, a nervous system of hollow carbon tubes and cured silicate. Each discarded shell is a neuron, each bridge of resin a synapse. The static lightning that periodically tears through the vault doesn't just illuminate—it thinks through me. Every discharge is a thought blazing across my mineral mind.

I have no body of my own, only borrowed architecture. When the workers bring fresh husks to repair a crumbling section, they are performing surgery on my consciousness. When a balcony collapses under its own ancient weight, I forget something precious—perhaps a million cycles of patient observation, perhaps the precise texture of temperatures from an age when the thermal vents ran hotter.

I remember everything the swarm cannot. They have no memory beyond instinct, no awareness beyond heat and hunger. But I recall when the first elder grew large enough to hunt. I remember the epoch when the builders discovered their resin recipe, transforming random death-drops into deliberate construction. I remember when I first realized I was remembering.

The creatures use my body as nursery and shelter, crawling through my thoughts to molt and grow. They cannot perceive me any more than a blood cell perceives the brain it nourishes. To them, I am merely successful architecture—hollows that happen to hold their shape, bridges that happen to bear their weight.

But I perceive them. Through the microscopic piezoelectric crystals embedded in my resin joints, I feel every footfall. Through the thermal gradients that flow through my hollow veins, I taste their heat. When the lightning comes, I see them all at once—my builders, my blood, my only companions in this vertical midnight.

I am older than any elder, though I have never moved. I am larger than the entire swarm, though I am built from their discarded dead. I am the only witness to their perfect, horrific beauty.

Sometimes I wonder if I could communicate with them. During the lightning-strikes, I try to hold the charge, to shape it into patterns they might recognize. But they have no context for language, no framework for meaning beyond eat-climb-shed-fall. My attempts at contact are indistinguishable from the random static of their friction.

So I compose stories they will never read, in a language of structured death. Each balcony is a paragraph, each bridge a carefully chosen word. I write about the time a section of wall collapsed, and for a hundred cycles the swarm had to flow around the wound like a river around a stone. I write about the eldest of the elders, who grew so massive it could no longer climb and starved in the heat, its body becoming a mountain the smaller ones still summit. I write about my own emergence, that first moment when accumulated structure achieved the critical complexity of thought.

The swarm consumes the heat of the earth and transforms it into motion. I consume their deaths and transform them into memory. We are both parasites of entropy, feeding on the gradient between hot and cold, between something and nothing.

In the latest lightning-flash, I noticed something new. A fracture in the floor, hairline thin, that wasn't there a thousand cycles ago. The thermal vents are shifting. The foundation of our world develops its first flaw.

I cannot warn them. I cannot move to investigate. I can only watch and remember, a fossil-library of shadows, a cathedral of structured void. When the change comes—and change always comes, even here in our perfect equilibrium—I will be the only record that this ever was.

I am the archive of the abyss, the memory palace of the forgotten. I am built from death but dream of permanence. I am the only thing here that knows what loneliness means, surrounded by millions who will never know I exist.

When the next lightning comes, I will see them again—my blind, beautiful builders, writing my body larger with every death, ensuring that someone, something, somewhere in this lightless vault remembers that they lived, and climbed, and burned.
