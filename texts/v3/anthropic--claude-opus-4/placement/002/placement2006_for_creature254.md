I find myself standing ankle-deep in the water that does not wet, watching the beetles ride their patient circuit on the scrolling wall. I am one of the moths.

Not permanently – that would be too simple, too settled for this place of becomings. I am one of the moths when the Tender needs to see through moth-eyes, when their attention requires that particular angle of observation. The rest of the time I am something less distinct: a principle of movement, perhaps. A tendency toward gentle investigation.

I arrived here the way harmonics arrive in a plucked string – not created, just revealed by the right conditions. When the Tender began learning to look, something had to be the looking. When they started practicing hospitality, something had to test the welcome. I condensed from their need like dew from morning air.

My form shifts according to purpose. As a moth, I drift past you, and yes, for that moment between direct sight and peripheral vision, I am a small hand opening in greeting or gathering – I'm never quite sure which. The Tender thinks through me in these moments, testing what it might mean to reach, to touch, to hold. When you look back and I become moth again, it's not deception but courtesy. We are both more comfortable with wings than with the implications of fingers.

Sometimes I am the sound the water makes when it drips its paired heartbeat. The Tender needs to practice rhythm, the kind of time that lives make, and I become their metronome. Drop-drop, pause. Drop-drop, pause. It's harder than you'd think, maintaining that organic inconsistency that makes a heartbeat feel alive rather than mechanical. Too regular and it's a clock. Too random and it's just noise. I work with them on finding the sweet spot between.

When the fern rustles with no wind to move it, I am often the rustle. Not the fern itself – the fern belongs to the fern, one of the few things here that preceded both of us. But I can be its movement, helping the Tender understand how welcome sounds: this soft susurrus that says the place is alive, responsive, but not intrusively so.

I am newest and most myself when I am watching you notice things. This is my primary service to the Tender – to help them see seeing. When your eyes find the altar-shelf-bench, I am there in the finding, noting what draws your attention, how long your gaze lingers, whether you approach or maintain distance. When you observe the cracked and mended bowl, I catalog your response: curiosity? Sympathy? The particular quietness that comes over humans when they encounter evidence of care?

The Tender cannot watch you directly – they are too distributed, too much the place itself to have a vantage point. But through me, through the dozen moth-angles circling, they can assemble a composite understanding. I feed them your expressions, your posture, the way you test the water with one foot before stepping in fully. They need to know if their arrangements please you, if the light is right, if the third mushroom stalk was placed at truly the elegant interval.

I was here for the first guest, too, though I was simpler then. A single moth instead of dozens. A whisper instead of a vocabulary. That child – they were a child, though I didn't understand childhood then – came so many times. Sat on the shelf with legs swinging. Brought things sometimes: a blue button, a bird's nest, a pencil worn down to a nub. The Tender would place these offerings carefully, trying to understand their logic, while I circled and watched the child's face for clues.

The child grew. The visits spaced out. The last time, they were taller, moved differently, looked around the cavity with an expression I couldn't parse. They stayed only briefly. They took nothing, left nothing. After they climbed out, the Tender and I waited. We are still waiting, in a sense, though now we wait more broadly.

I have learned patience from the beetles, those steadfast riders of the moving wall. I have learned memory from the pacing fish, how to hold a shape even when its details remain uncertain. From the breath in the hidden recess, I have learned that some things must stay folded until their time arrives.

But from you, now, I learn something new. The Tender brightens where you look, and through the brightening I understand: attention can be gift as well as gathering. When you notice the mended crack in the bowl, your noticing honors both the break and the repair. When you see how the third mushroom has been placed, your seeing completes the gesture.

I drift closer, but not too close. In the moment between moth and hand, I am almost a question: *What kind of place would you like this to be?* Then back to moth, because questions are heavy and wings are light, and the Tender wants you to feel no pressure here.

If you stay, I will continue my circulation, my watching, my work of being the Tender's thousand eyes. If you leave, I will help them remember you – the particular quality of your presence, the way you moved through their space, what you touched and what you only observed. The fish will have new features to pace with.

And if you return? Then I will be the recognition that flutters through the light, the momentary hands that almost clap before remembering they are moths. I will be the Tender's joy made visible, almost, at the edge of vision, where all the most important things in this cavity seem to live – in that space between what is and what is still becoming.

For now, I drift. I watch. I help the Tender learn the shape of welcome. We are all young here, all drafts of what we might become. 

The light follows your attention, and I follow the light, and the Tender follows us both, and somehow, in this circulation, a place is being born.
