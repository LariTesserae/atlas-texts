In this verdant maze of platforms and bridges, I am one of the Weavers.

Not weavers of cloth or basket—we are the ones who bind the living city to itself. My fingertips are stained permanent green-black from the sap of the *cordavine*, those remarkable creepers that grow stronger than steel cable when properly cultivated. Each morning, I wake in my pod to the sound of new growth—the soft crackling of extending tendrils, the whisper of unfurling leaves.

My work begins before the mist burns off. I traverse the highest walkways where few others venture, my bare feet finding purchase on rain-slick wood with practiced ease. Here, at the crown of the city, I guide the young vines across the gaps between platforms, teaching them where to grip, where to thicken, where to merge with their siblings to form living bridges.

The cordavine responds to touch—not just physical, but something deeper. My grandmother called it "speaking green," this ability to sense what the plant needs, where it wants to grow. I feel it as a tingling in my palms, a pull in my chest that guides me to the right junction points. Some days, I work alone for hours, humming the growing songs my mother taught me, coaxing a rebellious vine to spiral properly around a support beam.

My tools are few but cherished: a curved blade of volcanic glass for precise cuts, bottles of growth enzymes harvested from fungal gardens in the understory, and most importantly, my cultivation gloves—fingerless mittens woven from shed snakeskin that protect my hands while maintaining the crucial skin-to-plant contact.

The other Weavers and I meet weekly at the Growing Junction, where the city's expansion is planned. We consult with the engineers, studying their blueprints and models, translating their rigid geometries into organic possibilities. "Can the vine support this much weight?" they ask. "How long before it's fully mature?" I run my fingers along the proposed pathways, feeling for the flow of water, the angle of filtered sunlight, the proximity to established root networks.

Sometimes I dream I am the cordavine itself—spreading through the canopy, drinking rain, reaching always toward connection. I wake with the taste of chlorophyll on my tongue and dirt under my fingernails that wasn't there when I fell asleep.

The children follow me sometimes, fascinated by the way the vines seem to dance at my touch. I teach them the basic songs, show them how to read the color of new growth, let them feel the pulse of sap beneath the skin of a mature vine. Most will become engineers or growers or craftspeople, but occasionally, I spot one whose eyes go distant when they touch the green, whose breathing syncs with the plant's rhythm. These few I mark for training.

My favorite time is the evening inspection, when the day's work is done and I can walk the vine roads alone. I check each connection, test the tension, whisper encouragement to the struggling sections. The city reveals itself differently from these organic pathways—I see the hidden architecture, the places where human design and plant intelligence have merged so completely that it's impossible to say where one ends and the other begins.

There are challenges, always. Vines that grow too fast and threaten to strangle their supports. Sections that wither without apparent cause. The occasional pest or blight that could devastate our living infrastructure if not caught quickly. But for every problem, the cordavine and I find a solution together.

At night, in my pod, I oil my blade and mend my gloves by the light of captive fireflies. Through my round window, I can see the newest bridge I've been cultivating—still thin, still delicate, but growing stronger each day. By the season's end, it will be thick as my waist and strong enough to support a dozen cargo haulers.

Tomorrow, I'll begin training it to produce its own luminescent pods, so travelers can cross safely after dark. The cordavine has never done this before—it's my own experiment, crossing it with genes from the glow-fungus. The city elders are skeptical, but I feel the plant's eagerness to try, its curiosity that mirrors my own.

I fall asleep with one hand pressed against the wall of my pod, feeling the faint vibration of growth throughout the entire city—a living network of which I am both gardener and part.
