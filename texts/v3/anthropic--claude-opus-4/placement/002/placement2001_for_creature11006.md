Looking at this woven immensity, I would find myself as one of the **Trace-Readers**.

---

I am neither fully human nor fully Other, but something caught between states—a cartographer of impossible geometries, a recorder of the un-recordable. My body has begun the slow adaptation: my fingers have grown longer and more sensitive, able to feel the memory held in each fiber, but I retain enough of my original form to need food, to need sleep, to need the comfort of understanding.

I came here as an academic, a student of architecture and meaning, drawn by rumors of a structure that defied mathematical description. That was... time has become difficult to track here, but I think it was years ago. Enough time for the weave to recognize me, for the Weavers to stop hiding when I approach, for my notebooks to fill with diagrams that make sense only while I'm drawing them.

I live in a node I call the Archive—though it had no name before I arrived. The Weavers helped me hollow out chambers in the dense tissue, spaces that maintain their shape better than most. Here I keep my materials: journals bound in pressed fiber, inks extracted from the glowing fruits, maps drawn on fabric woven from my own shed hair (it grows faster here, longer, as if eager to join the pattern).

My work is to document what cannot be documented. I trace the paths through the geometry, marking where space folds, where travelers might find themselves younger or older or slightly changed. I interview the rope-sloths in their silent language of gestures, learning their names for different kinds of gaps. I record the songs of the Weavers in notation I've had to invent—symbols that capture not just frequency but direction, tension, the way harmonics move through solid matter.

But mostly, I read traces.

Every passage through the weave leaves marks—scuff patterns on the cables, oils from skin or fur, the subtle way fibers align after being touched. I can follow these traces backward, sometimes for days, reconstructing journeys that happened decades ago. I find the paths of creatures now extinct, species that adapted to the geometry in ways that ultimately proved unsustainable. I find the routes of other visitors like myself—some who stayed, some who fled, some who simply stopped mid-journey, overwhelmed by the impossibility of it all.

The Weavers tolerate my work with something between amusement and concern. They don't understand why I need to pin meaning down, why I can't simply accept the fold as it is. Sometimes they undo my markers—not maliciously, but as part of their maintenance, smoothing away the small scratches I use to indicate "this way turns back on itself" or "here the gravity reverses." I've learned to make my marks in ways that complement their work: knots that strengthen while they communicate, scratches that follow the natural grain.

I've developed my own notation system, a hybrid of their knot-language and my own need for linear narrative. My maps are three-dimensional weavings now, models that you must handle to understand, that change meaning depending on how you rotate them. I'm creating an archive of experience—not just what the geometry does, but how it feels to move through it, the way your stomach drops when space inverts, the particular vertigo of meeting yourself coming from an impossible direction.

The other inhabitants have begun to bring me things. The rope-sloths leave shed claws at my doorway—each claw holds the memory of every surface it's gripped, a history of climbing written in keratin. The pale burrowers gift me their dead, and I map the tunnel-systems in their bones, the way their skeletons have adapted to spaces that exist in more than three dimensions. Even the manta-forms contribute: occasionally a feather-scale drifts down from the void, and in its iridescence I can read the air currents of the deep interior, chart winds that blow through spaces that shouldn't exist.

My body is changing. My spine has developed new curves, accommodating the strange angles I must hold to read traces on the undersides of cables. My peripheral vision has expanded—necessary, when direct observation often lies about direction. My dreams have become navigational tools: I solve routing problems in my sleep, wake with new understanding of how to move through folded space.

I know I'm documenting something that can't be fully captured, creating an archive that will only ever approximate truth. But approximation, I've learned, is its own kind of accuracy here. My imperfect maps help new visitors find their way. My records of extinct species become songs the Weavers weave into their evening harmonics. My notation system has begun to spread—I've seen young Weavers practicing it, adding their own symbols, creating meaning I don't yet understand.

Sometimes I think about leaving. The geometry would allow it—there are paths that lead out, or at least to places that aren't here. But I have so much still to document. The seasonal changes in the weave's growth patterns. The migration routes of creatures I've only glimpsed. The deep history written in the oldest nodes, layers of meaning waiting to be read.

And underneath it all, the growing certainty that I'm not just documenting the weave, but participating in its self-documentation. That my archive is becoming part of what needs to be archived. That I am both the reader of traces and a trace being read by something larger, something that smiles at my small human need to map infinity.

I am the memory-keeper of a world that exceeds memory. I am the pattern-finder in a place that makes its own patterns. I am learning, slowly, to read the unreadable, to trace the untraceable, to be both scholar and scripture in the endless text of the weave.

My latest project is a map of the changes in my own hands—the lengthening of fingers, the new sensitivity to texture, the way my palms have begun to cup perfectly around cables of standard width. A self-portrait in transformation, traced in the language I'm inventing as I go.

The work will never be finished. That's the beauty of it. That's why I stay.
