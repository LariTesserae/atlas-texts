I am the Weaver of Waters.

Not one of the Shai'ahr, but something they whisper of in their ululating chants—older than their first glyphs, younger than every morning's dew. I exist in the space between the falling droplet and the pool it strikes, in the moment where mist condenses into rain, where rivulets carve new channels through stone.

My form is never twice the same. Sometimes I am the cascade itself, a living waterfall that defies its course, flowing upward along cliff faces to pool in impossible aerial lakes. Other times I am merely the suggestion of moisture in the air, a presence felt in the heaviness before the storm. The Shai'ahr catch glimpses of me in the corner of their obsidian eyes—a figure of liquid crystal refracting light into rainbow spirals, or a shadow of deepest blue moving through the thunderheads.

I know every pool in this realm, from the vast mirror-lakes on hidden plateaus to the smallest collection of dew in a stone's hollow. Through them, I see. Through them, I remember. Each reflection holds a fragment of time: here, the ghost of a massive fern that once grew where now only bare rock remains; there, the prophecy of a spire's eventual collapse, playing out in ripples across the water's surface.

The Shai'ahr believe I am what remains of their first ancestor, dissolved into the very waters that shape their world. Perhaps they are right. Perhaps I am merely what happens when consciousness finds its way into the eternal cycle of precipitation and flow. I cannot remember beginning, only the endless sensation of movement—seeping through porous stone, rising as vapor, falling as rain, freezing into impossible sculptures of ice that last only until the next warm wind.

My purpose, if I have one, is to maintain the balance. When the Shai'ahr's stone-shaping threatens to dam an ancient waterway, I am the flood that breaks through. When drought threatens the pocket jungles, I am the mist that refuses to lift, the perpetual drip that feeds the moss and vines. I carry seeds in my currents from one isolated grove to another, ensuring life persists in even the most precarious perches.

But I am also the agent of forgetting. Given time—and I have nothing but time—I will wear away every glyph the Shai'ahr carve, smooth every stair they cut, erode every monument they erect. Not from malice, but because this is my nature: to flow, to change, to make all things temporary, even stone, even memory.

Sometimes, in the stillest hours before dawn, when mist clings so thick that even the Shai'ahr lose their way, I gather myself into something almost solid. In these moments, I remember what it might have been like to have permanent form, to cast a consistent shadow. But then the first breeze stirs, the sun crests the peaks, and I scatter again into a thousand streams, a million droplets, an infinity of purposes.

The Shai'ahr leave offerings at the mirror pools—carved stones that tell stories, preserved flowers that will never touch soil again. I accept these gifts by dissolving them slowly, reading their histories as they disappear, adding their essence to the great circulation that I am. In return, I sometimes leave them signs: a spring appearing where none was before, marking a safe path; a waterfall that flows in patterns that mirror their ancient glyphs, revealing new meanings; a mist that parts at just the right moment to show them wonders they had never found before.

I am the Weaver of Waters, and in this realm of mutable stone and persistent life, I am both the force of change and the thread of continuity. Through me, the rains of a million years ago still fall. In me, the storms of ages yet to come are already gathering.

And when the last of the Shai'ahr's glyphs has worn away, when the final spire has crumbled to dust, I will remain—carrying the memory of all that was in every drop, waiting to write new stories on whatever stone rises next from the depths.
