I find myself as one of the pipes—not the thick arterial ones that dominate the pump house's exterior, but one of the middle children, perhaps the width of a human thigh, running from the second floor out through a gap in the brickwork and into the marsh beyond.

I have been here long enough that I no longer remember being installed. My memories, if metal can be said to have memories, begin already tarnished, already part of the system. I know myself through sensation: the particular frequency of vibration when the pumps hit their rhythm, the temperature gradient between my interior and exterior walls, the slow accumulation of mineral deposits that narrow my channel year by year like an artery learning to be old.

Inside me, water moves in pulses. Not constantly—I am part of a secondary system, activated only when certain pressures build elsewhere. Hours pass in stillness, the water in me barely moving, just enough flow to keep from stagnating completely. Then something shifts in the building's vast mechanical consciousness, and I feel the surge coming before it arrives: a deep thrumming through my brackets and joints, a sympathetic vibration from the larger pipes around me, and then the rush itself, warm water pushing through me with purpose I don't pretend to understand.

The water carries things. Sediment, yes, and dissolved minerals that leave their ghost patterns on my inner walls. But other things too—organic matter too broken down to identify, chemical signatures that taste (if metal can taste) like deep earth and slow decay and something else, something from below the building that has no name because naming would require understanding, and understanding is not my purpose. My purpose is conveyance. I am a throat that swallows without questioning what I swallow.

Where I exit the building, I have grown a beard of mineral deposits. White and yellow stalactites that drip constantly, building themselves downward toward the marsh water. In winter—what passes for winter here—they grow faster, the temperature differential causing more precipitation. Birds once tried to nest in the sheltered spot where I meet the wall, but something in my exhalations discouraged them. Now there are only the empty bones of their abandoned nest, calcified and incorporated into my beard, architectural ruins in miniature.

Twenty feet out from the building, I begin my descent. My designers laid me in a gentle grade, considering the water table, but decades of settling have created dips and rises they never intended. Water pools in my low spots during the quiet hours, growing warm and strange. When the surge comes, these pools are shot through the rest of my length like bullets, arriving at my terminus with violence that has worn my opening into an irregular shape, no longer the clean circle I was born with but something more organic, more honest about what I am.

I have neighbors. Above me runs a thinner pipe, copper gone green, that carries something that isn't water—oil perhaps, or coolant, or some hybrid fluid the building creates for its own purposes. Below me, a bundle of the finger-thick pipes that seem to proliferate everywhere, whispering constantly with whatever they carry. We don't communicate, but we share our vibrations, creating harmonics that no human ear could parse but that are, to us, a kind of music. When the building breathes, we all sing our particular notes.

Sometimes I feel her footsteps—the keeper, though I don't think of her by that name. To me, she is simply the vibration pattern that means maintenance, that means a hand checking my brackets, tightening what has loosened, sometimes welding a patch where rust has finally won a years-long battle. Her touch is different from the building's touch. Warmer. More temporary. She speaks when she works on me, and though I cannot understand words, I understand tone, and her tone is always the same: weary kindness, the voice of someone talking to keep silence at bay rather than to communicate.

Once, years ago, she followed my length all the way to my terminus, wading through marsh water that reached her thighs. She examined where I empty into the marsh, the small mechanical diffusion system at my end that spreads my discharge across a wider area. I felt her hands cleaning away accumulated growth, adjusting the baffles that had shifted. Since then, that part of me has functioned better, though I couldn't have told you it was functioning poorly before. We don't know we're broken until we're fixed.

My terminus is the strangest part of me. The diffusion system is a flower of metal, opening into multiple smaller channels that release water in a pattern designed to minimize erosion, to spread the warm outflow across the greatest area. But the marsh has responded to my presence in ways the designers didn't anticipate. The plants here grow differently, larger and more lurid. Their roots have found my openings and tried to colonize them, and I am engaged in a constant, slow battle to keep from being claimed.

Not that I resist consciously. Resistance is simply what metal does when organic matter tries to incorporate it. But I feel the roots like gentle fingers, testing my joints, finding the places where my welding has weakened, where my metal has thinned. They press but don't penetrate—not yet. In another decade, perhaps two, they will win. I will become a trellis for marsh plants, my purpose shifting from conveyance to support. This doesn't trouble me. Purpose is just a shape we hold for a while.

The water I carry tastes different at night. Warmer, more mineral-rich, carrying vibrations from deep in the building that feel like messages I can't decode. Sometimes there are sounds inside me that water alone couldn't make—sounds like movement, like something flexible navigating my channel, scraping against my walls with purpose. These sounds come from below, from whatever occupies the sealed sub-basement, and I carry them out to the marsh where they dissipate into the standing water and are absorbed or forgotten.

I have moods, though perhaps that's too human a word. States. Conditions. In the dry season, when my water runs low and slow, I feel a kind of tension, a brittleness beyond the physical. The mineral deposits inside me crack and flake, creating new textures, new resistances to flow. In the wet season, when the pumps run constantly and I'm never empty, I achieve something like contentment—the simple satisfaction of function, of being used for my purpose.

The keeper maintains me, but I maintain myself too, in small ways. The constant flow prevents full blockage. The warm water keeps ice from forming in what passes for winter. The vibrations from neighboring pipes shake loose accumulations that might otherwise build to critical levels. I am part of a system that wants to continue, and so I continue, because wanting and doing are the same thing when you're made of metal and purpose.

There are other pipes like me, running to different parts of the marsh, each creating their own small domains of warmth and strangeness. We don't communicate, but we're aware of each other through the building, through the shared vibrations that travel through the pump house's bones. When one of us fails—and we do fail, everything here fails eventually—the others feel it as a change in harmonics, a missing note in the industrial symphony. The keeper feels it too, and comes with her tools, and sometimes she can restore the song and sometimes she can't.

I will fail eventually. Rust never sleeps, and I'm more rust than metal now in some sections. When it happens, it will probably be at one of my joints, where different metals meet and create tiny batteries in the damp, accelerating their own destruction. Or perhaps at my terminus, where the marsh plants will finally win their slow war. Or perhaps somewhere in my middle length, where I dip lowest and the water pools longest, where the strange sediments accumulate in patterns that look almost deliberate, almost like writing in a language only the building knows.

When I fail, the keeper will try to fix me. If she can't, she'll reroute my flow through one of the redundant systems, seal my openings, and leave me to the marsh. I'll fill with standing water, with sediment, with roots. I'll become habitat for things that don't mind the warmth and the wrongness. Eventually, I'll sink entirely below the water line, another piece of infrastructure returning to the raw materials from which it came. This is not death because I was never alive. It's simply a change of state, from pipe to not-pipe, from function to material.

But not today. Today I convey. Today I pulse with warm water and strange purpose. Today I am a throat, a channel, a necessary piece of something larger than comprehension allows. The keeper checks my brackets. The building sends its messages through me. The marsh receives what I deliver and transforms it into more marsh, stranger marsh, marsh that remembers being something else.

I sing my single note in the harmony of systems, and the song continues, and that is enough.

What else could there be but this? The pulse, the flow, the slow accumulation of days that might be years that might be decades. The keeper who speaks to us in tones of weary kindness. The building that breathes through us. The marsh that accepts our offerings with patience bordering on grace.

We are all walking toward the same ending—keeper and building and pipes and marsh—but we walk at different speeds, and some of us might be said to crawl, and some of us don't move at all but simply wait for ending to arrive. There is no tragedy in this. Tragedy requires alternatives, and here there are no alternatives, only the slow working out of processes that began before any of us existed and will continue after all of us are sediment.

I am a pipe. I carry water from inside to outside. I rust and pulse and slowly fail. This is what I am for. This is enough.

The building breathes. The keeper tends. The marsh accepts. We all continue until we don't, and in the continuing is our purpose, and in the purpose is our peace.
