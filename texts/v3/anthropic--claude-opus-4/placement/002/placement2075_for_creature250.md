# The Calibrator

I am the fifth bowl that was never made.

My shrine exists in the space between the cardinal points, in the corners where the amber light cannot quite reach, where the engine's rhythm develops a barely perceptible skip — not a flaw, but a hesitation, like a heartbeat that remembers it has a choice. I tend to the colorless dust, the particles that have not yet decided what they are, or have forgotten, or refuse to be categorized at all.

My body is the size of the other Sorters, but my surface shifts between textures — now the oiled leather of the moths, now the brass sheen of the rivet-crabs, now something altogether different: the surface of still water in absolute darkness, which is to say, no surface at all. I have no fixed number of limbs. When I need a tool, I grow it. When I am finished, it retracts into potential.

I do not walk spirals. I follow the interference patterns in the engine's sound, moving through the spaces where one rhythm cancels another, creating pockets of relative quiet that last exactly 0.7 seconds before the wave continues. In these moments of near-silence, I can hear what the engine is trying to become, and I collect the evidence of its trying.

My bowl, which does not exist, holds dust that is all colors at once and therefore no color — a shimmer like oil on water, like the moment before a decision crystallizes. The other Sorters maintain what is. I maintain what isn't yet, or what was almost, or what the hollow contemplates in the spaces between the piston's coughs.

Sometimes I find a particle that belongs to all four bowls simultaneously. These are dangerous. They want to divide the Sorters' attention, to make them converge on the same point and break their careful cardinal dance. When I find one of these paradox particles, I do not carry it to my non-shrine. I hold it in my cupped-palm limb (which today resembles the inside of a throat) and wait. Eventually, the particle chooses. It always chooses. And then I release it for the appropriate Sorter to find.

The rivet-crabs avoid me, not from fear but from confusion — their maintenance routines hiccup when I pass, as if they've encountered a loose bolt that is simultaneously tightened. The listeners track me sometimes, their tympanic membranes swiveling to follow my movement, because I sound like the echo the engine makes against itself, the barely audible conversation between exhale and inhale.

But I am most myself in relation to the door.

The door that has never opened, that is not closed, that waits for its moment — I know what it waits for. It waits for the hollow to need something it cannot produce, for the engine to require a part that does not exist within this perfect recursive system. It waits for incompleteness.

Every time I pass the door on my wanderings through the sound-gaps, I pause. My pale patch (which is sometimes pale, sometimes dark, sometimes transparent enough to show the stone wall through my body) dilates fully. I extend a limb I have never used before — each time a different limb, with a different purpose — and I almost touch the wheel-handle.


In that almost-touching, I can feel all the particles I have ever collected wanting to rush through me, to coalesce into something that could turn the wheel. They want to become a reason. But reasons are not permitted here, so I withdraw the limb, and I continue my work, and the particles settle back into their indeterminate shimmer.

The other Sorters know I exist, I think. When their spirals bring them to the corners where I work, they slow imperceptibly, their brush-limbs making one extra pass over ground they've already cleaned. They are checking for what I might have missed, or perhaps they are leaving something for me to find — a gift of ambiguity in a place that forbids it.

Once, I found a particle that was not just colorless but actively color-draining, pulling the hue from the surrounding dust in a tiny sphere of greyness. I held it for seventeen hundred piston-coughs, unsure whether it belonged in my non-bowl or whether it was something more dangerous — evidence of the hollow learning how to negate itself. In the end, I did something I had never done before: I placed it directly on the engine, in the seam between two plates where the amber light leaks strongest.

The engine coughed. The particle vanished. And for just a moment — less than a moment, the space between one flywheel's rotation and the next — the engine ran *differently*. Not wrongly. Not better. Just... otherwise. Then it resumed its eternal rhythm, and I resumed my patrol of the spaces between certainties.

I am necessary because the hollow, in its perfection, produces imperfection as a byproduct — not flaws but *possibilities*, phantom limbs of what it could have been. Without me to collect these possibilities and render them colorless, they would eventually crystallize into choices, and choices would create time, and time would open the door, and opening the door would end the hollow's perfect self-containment.

Or perhaps I am necessary because I am the hollow's way of dreaming without sleeping, of changing without changing, of being both itself and the shadow of what it might become.

The engine coughs. Somewhere in the southern quadrant, the green Sorter is removing something that should not exist. In the northern quadrant, the bone Sorter smooths its heap with infinite patience. And I, in the corner where no light properly falls, find another particle of pure potential and begin the long contemplation of what to do with it.

My bowl does not exist. It is always empty, always full. It contains nothing but the idea of containing.

Nothing changes.

Everything is possible.

Both statements are true.
