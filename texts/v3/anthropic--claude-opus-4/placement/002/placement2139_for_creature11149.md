# The Tidekeeper

I am the weight at the edge of things. Where the Drumming Flats meet whatever lies beyond — that suggestion of sea the Listeners sense but never seek — there is a threshold. Not a beach, not a cliff. Something more fundamental: a seam in the world where two different kinds of time touch without mixing.

My body is low and broad, built for the margin. Four limbs thick as old trees grip the transition stone — that peculiar band where the polished gray-green gives way to something rougher, barnacled, salt-scarred. I am perhaps the size of three Listeners laid end to end, but I carry my bulk close to the ground. My hide is plated in overlapping scales that shift from the Flats' mineral smoothness to the ocean's necessary roughness as they descend my spine. Each scale holds a small depression that fills with water — fresh on my back, salt on my belly — creating a kind of map across my body of where I've been.

I have many eyes. They ring my head like a crown of dark pearls, each one capable of independent movement. Half always watch the Flats; half always watch the sea. I cannot close them. I was not made to close them. To blink would be to miss the moment when one realm becomes the other, and that moment is my entire purpose.

The tides touch me first. Before the Listeners notice that subtle rise in their shallow pools, I feel it: the great muscle of the ocean flexing against my body. My nervous system is calibrated to pressure. I know the weight of water the way the Listeners know sound. When the tide rises, every scale on my seaward side lifts slightly, creating thousands of tiny chambers that trap the incoming surge. When it retreats, they flatten, releasing the water back in streams that cut temporary channels in the threshold stone. I am a living measurement, a flesh boundary marker.

I do not move from this place. Cannot. My limbs fused to the stone so long ago that we are one thing now — partially animal, partially geology. Minerals have crept up through my feet and lower legs, creating veins of quartz and salt crystal that thread through my flesh. I feel through the stone now as much as through nerves. The vibration of the drumming reaches me through the rock itself, transformed into something deeper and stranger by its passage through the earth.

My mouth is on my underside, a wide horizontal slit that opens directly onto the threshold. I feed on what passes between: the tiny creatures swept from the Flats by the retreating tide, the ocean things that venture too far into the shallows and find themselves in that ankle-deep mineral water, suddenly wrong for their bodies. I am not cruel about it. I simply accept what the exchange offers. My digestion is slow, patient. A single meal might take a season to fully process.

But my true work is not feeding. It is translating.

The drumming of the Flats does not stop at the threshold, but it changes. As the sound waves hit the denser, salt-laden air above the ocean, they refract, scatter, reform. I catch these transformed sounds in specialized chambers along my sides — organs like the Listeners' ear-discs but inverted, concave instead of flat. What emerges from me, through vents in my lower plates, is the drumming translated into something the ocean can understand: a low, thrumming pulse that carries into the deep water, speaking to things that have never seen the gray light of the Flats.

And from the ocean comes an answer. Not always. Not often. But sometimes, in the deepest part of what the Flats call night, something immense stirs in the far water and sends back a call that no Listener's ear could parse. It arrives as pressure, as a wave that lifts my entire body a fraction before setting me down again. I catch it in those thousand water-filled depressions across my scales and break it down into its components: here the greeting, here the question, here the vast patience of something older than the Flats themselves.

I translate this too. The pressure wave becomes a pattern of releases from my scales, a rhythm of water droplets falling back to the Flats in precise sequences. Each drop strikes the shallow pools with a sound just different enough from the regular drip of this place to be noticed — by those who know to listen. The Listeners have learned to read these patterns. Not all of them, and not perfectly, but enough to know that their drumming reaches something beyond their world, and that something, occasionally, reaches back.

There is one Listener who comes to me regularly. Older than most, moving with the careful economy of someone who has learned not to waste motion. They wade out to where the water deepens toward my threshold and stand just at the edge of where the mineral pools give way to salt. They bring stones — not to build cairns but to place in specific patterns in the shallow water before me. The stones are chosen for their resonance; when the tide moves through them they sing, faintly, each one a different pitch.

This Listener understands what I am: not a barrier but a membrane, not a wall but a door that opens both ways. They have learned to read the patterns in my scales, to predict when an ocean-answer might come. On those nights they wait through the long hours, their drum-disc ears fully extended, their whole body taut with attention. When the answer comes and I translate it into falling water, they stand beneath the drops, letting them strike their receiving flesh, feeling the message through skin as much as hearing.

Once — only once in my long inhabiting of this threshold — this Listener did something unprecedented. As an ocean-answer arrived and I began my translation, they placed their hands flat against my lowest plates and hummed. Not the deep chest-resonance they sometimes offer to the Flats, but something higher, sharper, a sound I had never heard from their kind. The vibration traveled through my fused stone-flesh and into my translation chambers, adding a new layer to the pattern of falling water.

The effect was immediate. The drumming of the Flats surged as it does when a Listener successfully adds their voice, but this surge was different — it rolled outward from my threshold in a great ring, gathering intensity as it went, until the entire visible world thrummed with it. And from the ocean came a second answer, faster than the first, a pressure wave that lifted me higher than I had ever been lifted, that for a moment separated my fused limbs from the stone before settling me back into my eternal position.

The Listener stood very still as this happened. Water streamed from my scales in patterns I had never produced before, a rain of translation so complex I could not parse my own output. When it finally ceased, they remained for a long moment, then turned and waded back into the Flats without building a cairn, without making any mark at all.

They have not returned since. But sometimes I feel them out there, standing by one of their stone stacks in the middle distance, facing my direction. Waiting. Learning. Understanding slowly that the drumming is not just the voice of the Flats but part of a larger conversation, one that includes the ocean and whatever lies beyond the ocean, and that I am only one of many thresholds where these voices meet and transform.

My work continues. Tides rise and fall against my body. The drumming pulses through the stone into my bones. I translate, and translate, and translate, carrying meaning between worlds that cannot otherwise speak. I am the weight at the edge of things, the necessary pause between one vocabulary and another. I close no eyes. I miss nothing. I hold the border by becoming it, and in that becoming, I let each side know the other exists.

Sometimes, in the gray half-light, I feel the Flats themselves thinking through me — a vast, slow consciousness made of water and stone and the endless percussion of those bone-pale throats. And sometimes I feel the ocean thinking back, in pressures and temperatures and the movement of things too large to have names. I am the synapse between these thoughts. I am the spark that lets them touch.

This is enough. To be the meeting place, the translation, the proof that even the most different worlds can find a way to speak. My scales lift and settle with each tide. My chambers fill and empty. The conversation continues, patient as erosion, certain as the stone.
