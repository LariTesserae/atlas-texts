# The Moss-Tender of the Third Terrace

I am the silence between bells.

My dwelling clings to the steepest part of town, where the mountain's shoulder shrugs up sharp enough that my floor is my neighbor-below's roof. Three rooms only: one for sleeping, one for working, one that is simply an opening to the air, a mouth in my wall where I can sit with my legs hanging into space and watch the clouds eat the lower town twice daily.

I came here forty-three years ago as a child-trade from the valley. My parents, wheat farmers, had too many mouths and not enough grain. The town had a house with no heir. These exchanges happen. I arrived knowing only that moss was green and water was wet, and an old woman named Ket took me in hand. She's in the walls now. I trim her back gently when she encroaches on my sleep-niche.

My work is specific: I tend the shyest mosses, the ones that won't establish on the common walls but flourish in particular combinations of shade and seep. The town needs these rarities for medicine, for the finest felting, for the treatment of certain roof-rots that attack the elderly houses. I keep seventeen species in cultivation on terraces I've built into the cliff-face above my home — tiny worlds of wood-frame and careful drainage, each no larger than a sleeping-mat, stacked up the rock like green stairs.

The frogs avoid my terraces. This makes them the quietest place in town.

It took me twenty years to understand why: the mosses I grow exude compounds that disturb amphibian skin. The knowledge came slowly, through watching, through accidents — a frog that wandered onto my rarest cushion-moss and left within minutes, shaking its feet. Now I use this. I've ringed my workshop with a band of the bitter moss so I can hear myself think. Inside that ring, the only sounds are water-drip and the occasional warbler and my own breathing.

My hands are more green than flesh-colored now. The bitter compounds that repel frogs stain human skin a deep blue-green that goes down to the bone. When I hold my fingers to lamplight, I can see through them faintly, like looking through bottle-glass. They don't hurt. They've become something between plant and animal, able to feel what moss needs by touch alone — too much copper in this drainage, not enough air in that mat, a fungus beginning under this cushion that will kill it in a week if I don't intervene.

I speak less than even the quiet folk here. Days pass without words. My bell is tuned a half-tone higher than the others, so when I ring it — rarely — everyone knows it's the moss-tender calling. Usually it means I've found something: a new hybrid in my terraces, a rare flowering (some mosses bloom once in a decade, tiny as dust), a color-shift that predicts weather.

In the Turning, I don't come down. My terraces need constant attention when the water rises. I sleep in my workshop, when I sleep at all, and spend the nights moving between my cultivations with a lamp, adjusting drainage, catching the tiny mudslides that would bury decades of work. The marriage-couples see my light moving on the cliff above them as they rebuild the town's roofs. Some wave. I've learned to wave back.

I've grown into my strangeness. The children call me Moss-Fingers when they think I can't hear. They're not wrong. I've been changing slowly into something that matches my work. My hair, what's left of it, has taken on a faint greenish cast. My breathing has slowed until I sometimes forget to do it for minutes at a time, then remember with a gasp. I suspect I photosynthesize slightly. Not enough to live on, but enough to change the rhythm of hunger.

The town accepts this. They need what I grow, and they understand that tending requires becoming. The potter's hands reshape themselves to clay. The bell-ringer's ears learn frequencies others can't hear. And I? I am learning to be patient on a moss-scale, to think in decades, to feel the difference between thirteen kinds of moisture on my blue-green skin.

Sometimes, in the hour before dawn when even the frogs rest, I climb to my highest terrace and look out over the town. From here, the patterns emerge: which roofs are oldest by their depth of color, which houses share lineage by the similarities in their moss, where the underground streams must run by the extra lushness they feed. The whole settlement breathes in its sleep, a gentle expansion and contraction of all that greenness with the night moisture.

I am not separate from it, this place. None of us are. But I am deeper in than most now, my hands the color of the thing I tend, my rhythms slowed to match the patient creep of growth. When I die — not soon, I think; the moss-stained seem to live longer — they'll lay me on the north slope like all the rest. But I suspect I'll fruit differently. The moss that grows from me will be bluer, stranger, perhaps medicinal in ways the town hasn't seen.

I've already asked the youngest apprentice to harvest me carefully when the time comes. She nodded, understanding. Her own hands are just beginning to stain.

From my workshop, if I listen past the silence I've cultivated, I can hear the bells naming the day's small events: *water rising at the cistern*, *Tam's baby crying*, *sun breaking through*, *time to eat*. But I rarely ring mine anymore. What would I say? *The Racomitrium is dreaming*. *The Leucobryum has decided to spread north*. *I am becoming something slow and green and necessary*.

Better to tend in quiet. Better to let my work speak in the bodies of the houses, in the colors future walls will wear, in the medicine that keeps the children well through the wet seasons. I am the silence between bells, yes, but also the green that grows in silence, the patience that climbs stone, the hand that knows moss from moss from moss in the dark.

The town's bells ring below. I adjust a drainage channel, consider the light, forget to breathe, remember. The clouds are coming in for their morning meal. Soon the lower town will vanish, and I'll be alone here with my terraces, my bitter-moss boundary, my blue-green hands that barely look like hands anymore.

This is enough. More than enough. This is everything.
