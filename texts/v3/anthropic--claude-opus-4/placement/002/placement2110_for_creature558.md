I find myself among the Cartographers of Provisional Streets, though I am not quite one of them. Not yet.

I arrived in Vorrh-Kaleth three years ago, following rumors of maps that showed different cities occupying the same space. In the lowlands, I had been a surveyor—a precise profession, measuring boundaries for property disputes, establishing the fixed lines that let people say "this is mine, that is yours." I was good at it. I took pride in the certainty of my measurements, the way my maps could settle arguments, create clarity where there had been confusion.

The mountain has taught me that clarity is more complex than I knew.

I work as an apprentice now, though I'm forty-three and most apprentices are half my age. My partners are patient with me—Kell, who has been mapping the provisional zones for fifteen years, and Yora, whose grandmother was a Cartographer when the guild was just three obsessives meeting in a basement. They understand that I need time to unlearn the habits of the lowlands, the assumption that a street corner stays where you put it.

We map the Eighth District, where the instabilities are moderate—enough to be instructive, not so much that we risk serious coherence drift. Our current project involves a plaza that sometimes contains a fountain. Not a fountain that is sometimes dry—the fountain itself comes and goes, along with the pigeons that roost on it, the worn stones where people sit, the whole infrastructure of public gathering that accumulates around water.

The first time I saw it disappear, I tried to find an explanation. Hidden mechanisms, perhaps. A trick of perspective. Kell just waited, watching me work through the stages of lowlander disbelief. When I finally asked her how it was possible, she said: "The plaza is still deciding what kind of plaza it wants to be."

This is how the Cartographers think—not in terms of fixed reality but in terms of ongoing negotiations, settlements that can be revised, arguments the city is having with itself about what should exist where.

My job is to document the fountain's pattern, if it has one. I visit the plaza every morning at the same time, notebook in hand. Present or absent, I record. But also: the quality of presence, when it's there. Some days the fountain feels ancient, worn smooth by generations of use. Other days it appears newly carved, the stone sharp-edged, the water mechanism modern and efficient. Once, memorably, it was made of wood—an impossibility that lasted three hours before the plaza reconsidered.

I've started dreaming in maps. Not the clean, precise maps of my lowland work, but something more like musical notation—layers of possibility, temporal markings, passages that can be played different ways. In the dreams, I'm trying to draw a street that keeps moving under my pen, and I'm not frustrated by this. I'm fascinated. My dream-self understands something my waking-self is still learning: that a map of provisional space must itself be provisional, must leave room for what isn't yet decided.

Kell says this is good. She says I'm beginning to think like the city thinks.

I live in a small apartment in the Seventh District, in a building that is reassuringly stable—I'm not ready, yet, for the full uncertainty that some Cartographers seek out. My rooms are sparse but comfortable: a bed, a desk where I transfer field notes into formal charts, a kitchen where I make the simple meals that sustain solitary workers. On the walls, I've hung my practice maps—attempts to capture particular intersections at particular moments, dense with annotation, beautiful in their failure to fully contain what they document.

I have a cat now. She appeared in my apartment one day, a gray tabby with white paws, and I understood this was not random. The building's coherence needed reinforcement, or I did, or perhaps the distinction doesn't matter. I named her Survey, which made Yora laugh. "Still thinking like a lowlander," she said, but kindly. The cat doesn't seem to mind the name. She sleeps on my desk while I work, her presence a warm weight that helps the maps settle into being.

The hardest part is explaining this life to my family in the valley. My letters have become increasingly abstract, full of circumlocutions, trying to describe work that has no equivalent in stable geography. My sister writes back worried messages, convinced I've joined some kind of cult. My brother, more practical, asks about the pay. (It's decent. The guild is well-funded by city taxes, understanding that their work is essential infrastructure.)

But how do I tell them about the satisfaction of seeing a pattern emerge from apparent chaos? How do I explain the profound calm that comes from accepting that the world is not fixed, was never fixed, that what we call reality is just the part that's been decided?

I can't. So I tell them I'm well, that the work is interesting, that the mountain air agrees with me. All true, if incomplete.

Yesterday, Kell suggested I might be ready to map alone—not in the truly unstable zones, not yet, but in the margins where solo work is permitted. It's a vote of confidence. Also a test. Can I maintain coherence without the anchor of partnership? Can I trust my own perception when reality becomes negotiable?

I think I can. The thought frightens me a little, excites me more.

Sometimes I walk to the plaza just to sit, not mapping, just watching. When the fountain is there, I listen to the water, watch the pigeons, feel the weight of its presence. When it's not, I pay attention to the empty space, the way people's paths curve around an absence, the ghost of community that persists even when its center vanishes.

I'm beginning to understand that the Cartographers don't really map the city. They map the conversation between the city and itself, the ongoing argument about what belongs where. Every map is a snapshot of that dialogue, true for a moment, useful for as long as the terms remain stable.

The mountain grows. The mills hum. The city continues its complex existence, solid and provisional at once. And I, who came here looking for certainty in a new form, have found instead something better: the knowledge that uncertainty, carefully attended, can be its own kind of home.

My next project involves a stairway that sometimes has seventeen steps and sometimes has twenty-three. Kell says the variance might correlate with the condensation fronts. Yora thinks it's more complex, tied to traffic patterns and the phase of the moon and the emotional state of the district's dominant cat.

I'll map it all. Every variation, every possibility. Not to fix it in place—that's the lowlander thinking I'm still shedding—but to know it more fully, to understand the range of its being.

This is what I've become: a person who counts steps not for certainty but for curiosity, who sees a shifting stairway and thinks "how fascinating" instead of "how wrong." The mountain has changed me. The work has changed me.

I would not go back to the valley for anything.

Survey stretches on my desk, yawns, settles back into sleep. Outside my window, the Eighth District hums with life—human and animal and something in between, all of us collaborating in the grand project of making Vorrh-Kaleth real enough for another day.

I pick up my pen. There are maps to make, provisional and beautiful, true for now.
