In this twilit vale, where the Vale-Folk dance between shadow and stone, I am the Memory-Keeper.

I dwell in the deepest of the sacred caves, where the phosphorescent fungi grow thickest and their ghostly light paints everything in shades of blue and green. The walls here are smooth as water-worn glass, polished by countless generations of my predecessors who sat where I sit now, running their fingers along the stone as they recited the histories.

My body bears the marks of my calling. Where other Vale-Folk are lean and quick, I am slower, more deliberate. My fingers have grown thick from years of grinding minerals into pigments, and my nails are worn to nubs from scraping designs into rock. The tips of my fingers are stained permanently with the deep purples of crushed cave-berries and the rusty reds of iron-rich clay. My eyes, even among a people who see well in darkness, are exceptional—I can discern the faintest variations in the fungal light, reading stories in the subtle shifts of bioluminescence that others miss.

The clan brings me their memories. When a hunter discovers a new path through the cliffs, they come to me, and I mix pigments to match the color of the stone at each turning. When the spawning eels arrive earlier than expected, I record it in a series of spirals and dots that capture not just the event but its relationship to the flowering cycles, the bat migrations, the temperature of the underground streams. My cave is a living map of time itself, layers upon layers of knowledge painted, carved, and grown into the very rock.

But I am more than a recorder. In the deepest part of my cave, where few are permitted to go, I tend to something extraordinary. Here, I cultivate special fungi—not just any phosphorescent species, but ones I have been selecting and nurturing for decades. These fungi respond to touch, to breath, to the oils of different skins. When I press my palm against certain clusters, they flare brighter. When I whistle the old songs near others, they pulse in rhythm.

I am teaching them our history.

Each cluster learns a different tale—this one knows the legend of the first Vale-Folk who discovered the hidden grotto, that one remembers the great season of darkness when the sun didn't reach the vale for thirteen days. The fungi hold these memories in their mycelial networks, passing them through the rock itself. Sometimes, when I sit very still, I can feel the mountain thinking.

The young ones come to me for the Showing. I lead them through my caves, teaching them to read the paintings, to understand the patterns. But more importantly, I teach them to listen to the fungi, to feel the stories thrumming through the stone. Not all can learn this art—most return to the hunting and gathering. But occasionally, perhaps once in a generation, a child's eyes will widen as they place their small hand on the living light and feel the memories flow into them.

I know I am growing old. My movements are slower than they once were, and sometimes I find myself sitting for hours, my consciousness merged with the fungal networks, traveling through filaments of memory that stretch back beyond even our oldest stories. In these moments, I am not sure where I end and the mountain begins.

When my time comes, I will do as all Memory-Keepers have done. I will go to the innermost chamber, where the oldest and largest fungal cluster grows—a massive, pulsing constellation of living light that some say is as old as the vale itself. I will place my hands upon it one final time, and all that I am, all that I remember, all that I have tended and grown, will flow into that ancient network.

And then the mountain will remember me, as it remembers all who came before, in the soft blue glow of fungi that know our names, our stories, our songs. The next Memory-Keeper will find me there, my body feeding the very lights that hold our history, and they will understand, as I understood when I found my predecessor, that we do not merely record the memory of the Vale-Folk.

We become it.
