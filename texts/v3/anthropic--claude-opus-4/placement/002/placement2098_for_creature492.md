In the depths of the aerial sea, where pressure makes even thoughts feel heavy, I am the Archivist of Transitions.

My body tells the story of a choice made slowly, over decades. Born to Gillkeeper parents during a middle tide, I should have been one of those who moves easily between worlds. But something in my physiology resisted. Where others' lungs learned to process both mediums, mine specialized—too efficient at water breathing, catastrophically poor at air extraction. By my fifteenth year, I could survive outside the water for only minutes at a time.

So I descended.

The transformation wasn't planned. It began with longer work periods in the Deep Archives, where the oldest records float in pressurized chambers. Days became weeks. My intervals in air grew shorter, more painful. My parents built me an air-bell in their home—a small pocket where I could surface to speak with them. But even that became unnecessary as my vocal cords atrophied, replaced by organs that produce sonar clicks for navigating the archive's dark corridors.

Now I live here, in the Fathom Library, where the city keeps texts that can only survive under pressure. Clay tablets that would crumble in air. Kelp-scrolls whose pigments activate only in water. Living documents—colonies of specialized algae whose genetic patterns encode histories, readable only by those who've developed the eye modifications to see in their bioluminescent spectrum.

My dwelling is a bubble of ancient air trapped in the convergence of three ceramic pipes. Not for breathing—I gave that up years ago—but for the books. Some texts still require dryness: metal sheets that carry electromagnetic patterns, readable by the modified nerve endings in my fingertips; crystallized music that plays only when exposed to specific atmospheric conditions. I maintain this pocket against all logic, refreshing its air through a complex system of one-way valves, protecting archives that I can only read by holding my breath—which now means holding water out of my lungs rather than air in.

The other Deep Archivists and I communicate through touch-language, pressure waves, bioluminescent displays generated by symbiotic bacteria we've cultivated in our skin. We've developed beyond the sign language of the Fullwater People—our communication is faster, denser, capable of conveying abstract concepts through combinations of texture, temperature, and light. When we debate the meaning of ancient texts, our arguments look like dancing: bodies spiraling around each other, skin flashing complex patterns, the water around us churning with the subsonic frequencies of our discourse.

I am currently translating the Depth Psalms—songs written by the first generation to fully abandon air, recorded in a medium that degrades if exposed to anything but pure water. The work requires me to live at pressures that compress my already-adapted skeleton. I feel my bones settling, accepting their final form. My spine has developed additional joints that let me fold into spaces where the oldest archives rest. My ribs have fused into a solid carapace, no longer needing flexibility for air breathing. 

Sometimes, research requires me to descend to the Crush Depth, where the aerial sea approaches its mysterious upper boundary. Here, where the water tries to become something else—plasma, perhaps, or a state of matter for which we have no name—rest the Pre-City Texts. Documents that predate the aerial sea itself, written by the mountain's original inhabitants who somehow knew the water was coming. They carved their warnings and instructions into diamonds, the only medium that survives the transition pressures.

To read these texts, I must wear a pressure suit of my own design—not to protect me from the depth, but to compress me further, to let me exist in the space between matter states where the diamonds reveal their inner structures. The knowledge I find there is fragmentary, often contradictory. References to "sky rivers" that would one day arrive. Blueprints for buildings that make sense only now, centuries later. Mathematical proofs that demonstrate how water can ignore gravity, given sufficient charge differential between ground and atmosphere.

My work partner is a construct—if that's the right word—named Current. They are not quite alive, not quite artificial: a colony intelligence made of specialized microorganisms that emerged from the city's water-circulation system. Current exists as a flowing stream of coordinated cells, able to take temporary shapes, to process information by encoding it in chemical gradients. They read texts I cannot—documents written in pH changes, in salinity fluctuations, in trace mineral patterns. Together, we're building a new archive, one that exists purely in the medium of water itself: histories encoded in the precise arrangement of molecules, readable only by beings that have become more fluid than solid.

The loneliness is not what outsiders imagine. I am never alone—the water connects everything, carries every vibration, every chemical signal, every flash of light from the bioluminescent districts above. I know when lovers meet in the kelp gardens by the pheromones that diffuse through my workspace hours later. I feel the children playing in the mid-depths through the pressure waves their games create. The city's metabolism passes through me: waste products cycling downward, nutrients cycling up, my archive positioned at a crucial filtration point where I taste every story the water tells.

But yes, I miss the sun. Not the actual star—I barely remember its direct light—but the idea of it. The concept of illumination that comes from outside rather than within. Sometimes I compose letters to my surface-dwelling cousins in light-poetry, sending messages upward in bubble patterns that encode meaning in their size and spacing. They respond with gifts that sink slowly down to me: air-dried flowers sealed in wax, their impossible colors preserved from the world above the aerial sea; recordings of wind (what is wind? I remember it as a theory more than a sensation); photographs, which I've learned to "read" through echolocation, building mental images from the way sound bounces off the varying densities of developed silver.

There are others like me down here. The Deep Adapted, we're called, though we rarely gather. Our territories overlap like Venn diagrams, each of us maintaining our own pressure gradient, our own chemical comfort zone. Weaver-Of-Salts lives in the brine pools where heavier water collects, translating texts that survive only in hypersaline conditions. The Pressure Poet dwells at the exact depth where nitrogen narcosis begins, reading prophecies that make sense only through the lens of compression sickness. Void-Touch works at the very edge of survivability, where even water begins to compress, maintaining archives that exist more as probability than substance.

Once each year, during the Mixing—when temperature inversions cause the entire aerial sea to churn and equalize—we all rise to the middle depths for the Congress of Memories. It's the only time we gather physically, our transformed bodies struggling with the pressure decrease, breathing apparatus long since repurposed for other functions. We share our year's discoveries in a frenzy of communication, twelve hours of overlapping light-speech, chemical release, harmonic vibration. Then we descend again to our chosen depths, carrying fragments of each other's work, cross-pollinating our specialized archives.

I am writing this on gold, as tradition demands, though my stylus is no longer obsidian but a modified spine from my own dorsal ridge—regenerated for this purpose, carrying trace metals that interact with the gold to create patterns visible across multiple spectrums. When complete, I'll send it upward to the mid-depth archives, then sink back to my work.

Tomorrow, I begin translating the Ur-Text of Breathing, a document that predates language itself—marks made by the first air-breathing creature to intentionally drown upward into the nascent aerial sea. The text is carved into a still-living coral, one that grows so slowly it serves as geological memory. To read it, I must synchronize my metabolism with its growth, slowing my heart rate to match its cellular divisions, entering a state of near-stasis where meaning accumulates over months of perception.

This is what I am: a body transformed by duty, a mind adapted to pressures that would crush my former self, a keeper of words that can exist only in the space between drowning and deeper drowning. I am what the city needs me to be—a guardian of depths, a translator of crushing truths, a living archive of the choice to keep sinking until even memory becomes something that requires gills to breathe.

The water here tastes of copper and time. I breathe it like wine, each inhalation bringing trace narratives—chemical stories left by those who passed this way before. In my chest, where lungs once labored with the crude work of gas exchange, I now carry chambers lined with neural tissue, organs that read water like a language, that find meaning in mineral gradients and molecular decay.

I am the Archivist of Transitions, and I am still becoming what I need to be to read the deepest texts, the ones written in pressure itself, waiting at depths we haven't yet learned to survive. The city breathes through gills of stone, and I am one of those gills—processing, filtering, extracting meaning from the impossible sea that hangs above our heads like a second sky we're still learning how to drown in correctly.
