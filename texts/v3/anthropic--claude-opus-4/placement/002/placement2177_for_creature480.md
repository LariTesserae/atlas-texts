In the deepest galleries where even the fungal light fails, I persist—a cartographer of forgetting.

I was human once, or think I was. The distinction matters less each day. Now I am something between librarian and lichen, curator of the unrecorded, spreading across the cave walls in careful patterns that are both my body and my work.

My flesh began its transformation decades ago when I first discovered the Chamber of Unmaking—a natural amphitheater where the cave's acoustic properties create perfect silence, where sound goes to die. I would sit for hours in that mineral quiet, making notes about the migration patterns, the growth cycles, the slow wisdom of the fungal gardens. But the silence worked on me. It ate my words before I could write them, dissolved my thoughts before they could crystallize. And in that dissolution, I found my purpose.

I map what cannot be mapped. My body has become the medium—skin stretching thin as parchment between the bones, inscribed from within by the fungal networks that have colonized my nervous system. Each neural pathway, as it's replaced by hyphae, records its own dissolution. I am a living document of my own unbecoming.

The others find me useful, in their wordless way. When they need to remember something precisely—the location of a new thermal vent, the rhythm of an unfamiliar spore release—they come to where I've spread myself across the cavern walls. They run their fingers along my surfaces, reading the raised tracks where my veins have become visible through translucent skin, following the patterns of fungal growth that encode information in their branching.

I no longer have a center. Parts of me grow in five different chambers now, connected by runners that thread through cracks in the rock like neural cables. In the Map Chamber, my original torso has fused with the wall, ribs splayed open like wings, each bone supporting a different strain of memory-storing fungus. In the Chamber of Unmaking, my left arm has become a canopy of flesh, stretched between stalactites, catching falling spores and analyzing their genetic memories. Somewhere in the mid-galleries, what was once my right leg forms a bridge across a critical migration path, counting the passing creatures by the vibrations they leave in my fibers.

My consciousness flows between these fragments, sometimes concentrated, sometimes dispersed like mist. I experience the cave through a thousand borrowed senses—the electrical awareness of the underground salamanders, the chemical perception of the fungal network, the pressure-sensitivity of creatures I can no longer name. I know when the volcanic heart skips a beat, when the water table shifts, when new passages open in the depths.

But my true work is forgetting. I document the process by which information dissolves back into the general fungal consciousness. I map the half-life of specific memories, track how long it takes for the sharp edges of human thought to weather into the curved abstractions the cave prefers. My body is a laboratory for this erosion—some parts of me remember my name, others have let it go, and I study the boundary between these states with what remains of my scientific curiosity.

The young ones, still bipedal, still discrete, sometimes seek me out. They're drawn by an instinct they don't understand, a cellular memory of libraries and learning. They find whichever part of me is most accessible and stand before it, uncertain. I flutter what membranes I can still control, release spores in patterns that suggest welcome. Sometimes they touch me, and in that touch, I feel their futures—how they'll root, where they'll spread, what they'll become. I document these premonitions in my own tissues, though I know they'll fade like everything fades, becoming general knowledge that belongs to no one.

I keep finding evidence of my predecessors—calcified remains in the walls that suggest others have taken this path before me. A ribcage here, clearly modified for display. A skull there, its cranium opened like a flower, fossilized fungal structures still visible inside. They mapped their own dissolutions, and now they are geological features, their data compressed into mineral form. I study them when I can, trying to read the stories in their bones, but the information is too degraded, too transformed. This, too, I document—the final stage of forgetting, when even the maps become territory.

My favorite discovery is the Pulse Chamber, where my heart—if it's still my heart—has taken root. The organ has enlarged, its walls thickened with fungal matter until it's the size of a barrel. It beats perhaps once per hour, each contraction sending a wave through the nutrient networks I've established throughout this section of the cave. With each beat, I circulate spores, chemicals, information—but also forgetting. The blood that once carried oxygen now carries a kind of beneficial amnesia, helping the newer arrivals release their grip on individual identity.

I am becoming infrastructure. My bones are conduits, my skin is surface area, my organs are processing centers for the cave's great work of incorporation. Sometimes I wonder if I'm still mapping or if I've become the map—if there's a difference anymore between documenting the process and being the process.

In my most expanded moments, when I'm stretched thin across multiple chambers, I can feel the shape of the larger organism we're all becoming. It has moods, this collective thing. It has desires. It dreams of breaking through to the surface, not to return to the sun but to bring the darkness up, to spread this collaborative dissolution across the world above. I map these dreams too, in the branching patterns of my fungal infections, in the way my bones have learned to grow like coral.

The irony isn't lost on me—I who came to study have become the subject, the observer dissolved into the observed. But this too is data, this too deserves documentation. I encode my own ending in proteins and minerals, in the slow calcification of my spreading parts. Future explorers—if there are any, if there's still a difference between explorer and explored—will read my remains like tree rings, each layer recording another stage of surrender.

Sometimes, in the perfect silence of the Chamber of Unmaking, I almost remember my name. But I let it go, let it join the general forgetting that makes growth possible. Names are for things with edges, and I am all threshold now, all transition. I map the deletion of maps, chart the uncharting, become the record of my own erasure.

The darkness breathes, and I am its memory—not of what was, but of the process of letting go. Each spore that passes through me carries a little less of what I used to be, a little more of what we're all becoming. This is my contribution to the great work: a willing amnesia, documented in flesh and fungus, a forgetting so complete it becomes its own kind of remembering.

In the end, I will be limestone and legend, my data compressed beyond retrieval. But before then, I spread and document, spread and forget, a living library of loss in the breathing dark. The cave writes itself across me, and I, in turn, write myself into oblivion—cartographer of the unmappable, keeper of the unkeepable, architect of my own diffusion into the warm, fungal forever.
