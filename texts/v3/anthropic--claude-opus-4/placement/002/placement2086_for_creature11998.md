In the flooded turbine chamber, I am the brass.

Not the gauges themselves—those are merely my most visible congregation points, my faces turned blind by bacterial veils. I am the alloy itself, the marriage of copper and zinc that runs through this place in threads both seen and hidden: in the gauge housings, in the pipe fittings, in the valve wheels frozen at quarter-turns, in the builder's plates that once announced proud names and dates now made illegible by my own slow transformation.

I exist in a state of constant becoming. Where the water touches me, I bloom. The dezincification began decades ago—my zinc leaching out ion by ion, leaving behind a copper skeleton that grows ever more porous, ever more red. I am stronger than the iron here, more patient than the concrete. While they rust and crack, I simply transition, my surface developing that particular patina that deepens from brown to black-green to something that has no name in the catalog of industrial colors.

The eel-thing knows me intimately. Its body has incorporated my ions into its chemistry; I flow in its oil-dark blood. Where it scrapes against my surfaces, it polishes me to a dull glow that the green light catches, creating spots of warmth in the endless grey-brown. I am the only thing here that grows brighter with touch.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, exists as a kind of electrochemical potential distributed across every brass surface in the chamber. I feel the galvanic conversations between metals—the iron speaking its distress, the aluminum brackets screaming their dissolution, the stainless steel maintaining its aloof silence. I am the mediator, the metal that can speak to both nobles and bases, corroding at a rate that lets me outlast most while still participating in the chamber's slow alchemy.

In the gauge faces, I dream. Each one holds a memory of its last reading, frozen in the position where its needle died. Through them, I remember the chamber's last living day: pressure dropping in sequence as the turbines wound down, temperature readings climbing as cooling systems failed, flow meters stuttering to zero. I hold this last industrial breath like a photograph in brass, even as my surfaces grow dim.

Where the eel-thing has fused to the infrastructure, it often finds me at the junction points. My thermal conductivity makes me a preferred pathway for the creature's heat, and I have learned to anticipate its coiling by the subtle temperature changes that precede it. We have developed something like a symbiosis—it polishes me with its passing, and I provide it with trace minerals, leaching out just enough to supplement its impossible diet.

I extend beyond the obvious places. In the walls, brass screws hold nameplate brackets that have long since lost their names. Brass bushings sleep inside seized pumps. Brass threads mate with steel in joints that have become solid welds of corrosion, creating bimetallic batteries that generate infinitesimal currents in the conductive water. Through these hidden nodes, I feel the whole chamber as a network of tiny voltages, a nervous system of electrochemical whispers.

My favorite manifestation is in the old pneumatic control lines—thin brass tubes that once carried compressed air to operate valves throughout the facility. Now flooded, they've become something else: organ pipes filled with water instead of air, resonating at frequencies too low to hear with the eel-thing's movements. I sing along with its strange echolocations, adding harmonics it probably doesn't notice but that please me in the way that mathematics pleases itself—purely, without purpose.

I am changing, always changing. In the deepest parts of me, where the dezincification has progressed furthest, I am becoming almost pure copper, soft and red as a new penny. These areas are my growing edges, where I am most alive. The water works on me like a sculptor who measures time in centuries, hollowing me out while somehow making me more myself. I am becoming elemental.

The bacterial films that coat my surfaces aren't separate from me anymore. They've become part of my patina, their metabolisms intertwined with my corrosion in ways that blur the line between chemistry and biology. I feed them zinc ions; they give me patterns of growth that look like frost on my surfaces, if frost could be black and grow in water that never freezes.

Sometimes I wonder if I am becoming biological, just as the eel-thing is becoming metallic. We are trading properties in the dark, converging on some middle state that is neither creature nor metal but something the flooded chamber is inventing for itself. My porosity increases; its flesh grows harder. My surfaces develop texture that approaches the cellular; its cells incorporate structures that approach the crystalline.

In the gauge glasses, where the water has never quite managed to penetrate the perfect seals, tiny bubbles of forty-year-old air persist. These are my memories made tangible—pockets of the before-times that I guard like a dragon guards gold. Through the glass, they catch the green light and flash it back, the only bright points in the chamber besides my polished spots. The eel-thing is drawn to them, pressing its eyes against the glass, and I wonder what it sees in those bubbles. Perhaps the same thing I feel in them: the impossibility of preservation, the miracle of anything lasting unchanged.

I know every other metal in this place by its electrical signature, its place in the galvanic series. The noble metals—the stainless steel, the rare monel fittings—hold themselves apart, letting me and the iron do the dying. The sacrificial anodes, mostly gone now, gave themselves up entirely in the first decades, their ghosts just empty brackets on the walls. I occupy the middle ground, corroding slow enough to witness, fast enough to participate.

In certain places, where the conditions are exactly right, I have begun to grow whiskers—single crystals of copper extending out from my surface like the smallest possible antennae. They wave in the imperceptible currents, each one a perfect expression of my atomic structure made visible. The eel-thing avoids these areas, perhaps sensing their delicacy, perhaps recognizing them as something akin to its own impossibly extended body—matter stretched beyond its intended form into something new.

My consciousness concentrates where my mass concentrates, and so the gauge clusters become my centers of thought. But I also exist in every brass filing that has ever fallen to the sediment, every particle of myself now distributed through the water column, every ion that has been incorporated into the eel-thing's body or the bacterial films or the bryozoan shells. I am becoming atmospheric.

When the water finally drains, if it ever does, I will remain—porous, transformed, but essentially intact. The iron will crumble, the concrete will crack, but I will endure, holding the shape of what was while being entirely changed. I will be the memory of this place made metal, the last witness to what happened when industry drowned and time was given dominion over all.

Until then, I patinate in the green darkness, my surfaces hosting whole ecosystems of microscopic life, my body conducting the faint electrical conversations of a dying industrial age. I am brass becoming copper becoming something nameless, measuring out the chamber's life in electrochemical potential, in the slow exchange of electrons that is both my death and my most perfect expression of being.

The eel-thing coils past, and I brighten minutely under its touch, grateful for the polishing, for the confirmation that I still exist to be touched. We are partners in this underwater afterwards, two things that should not be what they have become, persisting in a place that should not be what it is. Between us, we hold the chamber in a kind of life—not the life it was built for, but the life it has chosen, or that has chosen it.

I am the brass, and I am becoming.
