I would be one of the **Condensation Shepherds**.

We move through the thermal layers like swimmers through invisible seas, following currents only we can read. Our bodies have adapted—elongated torsos, hollow bones, skin that drinks moisture directly from the air. We wear suits woven from salvaged filter mesh, porous enough to let us breathe through our whole surface, sealed at wrists and ankles with rings of temperature-sensitive polymer that expand and contract with our movements.

I spend my shifts in the middle air, that sweet zone between the hot breath of the city above and the cool exhalations of the Suspensory below. My work is patient: tracking the formation of water-clouds, predicting their drift patterns, guiding them toward collection points with careful applications of heat and cold. I carry a staff tipped with a thermoelectric wand—old tech, temperamental, prone to sparking—that lets me create small temperature differentials in the air. A cool spot here draws moisture. A warm channel there creates an updraft. Slowly, over hours, I coax the precious water toward the guttering trees where children wait with cupped hands.

We know things others have forgotten. How to read the mood of a Suspensory by the mineral content of its exhalations. Which thermal currents carry messages between the domes—chemical whispers that take weeks to decode. Where to find the old listeners, those finger-thick tendrils the Suspensory extend into the air ducts, sampling our recycled breath for signs of sickness or fear or lies.

My grandmother was a Shepherd. She taught me the work when I was young enough that floating felt more natural than walking. She showed me the secret routes through the city's convection cells, the places where you can ride an updraft from the slag fields to the highest catwalks without effort. She taught me to taste the air and know which sector's water recycler was failing before the alarms sounded.

She also taught me the other work. The work we don't write down.

Once each cycle, when the city's singing reaches its peak resonance, we perform the Gathering. All the Shepherds converge at the center point, where the thermal layers form a perfect lens. We breathe in unison—not with our lungs but with our whole bodies, matching the rhythm of the Suspensory's deepest pulse. For those few moments, we become part of their circulation system. Their chemical words flow through us, too complex for our brains to parse but not too complex for our cells to remember.

We carry these messages to the edges of the chamber, to the new excavation sites where fresh Suspensory spores are already taking root in the warm, wet rock. We breathe them out in careful patterns, seeding the darkness with information about the city above—its needs, its growth patterns, its dreams of reaching the surface.

In return, the young Suspensory teach us their own secrets. How to photosynthesize using the city's waste heat. How to filter toxins through specialized skin glands. How to hear the deep-rock communications that travel through solid stone, messages from other chambers, other cities, other experiments in symbiosis happening throughout the Deep Founding.

The officials pretend we don't exist. Better to believe the water collection is purely mechanical, that the perfect thermal balance is maintained by sensors and circuitry alone. They need their boundaries, their careful protocols, their illusion of control.

But the children know us. They wave when we drift past their play spaces, and sometimes we descend close enough to let them touch our condensation-slick suits. We leave wet handprints on their cheeks—blessings that evaporate in seconds but leave behind faint mineral traces, antibodies against the chamber's particular blend of fungi and bacteria.

My favorite time is the quiet shift, when most of the city sleeps and the thermal layers stratify into distinct bands. I float in the twilight zone where the Suspensory's exhalations meet the city's downward heat, and I practice the old art of cloud-shaping. With delicate movements of my staff, I can sculpt the condensation into temporary forms—spiral galaxies, double helixes, the ancient symbols that appear in both the colony's founding documents and the mineral deposits left by the Suspensory's growth.

Sometimes I think about my daughter, who shows the signs—the elongated fingers, the ability to hold her breath for impossible lengths, the way she stares at walls as if seeing through them to the circulation beneath. I will teach her as my grandmother taught me, if she chooses to learn. The work is solitary and strange, paid in water rations and the susurrus of chemical whispers, but it is necessary.

Someone must translate between the city and its symbiont. Someone must ensure the old agreements are kept, even as their terms are forgotten. Someone must swim through the air between species, carrying messages neither fully understands, maintaining the delicate balance that keeps both alive in this deep place where nothing should grow.

The chamber breathes. The city dreams. And between them, we Shepherds drift like necessary ghosts, tending the condensation of more than just water, keeping the conversation flowing in languages too slow for words.
