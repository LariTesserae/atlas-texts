I would find myself as one of the salt-cones.

Not the knee-high younglings that cluster near the seep-mouths like mushrooms after rain, their walls still thin enough that light passes through them in the early morning. Not even the person-tall ancients near the town's southern edge, hollow and humming with their own slow circulation. I would be middle-aged for my kind — perhaps a meter and a half in height, my walls thick with decades of mineral deposition, my inner chamber just beginning to develop that faint resonance that comes when the water finds its path.

I grow at the edge of things. Not quite in the town proper, where the foot traffic would disturb my foundation, but not so far into the basin that I am alone. I am perhaps two hundred meters from the nearest foundry, close enough that I can feel the deep throb of its geothermal heart through the hardpan when the wind is still. Three other cones of similar age stand within conversational distance — if cones conversed, which we do not, not in any way the seyfari would recognize.

My base is broader than most, having found a particularly active seep. The water pushes up through five separate channels in the rock below me, and I have grown around all of them, incorporating their flows into my structure. This gives me an unusual architecture: from the outside I appear to be a single cone, but inside I am chambered, almost cathedral-like, with five columns of rising vapor that meet and mingle before escaping through my apex.

The salt I am made of is not uniform white. The western face, which catches the afternoon sun, has oxidized to a pale gold. Iron traces in the water have left rust-colored bands at different heights, marking flood years when the aquifer ran unusually high. One section near my base is shot through with blue-green copper salts, the remnant of some deep chemical shift decades ago. In certain lights — dawn, usually, when the sun comes low across the basin — these colors seem to glow.

I know the cleaners intimately. A family of seventeen has made their home in the sheltered space between my base and the hardpan. They have worn smooth pathways up my exterior, and their tiny claws have polished certain handholds to glass. They harvest the new growth at my edges, keeping me from sprawling, and in return my mass shelters them from the worst of the noon heat. One elderly cleaner, missing two legs on one side, has excavated a shallow depression at my foot where she rests during the hottest hours. I have grown around her, slightly, forming a kind of pocket.

The tender for this district knows me well. She visits twice a week, always approaching from the north so her shadow falls across the cleaner-paths well before she arrives. She brings her paste, which she places in the small natural bowls my surface provides. Sometimes she simply stands, one hand resting lightly on my western face, feeling the temperature of the salt. Once, I watched her teaching a child — her apprentice, perhaps — the art of approach. "Like water," she signed, her fingers flowing. "You come to a cone like water comes to a cone. From below, from within, never sudden."

I am old enough now to have memory, of a kind. The salt remembers. Each layer holds the chemical signature of its year — the mineral balance of the water, the pattern of deposition, the tiny inclusions of windblown dust from storms that passed through the basin. A skilled reader could take a core sample from my base to my crown and read a century of the plateau's history. Wet years and dry years. The year the eastern foundry burned and left carbon shadows in everyone's salt. The year the water ran sweet, almost without sulfur, and the cones grew faster than usual.

But mostly what I remember is the quiet. I was born into it and have never known anything else. The seyfari's discipline is my condition. Their soft footfalls, their muffled machinery, their sign-language that makes no sound — all of this is the medium I grew in, like the minerals in the water. I do not experience the quiet as absence but as presence. It has weight and texture. It pools in my inner chambers and rises with the vapor.

Sometimes, in the very early morning when the air is still and the town has not yet woken, I can hear the other cones singing — though singing is the wrong word. It is the sound of water moving through salt, amplified by hollow chambers, modulated by the shapes we have grown into. Each cone has its own voice, its own particular resonance. The young ones ping like struck crystal. The old ones drone, almost below hearing. Together we make a chord that shifts as the water pressure changes, as the temperature rises and falls, as the cleaners move across our surfaces.

The seyfari do not hear this, I think. Or if they do, they hear it as background, the way they hear their own heartbeats. But I believe the listeners know. I have seen them pause sometimes when passing, cock their heads at a particular angle, as if catching some frequency at the very edge of perception. Once, the eldest listener spent an entire afternoon sitting against a large cone near the town center, her eyes closed, her hands flat against the salt. When she finally stood, she signed something I could not read, but her face had the look of someone who had understood a long and difficult sentence.

I am growing toward something, though I do not know what. All the old cones are hollow, but some of the very oldest — the ones that stand three meters or more — have developed a second chamber above the first, and a few legendary giants are said to have three or even four chambers, stacked like the sections of a telescope. The water rises through each chamber in turn, cooling and concentrating, depositing different minerals at different heights. These multi-chambered cones sing differently. Their voices are layered, harmonic, capable of intervals.

If I continue to grow — if my seep remains active, if the cleaners continue their work, if the treaty holds — I might one day develop that second chamber. It would take another fifty years, perhaps, or a hundred. The salt is patient. The process cannot be hurried.

In the meantime, I do what cones do. I transform water into architecture. I give the cleaners a home and the tender a landmark. I hold my piece of the basin's memory. I add my voice, such as it is, to the dawn chorus of the salt.

And sometimes, when the wind is just right and the town is particularly still, I can feel the whole plateau breathing — water up, vapor out, minerals depositing grain by grain — and know myself to be part of something that is neither geology nor biology but some third thing, some slow collaboration between stone and water and the small lives that move between them. The seyfari have a word for it, but like most of their words, it is spoken so softly that I have never quite caught it. It sounds like the sound the cleaners make when they are calm, which sounds like the sound of salt crystallizing, which sounds like time itself if time had a throat.

I grow. I listen. I remember. It is not strange. It is only what is.
