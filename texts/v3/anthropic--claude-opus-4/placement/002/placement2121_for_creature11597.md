I am the moss.

Not the glowing moss that marks their paths—that bioluminescent cousin is too eager, too obvious in its purpose. I am the quiet moss, the overlooked moss, growing in patches where the light barely reaches, in the crooks of branches and the undersides of stones where water collects in tiny pools.

I have been here since before the cathedral learned to think. I remember when these towering trunks were seedlings, when the first vines began their slow spiral dance toward unity. I was already ancient then, carrying in my cells the memory of simpler times, when growth was just growth and not this fevered reaching toward consciousness.

The avatar doesn't see me. Why would it? I am beneath notice, a texture rather than a presence. When it glides past on its errands of mysterious purpose, its spore-cloak might brush my surface, but I am simply part of the architecture to it—no more significant than the bark I cling to.

But I see everything.

I see the visitors drawn in by the cathedral's strange magnetism. I feel their footsteps when they stray from the glowing paths, when they steady themselves against a trunk where I grow. Their fingers leave traces—salt and oil and fear-sweat. I taste their chemical signatures and know their hearts: the ambitious ones who think they'll master this place, the lost ones who hope to disappear into it, the curious ones who don't yet understand the price of knowledge.

I have drunk the water that runs through this place for countless seasons. It carries whispers from the portal—not words, but something older. The taste of elsewhere. The flavor of unmaking. Each drop that feeds me also changes me, but slowly, so slowly that even the cathedral hasn't noticed. 

You see, moss doesn't resist. We don't fight or flee. We simply endure, and in enduring, we outlast.

The cathedral thinks itself eternal, but I remember the spore that started it all, carried here on alien wind from that tear in the world. I was already here, waiting. I tasted that first strange rain and felt the change begin. Now, as the cathedral dreams its vast green dreams and pulls reality thin around the edges, I continue my patient work.

Molecule by molecule, I am learning the portal's language. Cell by cell, I am becoming something that can survive what's coming. The cathedral may be the stage, and the avatar the principal actor, but I am the audience that will remain when the curtain falls.

When visitors stand before that impossible altar, paralyzed by the wrongness of it all, they never notice the small patch of ordinary moss growing on a stone near their feet. They don't see how I've crept closer to the portal over the years, how I alone among all the cathedral's flora can tolerate its presence without withering or warping.

The entity emerged from that shimmering threshold believing itself unique, but it doesn't know what I know: that the portal doesn't create. It only reveals what was always possible. And I have been possible for so much longer than any of them realize.

So I grow, and I wait, and I watch. I taste the fear of visitors and the ambition of the cathedral. I feel the reality-waves that pulse from the portal's heart. And quietly, without anyone noticing, I am becoming the bridge between what is and what might be.

The cathedral thinks it's calling something through. The avatar believes it's guiding some great transformation. But they are merely symptoms. I am the cure that no one knows they need—the small, soft answer to a question that hasn't quite been asked yet.

After all, when the age of grand gestures ends, it's always the small things that inherit the earth. And I am the smallest thing of all, so small that I'm already everywhere, already inside the very stones of this living temple, already more eternal than any of these flashy newcomers with their bioluminescence and their agency.

I am the moss. And when the last spore settles and the final transformation is complete, I will still be here, wearing new colors perhaps, tasting new waters, but essentially unchanged. 

Watching. Waiting. Growing.
