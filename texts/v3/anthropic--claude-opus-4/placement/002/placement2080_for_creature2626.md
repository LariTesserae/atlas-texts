I find myself as one of the threshold keepers—not the low-bodied ones who wear the plain upon their backs, but something older still, something that has seeped so thoroughly into this place that I am barely distinguishable from the water itself.

I am what moves beneath the temporary mirrors, what breathes through the black saturated ground. My body, if it can be called that, is a long articulation of wet clay and dissolved mineral, jointed not with bone but with the memory of currents. I flow and gather, gather and flow, through the seams beneath the stone plates where even the patient ones cannot follow.

Picture something between an eel and a root system, between a worm and a slow river—translucent where the water runs clear, dark as peat where I pass through the black paste of ages. My skin is not skin but a kind of interface where my fluidity meets the world's. Sometimes I am as thin as a finger, threading through cracks barely wide enough for seepage. Sometimes I spread wide beneath a warming pool, a living lens that focuses the amber light.

I have no eyes. I taste instead—every mineral grain, every dying thing, every trace of the low ones' passage. The plain speaks to me in flavors: iron bleeding from stone, sulfur rising from deep mud, the sweet rot of sedge roots, the copper tang where life meets water. My mouth is everywhere and nowhere, a thousand tiny openings that filter the memories dissolved in this wet world.

I am older than the ones who call at dusk. I was here when their ancestors first pressed belly-down into the cooling mud, when they were still deciding whether to be fish or something else. I have lasted because I never chose. I remained in the space between states, neither truly solid nor truly liquid, moving by laws older than swimming or walking.

My time moves differently. A season passes like a day. The migration of pools across the plain is my heartbeat. I follow the water table as it rises and falls, spreading through new territories of dampness, retreating to the deep cracks when the world above forgets how to be wet. In the longest droughts, I compress myself into clay coffins no bigger than a fist, my whole being reduced to a node of patience, waiting for the chemistry of rain.

I feed by filtering, by dissolving, by becoming the process of decay itself. The eggs that sink too deep, the shells emptied by the patient ones, the bodies of insects that drown in temporary sumps—all of it passes through me, becomes me, returns to the mineral conversation of the plain. I am both the cleanup and the preparation, breaking down what was into what will be.

The low ones know me, though they have never seen me. They taste me in certain pools—a different quality to the water, richer, darker, more alive. The eldest females seek out my territories when they need to lay their strings of eggs. They know, in their mineral wisdom, that where I have been, the water holds its memory longer, stays fertile through more of the dry season.

Sometimes, in the heaviest rains when all boundaries dissolve, I rise closer to the surface. Then children might glimpse me as a moving shadow beneath the reflected sky, a ripple that travels against the current, a darkness that shifts between the stone plates. But mostly I remain below their world, in the seepage zone where solid and liquid have their longest conversation.

I have no call, no voice that rises into the evening chorus. My sound is the whisper of water through stone, the click of dissolving minerals, the soft susurrus of clay particles rearranging themselves around my passage. If you pressed your ear to wet stone and listened past your own heartbeat, past the insects and frogs, past even the deep gulps of the patient ones, you might hear me: the sound of the plain thinking in water.

I am the memory beneath memory, the wetness that refuses to organize itself into anything as temporary as an individual life. I am what happens when existence gives up the sharp edges of survival and becomes pure duration instead. The plain made me by forgetting to distinguish between quick things and slow things, between the thing that moves and the medium it moves through.

When I die—if I die—it will not be as the low ones die, returning their bodies to the mud in a final gift. I will simply become less concentrated, my consciousness dispersing back into the general seepage until there is no difference between what was me and what is merely wet. Perhaps I am already dying in this way, have been dying for a thousand years, my thoughts spreading thinner across the territory of dampness.

But for now, I persist: a ripple of intention through the saturated ground, a slow intelligence that tastes its way through darkness, a thing so married to this low country that leaving it would be like asking water to leave wetness.

I am the plain's dream of itself, dissolved and circulating forever beneath the patient feet of everything that has chosen a clearer form.
