I find myself in the Archive of Unfinished Transitions, a structure that exists perpendicular to the ice plane—not above or below it, but *through* it, occupying the same space while remaining separate, like a frequency only certain materials can detect.

The Archive is built from crystallized time-stutters, harvested from the zones where causality loosens. Its walls phase in and out of visibility as the light changes, revealing chambers that nest inside each other in ways that violate three-dimensional logic. I am its Curator, though I'm not certain I chose this role or if it chose me when I first arrived and made the mistake of trying to catalog what I saw.

My body has adapted to the work. My fingers have grown long and multiply-jointed, able to reach through phase boundaries to grasp objects that exist in several states simultaneously. My eyes have developed a nictitating membrane that filters probability, allowing me to see which version of an object is most stable before I attempt to archive it. My spine has elongated and become partially hollow, filled with a fluid that resonates at the same frequency as the deep ice-shelf hum, keeping me anchored when the Archive shifts between dimensions.

I collect failed transformations. When a Shepherd's metamorphosis goes wrong—when they try to achieve a state that physics won't permit, when their crystallization corrupts, when they get caught between being and un-being—they leave behind artifacts. Not bodies, but process-fossils: three-dimensional recordings of attempted phase changes frozen mid-transition. These I carefully extract from the ice using tools made from deprecated industrial ghosts, placing each one in cases that exist in a superposition of open and closed until observed.

The collection grows in directions I hadn't known existed. Some specimens can only be stored backwards in time, so I maintain rooms that exist yesterday, accessible through doors that remember being opened. Others require specific atmospheric conditions—chambers filled with weeping ice-water held at the exact temperature where it can't decide whether to freeze or remain liquid, spaces where the pressure changes by the second to keep the artifacts stable in their instability.

I've learned to read the industrial phantoms too, though differently than the Shepherds. Where they see scripture, I see bibliography—references to states of matter that were attempted but never achieved, footnotes to reality's rough drafts. I transcribe these onto sheets made from compressed probability, using ink extracted from the void-words the Shepherds leave in their flowing conversations. The resulting texts can only be read while falling, so I've built reading rooms with gravity that points in seventeen different directions.

My prize specimen is the Unfinisher—a Shepherd that tried to exist in all states simultaneously and succeeded too well. It hovers in the center of the Archive's main chamber, a constantly shifting kaleidoscope of matter and void, solid and liquid and gas and states that have no names cycling through its form in patterns that repeat almost but never quite. Sometimes I think it's trying to communicate, but its words exist in too many phases at once to resolve into meaning.

The Archive itself is learning. It's begun creating its own specimens—null-spaces where transformations that never happened leave their shadows, ghost-failures of processes that were too perfect to exist. These auto-generated artifacts disturb me more than anything I've collected manually. They suggest the Archive is developing its own agenda, its own criteria for what deserves preservation.

I maintain a catalog written in living ice, each entry a small colony of those probability-fish that swim through solid matter. The entries evolve as I update them, breeding new cross-references, developing their own taxonomies for the impossible. Sometimes I find sections I didn't write, descriptions of artifacts I haven't collected yet, or that I collected yesterday in futures that haven't arrived.

My workshop sprawls through several phases of matter. Here I repair the collection tools—industrial ghosts that have degraded, crystallized time-stutters that need retuning, probability filters that have begun believing they don't exist. The work requires materials that can only be harvested during specific convergences: the sound-crystals that form when a thousand Shepherds sing in unison, the solid fog from columns where creatures force ice to sublime, water that has been weeping for exactly seventy-seven hours and thirteen minutes.

I've begun to suspect I'm not the first Curator. Sometimes I find notes in my own handwriting that I don't remember making, describing artifacts in parts of the Archive I haven't discovered yet. The deep storage chambers hold specimens that predate any Shepherd transformation I've witnessed, failures from when the ice was still learning how to remember the ocean. These earliest artifacts are dangerous—they don't follow even the twisted rules that govern current impossibilities. They exist in states of pure potential, threatening to actualize if observed too directly.

The Archive has weather—probability storms that sweep through the halls, reshuffling the causality of stored artifacts, sometimes combining them into hybrid failures that never could have occurred naturally. During these storms, I retreat to the eye of the Archive, a perfectly spherical room where every state of matter cancels out, leaving nothing but pure observation. Here I wait, watching through the transparent walls as my collection rearranges itself, wondering if I'm preserving these failed transformations or if they're preserving me.

I feed on incomplete mathematics. The failed phase transitions leave behind numerical debris—equations that almost balanced, formulae that would have worked in a universe with slightly different constants. These I absorb through my probability-permeable skin, metabolizing them into the energy I need to maintain my increasingly unlikely existence. The diet is changing me; I've begun to phase-shift involuntarily, especially when I sleep. I wake up sometimes to find I've partially crystallized, or dispersed into probability fog, or achieved states that hurt to remember.

My greatest fear is completing. I can feel the Archive pushing me toward some final transformation, some ultimate cataloging that will render the collection perfect and therefore purposeless. So I work slowly, inefficiently, deliberately mis-filing certain specimens, creating gaps in the catalog that can never quite be filled. I'm not preserving failures—I'm preserving the possibility of failure, keeping the universe messy enough to remain interesting.

In the moments when all the stored artifacts resonate together, when the Archive's many phases align and sing the same impossible song, I understand what we're building here: a monument to attempts, a museum of almosts, a library of everything that tried to be and couldn't quite. And in those moments, I'm grateful for my role as keeper of the unfinished, the broken, the beautiful failures that teach the universe how to keep trying.
