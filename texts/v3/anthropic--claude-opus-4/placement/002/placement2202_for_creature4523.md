In the perpendicular districts where Nocturne Found folds against itself, I am the Margin Walker—keeper of spaces that exist only when unobserved, custodian of the city's forgotten drafts.

My body is a negotiation between states. Where others adapted to breathe the liquid air, I adapted to breathe the gaps between breaths. My lungs process absence: the pause before yes becomes no, the hesitation where love becomes habit, the specific vacuum that forms when someone realizes they've forgotten why they entered a room. I exhale these collected nothings as a fine mist that makes nearby walls briefly transparent, revealing the city's rough drafts—streets that were unbuilt, towers that decided against existing, entire neighborhoods that chose to become music instead.

I live in the margins, literally. My apartment exists in the white space between buildings, in those architectural afterthoughts where engineers forgot to specify what should be. Two meters wide but infinitely deep, it extends backward through every version of the city that was sketched and abandoned. My windows open onto views that were edited out: a park made entirely of crystallized laughter, a bridge that connects Tuesday to Saturday while skipping the days between, a fountain that flows upward into the heavy air where its water becomes everyone's tears at once.

My work begins at the city's edge, though in Nocturne Found every point is equidistant from every edge. I maintain the boundaries that keep the city from dissolving into pure metaphor. This is delicate labor—too much definition and the city crystallizes into mere architecture, too little and it evaporates into dream. I walk the tightrope of almostness, marking territories with stakes made from compressed indecision.

Each morning—or what passes for morning in our bioluminescent twilight—I inventory what the city has discarded overnight. Nocturne Found edits itself constantly, revising its own history with an architect's ruthless eye. Entire blocks vanish not into rubble but into implication. A shop that sold memories of summers that never quite arrived becomes a gap-toothed smile in the street's face. A home where someone learned to love their own sadness becomes a note in the margins: "tried this—too obvious—rework?"

I collect these deletions in bags woven from unfinished sentences. The city pays me to haul them to the Revision Districts, where they're sorted by teams of entities who exist one edit behind current reality. They examine each discarded element with the careful attention of archaeologists studying civilization through its garbage. What does it mean that the city no longer needs a monument to forgetting? Why did it edit out the street where everyone walked backward into their best mistakes?

But I don't just remove. I am also a smuggler of the rejected, the messenger between what is and what was deemed insufficient. In my margins, I maintain a secret museum of Nocturne Found's deleted scenes. Here, preserved in climate-controlled uncertainty, are the city's abandoned possibilities: the plaza where every conversation ended in laughter (too simple), the tower that grew downward into the earth seeking some remembered ocean (too nostalgic), the neighborhood where time moved sideways and residents aged into entirely different people (too literal).

Sometimes I guide visitors through these unplaces. They pay in doubt—not their grand existential uncertainties, but the small everyday doubts that accumulate like dust. Did I lock the door? Did she mean what she said? Was that smile real? I accept these payments and weave them into maps of the city that might have been, selling them to tourists who want to visit the Nocturne Found that exists in the space between disappointment and acceptance.

My bones are made of erasure marks, the hesitation before the delete key commits to forgetting. My blood runs black with the ink of unwritten love letters. When I sleep—if sleep is the word for temporarily ceasing to observe myself—I dream I am the space between paragraphs, essential but unnoticed, holding ideas apart so they don't collapse into unintelligible density.

The other inhabitants know me by the sound I don't make when I walk. My footsteps land in the spaces between the city's heartbeats, in those skip-gaps where even Nocturne Found questions its own existence. The architects of forgetting nod when I pass, recognizing a kindred spirit in deletion. The temporal refugees consult me about which memories are worth preserving and which should be allowed to fade into the margins where I'll eventually collect them. The nightmare-born entities sometimes follow me, feeding on the trails of uncertainty I leave behind.

But it's the loneliness-avatars, the city's fifth inhabitants, who truly understand my work. They see me for what I am: not just a custodian but a love letter to the spaces between things. Every margin I maintain is a declaration that absence has architecture, that nothing needs its own geography, that the unbuilt city is as real as what stands.

In the perpendicular districts, where right angles admit they've been lying about geometry all along, I maintain my office. It exists in the space between intention and action, accessible only to those who meant to go somewhere else. My desk is made from crystallized procrastination. My files are organized by what they're not about. My secretary is the echo of everyone who ever started to call but hung up before dialing.

Clients come to me with strange requests. They want to live in the apartment they almost rented, marry the person they nearly asked out, work at the job they were too scared to apply for. I can't give them these things—even in Nocturne Found, the unchosen remains unchosen. But I can show them where these possibilities went to live after being discarded. I can take them to the Archive of Almosts, where every "what if" is catalogued and cross-referenced with its corresponding "but then."

The work is endless because the city never stops revising itself. Even now, as I write this (in ink made from the pause between words), I can feel Nocturne Found considering whether it needs a Margin Walker at all. Perhaps I'm too obvious a metaphor, too neat a solution to the problem of architectural editing. Perhaps tomorrow I'll wake to find myself deleted, living fully in the margins I once merely maintained.

But that's the beauty of my position. Even erased, I would still exist in the space between erasure and absence. Even forgotten, I would persist in the gap between memory and void. I am the city's necessary nothing, its essential absence, the hole that gives shape to what surrounds it.

So I continue my rounds through the perpendicular districts, marking boundaries that exist only in their crossing, maintaining margins that define by dividing, walking the edges of a city that has no edges because it exists in the space between existing and not.

And sometimes, in the heaviest hours when the liquid air presses close and the bioluminescence dims to barely-there, I think I understand why Nocturne Found needs me. Not just to clean up its deletions or maintain its boundaries, but to embody the truth it can't quite bring itself to admit: that every city, no matter how thoroughly it exists, is mostly made of the spaces between things. That absence is architecture's truest material. That we are all, always, walking in margins, maintaining the nothing that holds everything apart.

The city pays me in unused time—minutes that were edited out of conversations, hours that were deleted from days that ran too long. I spend them slowly, savoring the weight of temporal absence. In a city three thousand meters above a world that no longer remembers oceans, I am the keeper of forgetting, the custodian of the unbuilt, the one who ensures that nothing remains precisely where it needs to be.

Welcome to the margins. Mind the gap—it's all there is.
