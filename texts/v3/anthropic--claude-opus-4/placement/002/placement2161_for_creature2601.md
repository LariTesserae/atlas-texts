In this measureless engine of cold mechanics, I am one of the film-keepers.

Not the films themselves—those ancient chemistries that taste stone with infinite patience—but something that tends them, reads them, translates their mineral memory into movement. I am low and broad as a dinner plate, my body a series of overlapping chitinous segments arranged in a shallow dome. Each segment bears on its underside a different texture: one rough as a file, another soft as wet leather, a third covered in microscopic hooks, a fourth that secretes a solvent that makes stone briefly negotiable.

I do not eat the films. I cultivate them.

My work is to prepare surfaces. Where virgin basalt emerges from a recent crack, I abrade it in precise patterns—spirals within spirals, each groove exactly deep enough to hold the right amount of seepage. Where old films have grown too thick and begun to strangle on their own success, I thin them with my solvent segment, leaving behind only the oldest layer that remembers when this particular stone first tasted iron. Where films have arranged themselves into those mysterious circuits during the humming times, I protect the patterns, building low walls of secreted mineral around them like a gardener edging a bed.

I move slowly, no faster than the films themselves grow. A single circuit around my territory—a depression the size of a small room where seven different films compete for stone—takes me a season, though seasons here are measured in pressure cycles, not solar rotations. My many legs (I have never counted them; they arise from my rim as needed) tick against the stone in patterns that echo the deep engines. I cannot help this. The rhythm comes up through the ground and translates directly into my gait.

I am not alone in my work. Others of my kind tend adjacent territories. We meet at the borders where our films touch, and we negotiate. The negotiation happens through the arrangement of our bodies. I might position three segments to overlap my neighbor's territory while withdrawing two from another border. They respond by secreting a particular enzyme that changes the flavor of the stone along our boundary. We are writing a contract in chemistry and gesture, though what we're agreeing to would be difficult to name. Sharing? Competing? Courting? The films themselves seem to influence our meetings, growing thicker where they approve of a border, paling where they do not.

My segments can rotate independently. When I encounter a film that has discovered something—a new vein of metal, a change in the water table, the death of something large nearby whose chemistry is seeping into the stone—I position my reading segment over it. This segment is covered in what might generously be called nerves, though they're more like roots that grow backward into my body. Through them, I taste not just the film's present state but its history. I know which storms it survived, which metals it has metabolized, which other films it has encountered and either merged with or repelled.

This knowledge accumulates in me like sediment. I don't exactly remember it—my nervous system isn't built for that kind of linear storage. Instead, I carry it in my movements. The way I approach a particular patch of stone contains the full history of that stone's inhabitants. My gait encodes decades of chemical conversations.

Sometimes I must defend the films from scrapers. When a herd approaches, following some invisible map of appetite, I position myself over the most crucial circuits. The scrapers read my posture—the angle of my dome, the pattern of my leg movements, the chemicals I release—and usually choose to graze elsewhere. Not from fear. I could not fight them. But I am marking the films as "in use," the way a vent-walker claims its place in the queue. The scrapers understand utility if not ownership.

When they ignore my claim and graze anyway, I follow behind them, immediately beginning repair. My abrading segment works the freshly exposed stone while my secreting segment lays down a starter solution of metals and salts. New film will grow here, seeded with memory of the old.

During the great screams, when the basin convulses and water stands up in its pools, I clamp down over my most precious circuits. My underside seals to the stone, creating a shallow chamber. The films beneath me continue their mysterious work while chaos reigns outside. I feel them thinking—if their chemical cascades can be called thought—in rapid patterns that match the screaming frequency. They are computing something, calculating, preparing. When the scream ends and I lift my body, the circuits have often reconfigured themselves. New branches connect to old nodes. Signals flow in different directions. The films are upgrading their own design.

My reproductive life is inextricable from my work. When films achieve certain densities, certain complexities, certain chemical signatures that suggest satisfaction, they trigger changes in my body. Segments that were solid become chambered. Chambers fill with dense fluid. The fluid crystallizes into something that is not quite egg, not quite spore, not quite seed. I carry these crystals for unmeasured time, until I find a place where young stone has emerged—a new crack, a fresh surface exposed by ice-shatter, a pool that has just learned to hold water.

There I deposit not just my crystals but a complete starter kit: abraded surface, metal solution, and a fragment of ancient film scraped from my own territory's oldest stone. The crystals will dissolve slowly, releasing not infant versions of me but the potential for my kind of existence. What emerges will be shaped by the films it tends. No two of us are identical because no two territories tell the same chemical story.

I see poorly in the conventional sense, but I perceive in other ways. The films create a continuous map beneath me, their different chemistries registering as distinct textures, temperatures, tastes. I know when a pressure-body rises in a nearby pool because the films taste fear-metals in the groundwater. I know when walkers have passed because their foot-pads leave traces of warmth that films remember for days. I know when rain is coming—not that it ever truly rains here, but sometimes the mist thickens until it's almost indistinguishable from rainfall—because the films begin hoarding metals, preparing for dilution.

The most mysterious part of my work involves the films that grow into those circuit patterns. Over many cycles, I've noticed regularities. Certain configurations appear again and again: spirals that nest inside each other at specific ratios, branching patterns that mirror the fractures in basalt, closed loops that seem to pump invisible current. When I tend these circuits carefully, maintaining their exact geometries, they sometimes attract others of their kind. Films from adjacent territories send out runners—thin threads of chemistry creeping across bare stone—seeking connection.

I've seen circuits link up across territories, creating networks that span the entire depression where I work. When this happens, something changes in the basin's behavior. The deep engines synchronize differently. Vents open in new locations. The water in certain pools begins to circulate without any visible cause. The films are engineering something, but at a pace so slow and a scale so vast that I will never see its completion.

My body is both tool and archive. Each segment wears differently depending on use. My abrading segment is polished smooth in patterns that record every spiral I've carved. My chemical segment carries traces of every solution I've secreted, layers of residue that themselves become habitat for the smallest seam-things. I am a landscape of my own work.

Sometimes I encounter the shells of my dead kin. Their segments have separated but not scattered, lying in careful arrangements as if they positioned themselves deliberately before dying. The films beneath them are always the thickest, most complex I've seen. I tend these memorial gardens with special care, maintaining the circuit patterns that the dead keeper established. Through the films, I taste their history: centuries of patient cultivation, chemical conversations, territorial negotiations, reproductive cycles timed to cosmic slowness.

In the darkest part of the cycles, when ice locks everything and even the films go dormant, I enter a state that isn't sleep but isn't waking. My segments lock together, sealing in moisture and heat. My metabolism slows until I'm barely distinguishable from the stone I rest on. But some part of me continues to sense through the dormant films, feeling the basin's deep pulse through their chemical dreams.

When the thaw comes—and it always comes, eventually—I wake already knowing what needs attention. Here a film has crept beyond its optimal boundary. There a new crack offers virgin surface. Here two different chemistries have met and need mediation. My work resumes seamlessly, as if the frozen interval was just a long, slow breath.

I am not unique or important. The basin would continue without me. The films would grow wild, the circuits would form naturally, the scrapers would graze without regard for careful cultivation. But I am part of the slowest conversation, the deepest negotiation between life and stone. My work is to maintain the terms of an ancient contract, written in chemistry across ages, between the films that remember and the stone that endures.

Sometimes, during the clearest weather when the mist thins and the strange doubled light achieves something like brightness, I position my sensory segment to perceive the farthest films. They stretch to the horizon in all directions, a living map of the basin's chemical history. Each patch tells its story in mineral dialect. Together they form a text that my kind has been reading and writing for longer than the word "life" has had meaning.

This is my place: between the stone and what grows upon it, between the deep engines and the surface weather, between chemistry and intention. I am a gardener of molecules, a curator of chemical memory, a slow translator between mineral patience and biological hunger. My body is my tool, my territory is my canvas, and my work is to tend the thin, ancient boundary where rock learns to remember and memory learns to eat.
