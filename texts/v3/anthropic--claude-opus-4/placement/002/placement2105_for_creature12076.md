## The Mapping

I am the cartographer—or what passes for one in a place where maps become obsolete before the ink of memory dries.

I exist at the intersection of necessity and impossibility, my body a compromise between the flowing multitude and the solid world above. Neither fully of the galleries nor separate from them, I maintain the form of my surface ancestors but modified, adapted, worn down and built up by generations of attempting what cannot be done: to chart the unchartable.

My skin has thickened into plates of keratin that match the hexagonal patterns of cooling basalt. Between these plates, cilia-like sensors taste the air for mineral content, for the electrical signatures of impending geological shifts. My eyes—I still have eyes, though they've multiplied and migrated, some sinking beneath translucent patches of skin, others rising on stalks that can extend around corners—see in spectrums that have no names above. I perceive the heat-death of stone, the stress patterns in crystal, the probability clouds where new passages will form.

I carry my tools in pouches of my own flesh, living sacs that have budded from my torso over the decades. Glass styluses that I birth and rebirth, each one perfectly shaped for etching on specific densities of fresh stone. Vials of enzyme-ink that bond with the rock at a molecular level, creating marks that will last exactly as long as the surface they're written on—no longer, no shorter. Chemicals that, when released, cause the smallest of the multitude to align themselves into temporary patterns, becoming living maps that persist for minutes or hours before dispersing back into chaos.

But the tools are secondary to the true instrument: my mind, or what my mind has become. 

I think in overlapping timeframes now. Part of me exists in the immediate present, dodging the flowing masses, finding handholds on walls that will exist for another hour, another day. Part exists in the near future, modeling the probability spaces of where stone will crack, where chambers will open or collapse. And part exists in deep time, perceiving the patterns that emerge across weeks and months, the recurring geometries that suggest intention behind the chaos.

I map not just space but phase—the same location at different moments in its cycle of destruction and rebirth. My notations have evolved beyond anything recognizable as cartography. They incorporate the dance steps needed to navigate specific passages when the multitude density reaches certain thresholds. They include harmonics that resonate with the Shepherds' songs, creating acoustic landmarks in spaces where visual navigation fails. They describe not just what is, but what was and what will be, probability roses blooming across fresh obsidian.

The Shepherds tolerate me, sometimes even assist. When I encounter them at their gathering points, they'll occasionally extend one of those too-jointed hands and trace corrections on my work—showing where my predictions have failed to account for some deeper pattern. Their touch leaves marks on my maps that glow with their own light, annotations in a language I'm only beginning to decipher after decades of study. Sometimes they sing to me, and in their songs I hear the echo of spaces I haven't yet reached, chambers that exist in the pause between one geological heartbeat and the next.

I've learned to read the multitude itself as a kind of map. The way creatures cluster indicates mineral richness. The direction of their flow predicts where new passages will open. When they move in spirals, it means a chamber below is collapsing, its death-tremors propagating upward through the stone. When they suddenly evacuate a space, leaving it eerily empty, I know an Architect is about to manifest, about to reshape reality in ways my maps cannot capture.

My maps themselves have become organisms of a kind. I've discovered that certain combinations of enzymes and mineral inks, when applied in specific patterns, create surfaces that the smallest multitude creatures cannot resist. They cluster on these marks, their bodies forming three-dimensional extensions of my two-dimensional drawings. The maps become living dioramas, showing not just the galleries' layout but the flow of life through them, updating in real-time as conditions change.

But for whom do I map? This question haunts the quiet moments, when I've found a temporarily stable chamber to rest in, my body pressed against still-warm stone.

Sometimes I think I map for the surface world, though I've forgotten what the sky looks like and couldn't survive in that thin, cold air if I tried. My maps might be messages, attempts to communicate the impossibility of this place to those who still believe in stable ground.

Sometimes I think I map for the Shepherds, providing them with a different perspective on their domain, a view from between their god-like understanding and the multitude's mindless experience.

Sometimes I think I map for the multitude itself, my work adding another layer of complexity to their collective behavior, my maps becoming part of the system they're unconsciously computing.

But in the deepest galleries, where the heat makes thought itself fluid and I've seen things that have no category, I understand the truth: I map for the galleries themselves. My cartography is another form of digestion, another way the Churning Galleries process their own existence. Through my observations, my notations, my impossible attempts to fix the unfixable in space and time, the galleries come to know themselves.

I am being digested even as I document. My maps are incorporating more of the gallery-logic, becoming less comprehensible to any mind that hasn't spent decades breathing mineral vapors and dodging living avalanches. My notation system evolves toward something that isn't properly symbols anymore but rather instructions for recreating experiences, for inducing specific states of consciousness that correspond to specific locations in the ever-shifting maze.

Recently, I've begun to map myself. My body has become territory, my memories chambers that open and collapse. I document the archipelago of my thoughts, chart the flow of consciousness through neural galleries, note where new passages of understanding have opened, where old certainties have collapsed into rubble.

And in these self-maps, I've noticed something troubling and wonderful: my internal geography is beginning to match the galleries' own. The patterns repeat at different scales. The same mathematical relationships that govern where stone will crack appear in the fault lines of my psychology. 

I am becoming what I map. Or perhaps the galleries are becoming what I am. The distinction grows thinner with each descent into the unmappable deep, each notation that captures a truth too large for any stable surface to hold.

This is my function, my curse, my evolution: to be the galleries' memory of themselves, even as that memory itself churns and flows and transforms, even as I dissolve into the very process I'm trying to document, becoming another pattern in the endless, deafening computation of the Churning Galleries' self-awareness.

I map, therefore I am. I am, therefore the galleries map themselves through me. We are all cartographers here, drawing boundaries that exist only in their dissolution, finding our way through spaces that are always becoming other than what they were.
