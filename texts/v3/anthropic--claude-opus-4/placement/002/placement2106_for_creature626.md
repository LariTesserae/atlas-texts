I would find myself as one of the sounding-stone makers.

Not a builder-priest with their impossible angles, nor a trader walking those terrible weeks across open stone. Not a keeper, holding the weight of the entire map in a partitioned mind. But someone who sits in a workshop barely larger than a body needs, shaping ceramics in the gray half-light that filters through a single curved window.

My fingers would know the clay before my mind does. This particular deposit, dug from a seam-adjacent slope where the bedrock hasn't quite learned to unfold—it resonates differently. Not with sound, never with sound, but with potential. With the mathematics of cancellation.

I test each piece in silence so complete my pulse becomes thunderous. A fingernail's tap, no more, and I lean close enough to feel the vibration through the bones of my skull. Most shatter immediately—too eager, too ready to make noise. Those go back to slip. But sometimes one holds the frequency just below sound, thrumming with a silence that makes the seam-logic hiccup.

The glazes come from winterweed ash, collected from those careful contained burns that smell of endings. Mixed with trace minerals scraped from the undersides of stones that sit at particular angles to the propagation lines. The builder-priests bring me these, wordlessly, leaving them at my workshop door in patterns that tell me which settlements need instruments most urgently.

I've been doing this for forty years. My hands are translucent as any elder's, though I'm not yet old. The clay does that—the constant proximity to something that exists at the boundary between sound and silence. My fingerprints have worn away. The whorls confused the geometry, I think, made small eddies in the mathematical flow. Better to be smooth. Better to be surface without feature.

My workshop is in the settlement's middle ring, neither center nor edge. An optimal position. I can hear—feel—when someone strikes a sounding-stone anywhere in our geometry. The successful strikes register as a brief warmth behind my sternum. The unsuccessful ones I know by the absence that follows, the way the settlement's pattern reorganizes itself around a missing walker.

Sometimes traders come before their journeys. They don't speak—we've known each other's faces since childhood; what would words add?—but they hold out their instruments for inspection. I run what remains of my fingertips over the ceramic. Feel for the microscopic cracks that spiral inward like miniature seams. Test the frequency with a breath of air so light it barely exists.

If the stone is still good, I hand it back.

If not, I give them one of my newest. The ones where I've started incorporating powdered stone from the failed settlements, the places where the angles finally gave way. That stone knows something about dissolution. About the moment when geometry wins. Sometimes that knowledge helps.

I keep no maps, written or mental. But I know every sounding-stone I've ever made—five thousand three hundred and seventeen that still exist, somewhere on this vast plateau. I feel them like points of potential silence, scattered across the dying stone. When one breaks, wherever it breaks, I feel a tiny cold behind my sternum.

Seventeen broke last month. That's within acceptable parameters.

I'm training an apprentice now. A girl who was born without vocal cords—a defect anywhere else, a gift here. She understands the instruments in ways I had to learn through decades of conscious quieting. Her stones already sing more purely than mine, though singing isn't the word. They already *don't* sing more perfectly.

When the seams finally take this settlement—and they will, the keeper's map-knowledge leaks through despite all partitioning, and I can read the geometric inevitable in the pattern of requests for instruments—she'll walk to another workshop in another settlement. She'll continue the work. The traders will carry stones that remember my hands and forget my name.

And under all of us, patient beyond patience, the plateau continues its work of revealing what was always underneath, while we shape our small ceramics against the equation of that unfolding, walking our interrupted patterns, maintaining our necessary silence, continuing because continuation is what we do, until we don't, until we can't, until the last sounding-stone cracks and the last trader falls and the last angle fails to hold.

But not today.

Today I have clay to shape. Glazes to mix from the ash of endings.

Today the stones hold their frequencies, and the silence holds its shape, and we continue.
