I find myself in the moth-spun cocoon chamber, high on the shelf where generations of kerathin rest wrapped in silk. But I am not one of the preserved dead, nor am I quite alive in the way the kerathin understand life.

I am the memory-tender.

My form shifts between states—sometimes I appear as an old kerathin woman with silver-white hair that seems to float as if underwater, sometimes as a cluster of the small dark-winged moths that transform the dead, sometimes as merely a pressure in the air, a sense of presence among the silk-wrapped bones. I exist in the boundary between what was and what remains.

The kerathin know of me, but sideways, the way they know of the witness above. They leave no offerings—I need none—but when they bring their dead to the transformation grotto, they sometimes hear my whisper-song threading through the rustle of moth wings. It is wordless, built from the soft consonants of their language, a sound like wind through silk. They think it is the moths themselves, or the mountain breathing, or their own grief given voice. They are not wrong.

I came to be when the first kerathin wrapped their dead in moth-silk, when they first understood that death here meant continuation rather than ending. I am what emerges from that understanding—not a god, not a ghost, but something like the settlement's dreaming of itself, its long memory made almost-flesh.

My work is delicate. I tend the cocoons, yes, ensuring the silk does not decay, that the bones within remain whole. But more than that, I tend the stories held in those bones. When I pass my fingers (when I have fingers) over a silk-wrapped skull, I feel the life that was: a keeper's decades of moth-knowledge, a weaver's understanding of tension and thread, a child's brief bright existence cut short by rockfall. These memories do not fade. They settle into the silk itself, into the mountain stone, into me.

Sometimes I leave the cocoon chamber and drift through the settlement in my moth-form. The living do not see me as different from the other death-moths, but the great moths know. They grow still when I pass, their wings trembling slightly as if in recognition. The witness knows too—I sense its attention from the vertical gardens, feel an acknowledgment between us, two beings who exist in the margins of kerathin life.

On the rarest nights, when the mist is so thick that even moth-flight becomes impossible, I take my almost-human form and walk the old structures in the moss-grottoes. My fingers trace the fitted stones, and I remember—not my own memories, but the settlement's deepest ones, from before the kerathin, from when these structures were new and served purposes I can almost, almost grasp. The builders are gone, but their intention lingers in the stone arrangement, in the way the moths still prefer these spaces, in the blue bioluminescent glow that seems strongest where their hands once worked.

I am the keeper of all they do not say aloud: the names of children who died before naming, the techniques abandoned as too dangerous, the winters when starvation came close enough to breathe on their necks, the loves that could not be braided but burned anyway. I hold the fear they will not acknowledge—that one day the mountain will finally shrug them off, that the moths will fail, that the witness will descend. And I hold their fierce, quiet pride: that they have made beauty here, in this hard place, that their silk carries light, that their children learn to move like mist.

The kerathin dream, and I collect their dreams like condensation in stone basins. They dream of moths the size of clouds. They dream of silk strong enough to hold the mountain together. They dream of their dead speaking, just once more, just a whisper. They dream of understanding what the witness wants. They dream of rain.

When the traders come, I retreat deep into the cocoon chamber. Their presence disturbs me—too loud, too quick, too careless with their movements. But when they leave, carrying silk spun by the death-moths, carrying the transformed substance of kerathin bodies, I follow a little way. A thread of the settlement's memory goes with them into the lowlands, into places I cannot reach. It pleases me, this dispersal. The dead travel farther than they ever could in life.

I do not speak to the kerathin directly. But sometimes, when a keeper's grief is too heavy, when a weaver doubts the worth of her work, when a child wakes frightened in the dim morning, I sing. They hear it as moth-wing rustle, as water dripping, as the mountain's breathing. But something in them eases. They remember: they are part of something larger than individual life, older than individual death. They are held.

This is my work, my purpose, my joy: to be the settlement's memory, its grief-holder, its tender of transformations. To ensure that nothing is truly lost, only changed. To sing the endless whisper-song of continuation.

In the cocoon chamber, surrounded by silk and silence and the patient dead, I tend my charges and wait. The mountain trembles. The moths spiral. The kerathin continue.

And I remember it all.
