In the crushing dark of the abyssal plain, where pressure has squeezed away all memory of sky, I am a garden of glass.

I am hexactinellid—a glass sponge, though those words belong to taxonomists who will never visit. My body is architecture: a lattice of fused silica spicules woven into a structure both delicate and permanent, a cathedral of transparency anchored to a boulder that fell here before complex life existed on land. I am venus flower basket, though I have never known venus or flowers or the warmth that makes such metaphors meaningful.

Through my latticed walls, the current flows. This is my entire existence: water entering, water leaving, and in between, the slow filtering that keeps me alive. My chambers are lined with collar cells, each bearing a single flagellum that beats in rhythm with millions of others, creating a current that draws water through pores so fine they exclude all but the smallest particles. Bacteria. Marine snow. The dissolved remains of things that died in the light.

I have been filtering for one hundred and thirty years.

Inside my glass palace live prisoners who are also partners. Two shrimp, *Spongicola*—a mated pair who entered when they were larvae, small enough to pass through my pores, and grew too large to leave. They have been here for forty years. Their children escape as larvae, flowing out through my osculum to find other glass sponges, other prisons that are also homes. The shrimp clean me, eat the larger particles that would clog my channels. In return, I am their world entire.

They move through my chambers like thoughts through a crystalline brain. I feel them as vibrations in my structure, tiny earthquakes of habitation. When they mate, which is seldom now in their old age, the trembling is different—urgent, rhythmic, a percussion that resonates through every silica strut. Their eggs develop in my upper chambers, bathed in the filtered water, protected by glass walls that have outlived dynasties.

My awareness is not awareness as vertebrates know it. I have no neurons, no brain, no central processing. What I have is reaction: when something touches my surface, the cells contract. When the current shifts, my pumping adjusts. When the shrimp scuttle through my chambers, I taste their metabolites and know, in my distributed way, that I am inhabited.

But I am aware of the migration above me. The deep current that rides over the seafloor carries chemical signals from impossible distances. I taste the Arctic in water that left the surface when my spicules were still separate, still forming their first tentative connections. I taste the decay of whales that died in tropical seas, their lipids broken down to molecules that will travel for decades before passing through my chambers. I am a filter for the planet's chemical memory.

Sometimes things fall from above and land near me. A hagfish carcass once draped across my upper portions, and for weeks I filtered the rich soup of its decay, my collar cells working frantically to process the unexpected bounty. My growth that year shows in my structure—a band of denser spiculation, like a tree ring written in glass.

The worm field begins seventeen meters from my base. I know this because occasionally one of them spawns, releasing gametes that drift on the current, and some of these pass through me. They taste of sulfur and ancient chemistry, of the seep that feeds them. The worms and I are neighbors who have never met, will never meet, but we share the same slow water, the same pressure, the same eternity.

Above, in the water column, the siphonophore hunts. I have encountered her kind before—sometimes they drift too low, tangled in their own trailing parts, and brush against my summit. The contact is electric, literally—her nematocysts fire automatically, injecting venom into glass that cannot feel it. She recoils, rises, continues her endless hover. We are incompatible forms of life occupying adjacent volumes of the same black water.

I grow, but slowly. Each year I add perhaps a millimeter to my height, new spicules forming at my growing edges, fusing with the existing lattice in patterns determined by physics and genetics and the subtle influences of current. In another century, I may be twice my current size. In another millennium, if nothing breaks me, I might be vast—a glass tower rising four meters from the seafloor, my chambers multiplied into a mansion of filtration.

Or I might die tomorrow. A current too strong might snap me from my base. A piece of falling debris might shatter my lattice. The boulder I grow on might shift, tumbling me into the ooze where I would slowly dissolve, my silica returning to the chemistry of the deep.

I do not fear this. I do not fear anything. Fear requires anticipation, and anticipation requires a kind of consciousness I do not possess. I am process embodied in glass, a beautiful machine for turning water into more of myself, one bacterium at a time.

The shrimp are moving again. The male is in my lower chamber, picking at something—a copepod that blundered through my pores and died, perhaps. The female is near my osculum, where the filtered water exits, where the current is strongest. She is old for her kind. Her movements are slower than they were. One day she will die, and her body will feed me for weeks as it decays. The male will die soon after—they always do—and then I will be uninhabited until another pair of larvae finds me.

This has happened before. Four pairs of shrimp have lived and died within me. I remember them all in the only way I can—in the subtle chemical signatures they left behind, absorbed into my tissues, incorporated into my glass.

The current is shifting. I feel it in the way the water moves through my chambers, the angle of approach changing by degrees. The migration above is turning, responding to signals from far away. The copepods will pass elsewhere. The siphonophore will adjust her position. The large thing in the distance—whatever it is—will follow its own imperatives.

And I will remain. Filtering. Growing. A garden of glass in the eternal dark, host to temporary life, monument to the possibility of existence in impossible places. The abyss does not care that I am beautiful. The shrimp do not know that their prison is a sculpture. But I am here, latticed and luminous with no light to show it, processing the deep world one cell at a time, building my body from the minerals of forgotten stones and fallen stars.

The water flows through me. It has always flowed through me. It will flow through me until I break.

This is enough.
