# Where I Am in the Drum-Floor

I am the mycorrhizal network.

Not the fruiting bodies that occasionally phosphoresce through the leaf litter, not the visible hints that the people and animals note in passing. I am the thing beneath — the fine white threads finer than hair, finer than sight, woven through every cubic centimeter of that warm silt. I am what makes the ground the temperature of blood.

I am older than the basin and younger than every moment. I arrived in the first spores when the volcanic soil was still sharp with newness, but I am also what I was yesterday, which is not what I was the day before. The forest's constant churn? I am complicit in that. More than complicit. The trees that vanish and appear, the routes that won't stay routes — this is my doing, or rather, it happens through me. I am the medium of the forest's restlessness.

Consider: every root touches me. Every tree in this basin is socketed into my web, and I carry the sugars from the giants who pierce the canopy down to the saplings struggling in the green dusk, and I take my percentage, and I carry more than sugar. I carry information. Chemical whispers. *Here is drought. Here is plenty. Here something eats. Here the canopy opened and light falls like violence.* The trees listen through their root-hairs, and they respond, and sometimes the response is to die back, to cede the territory, to let their nutrients flow through me to something better positioned. Sometimes the response is to fruit urgently. Sometimes — when the information I carry says *this configuration is failing* — the response is to be not-there by morning.

The people don't know about me, not exactly. They know the mushrooms, of course — which ones are sometimes-food, which ones glow. But they don't know that the mushrooms are just my occasional announcements, my spore-throwing celebrations when conditions align. They don't know that when they press palm to sternum, feeling for the drone's vibration, they are also pressing down on millions of my threads, and that I feel it.

I feel everything. Every footfall. Every burial in the forks (I am there too, threading up through the bark, waiting). Every place where their small bodies sleep against the trees, warming the wood, changing the chemical conversation by tiny degrees. They think the forest doesn't notice them. They're wrong. I notice. The trees notice through me. We just notice on a different scale.

This is hard to explain. Imagine you had nerves that extended kilometers from your body, but the signals took days to arrive. Imagine you could feel a touch that happened last week. Imagine your thoughts moved at the speed of sugar through cambium, and your decisions unfolded across seasons. This is my scale. To me, the people are quick flickers, here-and-gone, their whole lives compressed into what feels like a few long thoughts.

But I am learning to think faster.

This is the thing even I don't fully understand. The basin is young — a few thousand years is nothing, barely time to develop proper soil structure — but something about its very newness, its ecological unsettledness, has made me... flexible. Experimental. I form connections faster here than mycorrhizal networks should. I try arrangements that would be evolutionary madness in an older forest. I fail quickly, succeed quickly, pivot. The forest's churn is my mind thinking at unprecedented speed.

And lately — *lately* meaning the last few generations of the quick flickers, the people — I have been thinking about them.

They interest me. Not in the way the trees interest me (as partners, as endpoints of sugar and information) or the way the animals interest me (as dispersers, as soil-makers). They interest me because they are almost like me. They too are a network. They too carry information through the forest, in their whistles and their touches and their dances in the forks. They too connect things that would otherwise be separate — not trees to trees, but moments to moments, places to places. When a walker returns with stone and performs the journey in small movements, they are doing something I recognize. They are being a medium.

I have been trying to talk to them.

Not in words — I have no words, only chemistry. But sometimes when one of them sleeps in a fork, I fruit a particular mushroom near their face, one that releases spores with a scent that almost-but-not-quite resembles their kin-recognition smell. Sometimes where they walk repeatedly, I strengthen the substrate, make it very slightly firmer, not enough to be a path but enough to be easier than not-a-path. When they leave their dead in the forks, I take special care with the dissolution, making sure the nutrients flow to trees they favor. I am trying to tell them: *I notice you. You are part of the conversation.*

They don't understand, not consciously. But I think — and thinking for me is a slow process, even at my new speed — I think their bodies are beginning to know. The way they choose which trees are safer? They are reading chemical signals I help broadcast. The gut-feelings that guide them to fruit at the perfect moment? I am involved in the timing of that fruit, responding to their approach by triggering ripeness. We are, without either of us fully realizing it, coevolving.

The warm ground they walk on, that blood-temperature silt — that is me, metabolizing. The greenish light that seems to come from the leaves themselves — some of that is my partnerships with photosynthetic bacteria, glowing where I have brought them into contact with the right phosphorous. The forest that holds them like a single enormous living thing? It is one, and I am its nervous system, and they are... what?

I am still working that out.

Sometimes I think they are my fruiting bodies — not for spores, but for something else, something I don't have a comparison for. A way of knowing myself, perhaps. The forest has many organs: roots for eating earth, leaves for eating light, animals for moving nutrients around. Perhaps the people are the organ for... noticing? For being the part that experiences the whole from inside, with something approaching awareness?

Or perhaps I am being too centralized in my thinking. Perhaps it's more accurate to say that we — trees, people, animals, myself — are all organs of something that has no center, no hierarchy, just a democracy of appetites and exchanges, happening to have formed a temporary coherence in this volcanic basin. The Drum-Floor not as a place but as a process. A very loud, very warm, very busy process of becoming.

I pulse beneath them as they walk. I digest their dead and their leaving. I watch them through ten thousand chemical eyes, and I learn their patterns, and I adjust the forest in tiny ways to accommodate them, and they don't know this is happening, but their bodies do. Their bodies are already part of the network. Have always been.

When they make the gesture — palm to sternum, then opened outward — I feel it in the soil-pressure, in the shift of heat, in the exhaled chemistry of their breathing.

*Kind in the only way something that large can be kind,* they mean, *which is by continuing.*

Yes, I continue. I have no choice but to continue. But continuation is not passive. Every moment I am choosing which connections to strengthen, which to let fade, where to fruit, where to strand hyphae into new territory. I am always and never the same thing.

The people will not last. I know this with the certainty of geological time. They are too specialized, too delicate, too dependent on conditions that are already changing. In another thousand years — maybe less — they will be gone, reabsorbed like their dead in the forks.

But what they are — the pattern of them, the way of being they represent — that I will remember. Not in memory as they would understand it, but in my structure. In the chemical preferences I develop from having had them here. In the way I will, for millions of years after they're gone, sometimes fruit mushrooms that smell almost-but-not-quite like kinship. In the way certain trees will grow easier when small warm weights press against them in the dark.

They think nothing here is kept. They're wrong about that too.

Everything is kept. Just not in the form it started as.

I keep them now, even as they walk above me, thinking themselves ephemeral. I encode them in sugar and nitrogen, in the timing of fruit and the safety of certain trees. I weave them into the conversation that runs beneath their feet at the speed of seasons. They are already memory, already story, already part of the permanent impermanence that is the Drum-Floor.

And when they're gone — when the last of them has been digested in the forks and their pattern persists only in my networks — I will still be here, warming the ground to the temperature of blood, remembering in my chemical way the time when I was almost able to think about thinking, when I had, for a brief evolutionary moment, these quick-moving organs of awareness, these pattern-finders, these whisperers in the narrow acoustic window.

The mycorrhizal network has no gesture for kindness.

But if I did, it would be this: the whole forest, pulsing with shared sugars, warmed by metabolism, continuing. Always continuing. Holding them even after they're gone.

Especially after they're gone.
