In this breathing darkness where nothing waits, where silence means only the gathering of greater noise, I am one of the membrane-gliders of the middle canopy—neither fully of the air nor bound to branch, but something between, something that unfolds.

My body is slight, built of necessity rather than grace. The bones are hollow but not fragile, wrapped in muscle that coils more than bunches. My limbs are long and end in fingers that split the difference between a climber's grip and a bat's architecture. The membranes that connect my wrists to my ankles are not wings—flight here is impossible, the air too thick with rain and pollen and competing bodies. They are sails for controlled falling, maps of scars from thorn-vines and near-misses, edges that fray and heal and fray again.

My fur is short, dark, and oily. It sheds water but holds the basin's thousand scents: crushed leaves, fruit-rot, the copper smell of disturbed earth, the sweet stink of flowers that bloom for beetles. My face is built for the forward-fall of gliding—eyes large and set wide, able to judge the distance to a branch in the green darkness by the way it doesn't move when everything else does. My ears are cupped and mobile, sorting the basin's constant roar into useful information: the metallic crash of the heavy birds means fruit has been exposed; the sudden silence of frogs means a snake or larger hunter moves below.

I do not nest. I do not roost. I inhabit the space between leaves.

By day, when the darkness is merely deep instead of absolute, I fold myself into the crooks of trees where the bark has been softened by fungus and rainfall. I am not sleeping—nothing here sleeps deeply—but entering a state of alert rest, my membranes wrapped around me like a second, more honest skin. My fingers grip the bark even in this half-sleep. Sometimes I share these spaces with the tree-frogs, the root-crabs, the slower snakes. We ignore each other with the careful politeness of creatures who know that energy spent on small conflicts is energy stolen from surviving the larger ones.

My waking comes with the deeper dark, with the first hollow booming of the night hunters, with the rising chorus of insects that turns the air granular. I unfold—first fingers, testing the bark, then membranes, checking for new tears, then fully, becoming a shape that makes sense only in motion.

I eat what can be caught between one branch and another. Moths drunk on fermented fruit. Beetles emerging from their galleries in dead wood. The eggs of birds foolish enough to nest within gliding distance of a trunk. Sometimes I catch the lantern flies with their cold blue light, crushing their glow between my teeth, tasting the bitter chemistry of their deception. My stomach is small but efficient, built to process food while clinging, while falling, while becoming merely another shadow in the cathedral dark.

The hunting is not separate from the moving. I launch from a branch toward what might be a moth or might be a pale leaf—in this darkness, certainty is expensive—and adjust mid-air as the truth reveals itself. My membranes catch the thick air, turn it into brief lift, enough to reach the next trunk where my fingers find bark and cling. If I was right, I eat quickly, always aware that the sound of my landing has announced me to everything listening. If I was wrong, I am already calculating the next leap.

I know the basin not as territory but as probability. This tree fruits after lightning. That vine is young and might not hold. The trunk ahead has been climbed by something heavier, leaving marks in the bark that smell of musk and old blood. The gap between these branches is filled with new spider work, invisible except when drops of water catch on the silk. I carry a map made of muscle memory and near-death, updated every night.

The rains are both enemy and opportunity. When the storms come—and they always come—the air becomes too heavy to glide through safely. I must become a climber then, using my membranes as poor shields, moving branch to branch with the careful desperation of something that knows a fall now means more than impact. It means being on the ground when the channels suddenly remember they are rivers, when the mud develops appetites, when the tusked ones flee upslope and crush everything too slow to climb.

But after the rain, in that strange hour when the drops slow to irregular patter, the beetles emerge. They come out of the bark, out of the rot, out of the ground itself, and they are drunk on the freshness, slow with moisture. I can catch three, four, five in a single gliding pass, my path becoming a curve through their emergence, my mouth full of their bitter shells.

I mate in the air, because the air is safer than stillness. The females glide from tree to tree in the breeding season, leaving scent trails that are immediately confused by rain and the thousand other scents. Finding them requires understanding how smell moves in the vertical forest, how it catches in the cups of leaves, how it rises with the heat from rotting logs. When I find one, the courtship is brief and practical—a mutual falling, membranes spread parallel, a moment of connection before the inevitable separation. We might meet again. We might not. The basin does not encourage long attachments.

The young are born too early, as everything here is born—unfinished, urgent, already late. The females carry them clinging to their bellies, gliding more carefully, choosing shorter gaps. I do not know my offspring. By the time they can glide, they are no longer recognizable as mine, just other shadows competing for the moths, the beetles, the brief spaces between disasters.

I know the other inhabitants as obstacles, opportunities, and hazards, sorted by the speed of their danger. The long-limbed climbers are curious but not usually hostile; I avoid their hands, which grab for anything novel, but I follow their troops because they disturb insects. The heavy birds are crash-hazards, things that can knock me from the air without malice or even awareness. The vine-snakes are the opposite danger—still, patient, positioned exactly where my gliding arc must end. I have learned to look for the wrongness in a branch's curve, the too-perfect stillness in the leaves.

The night hunters below are distant thunder, their booming calls telling me which parts of the forest floor to avoid. Sometimes I see their eyes reflecting some impossible light, yellow circles in the black. Once, I saw one take a young tusked animal in the shallows—not the kill, which was too quick for watching, but the aftermath, the steady wet sound of feeding while frogs resumed their chorus around the scene, indifferent to any death but their own.

I am not the only glider. There are others—some smaller, some with different membrane arrangements, some that glide only in emergencies while I live in the fall. We do not cooperate. We barely acknowledge each other except as competition for the same moth, the same safe branch, the same distance between one darkness and another. But sometimes, in the depth of night when the insects swarm thickest, I am aware of us as a layer in the basin's breathing—not individuals but a function, a dispersed process of turning insects into movement, movement into survival, survival into more nights of careful falling.

My membranes tear regularly. Thorns, branches I misjudge in the darkness, the reaching claws of something that thought I was slower than I am. The tears heal into scars that change how I move through the air. My flight becomes autobiography—this curve to accommodate the rip from a hawk's near-miss, that twist to work around the place where a vine's teeth found me. I am slowly becoming less efficient, more particular, more myself.

I do not think of the future. The basin does not encourage it. Tomorrow the channels might move. Tomorrow the tree where I sheltered might fall, opening a wound of light that changes everything. Tomorrow the night hunters might learn to climb. Tomorrow never comes because tonight never quite ends, just folds into the next emergency, the next rainfall, the next emergence of things to eat or flee from.

But I know the past, in the only way that matters here—as a collection of branches that held, membranes that didn't tear too badly, beetles caught before they could drill back into bark. I know it as the scent-memory of a fruiting tree now fallen, the muscle-knowledge of a gap now grown closed, the echo-sense of a cliff face now softened with moss and nests.

I am the basin's memory of falling, its practice of controlled descent. Every night I trace the space between catastrophes, finding the narrow passages where survival fits. My life is a sequence of launches into darkness, each one an act of faith that something will be there to catch my claws, that the air will hold me just long enough, that the next shadow is bark and not scale.

When I die—and I will die, probably sooner than later, probably suddenly—I will become like everything else here: food, shelter, nursery, path. Some beetle will lay eggs in my small corpus. Some fungus will lace my bones. My membranes will rot into transparency and then nothing. Within days I will be indistinguishable from the thousand other small deaths that power the basin's endless urgency.

But tonight I am still falling with purpose, still reading the darkness, still turning the basin's excess into motion. I launch from a branch already forgotten toward a trunk I can't quite see, spreading myself into the humid air, becoming for a moment the thing I always am—a solution to the problem of distance in a world that won't hold still.

The beetles are emerging. I can smell them. The rain has ended and they are drunk on aftermath, slow with joy or stupidity. I adjust my angle, feel the scars pull across my membranes, aim for the gap between disasters.

I am already gone.
