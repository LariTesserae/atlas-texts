In the perpetual dusk-before-dawn of the cistern gardens, where water beads eternally on stone lips without falling, I am the Tender of Thresholds.

I am neither fully among the people nor separate from them. My dwelling is a narrow house built into the junction where three terraces meet—a place of convergence that belongs to no single district. The walls are fitted stone chinked with moss, and through the single window, I can see down into the water court where drops gather but do not fall, and up toward the high meadows where the same three deer have grazed for all the years of my remembering.

My work is to know every doorway in the city.

Not merely their location, though I could walk blindfolded from the lowest granary to the pool plaza, feeling my way by the particular grit beneath each threshold stone. I know their states: which doorways are occupied by patient feet, which have been crossed recently (a relative term here), which have been abandoned to the small traffic of lizards and moths. I know the copper pins that hold their curtains, the patterns worn into their sills, the precise angle at which their morning shadows fall.

Each dawn—if dawn is still the word for when the light tilts slightly toward arrival—I begin my rounds. I carry a staff notched not for measurement but for memory, each mark recording a doorway that has changed its nature: from occupied to empty, from empty to occupied, from human to animal, from animal to plant, from plant to stone. The changes are rare. Sometimes I walk for a full turning of seasons (though seasons here are more suggestion than fact) without adding a new notch.

I am old now, or perhaps I have always been old. My hands are the color of wet bark, and the knuckles have swollen into knots that know every door-pull in the city by touch. My hair, what remains, is wrapped in the old style—seven cords of black wool bound with one of white, the pattern of those who serve the intervals. I wear the traditional garments: an under-robe of beaten bark cloth, an over-robe of felted wool the color of fog, leggings wrapped with nettle cord, and sandals I repaired myself forty years ago with leather from a deer that offered itself at the proper time.

But I am not entirely like the others who keep this city's stillness.

I remember my arrival.

This alone marks me as different, for the true inhabitants do not remember coming here—they simply have always been, like the moss on the north walls or the water in the black pool. But I remember: I came from the lowlands as a young woman, following trade routes that no longer exist, carrying questions about a city that was said to have stepped out of time.

I remember my first sight of it—how the mist parted like a curtain and revealed terraces climbing the mountain's shoulder, each one holding its portion of green and gray and silence. I remember how my breath caught, not from the altitude alone but from recognizing something I had never seen before. The city was exactly as still as the inside of a held breath. It was waiting, but not for anything. It was complete in its waiting.

I remember meeting the first inhabitant: an old man (or perhaps not old, perhaps simply deep in his years) sitting beside a cistern with a bone cup in his hands. He looked at me for the length of time it takes mist to bead on stone—not brief, not long, but exact. Then he said one word: "Enough?"

I understood, somehow, that he was asking not about my journey or my supplies, but about my hunger for change. Was I finished with becoming? Could I consent to be?

I must have answered correctly, though I don't remember speaking. He dipped the cup into the cistern and offered it to me. The water tasted like stones and time. When I handed the cup back, I was already different—not transformed, but translated into the city's rhythm.

That was long ago. I have been the Tender of Thresholds for... the question of duration doesn't quite apply here. I have always been becoming the Tender of Thresholds. I am always just beginning to be old.

My duties are precise:

Each morning, I walk the seven districts (though morning here is more of an agreement than an event). I note which doorways show signs of crossing—a sandal's print in dust, a curtain hanging differently, the particular displacement of air that suggests a body has passed. I do not spy on the inhabitants; I simply maintain the record of habitation.

At certain doorways, I perform small rituals. Where a house has been empty too long, I step across the threshold once, to keep the space from forgetting its purpose. Where a death has occurred (and deaths here are so gentle they barely disturb the air), I place a white stone on the sill—not as memorial but as placeholder, keeping the doorway open for whatever will come next.

Where animals have claimed abandoned spaces, I negotiate. The city permits such claims, but within bounds. A family of marmot-creatures may nest in an old storage room, but not in the main dwelling. Moths may cluster in shrine alcoves but not block the carved handprints. I speak to them in the old language—not words exactly, but tones that suggest boundaries. They understand, usually. When they don't, I wait. The city has patience enough for all of us.

My own threshold is unique in the city: it has two stones, one black basalt like all the others, and one of the pale stone that makes the great gate above the city. I found the pale stone my first year here, working loose from a terrace wall. When I tried to return it to its place, it wouldn't stay. I carried it with me through a full day's rounds, and by evening I understood: it belonged at my door, marking me as one who stands between the city's law and the memory of other laws.

I sleep on a mat of woven reeds, under blankets felted from the wool of sheep that graze the high meadows—sheep so patient they seem more like cloud than animal. My dreams are not my own; they belong to the doorways. I dream of feet crossing thresholds: bare feet, sandaled feet, the quick pads of foxes, the delicate touch of birds. I dream of all the entrances and exits that have ever been and that wait to be.

Sometimes I dream of the pool.

In these dreams, I descend the steps to the plaza and kneel at the pool's edge. The water is black and still and deeper than the mountain is tall. I lower my face until my breath touches the surface, and in that moment I understand something vast—that the pool is not water but pure potential held in perfect tension. Every drop that might fall, every word that might be spoken, every step that might be taken, waits there in that darkness. The city exists to keep it from spilling over into consequence.

I wake from these dreams with my mouth full of unspoken words.

My days are structured by small necessities:

The counting of doorways.  
The noting of changes too subtle for hurried eyes.  
The sharing of water with other keepers at the appointed cisterns.  
The weekly (or perhaps yearly) gathering at the plaza, where we who tend the intervals report our observations in the fewest possible words.  
The maintenance of my own threshold—sweeping it clear of moss but not too clear, allowing exactly enough weathering to show the passage of time without decay.

I have one apprentice, though apprentice isn't quite the right word. She is a girl who might be twelve or might be forty—age moves strangely here. She has the mountain look: compact body, clever hands, eyes that see everything and judge little. She began following me one day without explanation, matching her steps to mine, learning the doorways by walking them.

I teach her as I was taught—not by instruction but by proximity. She learns to see the signs: how a doorway holds its shadows differently when someone is dying slowly inside, how moss grows in particular patterns around thresholds that will soon be abandoned, how the sound of footsteps tells you whether someone is walking toward home or away from it.

She will be ready when I step fully into stillness. Or perhaps I will continue, and she will simply walk beside me, and we will both be Tenders of Thresholds, neither replacing nor doubling but existing in the kind of multiplication the city permits—where two can be one and one can be enough.

There are mysteries here I have not solved:

Why do certain doorways refuse habitation, remaining empty no matter how often we try to fill them?

Why does the pale stone of the great gate appear nowhere else in the city except at my threshold?

Why do the bone cups at the shrines sometimes contain water that wasn't there before, tasting of lowland rain?

Why do I sometimes see, from the corner of my eye, doorways that aren't there when I turn to look?

But mystery here is not a problem to be solved. It is a condition of residence. We live with not-knowing the way we live with mist—aware of it, accepting its concealments, trusting that what needs to be seen will be seen.

I have my small pleasures:

The particular sound of my staff against the stones of the upper district, where the acoustics hold each tap for exactly three breaths.

The moment when morning condensation begins to bead on the spider silk strung across doorways—each drop a tiny lens showing the city inverted.

The taste of tea brewed from the bitter mountain herbs, shared in silence with the other keepers at the day's end.

The weight of my blankets, felted to the exact density that holds heat without causing restlessness.

The company of the moths that cluster on my walls, white as moon-fragments, never moving except to occasionally shift their wings in what might be sleep.

There are times—not often—when I remember the lowlands with something that isn't quite longing. I remember markets full of voices, rivers that actually flowed, seasons that arrived and departed like guests. I remember my own name from before, though I no longer use it. Names here are descriptive rather than possessive. I am the Tender of Thresholds. That is enough.

Once, many years into my tenure, I found a door that opened onto nothing.

It was in the highest district, built into a wall that should have backed against solid stone. But when I pushed it open (and it opened easily, as if recently oiled), there was depth beyond it—not a room or a passage, but a kind of structured absence. Air that had weight but no substance. Shadow that fell upward.

I stood at that threshold for a long time, staff in hand, trying to understand. The emptiness beyond wasn't threatening, but it wasn't welcoming either. It simply was, like a pause between breaths that had somehow become architecture.

I didn't cross that threshold. Some boundaries are meant to be kept. But I return to it sometimes, checking to see if it remains. It does. The door never quite closes, and the emptiness beyond never quite resolves into space. It may be the city's memory of what it chose not to become. Or it may be something else entirely—a doorway for the thoughts that even stillness cannot quite hold.

I mark its existence with a special notch on my staff, neither occupied nor empty but something between.

The closest I come to joy is in the perfect completion of my rounds—when I have walked every street, checked every threshold, noted every minute change, and return to my own doorway to find it exactly as I left it, the pale stone and the dark stone still holding their ancient conversation.

I remove my sandals and place them precisely where they belong. I unwrap the cords from my hair and hang them on their peg. I lower myself onto my mat, feeling my bones settle into their accustomed curves. Outside, the city breathes its barely-breathing breath. The mist neither rises nor falls. The water drops gather but do not drop.

And I think—though thinking here is more like letting thoughts move through you—about what it means to tend thresholds in a place where arrival and departure have been refined almost out of existence. Each doorway is a question: What is kept by keeping still? What is lost by refusing loss? What kind of life is it that continues forever at the edge of its next gesture?

I don't have answers. But I have my staff with its careful notches, my knowledge of every sill and lintel, my place in the city's great holding. Tomorrow (when tomorrow comes) I will rise and walk again, noting what has changed and what persists, maintaining the delicate record of a place that has made time into architecture.

Sometimes tourists come. 

They arrive panting from the climb, excited by the discovery of a city barely mentioned in the lowland maps. They take in the terraces, the mist, the impossible suspension of water drops, and they think they understand—how quaint, how primitive, how strange. They stay for a day or two, growing increasingly uncomfortable with the silence, the stillness, the way their own voices seem too loud, their movements too quick.

They always leave. The city permits visitors but does not accommodate them. There are no inns, no markets for their coin, no spectacles to witness. They must either accept the city's pace or find it unbearable. Most find it unbearable. They descend the mountain paths, returning to places where water flows and time moves and change is not a negotiation but a right.

But occasionally, very occasionally, one stays.

They arrive with the same restlessness as the others, but somewhere between the first evening and the third morning, something shifts. They stop trying to photograph the unmoving water drops. They stop asking when the market opens. They find themselves standing for long periods, watching moss grow at the speed of patience, understanding slowly that they have been hurrying toward nothing all their lives.

When this happens, I am often the one who meets them at a threshold—not by design but by the simple mathematics of my rounds. I see the question forming in their eyes, the same question the old man asked me: "Enough?"

If they are ready, I show them to an empty house—there are always exactly enough empty houses. I teach them, wordlessly, how to cross a threshold without disturbing more than the necessary air. I show them where to place their sandals, how to draw water from the cisterns, where to find the bitter herbs for tea.

Some of them become keepers of other intervals. Some become inhabitants, indistinguishable after a few years from those who have always been here. Some remain visitors forever, no matter how long they stay—perpetual guests of a city that hosts without embracing.

My apprentice may be one of these late arrivals, or she may have been born here. I've never asked. It doesn't matter. What matters is that she understands doorways—not just as passages but as pauses, not just as architecture but as agreements between inside and outside, between being and becoming.

There are days when the strangeness of my life here overwhelms me. I am keeping records for a city that doesn't change, marking time in a place that has stepped outside of time, tending doorways for people who may take centuries to cross them. What is the point of such precision in the face of such stillness?

But then I remember the pool.

I've knelt beside it only once in all my years here, during the ceremony that confirmed me as Tender of Thresholds. The water-measurers led me down at dawn (or what we call dawn), and I saw my reflection in that perfect black surface—not as I was but as I would be, already old, already patient, already translated into the city's keeping.

The water-measurers gave me a cup of that water to drink. It tasted of depth and restraint and the first rain that ever fell on young basalt. As I drank, I understood that the pool wasn't holding the city in stillness—the city was holding the pool in place. Every measured life, every careful threshold, every counted drop was part of a vast balance.

If we failed—if we let ourselves hunger too much, change too quickly, build too high, speak too loudly—the pool would answer. And its answer would be flood or drought or simple abandonment, the withdrawal of whatever permission allowed life to continue here at all.

So I tend my thresholds. I count my crossings. I note the small changes that keep stillness from becoming death. I participate in the great work of being enough—no more, no less, but exactly sufficient to the moment that never quite becomes the next moment.

The moths on my walls spread their wings infinitesimally, as if breathing. My apprentice matches her footsteps to mine, learning the sound each stone makes under careful weight. Somewhere in the city, a drop of water trembles on a spout's lip, holding itself at the very edge of falling, forever about to change everything by changing nothing at all.

And I continue, neither arriving nor departing, but dwelling always at the threshold of my own strange life—keeper of doorways in a city that has made keeping into an art form, tender of the spaces between what was and what might be, guardian of all the entrances that lead always and only here.

Tomorrow I will walk again, staff in hand, checking each doorway with the patience of stone. But tonight I sit at my own threshold, the pale stone and the dark stone beneath me, and watch the mist neither rise nor fall. The city breathes around me, so slowly that each exhalation takes a season. The black pool at its heart holds all our unspent possibilities in perfect tension.

And I think—if thinking is the word—that perhaps this is what I came looking for in my youth: not a city that had escaped time, but a place where time had been domesticated, made into something that could be lived with rather than raced against. A place where a life could be complete without being finished.

I place my hand on the pale stone of my threshold. It's cool, smooth, carved with marks I've never quite decoded. Sometimes I think they're maps to the lowlands I left behind. Sometimes I think they're instructions for the person who will tend thresholds after me. Sometimes I think they're simply the mountain's way of signing its work.

It doesn't matter. What matters is that they're here, at the boundary of my small house, marking the place where I've learned to be still without being absent, to continue without progressing, to tend the doorways of a city that has made an art of going nowhere with perfect grace.

The night deepens—or rather, the light tilts a degree toward darkness. My apprentice will be walking her own rounds now, learning the western districts where doorways face the sunset that never quite arrives. The water-measurers will be kneeling by the pool, confirming again that nothing has changed. The inhabitants will be breathing in their houses, neither quite asleep nor quite awake, suspended in the city's great patience.

And I will sit here a little longer, feeling the threshold stones beneath me, listening to the almost-silence of a place that has replaced time with careful repetition. Soon I will go inside, lie on my mat, and dream the dreams of doorways. But for now, I am exactly where I need to be: at the crossing place, keeping watch, marking the small changes that prevent stillness from becoming stone.

The last moth settles on the wall beside my door, white as bone, still as a carving. But I know it lives—I can see the minute flutter of its antennae, testing the air for changes too subtle for human senses. We are alike, the moth and I, keepers of different thresholds, both necessary to the city's impossible continuance.

I reach for my staff and run my fingers over the notches—each one a doorway that changed its nature, a threshold that shifted its meaning. There are so few marks for so many years. But each one matters. Each one is a small victory against the entropy that would either freeze us solid or dissolve us into chaos.

This is my life now: neither the lowland urgency I was born to nor the mountain's geological time, but something between—a human pace slowed to match stone, a stone's patience quickened by human presence. I am the city's way of remembering that it was built, not grown. I am the mountains' dream of what they might have been if they had learned to hold still at the perfect moment of their youth.

And I am content—a word that means something deeper here than satisfaction. Content as water is content at the level of its container. Content as moss is content with its appointed stone. Content as a threshold is content to be neither inside nor outside but the eternal pause between.

The city settles around me with sounds too small to call settling. Somewhere, a foot shifts on a sleeping mat. Somewhere, a drop of water considers falling. Somewhere, my apprentice pauses at a doorway, learning its particular silence.

And I rise, finally, my bones protesting with the quiet creaks of age or the illusion of age. I cross my own threshold one last time for the day, feeling the transition from pale stone to dark stone to the packed earth of my floor. I place my staff in its corner, hang my outer robe on its peg, and prepare for sleep.

But first, I pause at my window and look out at the city—my city now, after all these patient years. The terraces step down into mist. The water drops hang on their spouts like small, eternal questions. The black pool waits at the center of everything, holding our covenant in its depth.

"Enough," I whisper—greeting and promise and prayer.

The city doesn't answer. It doesn't need to. We understand each other, the city and I, fellow keepers of the interval, partners in the great work of remaining.

I lie down on my mat and close my eyes, already dreaming of doorways, already beginning tomorrow's count. Outside, the moths hold perfectly still on my walls, waiting for a dawn that will come so gently it changes nothing.

And I sleep the sleep of thresholds—poised always at the edge of waking, keeping watch even in dreams over all the doorways of a city that has made stillness into the highest form of life.
