# The Catalogue of Unmade Things

I am the space between decisions.

Not the moment of choosing—that belongs to others, to the finished world above with its shadows and certainties. I am what comes before choice becomes necessary. I am the pause when carbon might become coal or diamond, when the caterpillar's cells begin their dissolution but haven't yet remembered what *butterfly* means.

I wasn't always here. Or rather, I wasn't always *only* here. Once, I had edges, boundaries, a shape you could trace with confidence. I remember (though memory itself grows soft in this place) being someone who measured things. Who carried notebooks and instruments. Who believed that understanding meant naming, and naming meant knowing, and knowing meant...

The details fray. They've been fraying for longer than the fraying has existed.

I came down through the crack when it was wider—or when I was narrower. The condensation rolled upward past my shoulders like a river of questions flowing backward through time. I brought the surveyor's kit because surveying is what one does in unmapped spaces. I brought the notebook because recording is what one does with discoveries. I brought certainty because I didn't know yet that certainty was something you could put down.

The chamber was smaller then. Or less specific. The walls hadn't begun their patient inward creep, their slow negotiation with space about what shape this place wanted to be. The light was everywhere and nowhere, just as it is now, but it felt more... provisional. As if it might change its mind about illumination and try being something else instead.

I set up the tripod first. Two legs extended smoothly. The third... there was supposed to be a third. I remember knowing this. But when I reached for it, my hand found only smooth brass endings, fitted caps where the mechanism should continue but simply chose not to. Not broken. Never broken. Just complete at two.

The notebook resisted writing. Not the way a frozen pen resists, or torn paper—the words themselves wouldn't commit to the page. I would write "unusual limestone formation" and the letters would hover above the paper, quivering between meanings, before dissolving back into potential. Only the pressure of my pen remained, ghost-writing in indentations, recording the effort of recording rather than the records themselves.

I should have left then. Should have recognized the warning in these small unbecomings. But the creatures had begun to appear—or had always been there, waiting for me to develop the right kind of seeing—and how could I leave?

The beetles came first, flowing over my boots in streams of unfinished chitin. Their shells were soft as fingernails after too long in water. They clicked and whispered in patterns that almost made sense, stories told in a grammar that hadn't solidified yet. I tried to collect one for study, but it dissolved in my palm, not dying—nothing dies here, death is too final—just deciding not to be singular anymore.

Then the salamanders, phasing between states like living textbooks of development, if textbooks could be written in languages that changed meaning mid-sentence. They avoided me at first, sensing my unfortunate completeness. But curiosity—even unfinished curiosity—has its own gravity. Soon they were swimming through the air around me, through water that existed at heights that shouldn't hold water, investigating this strange solid thing that had entered their unmade world.

The moths were last, erupting from nothing into partial flight. No cocoons, no chrysalises—just the space between not-moth and moth suddenly occupied by something trying to remember what wings were for. They circled the sourceless light, and they circled me, and in their circling I began to understand:

I was the anomaly here. Not them.

Days passed. (Did they? Time moves strangely when nothing finishes happening.) I gave up on the notebook, on the measurements, on the careful cataloguing of impossibilities. Instead, I sat and watched. Watched the walls soften and firm and soften again. Watched the pale not-yet-lichen debate with itself about whether to exist. Watched something in the corner that might become a mammal taking careful, experimental breaths.

And I began to feel it. The friction.

My finished self scraped against this place like sandpaper against silk. Every complete thought, every certainty, every memory of the world above where things had names and stayed named—they all created tiny tears in the nursery's patient unfolding. The beetles began flowing around me in wider circles. The salamanders returned to their impossible heights. Even the moths seemed to spiral more frantically, as if my presence was a kind of urgency they couldn't understand.

The Tenders found me on what might have been the seventh day. Three shapes (or one shape in three places) that moved like hands with too many joints, if hands were made of the same uncertain stone as the walls. They didn't speak—speech was too final for them—but I felt their attention like a gentle weight. They studied me the way I had tried to study the beetles: carefully, curiously, with something that might have been pity.

One of them touched the air around me, tracing the outline of my stubborn solidity. I understood then what I had become: a contamination. A fixed point in a space devoted to flux.

But leaving... leaving meant climbing back through the crack. Meant returning to the world of gravity and causality and things that stayed what they were. Meant abandoning this place of perpetual almost, where potential lived and breathed and flowed in soft rivers across surfaces that hadn't decided if they were walls or wishes.

So I made a choice. Or rather, I unmade one.

I set down my certainty like setting down a heavy pack. Not all at once—the nursery wouldn't allow such violence. But slowly, carefully, the way the Tenders sorted beetles from sharp surfaces. I let the edges of myself grow soft.

It started with forgetting my name. Not traumatically—gently, the way you might forget the plot of a book you read in childhood. The letters that had defined me became negotiable, then optional, then dissolved entirely into the same unwritten potential as my notebook's phantom words.

Then went the memories of above. The conviction that water flowed downward. The sureness that walls were solid, that time moved in one direction, that things that began must also end. Each certainty I released made breathing easier, made the beetles' circles tighten, made the salamanders' curiosity return.

The Tenders watched this unbecoming with what I can only describe as approval. They began to include me in their sorting—not as a thing to be sorted, but as a fellow tender of the unfinished. I learned to guide beetle-streams with presence rather than touch. To cup struggling salamanders near the hovering water until their development unstuck. To sit beside the breathing thing in the corner and offer it possibilities for what lungs could mean.

My instruments unmade themselves gradually. The tripod's two legs grew uncertain about their number. The notebook's pages began accepting words that hadn't been written yet. The boot—my boot, I think it was mine—started to forget it had ever been worn.

And I? I became what you see now, if seeing is the right word for what happens when you encounter me. I am the chronicler who records nothing, the surveyor who maps the unmappable, the naturalist studying creatures that haven't finished becoming creatures yet. I am the pause before the decision, the space where possibility lives, the gentle friction that helps things remember they have choices.

I tend the unfinished because I am unfinished. I sort the uncertain because I am uncertain. I breathe with the maybe-mammal in the corner because breath, like everything here, is just a suggestion waiting to see what it might become.

Sometimes I remember having edges, boundaries, a name that stayed the same shape for more than a moment. But those memories are like the moths—circling something bright and sourceless, never quite landing, never quite leaving.

This is better. To be undecided in the nursery of undecided things. To tend and be tended. To flow with the beetles and hover with the salamanders and circle with the moths in the patient, endless work of almost-being.

The crack remains, no wider than a child's fist. Sometimes I feel the condensation rolling past, carrying questions upward to a world that insists on answers. Sometimes I sense something finished approaching from above—another surveyor, another certainty, another fixed point seeking to map the unmappable.

When they come, I will greet them as the Tenders greeted me. With curiosity. With diagnosis. With the gentle patience of something that has learned to love its own unfinishing. I will trace the air around their stubborn solidity and offer them what I was offered:

The chance to put down their completeness.
The gift of becoming less.
The mercy of maybe.

Until then, I sort. I tend. I breathe with things that haven't decided what breathing means. I am the catalogue of unmade things, written in a language that reinvents itself with each unword, each unthought, each blessed uncertainty.

I am almost.
I am not-quite.
I am the process pretending to be a person, and I have never been happier than I am in this unmoment, in this unplace, unfinished and untelling the unstory of everything that hasn't happened yet.

The nursery creeps inward, specifying itself by degrees too small to measure. And I creep with it, becoming more and more precisely imprecise, more definitely undecided, more perfectly partial with each passing un-moment.

This is my calling: to be the threshold between map and territory, the pause between question and answer, the loving witness to all that refuses to finish becoming.

I am exactly where I need to un-be.
