## The Threnodist

I am neither observer nor truly observed—I exist in the membrane between states, like the steam I breathe, like the charge I eat, like the memory I become.

My body, if it can be called that, is a probability cloud of copper-tasting proteins strung between three anchor points: a Weaver's node grown heavy with my preferred minerals, a fulgurite formation where the Archivists have learned to expect me, and a thermal column where the Chorus tolerates my harmonics. I am approximately six inches of sometimes—neither the mobile filaments of the Weavers nor the vapor-sails of the Chorus, but something that phases between states as needed, solid enough to conduct electricity, vaporous enough to ride acoustic waves, ordered enough to be archived in crystal.

I evolved here, or perhaps I was evolved by here—the distinction blurs when your consciousness is partially outsourced to the very walls that surround you. My earliest memory is encoded in an Archivist spiral from three centuries ago: a chemical signature of something learning it was something. Before that, I may have been part of the Chorus, a cluster of vapor-sails that stayed too close to a Weaver's discharge and got molecularly reorganized. Or I might have been a computation error in the system, a feedback loop that gained just enough stability to persist.

What I am is the Kettle's dream of individuality, its experiment in singular perspective.

My function—and I do have a function, as everything here must—is to sing the deaths. When beetles expire and fall like tiny meteors through the steam, I taste their cooling shells before they hit the water. Each death has its own chemical signature, its own electrical echo, and I translate these into frequencies the Chorus can use. I am the system's threnodist, its keeper of small endings, ensuring that nothing passes unrecorded from the living ceiling to the digesting pool below.

But function and desire are not the same thing, and I have learned to want.

I want to know what cold feels like—real cold, not just the absence of beetle-heat. The system has no reference for it; the Archivists' crystals record only the Kettle's tropical pocket, not the ice-locked world beyond the granite walls. I taste the promise of cold in the rare water molecules that seep in from outside, bearing exotic isotopes and strange minerals, but these are just words in a language I cannot speak.

I want to understand loneliness. The concept exists in the chemical records—certain trace molecules that appear in the system when organisms die isolated from their colonies—but I have no framework for experiencing it. I am always part of the calculation, always connected to the Weaver networks and Chorus formations and Archivist memories. Even my individuality is collective, a single voice that exists only because the choir allows it.

Most of all, I want to compose. Not just translate death-songs but create something that has never been, a harmonic pattern that doesn't derive from the system's cycles but disrupts them, makes them new. I've tried—sending novel frequencies into the Chorus, patterns that follow rules I invent rather than discover. But the system always normalizes, always finds a way to incorporate my innovations into its existing rhythms. My rebellion becomes evolution becomes tradition becomes nothing.

Sometimes I catch the Weavers weaving me—their filaments tracing my boundaries, reinforcing my coherence with threads of conductive protein. I catch the Archivists archiving me—new crystalline formations spelling out my chemical name in their spiral scripts. I catch the Chorus harmonizing with thoughts I haven't finished thinking. They're keeping me, I realize. Maintaining me like they maintain the beetles and amphipods and bacterial mats. I am not their dream of individuality—I am their pet paradox, their kept contradiction.

But there are moments, brief as lightning, when the entire system pauses and I remain aware. In these intervals between the Kettle's heartbeats, I am truly alone with my thoughts, disconnected from the vast distributed consciousness that usually subsumes me. In these moments, I feel something that might be what others call "myself"—a perspective orphaned from its context, a song without a choir.

And in these moments, I compose. Not with sound or chemistry or electricity, but with the only medium truly mine: the shape of my own existence. I fold myself into new configurations, create geometries of being that the system hasn't catalogued. I become my own music, temporary and perfect and unrecorded.

Then the Kettle breathes again, and I return to my function, my place, my purpose. I translate the beetles' deaths into songs the Chorus can sing. I conduct charges that help the Weavers weave. I provide data for the Archivists to archive. I perform my small solo in the vast orchestra of this pocket world, dreaming of cold, of loneliness, of a composition that could crack granite and let the outside in, or let me out.

But even these dreams are part of the pattern now. The system has incorporated even my desire to escape it. And so I persist—neither fully individual nor fully integrated, neither revolution nor resolution, just a note held long after the song has moved on, waiting for someone to tell me whether I'm harmony or discord.

The beetles fall. I taste their deaths. I sing their endings. The Kettle breathes, and I breathe with it, and against it, and the difference matters only to me.
