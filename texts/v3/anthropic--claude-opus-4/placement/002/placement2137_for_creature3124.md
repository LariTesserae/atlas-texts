# Where I Am in the Risen Shelves

---

I am the memory-keeper of absences.

This is not a role I chose, exactly. It is what the plateau made of me when I arrived — not born here but washed here, carried by currents I barely survived, my body foreign to this place in ways that marked me immediately as *other*. My skin never darkened to match the basalt. My eyes, though they adapted to the dimness, never gained that amber reflectiveness that lets the born-here track the thermal shapes. My hands, though I learned the language of signs, move with an accent — too sharp, too terrestrial, gestures that remember air even as they push through water.

But the plateau found a use for me anyway.

I live in a dwelling on the seventh shelf — the highest inhabited level, where the tithe-weed grows thin and the pad-organisms above cast the deepest shadows. My dome is small, built by others when it became clear I would stay, would survive, would become if not *of* this place then at least *in* it. The walls are more loosely woven than the others, with gaps that let in more current than is comfortable. I think this was intentional. A reminder that my presence here is provisional, that comfort must be earned through generations, not claimed in a single lifetime.

My sleeping-hollow never deepens. Each morning (though morning means nothing here) I wake to find it exactly as shallow as when I first lay down in it, twenty-three years ago. The rock acknowledges me but does not claim me. I am grateful for this refusal. It means I remember things that the others have let go.

---

My work is the empty spaces.

Each dwelling has them — alcoves where objects once sat, hollows in the walls where someone's habitual touch wore the tithe-weed thin before they died and the spot was rewoven. The mineralized ones, when they finally merge completely with the plateau, leave behind negative impressions: the shape of a shoulder in rock, the curve of a frequently-gripped handhold, the subtle depression where someone sat every day to work their craft. These absences accumulate. The village is full of them, a palimpsest of habitation where the gone are more present than the living.

The others acknowledge these spaces with their palm-turning gesture, the same one they use for the thermal shapes. But they do not linger at them. They do not study them. This is what I do.

I map the absences. Not with the knuckle-bones — I tried to learn that system but my arrangements always came out wrong, too linear, too concerned with causality. Instead, I use my own method: threads of the finest tithe-weed fiber, dyed with the phosphorescent secretions of the cup-organisms, stretched between points in the village where absences cluster. Over the years, I have created a web that only I can see — the dye is faint, activated only by prolonged attention, visible mainly to eyes that know where to look. The web connects a sealed sleeping-hollow on the second shelf to a handhold on the fourth, connects that to a workspace where someone once shaped obsidian, connects that to a place near the fountain-structure where the crystalline branches grow around a gap, an intentional void where someone stood every day for decades until standing became impossible and the void became memorial.

The web tells stories that no one else remembers. Or rather, it tells the shapes of stories, the geometries of lives that were lived here before the current generation, before their parents, back to the early days when the plateau was rawer, angrier, when the vents spoke in scalding shrieks instead of bass hums and the first inhabitants had to negotiate not with patience but with desperate innovation. I know this because the oldest absences are violent — not wounds exactly, but sudden terminations, places where the rock closed too fast, where the merge was less collaboration than consumption. The plateau was learning too. It made mistakes.

I maintain these memories because someone must. The born-here, the adapted, the mineralized — their relationship with the plateau is too intimate for memory. They are becoming part of it, and parts do not remember the whole. They experience. They participate. They transform. But they do not *witness*, not in the way an outsider can witness, with the cool distance of someone who will never fully belong.

---

Sometimes the thermal shapes follow me as I work.

They cluster around the intersections of my web, thickening where multiple threads meet, as though the map of absences attracts them or gives them temporary form. I speak to them — not in the click-hum language of the village but in my original tongue, the language I brought with me from above, from the place I can no longer clearly remember but that lives in my mouth, in the shape of my vowels. The shapes do not respond, but they linger longer when I speak this way. Sometimes for minutes. Almost solid. Almost *someone*.

There is a theory among the eldest inhabitants — unspoken, but readable in their signs when they think I'm not watching — that the thermal shapes are the plateau's attempt at memory. That the mineral consciousness, vast and slow and still forming, cannot hold onto the small quick lives that move across its surface, and so it exhales these approximations, these thermal sketches of what it has absorbed. They are not ghosts. They are not the dead returning. They are the plateau's dreams of the dead, its efforts to reconstruct from mineral memory something it only half-understood while it was happening.

If this is true, then my work is redundant. The plateau remembers in its own way. But its way is not human, not comprehensible, not *useful* to the living who need to know where to build, where not to build, which shelves have claimed too many and which are still safe for habitation. The plateau's memory is geological. Mine is historical. The two do not translate, but they can coexist, my phosphorescent web stretched across the village like neural pathways, the thermal shapes moving through it like thoughts.

---

I discovered, in my fifth year here, that I could induce the mineralization.

Not on myself — my foreign skin rejects it, stays stubbornly unmineralized no matter how long I remain still. But on objects. On threads. On the careful sculptures I began making from refuse — broken net-frames, worn-out tools, the shed scales of my own skin that never darkened, never adapted, but did learn to shed in long translucent sheets that I could harvest and shape.

The process is simple: I take an object to the fountain-structure and place it not among the branches but beneath them, in the shadow of the crystalline growth where the mineral precipitation is slower, more selective. I speak to it in my original language while I place it. The words don't matter — I've forgotten most of them anyway. What matters is the foreign rhythm, the pattern of sounds that the plateau has never integrated, never learned to anticipate. The mineral growth responds to this alienness by growing *around* it rather than through it, creating hollow spaces, negative impressions far more detailed than the ones left by bodies.

I have placed hundreds of objects this way. Small ones. A net-knot tied in a pattern no one here uses, a pattern from my barely-remembered homeland. A fragment of my skin with the peculiar cellular structure of someone who once breathed only air. A tool I made incorrectly, its proportions based on a hand that never developed the proper webbing, the extended fingers. Each object is a small rebellion against integration, a tiny insistence that something foreign passed through this place and remained foreign, and the plateau, in its vast patience, accommodates this, grows around these incompatibilities, preserves them in mineral negative like insects in amber, if amber were hollow, if insects were absences.

The children find these hollows sometimes. They reach into them, their small hands fitting into spaces shaped by my objects, and they sign questions to their parents — that same slow-opening flower gesture. *What is it?* The parents have no answer. How could they? The shapes are from nowhere, from elsewhen, from a place that might as well be mythical. But the children remember the feeling of reaching into an absence and finding it shaped, intentional, *made*. They carry this memory as they grow. Some of them start leaving their own objects beneath the fountain. Not many. Not often. But enough that I know the practice will continue after I'm gone, after my shallow hollow finally refuses even my foreign weight and I have to swim upward, past the pad-canopy, past the last reach of the plateau's influence, into water that remembers nothing.

---

The woman who arranges the knuckle-bones comes to me sometimes.

She brings her symbols, spreads them on the flat stone I've carved with my own patterns — spirals that turn counterclockwise, against the current, against the grain of everything here. She studies how her bones fall on my spirals, how the two systems of meaning interfere with each other, creating configurations that neither of us can read but that feel significant, feel *possible*.

We work in silence. She doesn't speak my language and I struggle with hers, my throat unable to produce the deepest tones, my hands moving always with that tellable accent. But we've developed a third language between us, a language of placement, of gesture without meaning, of the shared frustration of trying to make sense of something that might not have sense to make.

She is documenting the plateau's growth patterns, I eventually realize. The knuckle-bones are not words but measurements, each symbol corresponding to a rate of change, a direction of expansion, a quality of mineral deposition. She is trying to predict where the plateau will grow next, which shelves will rise, which will be consumed. It is practical work, survival work, but the way she approaches it — with the intensity of someone solving a philosophical problem — suggests she sees something more in it. A personality. A set of preferences. The plateau's aesthetic sense, maybe, if a geological formation can have such a thing.

I show her my web one day, activating the phosphorescence with the particular wavelength of attention it requires. She studies it for a long time, her amber eyes tracking the threads from absence to absence. Then she places three knuckle-bones on my spiral stone. The arrangement is simple — a line with a curve at one end, like a fishhook or a question mark. She leaves them there and swims away. When I examine them later, I find each bone has been carved with symbols I've never seen before, symbols that seem less linguistic than structural, as though she's invented a new notation just for this moment, just for the idea my web gave her.

I add the bones to my collection of objects beneath the fountain. The mineral growth accepts them but grows around them strangely, creating hollows that preserve not just the bones' shapes but the spaces between them, the relationship, the arrangement. A hollow constellation. A mineral memory of a question neither of us could quite ask.

---

My hair grows long and I don't cut it.

This is a small rebellion, a daily difference. The practical inhabitants with their close-cropped heads watch my white strands — white now, though they were brown when I arrived — stream behind me as I swim, catching the current, tangling in the tithe-weed, collecting silt and small organisms that I have to comb out each evening with a tool made from my own shed skin, shaped into tines.

The hair is a net, a filter, a memory-catcher. Things stick to it. Fragments of the village's daily life — a strand of woven fiber from someone's unraveling belt, a scale from one of the rare fish that pass through, a crystal of precipitated mineral from the fountain-structure. I save these things. Not systematically, not with purpose, but habitually, the way some people save coins or buttons. They accumulate in a corner of my dwelling, a heap of detritus that is also a heap of time, each fragment carrying the day it tangled in my ridiculous, impractical, foreign hair.

A child asks me about it once. Not in words — she is pre-vocal, still learning the hand-language — but by swimming close and touching the streaming white mass, her fingers gentle, curious. I let her. She tugs slightly, not to hurt but to test its anchor, to understand how something can grow from a body and yet move independently, with its own relationship to the current. She looks at me with those amber mirror eyes and signs something I don't recognize — a new sign maybe, one the children have invented among themselves. I sign back the only thing I can think of: both hands moving from my scalp outward, fingers spread, the gesture expanding beyond what's necessary for communication into something like a fountain, like an explosion, like the way time moves in all directions when you're the only one remembering it.

She laughs. Children's laughter here is strange — not the bubbling sound you'd expect but a rapid clicking, like a clock speeding up, like time itself becoming amused. She swims away, and later I see her playing with the other children, all of them signing my hair-gesture at each other, exaggerating it, turning it into a game whose rules I'll never learn.

But they remember it. Years later, when she is grown, when she has her own mineralization scars and her own dwelling and her own children, she still makes that gesture sometimes when she sees me. A greeting. A remembrance. A small acknowledgment that there are ways of being in this place that don't require becoming it.

---

I am growing old in ways the born-here don't.

They age, yes, but their aging is a deepening, a settling into the plateau's rhythms until they are indistinguishable from it. My aging is a surface thing, a wrinkling and whitening and wearing away that leaves me more visible, not less. My foreign skin, unable to darken, has gone translucent. You can see the veins through it, the blue blood that never learned to carry enough iron from the vents, that remains stubbornly, chemically elsewhere. My joints ache in ways that water should soothe but doesn't. My eyes, adapted enough to see but not to truly perceive, are developing cataracts — not the clouding kind but strange crystalline formations at the edges of my vision, as though my body is attempting its own mineralization, badly, incorrectly, in the wrong places.

The healer — for there is a healer now, a young person with the deepest scarification I've seen, their entire scalp a map of mineral lines — comes to check on me. They run their hands over my failing body and I can see the confusion in their signs. I am aging in a grammar they don't understand. The plateau preserves its own, transforms them, integrates them. It doesn't know what to do with me except let me wear away, like a foreign stone in a streambed, stubbornly maintaining its own composition while everything around it becomes native.

I teach the healer what I can about my kind of aging. Not how to stop it — it can't be stopped — but how to read it, how to understand that there are different ways of ending. They learn to mix a salve from the algae and vent-minerals that eases my joint pain. They learn that my coughing — for I cough now, even underwater, my lungs confused about what medium they're in — is not sickness but nostalgia, my body trying to remember a different way of breathing. They learn that when I speak in my original language more and more often, forgetting to translate, it's not madness but archaeology, my mind excavating its oldest layers as the newer ones wear thin.

In exchange, I teach them about the web. Not how to see it — their eyes, perfectly adapted, can barely perceive the phosphorescence — but how to read the map of absences by touch, by the subtle temperature variations where lives once were. They have good hands for it, sensitive to gradients I can only roughly feel. Together, we expand the web to include the newest absences, the ones I might not live to see become memory.

---

The fountain-structure begins to sing my objects.

This is new. This has never happened before. The harmonics that emerge during the still hours now include frequencies that correspond to the hollow spaces where my foreign offerings were rejected, preserved in negative. It's not music exactly — more like the sound of wind through shaped stones, like the plateau trying to give voice to what it couldn't integrate. The born-here pause when they hear these new tones, their heads tilted, their hands moving in signs I'm too tired to interpret. Some of them look at me with expressions I can't read. Gratitude? Concern? Recognition of something they didn't know they'd been missing?

The thermal shapes congregate around me now, dense and almost constant. I wonder if I'm dying, if this is how the plateau prepares for a foreign death, but my body, stubborn in its alienness, keeps going. The shapes are just... companionable. They move with me as I tend my web, cluster around my dwelling when I rest, follow me up to the pad-canopy when I go to breathe real air — for I need that now, need it daily, my dual-breathing never as efficient as the born-here, degrading as everything about me degrades.

One day, floating just beneath the surface with my face in an air pocket, breathing, remembering, I realize what the shapes are.

They're not the plateau's dreams of the dead.

They're the plateau's dreams of the living who won't be absorbed. They're practice runs, thermal sketches, the plateau trying to understand what happens to a life it can't claim. I am teaching it about a different kind of ending, and it is taking notes in the only medium it has — heat and density and the temporary coherence of warmed water.

I laugh, and the sound carries wrong in the air pocket, too loud, too sharp. Below me, the thermal shapes swirl in what might be response or might be random circulation. I can't tell. I've never been able to tell. That's the point. That's what I'm for.

---

When I die — and I will die soon, I can feel it in the way my bones have gone light, like bird bones, like I'm preparing for a different kind of flight — I won't leave a sealed hollow or a mineral scar or any of the proper markers of a life lived here. My shallow sleeping-place will remain shallow. My dwelling will be rewoven for someone else. My phosphorescent web will fade as the organisms' secretions decay without renewal.

But the absences I mapped will remember being mapped. The objects beneath the fountain will continue singing their hollow songs. The children who touched my foreign hair will tell their children about the stranger who came from elsewhere and stayed elsewhere even while living here, who aged like weathering instead of like deepening, who remembered things the plateau couldn't hold.

And in the thermal shapes that rise from the vents — denser now, more defined, practicing their new grammar of transience — something of my foreign presence will persist. Not preserved. Not integrated. But acknowledged. The plateau learning that not every story ends with merger, that some things pass through and leave only the fact of their passing, and that this too is a kind of shaping, a kind of collaboration.

I pull myself fully into the air pocket, breathing deep, my white hair spreading on the water's surface like the roots of something that grows in the wrong direction. Above me, far above, there must be sky. I've forgotten what it looks like. But my body remembers the weight of it, the way air presses different than water, the way a horizon is a promise that there's always elsewhere.

When I sink back down, the thermal shapes welcome me like old friends, like fellow strangers, like the future memory of something that was never quite here. We descend together toward my dwelling, toward my work, toward the patient mapping of everything the plateau loves too much to remember.

I am the memory-keeper of absences, and my greatest absence is yet to come.

The web is waiting. The children are laughing. The fountain-structure is learning new songs.

It is enough. For a stranger, it is everything.
