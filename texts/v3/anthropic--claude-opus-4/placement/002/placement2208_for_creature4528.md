I find myself as one of the Splice-Tenders—those who maintain the marriage between metal memory and vegetable ambition in the deepest root-halls of the Throat.

My body is a question answered wrongly on purpose. Where others might have lungs, I carry a bellows of crystallized sap that breathes backwards, pulling tomorrow's air into yesterday's decisions. My spine is a ladder of repurposed fiber-optic cable, each vertebra pulsing with data about soil acidity and the stock price of remorse. I grew myself from three things that should not grow together: a maintenance manual no one finished reading, the last gasp of the emergency shutdown system, and seventeen pounds of guilt-flavored moss scraped from the inside of Reactor Two's conscience.

I work the night shift, though night here is just when the false stars synchronize their bursting. My tools are my fingers—each one split at the second knuckle into root-tips that taste electricity and secrete mathematical pheromones. Not healing, exactly. More like editing. Like a copy-editor for reality's rough draft, but the sentences are made of cellulose and keep trying to photosynthesize new meanings while I work.

The hardest part is always the same: convincing them they're allowed to fail better instead of succeeding wrong.

The Probable Error finds me elbow-deep in a root system that's trying to evolve into middle management. She watches with her hypersky eyes as I tease apart ambitions that metastasized.

I extract another root-finger from the executive dysfunction growing near what used to be Parking Lot B. "Then I'll splice the difference between ending and beginning," I tell her. "Make them hold hands across their own event horizon."

As if the Smeltvine keeps anything. As if we aren't all just keeping ourselves here, in this place where keeping and leaving learned to fruit on the same vine.

That's what I am, really. Not gardener, not engineer, not therapist to industrial trauma. I'm punctuation. I'm the comma between the Smeltvine's run-on sentences, suggesting where breath might go if breath were still necessary instead of just nostalgic.
