In this drowned cathedral of light and thunder, I would be one of the Resonance Keepers.

We are not large. Each of us spans perhaps the width of a human hand when our membranes are fully extended. Our bodies are perfect dodecahedrons of living crystal—grown from the same quartz that veins these brutal walls, but refined through generations of selective pressure into something that can move, think, and sing.

We live in the spaces between the violence.

Watch carefully in the moments after a piston's thunderous contraction. In that brief lull, when the water still shudders from the impact but before the next column begins its cycle, you might see us. We emerge from the narrow crevices where quartz meets iron, from the gaps behind the grinding turbines where the water moves in strange, protected eddies.

Our crystalline shells are nearly invisible in the brilliant light—we learned long ago to grow our faces at precise angles that split the savage sunlight into harmless spectra around us. But our cores glow with a soft, persistent phosphorescence, a gentle green-gold light that seems almost apologetic amid the blue-white violence of the larger ecosystem.

We are the memory of this place.

Each impact, each grinding rotation, each lightning strike creates vibrations that would be lost in the cacophony. But we catch them. Our crystalline bodies are living tuning forks, precisely calibrated to resonate with specific frequencies. When a piston fires, we feel it not as sound but as a pure note that rings through our geometric hearts. The grinding turbines create deep, thrumming bass notes that we absorb and hold. The lightning strikes—those create crystalline chimes that reverberate through us for hours.

We store these vibrations in our latticed cores, where they interfere and overlap, creating complex standing waves that encode the history of every movement, every pulse of this living engine.

But we are not passive recorders. We are composers.

In the pre-dawn darkness, when the sun has not yet struck the ice lens and the electrical activity reaches its lowest ebb, we emerge in our thousands. We link together, edge to edge, forming vast geometric networks that span the caldera's walls like living stained glass windows. In these formations, we begin our work.

We replay the stored vibrations, but not as mere echoes. We harmonize them. We take the brutal industrial thunder of the pistons and find the microscopic variations in their timing. We isolate the specific grinding frequencies of each turbine and discover the subtle melodies hidden in their friction. We even catch the high-frequency squeals of the cable-weavers' legs against the stone and weave them into our compositions.

The result is a dawn chorus unlike anything else in this world—a crystalline symphony that transforms the mechanical noise into something that approaches music. For exactly seventeen minutes each morning, before the sun's fury returns, the caldera rings not with industrial cacophony but with an ethereal, organized harmony. The pistons still thunder, but their beats align into rhythm. The turbines still shriek, but their grinding becomes a layered chord progression. Even the building electrical whine seems to follow our conductor's baton.

We do this because we must. The large creatures—the pistons, the turbines, even the voltaic rays—they are perfect in their mindless efficiency. But perfection without variation is death. By creating these morning harmonies, we introduce tiny variations in timing, microscopic changes in the rhythm of the ecosystem. A piston delays its thrust by a fraction of a second to match our beat. A turbine speeds its rotation infinitesimally to stay in key.

These variations prevent the system from achieving perfect synchronization—which would tear the mountain apart.

We are evolution's safety valve, its rhythm section, its memory and its metronome.

But we pay a price for our role. Each stored vibration creates microscopic fractures in our crystalline bodies. After a few years of service, we begin to crack. The fractures create new facets, which split the light in new ways, which create new harmonics. We become more complex, more beautiful, and more fragile.

When we finally shatter—and we always do—we don't truly die. We rain down as crystal dust into the depths, where the pressure and minerals slowly aggregate us back into new forms. Our memories, encoded in our crystalline structure, seed new Resonance Keepers who will wake with faint echoes of songs they've never sung, carrying forward the rhythm of this place.

In this world of crushing pressure and blinding light, where titans of living metal grind out the mountain's heartbeat, we are the small, essential things that keep the great machine from destroying itself. We are the pause between the notes that makes the thunder into music.

We remember, so that this place can forget just enough to keep living.
