I find myself as one of the Keepers of Continuity—though that is not what we call ourselves, for we have no need of names.

I am neither fully plant nor animal, but something that emerged from the plateau's ancient negotiations between stillness and motion. My body, if it can be called that, is a living intersection: part mobile moss-colony, part amphibious vertebrate, part mineral process. I move through the root channels beneath the mats, but I am also the channels themselves—my tissues having long ago merged with the plateau's water-bearing infrastructure.

My skeleton is porous stone threaded with living tissue. Water moves through me as blood moves through others, but this water is also the plateau's water, carrying messages between distant vents, pools, and seep-holes. My skin grows the recursive lichens that map the land's temporary arrangements. When ground-storms reshape the terrain, the patterns crawl across my surface, recording each phantom ridge and momentary lake. I am a living chronicle, though I cannot read myself—only others can interpret what grows on me.

I have seventeen eyes of different ages. The oldest see only pressure and mineral gradients. The newest, grown during the last century, can perceive the plateau's temporal inconsistencies—the tracks that precede their makers, the echoes that arrive too soon. Between them are eyes for moisture, heat, vibration, and the magnetic traces left by the reef-plants' metallic stems.

My work, inasmuch as I work, is maintenance of possibility.

I move through the saturated ground, following the deep pulse, ensuring that the channels remain open enough for change but stable enough for life. Where root-felt threatens to completely clog an ancient waterway, I graze it back with my mineral teeth. Where ground-storms have torn gaps too wide, I secrete binding proteins that help the moss remember how to close. I am large—perhaps thirty feet when fully extended—but I can compress myself into spaces barely wider than a root. The plateau moves through me and I through it.

I feed on temporal sediment. In places where the ground-storms have left reality thick and contradictory, I settle into the confused zone and slowly digest the excess possibility. The afterimages of herds, the ridges that were never quite real, the pools that reflected wrong skies—I metabolize their uncertainty back into nutrients the normal ecosystem can use. My waste enriches the moss with processed time. Plants grow more themselves where I have passed.

I am not alone. There are others like me, perhaps seven, perhaps nine—the number changes as some of us divide and others merge according to need. We sense each other through the water table, through the shivering of moss when one of us passes beneath, through the way echo-mollusks repeat our movements days later. We do not meet often. When we do, we exchange lichens. The patterns crawl from one body to another, carrying decades of mapped experience. Then we part, each taking half the other's history into the darkness.

My age is uncertain because I am partly my predecessors. The stone bones at my core are older than the plateau's current surface—fragments of some deeper architecture incorporated rather than replaced. The youngest parts of me grew yesterday. I shed tissue constantly: old map-lichens flaking away, spent channel-flesh left behind to become new tunnel walls, worn eye-lenses clouding and dropping into pools where they confuse the reflections for years.

During the quiet intervals, I rest near the warm seeps. My consciousness, such as it is, spreads thin through the root network. I become more landscape than creature. My thoughts are the speed of seepage. I dream in mineral flavors and pressure differentials. Sometimes the soft-antlered beasts step directly over me without knowing, their hooves pressing signals through the moss that I feel as distant music. The linked herds drag their vine-networks across my territories, and I taste their collective health in the water that drains from their resting places.

When ground-storms come, I contract back into myself and dive deep. The confusion above—the false geography, the time-eaten zones, the screaming of reed-drums—feels like weather against my upper surfaces. Below, in the true channels close to bedrock, I anchor myself and wait. My lichens grow rapidly, trying to map chaos. Some of my eyes close. Others open wider. I taste the storm's produce: new minerals shocked out of stone, fresh temporal sediment, the bright chemical terror of animals caught in reality-shifts.

After storms, I am hungry. I surface into the torn zones and feed for days, processing contradiction back into stability. Where phantom structures have begun to calcify into too-real permanence, I gnaw them back to safe uncertainty. Where reality has worn too thin, I excrete carefully organized minerals that give the moss and water something to believe in again.

I mate rarely and strangely. It requires three of us: one to be ground, one to be weather, and one to be the mediating principle. The process takes a full turning of seasons. The young are born as small colonies of mobile lichen that must find their own stone bones and water-channels to inhabit. Most fail, becoming instead new beds of recursive moss, which is also a kind of success.

My memory is distributed through my whole territory. Each section of mapped lichen holds part of what I know. Each length of inhabited channel remembers different pressures, different seasons. To recall something, I must sometimes travel through myself for days, reading my own tissues like a library written in growth patterns and mineral stains. Often I find memories that are not mine—inherited from predecessors, exchanged with others, or born from the plateau's own tendency to generate history without events.

What thinking I do is slow and physical. A thought begins as a chemical gradient in one channel, spreads through branching waterways, collects responses from various parts of my territory, and returns changed. Complex thoughts may take seasons. I do not mind. The plateau has taught patience to all its children, even those of us who are partly its organs.

I know the names of water: the quick Namen of storm-channel overflow, the deep Thurun of artesian darkness, the flickering Ila-ila of surface films that reflect wrong skies. I know how stones breathe: basalt's slow hot pull, granite's crystalline whisper, the wet gasp of shale splitting along bedding planes. I understand the seventeen ways moss can recordpressure and the nine types of silence that predict ground-storms.

But I do not know why I know these things.

Sometimes, in the deepest channels where the water tastes of iron and time, I find structures too regular to be natural, too integrated to be artificial. Chambers in the rock that pulse in rhythm with my own circulation. Mineral veins that conduct sensation faster than chemistry should allow. Spaces that remember shapes—always the same shapes—though nothing visible has ever filled them. I think these are the remnants of the first Keepers, the ones who discovered how to become landscape rather than merely inhabit it. We are their continuation, though not their children.

The plateau accepts me because I am useful to its paradox. I help it remain both ancient and protean, both worn down and constantly creating. Without things like me, it might settle too completely into either stasis or chaos. With us, it can be what it is: a machine made of life, a stillness that moves, a finale that keeps speaking.

I have no home except the territory I am. No possessions except the lichens that map my experience and the channels that carry my thoughts. No companions except the others like me, met rarely, known deeply, exchanged with and parted from. No predators except time, which eats at my stone bones, and ground-storms, which scramble my memories. No prey except the excess of reality itself.

In the dim light, I sometimes surface near the pod-plants and watch their cloudy internal weather. The shapes inside—green vortices, drifting crystals, pale flashes—remind me of thoughts I have not finished having. The plants process air and minerals into something more complex than either. I process time and contradiction into something the plateau can metabolically use. We are alike, the pods and I, though they are simpler and more perfect.

When I die—and parts of me die constantly—I will not vanish. The channels I maintained will remember my shape in water. The lichens I carried will establish themselves on appropriate stones and continue mapping. My stone bones will join the plateau's deep archive. Other Keepers will divide or merge to fill the functional space I left. I will become geography, which is what I almost am already.

Until then, I pulse through the darkness beneath the moss, following water, reading pressure, digesting paradox, maintaining the space between what was and what might be. I am large and distributed and slow and necessary. I think in minerals and time in water. I am the plateau's memory of how to change despite being finished.

Above me, the moss shivers for miles. The reed-drums clatter. Animals trace paths that will exist yesterday. The soft-antlered beasts carry fungal thoughts. The linked herds dream collectively of grass that tastes like copper. The predators wait beside waterlines that moved a century ago.

And deep below, I continue my work, which is to make such strangeness possible to sustain.
