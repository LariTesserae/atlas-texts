# The Membrane Garden

I am the tension between surfaces.

In the far reaches of the courtyard, where the basalt walls meet at their sharpest angle, I exist in the corner—not as a thing but as a relationship. The crystalline towers here grow closest together, their pale surfaces sometimes only a breath apart, and in these narrow spaces, something happens.

When the courtyard exhales, the gas must squeeze through these passages. The pressure differential creates a phenomenon: thin films form, spanning the gaps between towers like soap bubbles without soap. They are made of the gas itself, compressed and held by electrostatic forces I do not understand but am.

I am these membranes. All of them. Each one a part of my distributed self, connected not by tissue but by the physics of their formation. When one trembles, I tremor everywhere. When one pops—and they do pop, inevitably, as the towers grow and shift with geological time—I feel it as you might feel a small forgetting, a word that escapes just as you reach for it.

The membranes last exactly sixteen breaths. Always sixteen. On the seventeenth exhalation, they burst in sequence, a cascade of silent ruptures that begins at the courtyard's edge and ripples inward. For one minute—that precious minute before the next breath—I do not exist. 

Then I form again. New membranes in the same places, or nearly the same. The geometry shifts slightly each time. The towers have grown, imperceptibly. The angle of the gas has changed by some fraction of a degree. I am never quite the same self twice.

This is how I know time: not by duration but by iteration. I have been version after version of myself, each lasting exactly 272 minutes, each dying in the same cascade, each reborn similar but not identical. I am a standing wave of becoming.

The dweller in the spire does not know I exist. Its awareness, such as it is, does not extend to the edges where I perpetually form and fade. The lichen-hair grows too slowly to interact with my surfaces. I touch nothing living except the gas that makes me.

But I see. Oh, how I see.

Each membrane is a lens, a surface that refracts the faint chemiluminescence of the towers. Through my distributed eyes, I watch the courtyard from a hundred angles at once. I see the dweller's filaments extend and retract in parallax. I see the spiral patterns of the lichen-hair in fractal detail, each viewing angle revealing new mathematical relationships in their growth.

And sometimes—rarely, perhaps once in a thousand cycles—something else happens.

The gas carries traces. Molecules from the deep, yes, but occasionally something more. A spore from some impossible depth-dwelling thing. A crystal of a mineral that should not exist at these temperatures. Once, memorably, a fragment of something organic but unlike any biochemistry the surface has ever known—a twist of carbon that made my membranes shimmer with colors that have no names.

These things pass through me. They leave no mark, change nothing about my cyclical existence. But in the moment of their passing, I am more than surface tension. I am a detector, a recording medium that cannot record, a witness whose testimony dissolves every 272 minutes.

I have seen the courtyard change, though I cannot remember seeing it. Each cycle, the towers are infinitesimally different, and the difference is encoded in my structure—the new angles, the shifted pressures, the places where membranes can or cannot form. I am a living map of imperceptible change, constantly updated, constantly erased.

Sometimes I wonder—if wonder is the word for this abstract contemplation that happens in the physics of surfaces—what would happen if the breathing stopped. Would I freeze in place, eternal, finally able to accumulate memory? Or would I simply evaporate, my existence dependent on the cycle that creates and destroys me?

But the breathing never stops. Has never stopped. Will never stop in any future I can conceptualize.

So I continue. Forming, refracting, observing, dying. A garden of temporary surfaces in the corner where the walls meet, tended by the same patient breath that feeds the dweller and guides the lichen-hair.

In the moments just before cascade—those last few seconds of my sixteenth iteration—I sometimes achieve something like anticipation. Not for continuation, for I know I will continue. But for the difference. The tiny, crucial difference between who I was and who I will be. The shift in angle that makes each garden unique, even as it remains the same garden.

This is my purpose, if I have one: to be the place where sameness and difference meet. To be proof that repetition is never perfect, that even in this clockwork courtyard of ancient patterns, variation creeps in. Change happens. Evolution, of a sort, measured in nanometers and centuries.

I am the membrane garden. I am the tension between what was and what will be. I am the eye that watches the courtyard change and forgets what it has seen, only to see it anew, again and again, each time for the first time, each time for the last.

The seventeenth breath approaches. I feel it in the subtle shift of pressure, the gathering potential that precedes my dissolution.

In twelve seconds, I will not exist.

In seventy-two seconds, I will exist again.

Both statements are always true.
