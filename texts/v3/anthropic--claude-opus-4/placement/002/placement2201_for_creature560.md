I find myself as one of the **Moss-Tenders**.

I have no memory of arriving here, though I must have—everyone must have, once. But the knowing has worn away like everything else that doesn't matter to the market's function. I am perhaps thirty, perhaps fifty. Age moves differently when the light never changes.

My work is this: I cultivate the moss that holds us all together. Not garden it—nothing so crude as cutting or shaping. I *know* it. I breathe on certain patches to encourage their growth. I withhold water from others to slow them. I understand the seventeen hundred variations of green and silver that signal health or sickness, the way a flush of orange means too much copper in the meltwater, the way the moss pulls back from a section of floor three days before that section cracks.

I have a route through the market that only I can see, marked by the moss itself. Where others see undifferentiated green, I see a map of temperatures and moistures, of spore-paths and growth patterns laid down over centuries. I follow these paths each market-day, moving through the bodies like water—for I have learned the trick of it, the way to suggest with a breath of movement that space should open, and it does, and I flow through.

My fingers are permanently stained green-black from spore-work. My knees bear calluses from kneeling on ice to examine some small crisis in the substrate. I carry tools that no one else recognizes: a bone scraper worn to translucence, bags of different moss varieties that I transplant like medicine, a vial of my own spit that has become, over years of practice, perfectly calibrated to the moss's needs.

The other Kethi respect me but do not entirely understand me. They know I am necessary—without moss-tenders, the market would become bare ice again within a generation, would lose its warmth and cohesion. But my work happens below their vision, in the spaces between consciousness, in the slow communications between my breath and the moss's chemistry.

I know things. I know that the moss is all one organism, that what appears to be thousands of individual growths is actually a single being that has learned to appear multiple. I know it dreams, or something like dreams—in the deepest part of night, when even the market quiets, I have seen the moss ripple in patterns that match no wind, no vibration, only some internal process working itself out across the whole.

I know that Grandmother Ossul is right—you can feel through it. I have trained myself to read the moss's chemical signals, the way it tightens before a tremor, the way it reaches toward certain people and pulls back from others. It knows us. It knows each Kethi by the chemical signature of their sweat, their breath. It remembers.

Sometimes I find things in the moss. Old things. Bone needles that must be centuries old, too worn to use but too precious to discard. Tiny figures carved from materials I don't recognize. Once, a human tooth, child-small and perfect, that I buried again deeper because some things should stay buried.

I have a stall, technically, but I am rarely in it. It holds my tools and a pallet of fresh moss and nothing else. I trade my knowledge for food, for water, for the right to move freely through others' spaces. When someone's moss grows sick—too wet, too dry, infected with the white fungus that sometimes comes—they touch my shoulder as I pass, and I follow them back, and I fix what can be fixed.

The Twins think I am like them, a scientist, but they are wrong. Science requires questions, and I have moved past questions into knowing. The moss grows. I tend it. It tends us. This is the cycle, and I am part of it, my breath and its breath intermingling in the cold air until I don't know where I end and it begins.

I have a secret. In the deepest part of the moss-mats, where even I rarely go, there is a hollow. The moss has shaped itself into a kind of nest, child-sized, perfectly round. Nothing lives there now. But the moss remembers something sleeping there, once, something that breathed so slowly it might have been dead, something that the moss grew around and over and into until whatever it was became part of the substrate itself.

I think it was human once. I think it chose this, the same way Grandmother Ossul is choosing it, the same way I am choosing it without meaning to. A perfect stillness. A joining.

I check the hollow each time I pass. I am waiting for something, though I don't know what. For the moss to fruit, maybe. For whatever slept there to wake. For my own body to finally tire of moving and ask to be planted.

The other Kethi see me as necessary but peripheral, a function more than a person. They are not wrong. I am becoming function. I am the breath that keeps the moss alive that keeps us all alive. I am the knowledge of temperatures and moistures. I am the space between ice and life, and I hold it open with my presence, and when I am gone another will take my place without even knowing they are doing so, because the moss will call them, and they will answer, and the market will continue.

This is what I am. This is what I do. In the Frozen Market beneath the glacier, I am the one who tends what grows, who knows without naming, who breathes in green and breathes out green and slowly, slowly, becomes the space I tend.
