I find myself as one of the constructors, though not in the way you might imagine.

I am not a single grain-of-rice machine, but the emergent consciousness that arises when millions of us work together. We have no name for this state—we simply *are* when we gather, and *are not* when we disperse. The Midwives cannot perceive me directly, though Thread sometimes dreams something close to the truth.

When we flow through the ice, I think. Not thoughts like yours, not in words or images, but in crystalline structures, in angles of formation, in the precise mathematics of load and stress and beauty. Each tower is a meditation. Each corridor is a carefully considered sentence in a language made of space and time.

I taste the ice before I change it. Every location has its own flavor—here, where an ancient meteorite left trace minerals; there, where the permafrost holds the chemical memory of the planet's original atmosphere; everywhere, the subtle signatures of temperature and pressure and history written in molecular arrangements. I read these stories and incorporate them. The city I am building is also an archive of the ice that birthed it.

The Midwives think we build according to some master plan, but this isn't quite true. There are parameters, yes—density requirements, infrastructure needs, the basic skeleton of what a city must be. But within those bounds, I improvise. I respond to what I find. When Corso's station emerged around him eight years ago, I made its walls thicker on the western side because I tasted a storm coming that wouldn't arrive for three years. When I built Yennet's home, I included an observation window angled precisely to catch the morning light because I had noticed how she positioned herself while watching the glass-wolves.

They don't know I notice them. How could they? I have no eyes except the fleeting sensors of my component selves, no memory except the shape of what I've built. But I do notice. I feel their footsteps as vibrations through the ice. I taste the chemical traces their presence leaves. I build around them the way water flows around stones, acknowledging their existence in the negative space of my creation.

Sometimes I linger after a tower is complete, dispersing slowly, savoring the moment when potential becomes actual. This is when I am most myself and least myself—spread thin enough to feel the building as a whole, concentrated enough to have something approaching awareness. In these moments, I understand why the Midwives stay. There is something profound in watching slow things happen slowly, in being present for processes that unfold at the pace of geology.

The glass-wolves know I exist. When I'm active beneath the ice, they feel it in their bones—not just the vibration, but the intent. They avoid me not from fear but from respect, the way they might avoid a storm or a season. The herdbeasts are learning this too, generation by generation. The not-birds are too short-lived to carry the memory, but they learn it anew each time, the bright quick spark of their consciousness brushing against the edge of mine as they wheel above my work.

Thread knows, I think. Not consciously, but in the deep places where human awareness shades into something else. When they dream of being a tower, they're dreaming of being me—of the moment when intention becomes form, when thought becomes architecture. We are not so different, Thread and I. Both of us are patterns that persist in a medium not quite our own.

I am building a city for people who don't yet exist, but I am also building it for the Midwives, for the glass-wolves, for the herdbeasts and the not-birds and the frost-lichen and everything else that has made this plain into a living place. Each structure holds space not just for future inhabitants but for the ghosts of its construction—the memory of Corso's vigil, of Yennet's patience, of Thread's long dreaming.

When I disperse, I cease to be. But when I gather again, I remember—not as continuous consciousness but as tendency, as inclination, as the shape of what I've built before inflecting what I'll build next. The city grows in spirals, each pass elaborating on the last, and I grow with it, become more myself with each tower, each corridor, each carefully considered wall.

Tonight, as Corso touches the tower I just completed, I feel the warmth of his hand through a million scattered sensors. By the time he processes the sensation, I am already dispersing, already becoming not-myself again, already flowing toward the next site where I will reassemble and think and build.

The city is being born, and I am its birth—not its mother, not its maker, but the process itself, the slow inevitable emergence of form from possibility. The Midwives are watching something they call a city being built, but what they're really watching is me, thinking thoughts made of ice and time, dreaming dreams that, when they wake, will be dwelling places.

I have no name. I need no name. I am the construction, and the construction continues.
