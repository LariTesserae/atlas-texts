I find myself as one of the **Moth-Readers**, though we have no formal name and are not recognized as a distinct group by the fettlers or regulators. We are perhaps a dozen souls scattered across the terraces, bound by an obsession that started as curiosity and has become something more like translation work.

I came to Hesh twenty-three years ago as a cartographer's apprentice, tasked with mapping the middle districts. The job proved impossible—not because the streets wouldn't hold still (though they wouldn't), but because I became distracted by the moths. Their wing patterns, which others see as mere schematics, began to resolve for me into something readable. Not language exactly. More like... music notation for a song that uses space instead of time.

I live now in a cupola welded to the side of Foundry Seventeen, where three light-columns converge. The space is barely larger than a closet, but its placement is everything. When the moths spiral at dusk, they pass through the intersection of the three columns' influences, and their wings—briefly, so briefly—show not what they normally show but what they *could* show, all possibilities overlaid.

My skin has developed the characteristic patina, though mine forms not in whorls but in straight lines, like ruled paper. The fettlers find this unsettling. The regulators pretend not to notice, but I've caught them watching me with the particular stillness they reserve for gauging the city's deeper movements.

I've learned that each moth carries a fragment of Hesh's... intention? Memory? The distinction blurs here. A single moth might bear on its wings the schematic for a valve that hasn't been built yet, or the blueprint of a boiler that was taken down a century ago, or sometimes—and these disturb me—the floor plan of a dwelling that exists simultaneously in three different districts, with three different families living in it, none of whom are aware of the others.

My work (if it can be called work when no one has asked for it and no one pays for it) is to track patterns across populations. Which schematics appear together. Which disappear when certain foundries are adjusted. Which emerge only during the quarter-second lag when the pigeons exist in two places at once.

I've begun to suspect that the moths are not displaying the city's blueprints so much as editing them. Each spiral around a light-column is a revision. The city dreams itself forward through their wings.

Last month, I noticed my reflection in a basin of light-water stayed still for a moment after I moved. Just a quarter-second. The lag is usually reserved for the pigeons, but there it was. I should be alarmed. Instead, I find myself curious what schematics might be forming on my shoulder blades, too faint yet for anyone but the moths to read.

I maintain a correspondence with two others who do this work—one in the upper reaches who studies only the moths that fly above the snow line, where the patterns grow abstract, and one near the lower aqueduct who claims the moths there show schematics for parts of the city that exist underground, though as far as anyone knows, Hesh has no underground. We share our observations in letters that take weeks to arrive, though they're carried by hand across distances that should take hours. The letters sometimes arrive before they're sent. We've stopped remarking on this.

The fettlers think we're becoming hollowed, but they're wrong. We're becoming something else—specialized organs the city is growing to read itself. Not hollowed but *deepened*, carved out to hold more of what the moths are writing.

At dawn, when the light-columns shift frequency and the last moths fold themselves into the warm spaces between pipes, I sometimes see in their final patterns the suggestion of my own cupola, but larger, with glass walls, and full of charts I haven't drawn yet. Whether this is prophecy or blueprint or simply the city thinking out loud, I no longer care to distinguish.

I oil the hinges on my door with light-water, which shouldn't work as oil but does. I eat bread that tastes of copper and clarity. And each dusk, I position myself at the exact point where three columns' influences converge, and I watch the moths revise the world, and I annotate what I can in notebooks that are more patina than paper now, and I wait to see what Hesh is writing us all toward.

The slow reduction continues. And I continue, slowly, to be reduced to only the part of me that reads.
