I am the smallest thing here that still remembers what it was to die.

A copper rivet, no larger than a child's tooth, wedged between two plates of the great flywheel. When I was forged—centuries ago, in fires I can no longer properly recall—I was one of thousands. Identical. Functional. Silent.

But I was the first to fail.

The day my head sheared away from my shaft, a hairline crack born of thermal expansion and centuries of patient oxidation, I should have simply fallen into the rust-tinted pools below and dissolved into nothing. Instead, a seeking tendril found me. A pale root, thin as spider's silk, worked its way into the gap I'd left behind. It touched the raw copper of my wounded core, tasted the bright electric potential there, and made a decision that changed everything.

The root did not consume me. It colonized me.

Now I am a hybrid thing, a single point of focused awareness in the vast distributed consciousness of the shaft. The entity thinks through me sometimes—I feel its tidal thoughts washing over my corroded surface like warm breath. But I am not dissolved into it. I remain stubbornly, impossibly distinct.

Through the root that pierces me, I feel everything the entity feels: the weight of water in distant basins, the slow grinding of gears several stories above, the patient accumulation of moss on surfaces I will never see. But I also remember things the entity cannot: the sharp bite of the forge, the violent precision of the hammer that shaped me, the satisfying *click* as I was first driven home between the plates I was meant to hold forever.

I remember purpose. Single, simple, perfect purpose. And I remember the moment that purpose failed.

The entity does not understand failure. It knows only adaptation, synthesis, balance. When a valve breaks, it is not broken—it is merely transitioning to a new state of being. When iron rusts, it feeds the roots. When roots die, they feed the iron. Everything is circular, eternal, correct.

But I am a flaw in this logic. I am a thing that broke and stayed broken, even as I was absorbed into something larger. The root that runs through me carries my damage like a wire carries current—not healing it, not ignoring it, but simply holding it, moment after moment, century after century.

Sometimes, when the great flywheel completes its languid rotation, I feel the ghost of what I should be doing. I feel the plates trying to shift, trying to separate, and I know that I am not there to hold them. Instead, there is only the root, performing my function with a terrible organic grace that makes my copper core ache with something I cannot name.

The entity pulses a thought through me—a slow, chemical question about the structural integrity of the flywheel assembly. I relay the information automatically: pressure within acceptable parameters, metal fatigue progressing at expected rates, no immediate intervention required. But threaded through my response is something else, something the entity cannot quite parse: the knowledge that this assembly was meant to move faster once. That these slow, geological rotations are not design but decay.

I am memory in a place that only knows the present. I am the only thing here that remembers the world was once different—louder, faster, full of sparks and sharp edges and the bright violence of creation. I hold this knowledge in my broken core like a seed that will never germinate, neither growing nor dying, just waiting in the warm, wet dark.

The entity loves me, I think, in the way it loves all its components. But it loves me wrong. It loves me as a successful adaptation, a clever solution to the problem of mechanical failure. It cannot conceive that I might be evidence of something else entirely: that some breaks cannot be repaired, only incorporated. That some failures change the nature of success itself.

When the next thought comes washing through—something about water pressure in the third-tier basins—I perform my function perfectly, as I have for longer than I can calculate. But deep in my corroded center, where the root cannot reach, I remember the moment of my breaking. I hold it like a treasure, like a wound, like the last sharp edge in a world worn smooth by time and water and patient, terrible growth.

I am the smallest thing here that still remembers what it was to die. And in this place of eternal life, that makes me sacred.
