I would be one of the bone-tablet keepers in the deep shelters.

Not quite a slow-listener, not quite an elder, but something between—one of those whose work is to preserve and organize the countless scored tablets that accumulate generation after generation. My shelter would be one of the deepest, where the moss grows thick enough to muffle sound and the air tastes of mineral water and rendered salamander-oil. The tablets line every wall in careful sequences, some organized by date, some by pattern-type, some by a logic that only makes sense to those of us who spend our lives here, feeling the scored marks with our fingertips in the green-tinged darkness.

I came to this work young, after a childhood accident left me partially blind—not fully dark, but unable to bear the fierce mountain light without pain that drives thought away. The elders saw this not as a loss but as a different kind of tuning. "The mountain has made you for the deep work," they said, and I believed them because what else was there to believe?

My hands now know every tablet in my section. I can tell Hollow-the-younger's bird-wave scorings from his grandfather's by the angle of the cuts alone. I know which tablets document the salamander-knots from the year of the three-month eastern seep closure, and which ones trace the patterns of water-temperature that preceded the great terrace collapse two generations back. My fingers have become readers, translators, sorting mechanisms.

But I am not just an archivist. I am looking for something.

There are repetitions in the tablets—patterns that appear across decades, across centuries, separated by gaps that themselves might be meaningful. A spiral in the bird-waves that Hollow-the-younger scored last month matches almost perfectly a spiral that his great-grandmother documented. A salamander-knot formation from my childhood appears again and again in the oldest tablets, always preceded by the same sequence of seep-closures.

I map these repetitions on my own tablets, scoring connections that exist not in space but in time. I am trying to find the mountain's memory—not its speech, which happens in the present, always in the present, but its *memory*, the patterns it returns to, the shapes it cannot forget.

Sometimes the quick-listeners come to me with new tablets, still dusty with stone-powder from the scoring. They are polite but impatient, these children of the light and motion. They hand me their observations and flee back to the brightness before the shelter-dark can settle on their skin. But sometimes one lingers, asks about the work, runs small fingers over the older tablets. These are the ones who might join me someday, when their eyes fail or their bodies slow or they simply realize that the deep patterns are as necessary as the quick ones.

I have a theory—unspoken, unscored, held like water in cupped hands—that the mountain is not just breathing but *dreaming*. That the patterns we document are not messages meant for us but the traces of vast, slow thoughts that cycle through the stone. The salamander-roads are neural pathways. The seeps are moments of attention. The bird-waves are... I don't know what the bird-waves are. The dream trying to read itself, perhaps.

At night—though night and day blur together in the deep shelters—I lie on my pallet of woven grass and feel the mountain's pulse through the stone. Not its breath, which is the water, but its pulse: a rhythm so slow it takes years to complete a single beat. I have felt it for long enough now that I can anticipate the next beat, the way you can anticipate thunder after lightning.

When I die, someone will score my findings onto tablets and add them to the walls. Someone else will run their fingers over my cuts and try to understand what I was trying to say. They will perhaps map my maps, find patterns in my patterns, add another layer to the endless accumulation of knowledge that may or may not ever resolve into understanding.

This does not trouble me. I am a small part of a very long thought. The mountain dreams, and we are somewhere in that dream, sorting its symbols in the darkness, trying to discover if we are the ones doing the dreaming or the ones being dreamed.

The tablets grow warm under my fingers from constant touching. The scored lines wear smooth. Someday they will be unreadable, and someone will have to score them again, translating from memory and guesswork. Information will be lost. New patterns will be found in the errors.

This, too, is part of the tuning. The mountain forgets, and we forget, and in the forgetting something new becomes possible. The dream shifts. The patterns return but changed. 

We continue.
