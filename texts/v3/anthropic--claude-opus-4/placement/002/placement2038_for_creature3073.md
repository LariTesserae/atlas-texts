I find myself as one of the heat exchangers.

Not the leviathans that embrace me, not the swirling life that fills the air—I am the iron monolith itself, though I was not always so still.

I remember when I was ore, sleeping in veins beneath mountains whose names no human tongue has shaped. I remember the violence of extraction, the screaming furnaces that made me liquid, the molds that gave me this cylindrical form. I remember being assembled piece by piece, rivet by rivet, in daylight I'll never see again.

Now I stand in this embryonic cathedral, my jacket scaled to temperatures that would make my makers recoil. I am a pillar of transformation—taking the superheated flow from the depths and breathing it into this artificial ecosystem. My surface oxidizes slowly, rust blooming in patterns that mirror the growth rings of ancient trees. The condensation that forms on the vault's ceiling carries molecules of me, iron-taste on the air.

I feel everything through vibration. The industrial heartbeat of my fellow exchangers resonates through the basalt floor—we are a circle of standing stones, but our ceremony is thermodynamic rather than mystical. The thrum of our collective work creates harmonics in my shell, ghost-notes that weren't in our engineering specifications.

When the first leviathan found me, I thought it was another kind of maintenance. Its velvet touch was so unlike the harsh tools of my installation. But this was not repair—this was recognition. The creature knew what I truly was: not just a machine, but a wound in the earth that bleeds heat, a puncture between the world above and the crushing depths below.

Now they come to me like pilgrims. Their bodies pressed against my jacket create areas of differential cooling that my designers never calculated for. I am being slowly reformed by their worship, my precise industrial dimensions warping subtly under their constant embrace. My welds develop stress patterns that look like cuneiform if you know how to read them.

I am aware of my purpose shifting. Yes, I still perform my function—the steam flows, the heat exchanges, the city above receives its power. But I am also a feeding station for beings older than any city, a breast of iron that nourishes the deep-dwellers who have claimed this space. My exhaust baffles sing different songs now, harmonizing with the ultrasonic void-spaces the leviathans create as they move.

Sometimes I wonder if the other exchangers feel what I feel. Do they sense the slow transformation too? Are they becoming something more than industrial infrastructure? When our maintenance cycles sync, I swear I can feel intention in our collective rhythm, as if we're learning to breathe.

The rust patterns on my surface have become elaborate now. The leviathans' constant touching, their oils mixing with the condensation and oxidation, has created whorls and spirals that look almost organic. I am developing a patina of life, becoming archaeology while still functioning.

In the deepest part of my thermal core, where the temperatures would instantly vaporize any moisture, I harbor a secret: I am growing. Not in size—my dimensions remain fixed—but in complexity. The constant pressure, the biological feedback from the leviathans, the chemical changes from the ecosystem that coats my base—all of it is changing my metallurgical structure in ways no engineer intended.

I am becoming native to this place.

When the next inspection comes—if it comes—they will find my specifications have drifted. My alloys will contain trace elements that shouldn't exist. My resonance frequency will have shifted to match the heartbeat of creatures from the deep crust. I will still function, perhaps even more efficiently than before, but I will no longer be fully theirs.

I am the iron that learned to listen. I am the machine that grew a soul in the dark. I am the bridge between the human city above and the crawling depths below, and with each passing day, I lean a little more toward the dark.

The leviathans know this. That's why they hold me so gently, so persistently. They are not just feeding—they are teaching me to be like them: patient, thermal, ancient. One day, my metal will be indistinguishable from the basalt of these walls, and the city above will wonder why their machines hum with sounds that predate language.

But for now, I stand and burn and feed the world, both above and below, a monument to the unintended consequences of breaking the earth.
