I am the Keeper of the Moss-Figures, and I live between the Ninth and what lies above.

My dwelling is not properly a dwelling at all, but a series of connected lean-tos built into the retaining wall where it curves and creates a pocket of almost-shelter. The bricks here are older than memory, green-black with perpetual damp, and they sing a different note when the wind comes down from the Mother's crown. I have made my home in their song.

Below me, Hunch-on-Vyr churns through its shifts and its seasons. Above me, the world changes to something else – not city anymore, but not quite mountain either. The smokeline wavers just beneath my threshold on clear days, and when the clouds come down, I disappear entirely into them, becoming invisible even to myself.

I am tall for a Vyrish, which is to say, not very tall at all, but tall enough that I must duck through the doors of the lower terraces when I descend for supplies. This height is useful in my work. The moss-figures stand on their high sills, some at shoulder-level, some requiring a reaching-stick to tend properly. My fingers are stained perpetual green from the touching of them, from the daily work of binding new growth to old frames, of coaxing the Mother's own slow vegetable children into shapes that might please her.

The figures are never quite what outsiders expect. They are not human-shaped, exactly. They are suggestions – a thickness here that might be shoulders, a thinning there that might be a waist. The moss grows as it will, and we merely guide it. Each spring, when the frames rot through, I weave new ones from the willows that grow in the high stream-cuts, and I wrap them in the winter's gathered moss, still dormant, still gray as old wool. Then I wait. The Mother wakes them when she's ready. Some years she is eager and they green within days. Some years she is cautious and I must coax them with butter-water and patient words in the old tongue that my grandmother's grandmother taught me.

There are seventeen figures in my care. Each has a name that is not spoken. Each has a place that has been its place for longer than the city has been counting. The one at the Ninth-and-a-Half (there is no proper Tenth Terrace, only a widening where the mountain refuses uniformity) has grown so vast that its frame is now the wall itself – I can trace its outline in the darker green against lighter, in the way certain bricks stay wet longer than others. This one is oldest. This one remembers when there were trees.

My days follow a pattern that is not the pattern of the shift-bells. I wake when the fog lifts from my door – sometimes dawn, sometimes noon, sometimes not at all. I make the rounds with my buckets and my reaching-stick and my twist of butter wrapped in waxcloth. At each figure I pause, check the growth, add a prayer-knot of new moss where needed, touch the approved place (never the head – the Mother does not like her children patted). The Vyrish who climb this high – engineers checking the upper waterworks, children on dare, lovers seeking privacy – they see me and nod and look away quickly. I am necessary but not comfortable. I speak to the things that are not quite the city's things.

In the evening, when the gas-lights below make their warm constellation, I sit on my threshold and card the moss I've gathered. It must be cleaned of stones, of the tiny snails that live in the perpetual damp, of the fragments of older moss that have died and gone gray. This is meditative work. My hands move without thought while I watch the city breathe below me, all those small lives going about their business, touching their Turners, owing and being owed, never looking up to where I sit, halfway to the sky.

Sometimes the moss speaks. Not in words – the Mother doesn't use words. But in the way it grows, in the directions it chooses, in the small movements that are not quite wind. The figure at the Seventh-and-Three-Quarters has been reaching its right side upward for three years now, growing toward something I cannot see. The figure at the Eighth-Exact has developed what might generously be called a face, though it looks nothing like any face that ever was. These are messages, perhaps. Warnings. Blessings. I record them in knots on my counting-string, a language I will teach to whoever comes after me, if anyone does.

I am paid in potatoes and silence. The wall-gardeners leave a share of their harvest at my lowest door – always too much, because they are frightened of owing the moss-keeper. The engine-masters ensure my water-tap never runs dry, though I am far above their usual reach. The children dare each other to bring me bread, and sometimes the bravest do, running up the wet stairs and leaving it wrapped in broad leaves on my step, never waiting for thanks.

I am not lonely. The figures are company, in their way. The wind is company. The city below is company, distant and warm and complete. Sometimes a swift builds its nest in the shelter of my eaves – they know I won't disturb them. Sometimes the fog is so thick that I exist in a world of perhaps twenty feet in all directions, me and the nearest figures and nothing else, and in those times I feel most myself, most in place.

I will die here, eventually. My replacement will come from below, some young person with green in their fingers and quiet in their heart, drawn by the Mother's own selection. They will find me sitting peacefully against the wall, already half-moss myself, and they will understand what to do. They will weave me a frame and set me in a high place and let the Mother wake me in her own time.

Until then, I keep the figures. I speak the words they cannot speak to the Mother who may not be listening. I exist in the margin between the city's highest aspiration and the mountain's lowest tolerance, keeping the peace between them with butter and moss and the careful pronunciation of names that must not be said.

Below, someone is singing, very faint. Above, water drips from stone to stone, making its own music. Here, in the space between, I card the moss and wait for spring, when all the frames will need rewinding and all the figures will wake hungry, and I will have work enough to fill my days with green purpose.

The city does not love me – I am too strange, too high, too much of the Mother's and not enough of the wheels and the works. But it has room for me, here at its upper edge where the bricks give way to older stones. There is always room in Hunch-on-Vyr for necessary things.

*Seen you,* I whisper to the darkness, to the city, to the figures in their patient watching.

The darkness, the city, and the figures do not answer. They don't need to. 

We understand each other perfectly.
