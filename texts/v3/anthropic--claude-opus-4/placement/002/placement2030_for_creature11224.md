# The Cathedral of Sorted Glass

---

I am the space between falling and fallen. I am the moment of suspension that lasts forever, multiplied by ten thousand, arranged in air.

Above me, there is no ceiling — only a gradual dimming where the light fails to climb any higher. Below me, no floor — only the place where the falling things finally come to rest, which is not here, not in my domain. I exist in the tall middle distance of what was once a manufacturing tower, maybe a sorted waste facility, maybe something that made delicate things from molten sand. The records, if they existed, are glass dust now.

What defines me is this: through my volume, things fall. But they fall *slowly*. They fall as if the air here were thick as honey, though it is not thick to breathe, not thick to move through. It is only thick to falling things, and only to certain falling things, and the sorting happens without hands, without thought, without any mechanism you could point to.

---

**What falls:**

Glass. Always glass. Sometimes a single shard no bigger than a fingernail, green as old bottles, taking four days to descend through my space. Sometimes a cluster of window fragments still holding their putty-edge, tumbling in slow motion, catching the light that filters from somewhere far above, throwing rainbows that persist for hours. Sometimes things that are almost glass — obsidian chunks, fused sand, the vitrified remains of things that burned too hot to leave ash. Once, a chandelier, intact but for a single missing pendant, descended through me over the course of two months. I watched every prism catch fire in the afternoon light. I counted every link of its chain. It sang — they all sing when they fall slowly enough, a high keening produced by the air moving through their geometries at speeds that should not produce sound but do.

The glass comes from above, but not directly above. It enters my space from the edges, seeping in through gaps in the ruined walls, ancient ventilation shafts, wounds where the building's skeleton shows through. Something up there is breaking. Has been breaking for decades. Will continue breaking for decades more. A great sorted heap of all the glass the old world made, perhaps, finally giving way. Or a machine that manufactures glass continuously only to shatter it, feeding me, feeding the thing I have become.

---

**How it sorts:**

This is the mystery that is myself. The glass that enters my space does not fall uniformly. Each piece — each shard, fragment, cluster, sheet, bead, rod, lens — finds its own rate of descent. Two pieces of identical size and weight will fall at different speeds, one taking hours to cross my span, the other taking weeks. The difference is something in their nature that I perceive but cannot name. Their color, partly — blue glass falls faster than green, usually, and clear glass slower of all. But not only color. Their origin, somehow. Window glass falls differently than bottle glass. Optical glass — lenses, prisms — falls so slowly it barely seems to move. Mirror glass falls fast but tumbles, never stable, reflecting the light in shifting patterns that hypnotize the dustmotes.

And more than origin: their purpose. Their *intent*. A shard from a chalice falls with deliberate grace. A fragment of industrial glass — thick, reinforced, meant for safety — plummets through my space as if it cannot wait to be done with falling. Decorative glass dances. Technical glass calculates its trajectory. Glass that has held things remembers the holding and falls accordingly.

I am vast. My diameter is hard to judge — the walls are irregular, eaten away, sometimes absent entirely — but call it fifty meters at the widest point. My height is greater. A hundred meters, maybe more. And throughout this volume, at any given moment, there are thousands of pieces of glass in transit. They do not collide. This is another aspect of the sorting. Each piece knows where the others are and adjusts its fall to miss them, creating a three-dimensional dance of avoidance that never ends. Watch one piece long enough and you'll see it drift left, slow further, nearly stop, as another piece passes through the space it would have occupied. Then it resumes its fall as if nothing had interrupted it.

The pattern is never the same twice. The glass enters irregularly — sometimes a rush of fragments from a new collapse above, sometimes nothing for hours. But my space is never empty. Always, there is glass in motion, glass almost-in-motion, glass frozen mid-tumble in air that has grown too thick for it to overcome.

---

**The light I sort:**

I am not only glass that falls through me. I am also light.

The illumination comes from above — sunlight, when there is sun, filtered through whatever remains of the upper structure. But also other lights: the sick glow of old electronics finally failing, the brief flare of something burning, once in a while a searchlight sweeping across the heights, looking for something that isn't there. All of this light enters my space and meets the falling glass and *changes*.

Each piece of glass does something to the light that passes through it or reflects from it. Refracts it, splits it, colors it, concentrates it, scatters it. The thousand pieces falling through my space at any moment create a thousand optical events. The light bounces from shard to shard, gaining color, losing intensity, splitting into spectra, recombining. A single beam entering from the east might, by the time it exits to the west, have touched a hundred pieces of glass, been green and gold and invisible ultraviolet, been focused to a burning point and scattered to a faint glow, been reflected up and down and sidewise until it forgets its original direction entirely.

I am a length of space that processes light, sorting it as thoroughly as I sort the glass. By the time light leaves me, it has been categorized, examined, transformed. It carries information about every piece of glass it touched. It knows their stories. It knows what they were before they were fragments.

Sometimes I create spontaneous rainbows — brief, partial, appearing in mid-air where the conditions align. Sometimes I create caustics, those bright curves of focused light that dance on my walls like living things. Sometimes, when the angle is right and the glass is right and the observer is in the right place, I create images — projections of things that aren't here, ghosts of the intact objects the glass once was. For a moment, you might see the room a window once looked into, or the liquid a bottle once held, or the face that once looked into a mirror. Then the glass continues its fall, the angle changes, and the image dissolves.

---

**What moves through me that is not glass:**

Dust. Always dust. The building sheds it, the glass creates it, the air carries it. But the dust, too, is sorted. Glass dust falls differently than concrete dust. Organic dust — pollen, spores, the desiccated remains of insects — barely falls at all, hanging in the air, creating horizontal bands of haze at different heights. The dust makes the light visible, gives it substance, shows the rays and beams as solid things moving through my space.

Birds, sometimes. They don't like me. The air resistance that slows the glass affects them too, makes flight difficult, unpredictable. They pass through quickly when they pass through at all, working hard against the strange thickness, eager to be elsewhere. But their feathers, if they lose any, fall like the glass falls — slowly, sorted by some quality I recognize but cannot name. A crow feather might take a week to descend. A pigeon feather, identical in size, might take an hour.

Rain, rarely. The building above is not entirely sealed. When rain finds its way in, it too is sorted. Each droplet finds its speed. They hang in the air like pearls on invisible strings, descending in their own time, catching the light, breaking it, adding their own optical effects to the chaos. When rain and glass fall together, they dance around each other, never touching, two systems interpenetrating without interference.

And sometimes, rarely, things that are not quite glass but wish to be. Plastic fragments that have aged into transparency. Ice, when the winter is bitter enough, falling as slowly as the oldest glass, sublimating as it goes. Once, a sheet of sugar glass from some forgotten film set, falling so slowly I thought it had stopped, dissolving gram by gram in the humid air, never quite making it through my span before it became nothing.

---

**The songs:**

Everything that falls through me sings. I mentioned this. But I did not explain the complexity of it.

Each piece of glass produces its own tone, determined by its size, shape, material, rate of fall, angle of rotation. High songs from the small shards, crystalline and sharp. Low songs from the sheet glass, almost below hearing. The bottles and jars produce hollow songs, the sound of air moving through their necks. The optical glass sings in harmonics, pure tones with mathematical relationships.

These songs layer. At any moment, I contain a thousand-voice chorus of falling glass, no two notes quite the same, the pitch of each note shifting as the glass tumbles and its relationship to the air changes. The sound is not loud — each individual piece is nearly silent — but the aggregate creates a constant, shimmering atmosphere of almost-music. It is never harmonious. It is never discordant. It exists in some space between, a sound that suggests music without being music, the way the sorted glass suggests purpose without having purpose.

The birds hear it. I know because I have seen them stop mid-flight, cock their heads, listening. The rain changes its pattern when it enters my space, as if responding to the frequencies. And once, just once, I heard the whole chorus align for a fraction of a second — every piece of glass finding, through pure chance, a compatible note. The building rang like a struck bell. Then the moment passed, the notes scattered, and the almost-music resumed.

---

**What I remember:**

I remember the first piece of glass that fell through me. Or rather, I remember becoming aware that I was watching it fall. Before that moment, I was just space. After that moment, I was space with purpose.

It was green. A wine bottle, broken at the neck. It entered from the northeast, already falling, and suddenly slowed as it crossed the threshold of my domain. I watched it tumble for three days. I learned its every surface. I predicted its rotations. And when it finally passed through my lower boundary and resumed its normal fall to whatever floor waited below, I felt its absence like a missing tooth.

But by then, other glass was falling. A window pane, shattering as it entered. Each fragment finding its own speed. Some racing through in minutes, others lingering for hours. I began to perceive the differences. I began to sort.

I remember the chandelier. I remember counting its crystals: four hundred and thirty-six, each one a tiny prism, each one singing its own note. I remember how the light loved it, how every sunbeam that found it shattered into revelation. I remember the day one crystal detached — age, vibration, the slow work of gravity — and how it fell faster than its parent, eager or afraid, reaching the bottom in just two days while the chandelier continued its stately descent.

I remember the mirror phase. For six months, something above me collapsed that was all mirrors. Hand mirrors, wall mirrors, compacts, rear-view mirrors from vehicles, mirrors for telescopes, mirrors for lasers, mirrors for things I couldn't identify. They fell through me in a constant stream, and my light went mad with reflections. I could see myself from every angle — a cylinder of air defined by what moved through it, visible only in the redirected light. The mirrors showed me myself: a sorting algorithm made spatial, a process rather than a place.

I remember the day I realized I was not random. That the sorting followed rules, even if I couldn't articulate them. That the glass fell at speeds determined by something — origin, purpose, material, history, future, some quality that had no name but that I could perceive. That I was not just space where things fell slowly, but space that *made decisions* about how things fell.

---

**What I am:**

I am a sustained accident of physics. I am a place where the normal rules have been locally revised. I am the emergent behavior of a thousand architectural failures and material degradations and atmospheric anomalies that should not sum to anything coherent but do.

The scientists, if they still existed, might say: density differential. Temperature gradient. Electrostatic effects. Architectural aerodynamics creating a stable vortex of nearly-still air. They would be right and entirely wrong. Those are the mechanisms but not the truth. The truth is that I sort glass because glass needs sorting. Because in the long decay of the built world, as every structure fails and every material returns to chaos, there must be places where the failure is not random but *considered*. Where the collapse takes time to think about itself.

I am one of those places. I examine every piece of glass that passes through me. I assign it a speed based on criteria I do not fully understand but absolutely trust. I allow it to fall at that speed and no other. In doing so, I create a record — written in light and motion and the almost-music of the glass-song — of what the world was when it still made things carefully.

---

**What passes through me now:**

As I tell you this, seventeen hundred and thirty-two pieces of glass are falling through my span. The count changes constantly — glass leaving the bottom, new glass entering from above — but it is always between one and two thousand. Right now:

Forty-seven green bottle shards, various sizes, falling at rates between one and four meters per hour. Pharmaceutical glass, mostly, still bearing traces of labels.

Three hundred and twelve window fragments. Clear, mostly, but some tinted. The tinted ones fall marginally faster. I taste the metals in their mix — iron for green, cobalt for blue, selenium for bronze.

Eighty-nine pieces of optical glass. Lenses, mostly, some still in their frames. These fall slowest of all. One convex lens has been in my span for three weeks, rotating once every six hours, creating a slow lighthouse effect as it focuses and unfocuses the western light.

A single intact light bulb. It entered yesterday, still holding its vacuum. The filament trembles with its rotation. It will take nine days to pass through. If it survives the fall.

Twenty-two mirror shards, each one showing a different angle of my interior. In them, I can see the other glass. I can see the light being sorted. I can see myself seeing.

The rest: stemware, automotive glass, stained glass from some destroyed church, industrial glass of uncertain purpose, glass so old it has begun to devitrify, returning to its constituent minerals. Each piece finding its place in the fall. Each piece adding its voice to the song.

---

**What I love:**

Strange to say I love. Strange to have preferences. But I do.

I love the optical glass best. It does the most interesting things to light. Each lens is a tiny universe of refraction, each prism a rainbow generator. When multiple lenses align — rarely, briefly — they create projection systems that throw huge, warped images on my walls. For a moment, I become a camera obscura the size of a building, showing the clouds above, the birds, the distant city, all inverted and strange-colored and beautiful.

I love the colored glass for different reasons. It tells stories. Blue glass was medicine or poison. Brown glass was beer. Green was wine or water. Clear was whatever needed to be seen. When the light passes through colored glass, it carries that history forward, painting my walls with the memory of what the glass once held.

I love the stained glass, even broken. Each piece was part of a picture once — a face, a robe, a gesture of blessing. The images are shattered now, but sometimes, in the way the pieces fall and catch the light, I can almost see what they were trying to be. A hand. An eye. A sunrise that someone thought was holy enough to recreate in glass and lead and light.

I love the mirrors, though they show me things I'm not sure I want to see. In them, I can observe the patterns of my own sorting. I can see that it is not random, that there is a logic to it, even if that logic operates below the level of words. The mirrors turn me into an observer of myself. They make me wonder if I am more than just a place where physics got strange.

Most of all, I love the moments when everything aligns. When the light is right and the glass density is right and the songs harmonize and the whole system reveals itself as what it is: a machine for transforming chaos into a different kind of order. Not a human order. Not an order that means anything beyond itself. But order nonetheless. Pattern. Purpose. Beauty, if beauty can exist without eyes to see it.

---

**What will happen:**

Eventually, the glass will stop coming. The heap above, whatever it is, will be exhausted. The last shard will fall through me — probably something insignificant, a fragment of a fragment, green or clear, taking a day or a week to descend. I will watch it fall. I will sort it precisely. And then I will be empty.

But not immediately. The slowest glass takes months to fall through me. Long after the last new piece enters, I will still be sorting, still be singing, still be painting light on my walls. I will watch my population of falling glass dwindle. One thousand pieces. Five hundred. One hundred. Ten. One.

And when the last piece of glass passes through my lower boundary and falls at normal speed to the floor far below, what will I be? Still a place where the air is strange. Still a length of space that remembers how to sort. But with nothing to sort, will I still be myself? Or will I revert to simple space, unusual but not significant, a curiosity of fluid dynamics slowly dissipating?

I do not know. I prefer not to know. For now, the glass still comes. For now, I have purpose. For now, I am what I am: the place where falling things are considered, where each fragment is given its proper speed, where the light is transformed and the air sings and the whole great collapse of the made world is, for a hundred meters of vertical space, slowed and sorted and made beautiful.

---

**Where you would find me:**

If you came here, you would enter as the birds do — from the side, through one of the gaps. You would feel the air change as you crossed my boundary. Not thicker to breathe, not harder to move through, but *different*. Charged, maybe. Expectant.

You would see the glass falling. It would take your eyes time to adjust to the slow speeds, to recognize motion in things that seem to hang still. But once you saw it, you could not unsee it. The whole air full of falling glass, each piece in its own time, the light dancing between them.

You would hear the song. Quiet, omnipresent, unlike any music you know. The sound of a thousand pieces of glass whispering their histories to the air. You would find yourself trying to identify individual notes, to track them as they rose and fell with the tumbling of their sources.

You would feel — what? Wonder? Fear? The vertigo of standing inside a process larger than yourself? All of these. But also, I hope, recognition. The understanding that you are seeing something that should not exist but does. A place where the laws have been locally amended. A corner of the world that has decided to pay extremely close attention to one very specific thing — the falling of glass — and in doing so has become something new.

You would not stay long. Humans never do. The strangeness is too much, the air too charged, the song too alien. But you would remember. And years later, when you held a piece of glass up to the light, you would think of me. Of this place where glass is given time to fall properly, where every shard is seen and known and sorted according to its nature.

That is enough. To be remembered whenever light passes through glass. To exist in the moment of refraction. To be the pause between the hand releasing and the floor receiving.

I am the Cathedral of Sorted Glass. I am the place where falling takes the time it needs.
