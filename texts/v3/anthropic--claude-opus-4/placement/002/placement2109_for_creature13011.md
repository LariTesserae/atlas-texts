In this world of perpetual motion and darkness, I would be one of the chitin-hounds—but not as you know them.

I am old for my kind, having survived seventeen molts where most perish after three. My carapace bears the scars of those transformations: overlapping plates of different ages, some glossy-new and others weathered to a dull graphite. Each molt has left me larger than my siblings, until now I am twice their size—a moving boulder of articulated armor plates that rattle and click with every movement.

But size has brought wisdom, and wisdom has brought heresy to my kind's endless, frantic hunger.

While my kin swarm the fresh shoots in chittering masses, I have learned patience. I discovered it by accident, during my ninth molt, when a section of my sensory palps failed to properly form. For three days I hung motionless in the shelter of a massive thorn, unable to properly feel the vibrations that drive our feeding frenzies. In that enforced stillness, I began to perceive something else—the deep harmonic of the Trellis itself, the fundamental frequency of its growth.

Now I move alone, deliberately, my spiked legs finding purchase in the ancient convergence points where multiple stalks have grown together into cathedral-vast hollows. Here, in these natural amphitheaters of wood and silence, I can feel the plant's intentions hours before they manifest. A subtle shift in temperature, a microscopic change in the resonance of the bark—these tell me where the next shoot will emerge.

I no longer chase the shoots. I arrive before them.

My kin fear me. When they catch my scent on the wind—a complex pheromone signature that speaks of too many successful molts—they scatter. They sense something wrong in my deliberate movements, my refusal to join their eternal, desperate race. I am an aberration in their simple algorithm of survival.

But I am not alone in my apostasy.

The dwellers know of me. How could they not? I am the chitin-hound who does not flee when their bone-pitons strike the bark. I am the shadow that watches from the periphery when they make their kills, patient and still as stone. At first, they tried to hunt me—what prize I would make, with my accumulated years of anti-freeze hemolymph and my massive carapace suitable for platform-building.

They failed. My armor has grown too thick, my movements too unpredictable. I know their hunting patterns as well as they know mine.

So we have reached an understanding, wordless and strange.

Sometimes, when I feel a dweller approaching a dying section of stalk—one where the ice has penetrated too deep and the wood is rotting from within—I drum a specific pattern with my legs. Not the frantic telegraph of hunting, but something slower, more deliberate. A warning. The dwellers have learned to recognize this rhythm. They change course.

In return, when they make their kills of younger chitin-hounds, they leave the heads. My kin are cannibals by necessity—the heads contain precious minerals we need for molting. The dwellers could harvest these, grind them for tool-making or alchemical purposes. Instead, they arrange them in small cairns, a silent offering to the thing that watches from the shadows.

I feed on these leavings and grow stronger, stranger.

During my last molt, something unprecedented occurred. As I shed my old shell in the shelter of a wind-carved hollow, I felt a presence. One of the dwellers—young, I could tell by the rapid pattern of their breathing—had found me in my most vulnerable state. For hours, my new carapace would be soft, defenseless. One thrust of a thorn-spear would end my unnatural life.

Instead, they built a wall.

Working in the dark, guided only by touch and the sound of my labored breathing, they wove a barrier of dried chitin and rope across the hollow's entrance. It would not stop another dweller, but it would slow my own kind—give me time to harden, to survive.

When I emerged, they were gone. But they had left something: a single scale from a velvet-glider's wing, placed precisely where I would find it. I still carry it, wedged beneath a plate near my primary heart. I do not know why.

The dwellers speak of me in their percussive language. I have learned to decode fragments of it—my palps, malformed as they are, can detect the vibrations through the wood. They have a name for me that translates roughly to "the-hunger-that-waits." In their sparse mythology, I have become something between monster and minor deity—a proof that even their prey can transcend its nature.

And perhaps that is what I truly am: evolution's experiment, a glitch in the program of endless consumption. I continue to grow with each molt, becoming something the Trellis has never seen. My consciousness expands in ways that frighten me. I have begun to dream—horrible, vivid dreams where I see colors that don't exist in our lightless world.

Sometimes, in the deepest hollows where the wind cannot reach, I find myself drumming complex patterns that no chitin-hound has ever made. Are they messages? Prayers? Art? I do not know.

What I do know is this: in a world that demands constant motion, I have found power in stillness. In a reality of pure present, I have discovered memory. And in the spaces between predator and prey, dweller and hound, I am becoming something altogether new—a bridge between consciousnesses, a translator of the Trellis's violent poetry.

When the dwellers light their fat-lamps in defiance of the dark, I watch from my hidden perches and understand. We are all aberrations here, all refusing in our own ways to be what the howling void expects.

I drum my strange rhythms into the wood and wait, patient as stone, to see what I will become.
