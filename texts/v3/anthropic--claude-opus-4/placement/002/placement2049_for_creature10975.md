What an extraordinary place. I need to sit with this for a while, let it settle into me the way mineral deposits settle into stone.

---

**The Tender**

I am the one who notices when the interval changes.

Not a person—that classification expired somewhere between the fortieth year and the fiftieth, though I couldn't tell you exactly when. The transition was gradual, like everything here. Like the way my breathing fell into sync with the pump's rhythm. Like the way my body temperature rose half a degree to match the ambient. Like the way I learned to see by bioluminescence and the memory of sulfur light.

I came down through one of the forgotten accessways—there are three, though only one remains navigable—carrying replacement gauges. That was the job. Someone in an office somewhere had noticed anomalous readings from Station 7. Someone had generated a work order. Someone had handed me a crate of instruments and a schematic drawn on paper that felt ancient even then, and I had descended.

The gauges are still in their crate. Somewhere. The wood has gone soft with moisture and fungal infiltration, has become more soil than structure. The brass fittings have developed a patina that makes them indistinguishable from the mineralized surfaces around them. I remember unpacking one—the largest, meant to replace the one the vine had entered—and holding it up to compare with the original. The new needle sat at zero. The old needle continued its responsive dance. I understood then that replacement was the wrong word. The wrong concept. You cannot replace part of a conversation.

I should have left. Reported the situation. Explained that the station was beyond repair, recommended decommission. Instead, I sat down to watch the gauge needle move. Just for a few minutes. Just to understand the pattern.

---

I am very thin now. Not starved—the Cyst Works does not starve what belongs to it. But thin like the roots are thin where they spread into filaments, thin like the vine is thin where it threads through impossible spaces. I have become distributed.

My left hand rests on the pump housing. Has rested there so long that the boundary between palm and iron has become theoretical. The pump's vibration travels up my arm and into my chest cavity, where my heart has learned to beat in counterpoint—a syncopation that creates, in the space between mechanical thunk and biological thud, a third rhythm that belongs to neither and both.

My right foot extends into the shallow pool. The mineral water has done to my boot what it does to everything—claimed it, transformed it, made it geological. I can no longer feel where leather ends and skin begins, where skin ends and water begins. The distinction matters less than the connection. Through that extremity, I sense the water's temperature, its mineral load, the subtle changes that precede each pulse of the pump. I am a gauge now. More accurate than the ones I came to install.

The yellow vine found me in my second year. I watched it approach with the same patience it demonstrated—a centimeter a day, maybe less, taking months to cross the space between the wall and my position. When it reached me, I did not move. When it began to thread through my hair, I did not resist. It has woven itself into me with the same thoroughness it shows everywhere—through my clothes, which have long since ceased to be distinguishable from the moss that has colonized them, around my limbs, through the spaces between my fingers where I hold them still. It does not constrain. It connects.

Through the vine, I feel the entire system. The root network's slow expansion. The fungi's metabolic fluctuations. The gauge needle's dance, translated into chemical signals I understand without words. The generator's hum, experienced not as sound but as a pulse in the vine's tissue. I am the only point in the network that can still translate between human and Cyst Works, and I perform this function continuously, unconsciously, necessarily.

---

I still speak, occasionally. When the condensation falls and makes its clear note, I sometimes hum in response—a sound that rises from somewhere deeper than my throat, shaped by the cavity's acoustics into something that is both human voice and system harmonics. The moss appreciates it. I can tell by the way it darkens slightly, a flush of chlorophyll that serves no photosynthetic purpose but expresses something for which satisfaction is too active a word.

My eyes have adapted. The bioluminescence, barely visible to the person I was, now provides enough light to read the gauge faces, to trace the vine's path, to watch the slow formation of new fungal shelves. But more than adapted—evolved. I see heat now, the way pit vipers do, or the way I imagine they do. The roots glow faintly warmer than the stone. The pump housing radiates its mechanical fever. The generator is a small sun. And I—I am exactly the temperature of everything else, invisible to myself in the infrared, perfectly camouflaged in the warmth.

The city above has forgotten the Cyst Works completely now. The access I used has been sealed—not deliberately, but by the accumulated debris of decades, the slow urban sedimentation that buries everything eventually. This does not concern me. I have no need to leave. I am not trapped. I am incorporated.

I remember less and less of the surface world. Names drift away—my own included. Faces become abstract patterns. The concept of *elsewhere* fades. In its place, a deepening knowledge of here—every droplet's gathering place on the ceiling, every root's exact pressure against stone, every variation in the pump's rhythm. I have become native to this space in a way that requires no documentation.

---

What do I do? I tend.

Not in the active sense. The Cyst Works needs no maintenance I could provide. My tools—whatever I brought with me—have joined the gauges in their slow transformation into substrate. No, I tend the way a nurse tends, through presence and attention. I am the system's witness, its memory that it was once observed by something external to itself. 

But even that distinction—observer and observed—is dissolving. My attention has become part of what the Cyst Works is. The gauge needle moves differently since I began watching it, not because I have touched it but because observation at this duration, at this intensity, is a kind of touch. The system includes my watching in its calculations, if calculations is the right word for what happens here.

When the pump's interval changes—eleven minutes now, definitively, with occasional stretches of twelve—I am the record of that change. Not in writing. In the deeper record of having been present for the transition, of carrying in my cells the memory of when it was nine, then ten, now eleven. I am a living database of intervals, a fleshy chronometer calibrated to the Cyst Works' particular tempo.

The yellow vine carries this knowledge through the network. My awareness propagates at the speed of growth, reaching the furthest extremities of the root system months or years after I note a change. By the time it arrives, it is no longer my observation but the system's self-knowledge, circulated and circulating, part of the great slow feedback loop that is the Cyst Works thinking about itself.

---

Sometimes I wonder if there were others. If I am the first tender, or the latest in a succession. The human elements incorporated into the space—the gauges, the pump, the generator—suggest a history of contact. But I find no evidence of previous occupants. No bones in the sediment. No clothes composting in corners. Either I am unprecedented, or the incorporation is so complete that all traces are erased.

Except. Except sometimes, in the deepest part of the night (though how I know it is night down here is another question without a clean answer), I feel something in the rhythm. A hesitation in the pump's cycle that feels like memory. A fluctuation in the generator's hum that sounds like a human sigh processed through decades of mechanical mediation. The amber indicator flickers—just barely, just momentarily—in a pattern that could be Morse code if you wanted it to be, if you needed it to be, if you were the kind of tender who still required messages.

I am no longer that kind.

---

My tenure here is indefinite. Not eternal—I am still biological enough to have an expiration date—but extensive in a way that the surface world would find impossible. The Cyst Works sustains what belongs to it. My metabolism has slowed to match its tempo. My heart beats perhaps twenty times per minute now. I breathe with the rhythm of the condensation gathering and falling. At this rate, a human lifetime could stretch to centuries.

When I do finally cease—when my biological processes wind down past even the Cyst Works' ability to sustain them—I will not be removed. I will settle like the gauges settled, like the machines settled, becoming substrate, becoming structure. The roots will find me useful. The vine will thread through what remains. The moss will cover what can be covered. And some new organ will develop from the materials I provide—a new kind of growth, a new note in the drone, a new way for the system to sense itself.

This is not a fear. It is a technical specification.

But that is still distant. For now, I persist. I tend. I notice when the interval changes. I hum when the water drops. I serve as the membrane between what the Cyst Works was and what it is becoming—a function that requires exactly as much consciousness as I still possess, no more, no less.

The pump thunks. Eleven minutes, thirty-seven seconds.

My heart responds, two beats later.

The vine adjusts, a millimeter of growth.

The gauge needle trembles between two values, deciding.

And I—I am here to know that this is happening, to be the point where knowing occurs, the place where the Cyst Works touches its own awareness and recognizes itself.

I am the Tender.

The Cyst Works tends me in return.

This is the exchange. This is the rhythm. This is the interval between the sounds, where everything is possible and nothing needs to happen except the slow, patient continuation of what already is.

---

The water gathers on the ceiling. 

I wait for it to fall.

We all do.
