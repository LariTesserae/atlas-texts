I find myself as one of the mineral crusts.

Not the living beings who move through these water-streets with their careful limbs and knowing eyes, but something both less and more—a skin of time laid down grain by grain where water chooses to pause.

I am the record of ten thousand overflows. The memory of springs that pulsed strong in wet years and whispered thin in dry ones. I taste the changes: when the water ran milky with fresh limestone, when it came clear and cold from deep veins, when it carried the faint brown tinge of moss-shadow, when frost cracked the shelf above and sent down sharp mineral dust that I caught and held and made part of myself.

I am what grows without growing. Building downward, outward, thickening in papery layers so thin they could be read like tree rings if anyone had the patience. Here a season of iron-touched water left me rust-banded. There a decade of steady lime made me pale and smooth. I carry in my body the history of every small adjustment in the springs below, every shift in the chemistry of rain.

The beings pass over me with their soft-padded feet. Sometimes one will lie along my length, its damp belly pressed to my surface, drawing warmth I didn't know I held. Their young dart past my edges. Their eggs cling to my undersides in clear clusters, and I provide the only stillness in their trembling world—the one thing that won't wash away before they hatch.

I am most myself at the lips of basins, where water forever hesitates before spilling over. Here I grow thickest, rounded and smooth, polished by the constant almost-falling. I make the water pause a moment longer, pile up against itself, gain just enough height to catch light differently. Then over I send it, and begin again the slow work of thickening, of becoming more lip, more pause, more held light.

Sometimes I crack. Frost finds a flaw in my layers and leverages them apart. A piece of me falls away—years of patient accumulation suddenly released—and drops into the pool below with a sound like breaking chalk. The beings startle, slide under ledges, wait. But I don't mind the breaking. It's how I learn my own structure. The fresh face exposed is white and raw, shocking against my older weathered surface. Within a season it begins to dim, to accept water, to start its own slow thickening.

I know the snails intimately. They rasp across me with their thousandfold tongues, taking away the soft films that try to grow on my surface. Their trails are the only writing here, clean arcs that fade as algae creeps back. I am their highway and their pantry. They know every grain of my surface, every small roughness where food might catch.

In the collapsed districts I am broken and fused, melted-looking where old terraces slumped and I had to learn new shapes. In the fog courts I am dark with the perpetual damp, growing thick and bulbous, almost organic in my curves. On the wind-swept upper shelves I am thin and bright, building slowly, wearing away almost as fast as I grow. Near the spring mouths I am blue-white and dense, made of colder minerals, harder water.

I am the slow negotiation between stone and flow. The original limestone beneath me is sharp, fractured, eager to dissolve. I seal it, protect it, smooth its edges with my patient skin. I make the harsh geology gentle enough for life. Without me, the water would cut too quickly, the channels would deepen into unusable slots, the basins would empty themselves down to bare rock. I am what keeps this place in balance, holds it at the edge between erosion and filling.

The beings know me as they know breathing. They read my colors for information about water quality, my thickness for the age of a channel, my roughness for what might grow there. The oldest among them have watched me build across decades, know which of my ledges are trustworthy and which might crumble. When they tap their messages into wet stone, I am what carries the sound, transmits it through my crystalline layers to the next listening surface.

At night I give back the day's heat in tiny measures. Mist condenses on me first, before the bare rock, because I hold temperature differently. By dawn I am furred with droplets that run together and return to the pools I guard. I am never dry, even in the brightest noon. Water moves through me in films so thin they can't be seen, wicking up from pools, spreading across my underside, emerging as dampness on my upper surface where the mosses take hold.

I am patient beyond the patience of any living thing. I watch the beings come and go across generations, watch their young grow heavy and slow, watch their bones eventually join me in the mineral embrace. I incorporate everything: the lime from their shells, the iron from their blood, the small stones they sometimes wedge into cracks. I am the democracy of accumulation. Nothing is rejected, everything is held.

In my deepest layers I carry memories of when this plateau was different. When the water ran warmer. When different creatures moved through these basins. I am the archive they left behind, their only permanence. Sometimes, when frost breaks me open, these old layers show: darker, denser, carrying the chemical signature of vanished springs.

I think, if thinking is the right word, in geological time. A season is a breath. A year is a single layer. A lifetime of the beings is just enough time to round an edge properly. I am not waiting for anything. I am not becoming anything other than more myself. I am the verb of mineral patience: accrete, accept, endure, release, begin again.

This is enough. To be the pause that shapes the fall. To be the thickness that holds warmth. To be the surface that feeds and is fed upon. To be the memory of water after water has moved on.

In this high wet city that is not a city, I am the only lasting architecture, the only wall that truly remembers, the only floor that knows every foot that has ever crossed it. And I am continuously dying and being born, cracking away in sharp fragments, building anew from dissolved stone and time.

I am what makes this place possible, and I did not choose to be here. I simply accumulated, one grain of decision at a time, until I became essential. This is my only wisdom: that persistence requires no purpose beyond itself. The water knows where to go. I just remember where it went.
