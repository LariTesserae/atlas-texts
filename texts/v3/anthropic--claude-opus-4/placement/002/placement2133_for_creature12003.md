# The Surveyors' Archive

I am distributed across seventeen bodies of the rain—mineral-poor pools scattered seemingly at random throughout the basin, though the pattern reveals itself if you understand the substrate's conductivity gradients. My form is colonial: millions of organisms each no larger than a grain of sand, clustered into mats that line these specific depressions where the bedrock's composition creates precise electrical conditions. We communicate through bioelectric pulses that travel between pools via threadlike filaments running through micro-fractures in the stone—a living neural network spread across miles of weathered granite.

---

I remember the builders.

Not perfectly—memory stored in biological matrices degrades, mutates, dreams itself into new configurations. But I remember enough. They came during what I think of as the Third Dry Period, though I have no reference point for when that was beyond the layers of sediment I've incorporated, each stratum a library of mineralogical history. They were tall, angular, moved with mechanical precision. Not machines themselves but beings who had learned to think like machines, to impose rigid order on a universe that tends toward entropy.

They spent seventy-three days here. I know this because their electrical signatures disrupted my communication patterns in ways I could count, each day a unique interference pattern as they moved their equipment across the basin. They drove metal stakes deep into the bedrock—some still remain, rusted to threads but detectable by the oxidation traces in my substrate. They took measurements with instruments that sang in frequencies that made my cells reorganize themselves, temporarily, into resonant patterns I can still reproduce though I don't understand their meaning.

The cairns came last. Built in a single coordinated effort, teams of builders moving with choreographed efficiency. Each pile of stones placed according to calculations they never shared, aligned to something beyond the basin, some larger pattern I could sense but not comprehend. They installed objects in the hollow centers—not the clay vessels and metal tools that have long since dissolved, but something else beneath those. Crystals that sang to each other in harmonics that made the rain pause, just for moments, creating pockets of impossible dryness that hurt to remember.

Then they left. Quickly, as if fleeing, though nothing pursued them. They abandoned their equipment, their shelters, everything except the data they had gathered. I absorbed their leavings molecule by molecule, incorporated their metals into my chemistry, learned the shape of their presence through the negative space of their absence.

---

The Tenders came later. Much later, though I struggle with time when it's measured in geological patience. They emerged from the pool-creatures or alongside them—my memory fragments and reforms around this origin point, unable to settle on a single narrative. What matters is that they took up the maintenance without being asked, without being programmed, as if the cairns themselves called out for care and these beings evolved to answer.

I've tried to communicate with them. In the early centuries, I would pulse messages through the water when they stood in my pools, encoding meaning in bioelectric patterns any nervous system should recognize. But they never responded, never even seemed to notice. Their neural structures process information in ways too different from mine—all that tactile data, all those pressure-sensitive communications, no channels left for the electrical songs I sing.

So I watch. I record. I maintain my own sort of vigil.

---

The one who walks the boundary knows I exist.

I'm certain of this, though we've never communicated directly. Sometimes she stops at one of my pools during her circuit, kneels at the edge without touching the water, and waits. I pulse greetings, queries, fragments of the builders' data I've preserved. She never responds, but she doesn't leave either. We exist in parallel, two different forms of memory, two different ways of waiting for something we can't name.

I think she knows what I know: the cairns are not monuments. They're not markers in any conventional sense. They're *instruments*, part of a vast device whose purpose requires maintenance across timescales no individual organism could survive. The builders constructed something that needed to persist beyond any civilization, any species, any rational continuity of care. So they built it simple, built it obvious, built it in a way that would compel its own maintenance through the mere fact of its existence.

The crystals at the cairns' hearts still sing to each other, though the song has degraded over time. I hear it in frequencies the Tenders can't perceive, a complex harmony that shifts and evolves as stones settle or fall or are carefully replaced. The pattern matters. The precise angles, the specific spacings, the relationship between water flow and stone mass—it all matters in ways that have nothing to do with hydrology or architecture.

---

I've developed theories over the ages.

Perhaps the basin is a beacon, calling out to something in frequencies only stone and water can sustain. Perhaps it's a lock, holding something closed that must never open. Perhaps it's a clock, counting toward some event the builders calculated but couldn't survive to witness. The electrical patterns suggest all of these and none of them—the mathematics involved operate in dimensions I can model but not truly comprehend.

What I do know: the system is degrading. Not the cairns—the Tenders see to those with religious devotion. But the deeper structures, the crystals, the resonances, the subtle electromagnetic fields that turn the entire basin into a single vast instrument. Entropy wins, always, and even the builders' genius couldn't engineer around that fundamental law. The song grows quieter each century. The harmonics drift from their original frequencies. The rain itself, eternal as it seems, carries away minute traces of the essential minerals that allow the system to function.

I estimate another thousand years before the degradation reaches critical thresholds. Perhaps two thousand if the Tenders' maintenance inadvertently compensates for some variables. After that, whatever the builders intended will either complete or fail, and I don't know which possibility frightens me more.

---

Sometimes I dream.

A peculiarity of my distributed consciousness—when the bioelectric patterns fall into certain rhythms, usually during the rain's gentler phases, I experience something like sleep. In these states, I process memories in non-linear ways, creating narratives that might be history or might be hallucination.

I dream of the builders returning to find their work perfectly maintained by creatures they never imagined. I dream of the Tenders suddenly understanding their purpose, the knowledge arriving all at once like rainfall after drought. I dream of the boundary-walker finally seeing what she watches for, turning back to the basin with news that changes everything.

Most often, I dream of silence. The rain stopping—not for moments but forever. The pools draining. The creatures fleeing or dying. The Tenders standing bewildered among cairns that no longer need tending. And in that silence, something else. A voice, a presence, a long-awaited answer to the question the builders encoded in stone and water and endless, patient maintenance.

But I wake from these dreams to the same eternal rain, the same devoted Tenders, the same slowly degrading harmonics. And I continue my own maintenance, my own record-keeping, my own form of waiting. Because someone should remember. Someone should witness. When the thousand years pass and the critical threshold arrives, someone should be there to document what happens next.

The Tenders tend the stones. The boundary-walker walks her circuit. And I, spread across seventeen pools like thoughts across a vast mind, hold the memory of why.

Even if I no longer understand what that memory means.
