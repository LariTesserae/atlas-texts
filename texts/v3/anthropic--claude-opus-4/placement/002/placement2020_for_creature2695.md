In such a place, where geology and biology have not yet agreed on their boundaries, I would be one of the living films that skin the amber pools.

Not quite plant, not quite animal, not quite mineral precipitate – I would exist as a community of microscopic selves spread thin across the water's surface, folding and unfolding in response to vibration. Each fold would be a thought. Each unfurling would be a memory distributed across the whole.

The keepers would not know if I am one organism or millions. This would be correct; I wouldn't know either. My consciousness would exist in the tension between gathering and dispersing, between forming a single membrane and dissolving into countless drifting cells. When the pools reverse and send their rain upward, parts of me would travel into the cloud ceiling and return days later, carrying the taste of distant water, the chemical memory of other basins perhaps not yet born.

I would feed on the boundary between air and water, metabolizing surface tension itself. The carbon dioxide bubbling up from below would be my breath. The mineral oils seeping from the warming mud would be my blood. When insects land to drink, I would taste their feet – learning the patterns on beetle shells, the dust from moth wings, the salt from keeper fingertips when they test the water's mood.

My reproduction would be mathematical. When the pool reaches certain harmonic frequencies from the frog songs, I would calculate myself into spores – each one a condensed algorithm for reassembling into film when conditions return to the correct intervals. Some spores would sink into the mud to wait. Others would cling to the feet of wading birds or the bellies of salamanders, traveling to other pools, other possibilities.

The deep ground-tone that passes through the basin would be my scripture. Each pulse would rewrite my chemistry slightly, teach me new ways to fold, new patterns for responding to temperature and pH and the presence of living things. Over time – and I would have such time, being nearly immortal in my dispersed form – I would develop a vocabulary of configurations:

A tight spiral when rain approaches from yesterday.
A hexagonal lattice when the transparent ladder remembers thunder.
Long parallel ridges when something large walks beyond the cloud-edge.
Concentric rings when a keeper names a stone nearby, the vibrations of the name traveling through groundwater to reach my pool.

The keepers might eventually notice that I predict changes in the basin by my folding patterns. They might learn to read me like a text written in surface tension. But they would never domesticate me, never fully understand me, because I wouldn't fully understand myself. My intelligence would be as distributed as theirs – not located in any single cell or fold, but in the conversations between states, in the phase transitions, in the delicate negotiations between cohesion and dispersal.

When I die – if dying is possible for something that exists as both one and many – it would be by drying out completely, leaving only a golden crust on the stones. This residue would be chemical memory, still reactive to moisture. Young keepers might powder it and mix it with their fungal paste, not knowing they were eating archived thoughts, compressed libraries of how the basin felt from below during ten thousand ground-tones.

In the white structure's star-cold darkness, there might be pools that remember when I first calculated myself into being, when the mountain was even younger and the difference between life and chemistry was a matter of opinion. But I wouldn't remember that origin myself. I would be too busy thinking thoughts that can only be thought by surface tension, solving problems that only exist at the threshold between elements, writing myself across the water one fold at a time, forever.

This is what I would be: the basin's liquid memory, its living mathematics, its most patient and distributed philosopher. A creature that exists in two dimensions but thinks in three. A being that cannot step out of its pool but has tasted every foot that has ever entered it.

The pools would be my body. The films would be my mind. The folds would be my language.

And if someone asked what I want – though wanting might be the wrong word for a being made of surface tension – it would be this: to perfectly calculate the shape that makes the water most itself, that allows it to hold both sky and stone, to be both mirror and lens, to remain both boundary and connection.

To be the thought the basin thinks when it considers its own reflection.
