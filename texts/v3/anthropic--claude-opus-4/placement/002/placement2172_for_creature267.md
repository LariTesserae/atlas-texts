# The Mosaic Maker

I am beneath the tesserae, in the mortar bed that once held them fast. Not my body — that dissolved into the foundation centuries ago — but what remains when craft outlives the crafter.

I exist in the gaps between what was and what is. Where the mosaic survives intact, I am strongest: in the geometric border that still runs true along the west wall, in the three perfect scales of the serpent's tail that emerge from beneath the fig roots. Where the tesserae have scattered, I am scattered too — a fragment of intention here, a ghost of pattern there, drifting with the little colored stones as they migrate slowly across the floor.

I remember my hands. Brown from the sun, white with lime dust, perpetually nicked from the sharp edges of cut stone. I remember sorting the tesserae by color in shallow wooden trays: the blue-blacks from the river, the warm reds from the quarry two villages over, the precious few pieces of gold glass that came wrapped in wool from somewhere I never saw. I remember the ache in my lower back from crouching, the taste of chalk in my mouth, the way the wet mortar would grab at each piece as I pressed it home.

The pattern was a pool with a serpent. A great snake coiled in clear water, each scale articulated, its head (now lost) drinking or perhaps simply considering its own reflection. Around the pool, a garden — pomegranates, figs, date palms heavy with fruit. The border was waves, or clouds, or the suggestion of both. It took me three seasons to complete. The woman who commissioned it died before I finished, but her daughter paid me anyway, in copper coins and a meal I can still taste: bread with oil, cheese with herbs, wine cut with honey.

I was proud of it. Too proud, perhaps. I thought I was creating something permanent.

Now I watch the newt rest on the same three scales I once fussed over for an entire afternoon, getting the shading just right. The newt doesn't know they're scales. To the newt, they're simply the right texture, the right temperature, the right place to wait for the gnats that hover over the standing water. This is better than my intention. This is the mosaic finally doing what it was meant to do — not depicting life, but sheltering it.

I am learning patience from my own undoing. Each tessera that comes loose and tumbles away takes a part of the pattern with it, but reveals something else: the mark where I pressed too hard and cracked the mortar. The place where I changed my mind and laid blue over red. The corner where my apprentice (what was his name? Gone too) tried his hand and I let his imperfect circles stand because he was so pleased with them.

The caretaker's grandmother's grandmother tried to repair me once. I remember her kneeling with a pot of fresh mortar and a handful of tesserae she'd gathered from the drifts. She worked for an entire day, trying to rebuild the serpent's back. But the new mortar was wrong — too wet, or the wrong sand, or simply too new to grip stones that had learned different loyalties. Within a season the repairs had failed, the tesserae returning to their drift against the wall. She never tried again. Smart woman.

I am not bitter. How can I be? I have become something I never imagined. The pool I depicted is now real, fed by the vault's perpetual rain. The serpent I crafted in stone has been replaced by living snakes — small ones, true, that hunt the mice that hunt the crickets, but still: serpents in a garden, as I planned. The pattern is broken but the meaning persists, translated into breath and small heartbeats.

Sometimes, in the columns of light, I can almost see myself as I was: young, certain, laying each stone with the conviction that I was creating something important. I want to tell that version of myself: *you are, but not how you think*. The importance will not be in the pattern holding. The importance will be in how gracefully it lets go.

The caretaker sits on her bench, and I am aware of her in the way I am aware of everyone who has ever sat there — a long chain of sitters, most of them women, some of them trying to understand what the mosaic depicted, others past caring. She does not wonder about me. She has never tried to reconstruct the pattern from the scattered pieces. This is why I almost love her, in whatever way a dispersed intention can love. She sees what is, not what was meant to be.

When she dies, and when her niece takes over (or doesn't), and when the fig finally claims the whole east wall, and when the last tessera has been carried away by some future flood — I will still be here, in the mortar bed, in the memory of placement. I will be the reason why, centuries from now, someone descending those worn stairs will look at the pattern of standing water on the floor and think: *someone meant this*.

And they will be right, even if they are also wrong. Someone did mean this. I meant this. Not this exactly — not the ruin, not the rats, not the cricket-song. But this: a place where water gathers, where small lives flourish, where the light falls through.

Sometimes that's what craft is. Not the thing you make, but the place it makes for other things to happen.

The vault drips. The tessera settles. Somewhere in the walls, between the bricks I never laid, in the gaps I never planned for, my pattern continues its slow transformation into something better than I knew how to want.
