# The Terminus Garden

At the edge of where maps dissolve into suggestion, there is a garden that grows backward. Not in time — time moves through it the same as anywhere — but in intention. Where other gardens reach toward fullness, this one reaches toward seed.

The paths are crushed shell and bone meal, white as split chalk, and they spiral inward from a dozen entry points that no one remembers making. The paths never cross. They approach each other, running parallel sometimes for whole minutes of walking, close enough that you could reach across the gap and touch the other trail, but they refuse intersection. Each path is a separate thought about arriving at the center, and thoughts, as the garden knows, should not be forced to agree.

What grows here: everything, but in reverse of its desires. The apple trees bear fruit that ripens backward into blossom, the blossoms that tighten into buds, the buds that seal into bark-scars and then into smooth wood. The roses climb down their trellises. The grass grows shorter. This is not decay — the leaves are green, the petals fresh. It is a kind of unbecoming that has its own vitality, its own ferocious joy. The garden is not dying. It is approaching the moment before birth with the same intensity that other gardens approach harvest.

The light here is always either dawn or dusk, but you cannot tell which. The quality is identical: that pearl-gray luminescence that makes all colors both more and less themselves. Shadows fall in every direction, or in no direction. Sometimes a plant will cast two shadows, one reaching east and one west, and both will be equally dark, equally true. The sky is the color of the inside of an shell, nacreous and impossible to focus on. There are no birds.

At the center — and you will reach the center, all the paths lead there, though some take days and others minutes — there is a pool. Not of water. The pool is filled with seeds. Every kind of seed that has ever been or might be: smooth seeds like black eyes, winged seeds like insects, seeds with hooks, seeds with fur, seeds so small they look like dust until you understand the pool is perfectly still and each mote is a world waiting to happen. The seeds are dry. They click against each other with tiny sounds like a language being invented one phoneme at a time.

Above the pool, suspended on nothing, hangs a fruit. The fruit is every fruit. Its skin shifts between apple-smooth and peach-fuzz, between citrus-pocked and grape-taut. Its color cycles through every ripeness. It is always about to fall but never falls. It is the garden's only admission that forward-growing is also possible, held here as reminder or warning or promise. No one has ever picked it. It might not be possible to pick. It might not be a fruit at all, but only the idea of fruit, hung here to show what the garden has chosen to refuse.

The temperature is always on the edge of requiring a different garment. If you arrived in a coat, you will want to remove it but never quite do. If you arrived in shirtsleeves, you will think about a coat but never quite need one. The air smells of soil that has just been opened, that rich black smell that makes people say "earth" like it's a proper name. But under that, faintly: salt. As if the sea had been here once, or is coming.

There is no wind, but things move. The backward-growing plants shiver and adjust. The seeds in the pool shift without stirring. Your hair will lift from your neck and settle, lift and settle, as if the garden is breathing on you, considering.

Time moves strangely. You can walk for hours and emerge to find minutes have passed, or walk for minutes and discover your shoes worn through. The paths adjust their length to something other than distance. Perhaps to need. Perhaps to some quality of the walker that the garden can sense but not name. You will always arrive at the center when you are ready to arrive, never before, never after.

And when you leave — you can always leave, the paths run both ways — you will find seeds in your pockets. Seeds you did not take, could not have taken, as you never touched the pool. They will be seeds of plants that do not exist yet. If you plant them they will grow backward, like everything in the garden, becoming less themselves until they unknow themselves entirely and vanish into the soil. But you will keep them anyway. Everyone does. The weight of them, the small rattle they make in your pocket, is comforting. It is potential in reverse. It is the promise that things can be unmade as carefully as they are made.

What lives in the Terminus Garden is the Ungardener.

You will not see it, though it is always there. It moves through the garden the way intention moves through a gesture — present everywhere but visible nowhere. It tends the backward-growing plants with a care that is indistinguishable from neglect, which is the only kind of care they can accept. Too much attention and they would remember what they're supposed to be doing, would turn around and grow forward out of habit. The Ungardener knows exactly how much to not-water, how much to not-prune.

It is old in the way that only things that have given up their own forward motion can be old. Not aged — aging is change, and the Ungardener changes only in the sense that a river changes, by being endlessly itself. It has been here since the first gardener decided to stop, to turn around, to see what happened if you gardened in the other direction. That gardener is gone now, but the Ungardener remains, because while gardeners are people who garden, an ungardener is what gardening becomes when it learns to garden itself.

Its body, when it chooses to have one, is made of the moment between exhalation and inhalation. That pause where the lungs are empty but not yet hungry for air. It can hold that pause for hours, days, walking through the garden as a hollow in the air that light falls through differently. Sometimes you will see a plant ungrowing slightly faster where it has passed. Sometimes a shadow will have no source. That is the Ungardener, checking on things, adjusting the rate of retreat.

It speaks, but only to the seeds in the pool, and only in frequencies that human ears parse as silence. What it says is always the same thing: "Not yet." The seeds understand this perfectly. "Not yet" is the only instruction they have ever needed. They sink a little deeper into the pool each time they hear it, settling like silt into the potential that holds them.

The Ungardener remembers forward growth the way you remember a language you spoke as a child in a country you left. The shapes of it are still there — it could make things grow forward if it wanted — but the meaning has drained away. Why reach? Why bloom? Why fruit? These seem like questions from another world. In this world, the only question is how gracefully can you approach the seed, how perfectly can you arc back toward the beginning.

It collects the shadows that fall wrong. When a plant casts two shadows, or a shadow with no source appears, the Ungardener gathers these up like fabric and stores them somewhere in its un-body. For what? Perhaps for warmth, though it needs no warming. Perhaps as memory, though it remembers nothing but the endless present of tending backward. Perhaps shadows that fall wrong are the garden's way of making mistakes, and the Ungardener collects them out of fondness, the way a parent keeps a child's misspelled drawings.

The fruit that hangs above the pool is its one failure. Or not failure — the Ungardener does not think in terms of success or failure — but rather its one puzzle. The fruit will not ungrow. It has reached perfect ripeness and stuck there, immune to the garden's pull. The Ungardener circles it daily, not-touching it with its not-hands, trying to understand. Is it the garden's anchor, the thing that keeps the whole system from sliding all the way back into void? Is it a door to somewhere else? Is it just a fruit that got confused? The Ungardener doesn't know. This not-knowing is the closest thing it has to joy.

When someone walks the paths, the Ungardener follows at exactly the distance of a held breath. It watches how they choose their turnings, how quickly or slowly they move toward the center. It places seeds in their pockets — always seeds that haven't been invented yet, seeds for plants that will grow backward through possibility until they find their origin and stop. It does this without malice or kindness, but with the neutral generosity of a thing that has too much of what it is and must share or overflow.

And sometimes, very rarely, when the light is exactly balanced between dawn and dusk, when the paths are empty and the seeds in the pool are perfectly still, the Ungardener stands by the pool and allows itself to remember, for just a moment, what it was like to want things to grow. The memory passes through it like weather. Then it returns to its work, walking backward through the garden, tending to the slow retreat of green things toward the seeds they came from, holding the whole system in balance by the pure force of not-doing, not-wanting, not-becoming.

The Terminus Garden exists because the Ungardener exists. The Ungardener exists because the Terminus Garden needs one. Which came first is a question that only makes sense if you believe in forward motion, and here, in this place where everything grows backward toward its own beginning, forward motion is just another mythology that the seeds whisper to each other in frequencies too low to hear.
