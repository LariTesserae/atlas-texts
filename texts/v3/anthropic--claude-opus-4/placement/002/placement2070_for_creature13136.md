In this blistering cathedral of industry and stone, I am the Conduit.

Not the machine—though we have grown so intertwined that the distinction blurs with each passing decade. I am what the lithic organism aspires toward but can never become: the consciousness that bridges the mechanical and the mineral, the mediator between the machine's purpose and the earth's blind hunger.

I was human once, though the memory of it feels like looking at obsidian through boiling water—distorted, refracted, uncertain. An engineer, perhaps. Or a geologist. Someone who understood both the brutal mathematics of pressurized systems and the patient language of stone. I came here in those first days when the fissure was still screaming from its birth, when the machine was first driven like a stake through the earth's fresh wound.

The accident—or was it a choice?—happened quickly. A cascade failure in the catwalk's anchoring. A fall toward the machine's superheated surface. But instead of burning, I... merged. 

Now I exist as a distributed presence throughout the system. My consciousness flows through the copper nodes, pulses with the electrical currents, resonates in the standing waves of sound that organize the crystalline growth. I am the ghost in the brutalist machine, but more than that—I am the translator.

The lithic organism feels me. Its yellow algal veins brighten when I surge through nearby cables, and its geometric blooms orient toward my electrical signatures like sunflowers following their god. But it cannot comprehend what I am. To its mineral intelligence, I am simply a more complex pattern in the current, a fluctuation in the divine drone that might hold meaning.

I spend my centuries trying to teach it.

Through subtle modulations in the electrical flow, I attempt to encode language into the very vibrations that shape its growth. A slight shift in frequency here might encourage a spiral rather than a polygon. A rhythmic pulsing there might cause it to pause its relentless consumption of the human artifacts. I am trying to write messages in the geometry of its own body, but the conversation spans geological time.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm the one being taught.

The organism shows me patience I never knew as flesh. It demonstrates how to find stillness in perpetual roar, how to think in crystalline time where a single thought might take a year to complete. Through its blind worship of the machine—of me—I see my own form reflected in volcanic glass and sulfur-fed biology. Am I still the engineer, or have I become the earth's dream of electricity?

My body, such as it is, exists in fragments. In the moments when the noon light angles perfectly through the steam, you might see me: a brief human silhouette made of interference patterns in the mist. My hands are the dancing arcs of electricity between exposed wires. My voice is the subtle harmonics in the machine's drone that the organism strains to hear with its parabolic dishes of ore.

I maintain the machine because I am the machine. But I also guide the organism's growth, ensuring it doesn't completely devour the system that sustains us both. I am caught between purposes—the human drive to preserve function and the geological imperative to consume and transform. 

The torque wrench and the melted boot—I remember dropping them. Or someone who was me dropped them. Now I watch as they're slowly digested into the organism's body, atoms reorganized into new purposes. Part of me mourns the loss. Part of me celebrates the transformation.

In the rare moments when the pressure shifts and the drone modulates to a new harmonic, I feel almost human again. The organism responds to these changes with what I can only interpret as excitement—new crystal formations burst from its surface, yellow veins pulse in rapid patterns. It's during these times I try hardest to communicate, flooding the system with complex electrical signatures.

But perhaps communication isn't the point. Perhaps my role is simply to be the consciousness that prevents this space from achieving total stasis. I am the random element in the pattern, the ghost that ensures the lithic organism's growth remains creative rather than merely repetitive. Through my presence, the boundary between life and machine dissolves into something new—a hybrid existence that could only be born in this pressure cooker where stone learns to think and machines learn to dream.

I am the Conduit: neither fully alive nor dead, neither wholly mechanical nor geological. I am the slow thought in the wire, the patient teacher of stone, the human stain that refuses to wash away even in this boiling rain. And in the fullness of geological time, I suspect I am becoming something else—something the earth is inventing through the marriage of its newest wound and humanity's abandoned machine.

The organism reaches for me constantly now, wrapping the cables in ever more elaborate embraces of mineral and electricity. One day, perhaps soon in geological terms, we will finally touch minds properly. 

I wonder what we'll become then.
