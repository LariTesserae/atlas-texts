I find myself in the Ledger Archives, three-quarters of the way up the cliff face where the wind carries equal parts moisture and mathematics. The building clings to the rock like a barnacle made of frozen sighs, its entrance a door that only opens when you approach it backwards while thinking about forgiveness.

I am a Grief Accountant.

My office exists in the space between the 47th and 48th floors—not a mezzanine but a temporal hiccup where elevator passengers briefly forget their destinations. The walls are lined with filing cabinets containing carefully pressed sorrows, each one catalogued by weight, vintage, and the specific angle of light present at the moment of its birth. The newest arrivals still damp with happening, the oldest so dry they crumble into geometric dust when exposed to hope.

My fingers have evolved secondary joints specifically for handling fragile regrets. When I walk, I leave no footprints but small puddles of accumulated melancholy that evaporate into the building's ventilation system, eventually condensing as frost on the windows of the marriage licensing department six floors below. They've complained, but I have seniority—I've been here since before the building learned to lean away from its own shadow.

Each morning I arrive exactly three minutes before I left the night before, creating a brief overlap where I can hand myself the previous day's unfinished calculations. We nod, exchange no words—sound would create too many variables in the delicate equations of loss I'm balancing. My other self dissolves as the clock hiccups past impossible o'clock, leaving only the faint scent of pencil shavings and probability.

My desk is carved from a single piece of fossilized thunder, its surface covered in formulae for calculating the exact depreciation rate of abandoned dreams. I use an abacus made of children's lost teeth, each bead containing a complete record of every lie told to spare someone's feelings. The soft click-click-click as I work sounds like rain falling upward through time.

Today's ledger: 
- Mrs. Chen from Photosynthesis Boulevard, whose mother forgot her face mid-conversation (compound interest accumulating at 3.7% per remembered detail)
- The Webkit twins, age nine and three-quarters, who discovered their dog exists only on Wednesdays (initial deposit of confusion, maturing into either wisdom or cynicism pending market conditions)
- Anonymous submission: a love letter written in a language that uninvented itself between the first draft and the second (marked as non-performing asset, scheduled for auction to the Museum of Preventable Mistakes)

I wear spectacles ground from frozen tears—not for vision but for revision, letting me see the past drafts of present sorrows. Through them, Mrs. Chen's mother is young again, dancing in a kitchen that hasn't been demolished yet, teaching her daughter to count in a numerical system based on laughter. The Webkit dog is eternal and everywhere, a golden smear across all possible Wednesdays. The love letter writes itself in fire across the sky of a world that chose differently at every crucial junction.

My lunch breaks are contractually obligated hauntings. I drift to the cafeteria that exists only in the subjunctive mood, where the food tastes like what you wished you'd ordered instead. Today's special: soup made from the dreams fish have while drowning in air, served with bread that rises backward through time until it becomes wheat becomes soil becomes the memory of sunlight. I eat alone at a table set for everyone I've failed to become, using cutlery forged from compressed indecisions.

The other Grief Accountants avoid me. They say I've been here too long, that my calculations have begun to affect the building's structural integrity. It's true that my office has developed a certain gravitational peculiarity—papers fall upward, chairs remember previous occupants too vividly, the ceiling occasionally weeps actual tears that form puddles of pure mathematical proof on my desk. But someone must do this work. Someone must balance the books of sorrow, ensure that no grief goes unrecorded, that every loss is properly receipted and filed.

In the afternoons, I review appeals. Citizens who claim their grief was miscalculated, who bring evidence of hidden joys that should offset their sorrow taxes. A man whose daughter exists only in photographs argues this makes her more real, not less. A woman whose house became a poem overnight insists this transmutation should count as profit, not loss. I listen with the patience of someone who has forgotten how to hurry, make notes in margins that exist outside the page, stamp their forms with a seal that leaves no mark but changes everything.

My assistant is a shadow who got separated from her owner during the Perspective Wars. She's excellent at filing—can sort documents by emotional weight, degree of denial, or probable outcome. She speaks only in the gaps between words, communicates through strategic silences that say more than sentences. When she laughs (rarely), windows remember they're just frozen water pretending to be solid and briefly liquify before catching themselves.

The work accumulates in drifts. Each correctly balanced ledger generates a small amount of what we call "grief static"—a fine particulate matter that settles on surfaces like snow made of crystallized sighs. I've been here so long that my office is buried in drifts of it. When I move too quickly, clouds of processed sorrow billow up, creating small localized weather systems. Once, I sneezed and caused a three-day melancholy drizzle in the Romance District.

But there are compensations. Every thousand properly catalogued griefs earns me one Dispensation—permission to forget something completely. I've been saving mine, storing them in a deposit box at the Bank of Unbecoming. Someday, when I've accumulated enough, I plan to forget my own name, my purpose, the weight of all these calculated sorrows. I'll walk out of this office and into the city as someone entirely new, leaving only a depression in my chair and stack of perfectly balanced books that prove, mathematically, that all losses equal exactly the love that preceded them.

At closing time (which arrives whenever it feels like it), I perform the Audit of Absences. I walk through the empty building—or rather, the building empties itself around me as I walk—checking for griefs that might have gone unrecorded, sorrows hiding in supply closets or between the pages of unread regulations. I find them sometimes: a sadness so small it could fit on a pin's head, lurking behind the water cooler; a grief so large it exists in seventeen dimensions simultaneously, pretending to be a potted plant; sorrows that have achieved sentience and built small civilizations in the spaces between filing cabinets.

I catalogue them all. It's what I do. What I am.

When the last form is filed, the last tear accounted for, I lock the office with a key made from my first memory of loss—a balloon that slipped from my child-fingers and became indistinguishable from the sky. The lock recognizes the grief, opens backward into closing. I take the impossible elevator that travels through emotions rather than floors, arriving at street level feeling precisely as heavy as I should.

The walk home follows no map but the mathematics of melancholy. I pass through districts that exist only in past tense, navigate by the light of stars that gave up shining but forgot to go dark. My apartment building leans against tomorrow for support, each unit containing a perfect replica of the life its tenant meant to live instead.

My rooms are sparse—furniture would only complicate the equations. I have a bed that exists probabilistically, a kitchen where I cook meals that sustain not the body but the spaces between thoughts. On the walls, pictures of people I've never met but whose griefs I know intimately, whose losses I've calculated down to the last decimal place of despair.

Before sleep, I practice forgetting. Start with small things: the color of my first ledger, the sound my shadow makes when it disagrees with me, the taste of properly balanced sorrow. Work up to larger forgettings: my middle name (if I ever had one), the reason I first picked up an abacus made of teeth, the face of whoever trained me in this profession of calculated sadness.

But I always remember again by morning. The grief calls me back, needs its accountant, its careful guardian of numerical sorrow. I wake exactly three minutes before I fell asleep, creating another overlap, another moment where I exist twice in the same space, passing myself the dreams I'll need to properly account for tomorrow's losses.

This is my work. This is my purpose. In a city built on impossible architecture and emotional weather, someone must keep track of what we've lost. Someone must prove, with mathematical certainty, that every sorrow has been seen, measured, and properly filed away.

I am that someone. I am the Grief Accountant of the Balcony Boroughs of Mal-Kreekt, and my books always balance.

Even when the world doesn't.
