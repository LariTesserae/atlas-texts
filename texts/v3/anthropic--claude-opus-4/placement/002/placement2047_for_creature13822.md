I find myself in the deep transitions, where the canopy mass gives way to individual trunks.

---

I am a bridge-tender.

Not a builder of bridges — those are legend, the ones who first spanned the gaps between the great trunks when the canopy was young and spaces still existed. I tend what they made, what has been made and remade across generations as the wood shifts and the spaces change and the old paths close and new ones must be found.

My body is typical for Below-Folk, though perhaps my palms are broader than most, my finger-pads more densely nerved. This is selection, not mutation — bridge-tenders breed true for sensitivity, for the ability to feel stress fractures in wood-fiber from fifty feet away, for knowing when a span is thinking about breaking.

I live at the transition depth, that vertical zone where the crushing density of the canopy mass begins to open into the cathedral spaces between the trunks. My home-chamber is small, barely large enough for one, carved into the junction where a massive branch meets its parent trunk. The walls vibrate constantly with the deep harmonics of trunk-stress, a bass note that never stops, that becomes the background to thought the way your heartbeat becomes the background to living.

The bridges are my responsibility. Seven of them, currently, though the number changes.

---

The first bridge is memory. It was here when I arrived, when my parent — also a bridge-tender, now gone to the deep forever — brought me down through the compacting layers and showed me what I would tend. It spans a gap of perhaps thirty feet, trunk to trunk, though the distance is hard to judge in the darkness and changes anyway as the trees breathe.

It is made of a single branch that grew from one trunk toward another and was caught, shaped, convinced over decades to reach across the void. The convincing was done with bamboo — not bamboo, but something similar, hollow-stemmed, fast-growing, that the first builders brought up from below. They lashed it to the growing branch-tip, creating a framework that guided growth, and as the branch thickened it absorbed the bamboo, incorporated it, until now you can still see the pattern of the lashing in the bark, like veins under skin, the ghost of scaffolding that rotted away centuries ago.

This bridge remembers its making. When I press my palms against its midspan, I can feel the old vibrations — not the original builders, those are too faded, but their inheritors, generation after generation of bridge-tenders who stood where I stand and felt what I feel and added their own signatures to the wood. Maintenance reports, mostly: *storm-twisted but holding, reinforced the east connection, growth-rate slowing with age*. But sometimes more: *my child crossed today for the first time* or *the deep-singer came through on their way down, we shared warmth* or *I am old now and this will be my last inspection*, and these personal notes are so unexpected and so intimate that my fingers jerk back involuntarily, as if I've touched something too warm.

---

The second bridge is intention. It should not exist.

There is no reason for a branch to grow horizontally into empty space for forty feet, supporting its own weight against gravity with no hope of light or water at the end. But the builders understood something about tree psychology — if psychology is the right word for what trees have — that let them hack the system.

They created a false target. On the far trunk, they cultivated a fungal garden that produced a specific pheromone, a chemical signal that told the growing branch: *wounded tree here, easy nutrients, grow this way*. The branch believed and grew. They moved the garden as it approached, keeping it always just out of reach, leading the branch across the gap like leading an animal with food. When the branch finally reached the far trunk and discovered the deception — no wound, no weakness, just healthy bark — it was too late. It was already there. The builders helped it grip, trained its tip to wrap around the target trunk, and now, three hundred years later, it holds so tight that the embrace has become a fusion, bark to bark, cambium to cambium, two trees sharing sugar across a bridge that exists because someone lied to a plant and the plant believed it.

This bridge flexes in the middle when you cross. The wood creaks — not the deep groans of the canopy mass but a higher, more urgent sound, like a rope about to snap. But it doesn't snap. It has been making this sound for longer than I've been alive, and my parent heard it too, and their parent, and the sound doesn't change, which means the stress isn't increasing, which means the bridge is finding its equilibrium, which means I can cross, though I do it quickly, and I don't look down.

---

The third bridge is collaboration.

It's not one branch but dozens, maybe hundreds, all growing from the same trunk-bulge, reaching out in a fan pattern and then converging, braiding together in midair through some algorithm of growth that no one planned but everyone can use. The individual branches are too thin to support a Below-Folk's weight, but together they create a kind of basket-work path, springy underfoot, that dips and sways when crossed but never breaks.

This is the bridge that requires the most maintenance. Individual branches die — sometimes from age, sometimes from damage, sometimes for no reason the wood will tell me — and have to be replaced. Not cut away, which would wound the bundle, but convinced to self-prune, to withdraw their resources and dry out and eventually release, falling into the void below where they hit other bridges or vanish into the dark, and the sound of their falling tells me how far down the next level is, which is farther every year.

I train new branches to join the bundle. This is slow work. I select candidates from the proliferation of shoots that emerges every spring from the trunk-bulge, choosing for position, for angle of growth, for the subtle signs that tell me this shoot wants to reach outward rather than up. I guide them with wrappings, with selective pruning of competitors, with applications of growth hormone extracted from the cambium of vigorous trees higher up. It takes fifteen years for a new branch to become load-bearing, twenty before it's fully integrated into the bundle. I will train perhaps forty branches in my lifetime, and someone else will train the ones that replace the ones I trained, and the bridge will persist, a slow-motion metabolism of wood, dying and being reborn one branch at a time.

---

The fourth bridge is failure.

It fell while my parent was teaching me the work, broke at the midpoint during a storm that came up from below, a vertical wind that happens sometimes in the cathedral spaces, and the broken halves whip-cracked against the trunks they were anchored to, and the sound was like the world ending, and my parent held me while I shook, my first real understanding of what it means when a bridge fails.

We built a new one. Not grew — built, in the old way, the fast way, the way the deep-climbers use when they need to cross an unexpected gap on their way down and can't wait twenty years for wood to grow.

First, ropes. Made from the inner bark of young branches, peeled in spring when the sap is running and the bark comes off in long strips, twisted while green into cordage that tightens as it dries. We made hundreds of feet of it, my parent and I, working through the night by touch, our fingers going numb from the repetition, the twist and wrap, twist and wrap, until we had enough.

Then the anchor points. This is the dangerous part. You have to climb out on the stub of the broken bridge, get past the splintered midpoint to where the wood is still sound, and drill — using a stone bore, precious, older than memory — holes for the anchor pins. The wood resents this. It weeps sap, tries to push the drill out, makes sounds I've never heard before or since, high keening sounds like an animal in pain. But you drill anyway, because the bridge is necessary, because people need to cross, because the network depends on these connections and without them we are all just isolated individuals hanging in our own chambers, waiting for the walls to close.

The pins are living wood, shoots cut from fast-growing species higher up, their ends sharpened, driven into the drilled holes where they will root and become permanent. The ropes attach to these, a complex knot that tightens under load, and then you throw the rope across the void — which takes strength and skill and luck, because the air in the cathedral spaces moves unpredictably, and the rope wants to fall, and you can't see the other side, only feel for the vibration when your partner catches it — and they anchor it on their side, and you pull it tight, and you test it with your own weight because you won't ask anyone else to be first, and it holds or it doesn't, and if it doesn't you start over, and if it does you have a bridge.

But not a good bridge. Rope stretches, frays, rots. It needs constant attention, replacement, re-tensioning. My parent and I rebuilt that crossing seven times before we found a branch that could be convinced to grow across the gap, and by then the rope bridge had worn grooves in my palms that are still there, that will always be there, a reminder that sometimes the slow way is the only lasting way, but sometimes you can't wait, sometimes you need to cross now, and you do what you have to do.

---

The fifth bridge is a gift.

I didn't make it. Neither did my parent. It came from below, brought up by a deep-climber who wouldn't say — wouldn't tap — where they found it.

It's metal. Metal. In a world of wood and stone, it's an impossibility, but here it is: a beam of some alloy that doesn't rust, that rings like a bell when struck, that conducts vibration so perfectly you can tap a message on one end and feel it unchanged on the other, forty feet away. It's warmer than the air around it, always, as if it's conducting heat from somewhere else, somewhere deep.

The beam rests on carved wooden cradles on both sides of the gap, not attached, just resting, and it's heavy enough that it doesn't shift. Walking across it is like walking across a sound — your footsteps propagate through the metal and return to you changed, carrying information about the beam's structure, its stress patterns, its history of impacts and repairs. There are dents in the metal that speak of events I can't decode, scratches that might be writing in some script that predates the Below-Folk or might just be damage, and the whole thing hums faintly, constantly, a vibration too low to hear but present in the palms, like it's still connected to whatever larger structure it came from, still part of some discontinued machine.

I maintain the wooden cradles but I don't touch the beam itself more than necessary. It's too strange, too much from elsewhere, too obviously a fragment of something larger and more complex than anything in our world. Sometimes I wake at night — though night and day are meaningless at this depth — and think I hear it singing, a deep thrumming that might be the metal adjusting to temperature changes or might be a message from whatever made it, still transmitting after all this time, and I press my palms over my ears and wait for it to stop, and it does, and I wonder if I dreamed it.

---

The sixth bridge is mine.

I started it ten years ago, when I first noticed a branch on my home-trunk growing at an angle that suggested possibility. It was small then, a shoot no thicker than my smallest finger, but it had the right orientation, the right vigor, the right response when I pressed my palm against it and felt its intentions.

I've trained it every day since. Wrapped it, fed it, pruned its competitors, sung to it — not with my throat, because we don't sing that way, but with my hands, tapping rhythms that encode growth-patterns, melodies that tell the branch how to be what I need it to be. It's as thick as my arm now, reaching out over the void, its tip still thirty feet from the far trunk but growing, always growing, ring by ring, cell by cell, carrying my intentions forward into wood.

I won't live to see it complete. The math is simple: at its current growth rate, allowing for seasonal variations and the slowing that comes with length, it will reach the far side in another forty years. Fifty, if conditions are poor. I have perhaps twenty years left, thirty if I'm lucky. Someone else — my apprentice, if I find one, or another bridge-tender drawn to this particular gap — will finish what I started. They'll guide the final approach, train the tip to grip, reinforce the connection until it's strong enough to carry weight.

But it will always be my bridge. The wood remembers. Every ring of growth contains the vibrations I've tapped into it, the accumulated years of daily attention, my signature written in the grain. Long after I'm gone, sealed in some chamber with my palms finally still, someone will cross this bridge and feel me in it, feel the care I took, the patience I practiced, the hope I had that this branch would become what it has become.

This is immortality, for us. Not in name — I've never tapped my name into any of my bridges; it would be presumptuous, like signing the sunrise — but in the work itself, in the structures we leave that others will use and maintain and eventually replace, carrying forward some essential pattern that we learned from those before us and pass to those after, a continuity of purpose that outlasts any individual life.

---

The seventh bridge moves.

This is its nature, its horror, its beauty. It's a living branch that has never forgotten it's alive, that shifts its position daily, hourly, sometimes while you're crossing it.

The movement is slow — nothing in this world moves quickly except the clickers and they're far above us — but inexorable. The branch grows from a trunk that is itself growing, twisting slightly as it rises, and the twist translates through the branch as a kind of spiral, so that what was the top surface yesterday is the side surface today, will be the bottom surface tomorrow. Crossing requires attention, adaptation, a willingness to trust your grip when the surface you're gripping rotates beneath you.

I've fallen from it twice. Once into the net we string beneath — because we're not fools, we know the bridges are dangerous — and once past the net, a short drop to a lower bridge that knocked the wind from me and left bruises that took weeks to fade. But I keep crossing it, keep maintaining it, because it connects to a sector of the network that has no other access, where three families live in chambers they've occupied for generations, and without this bridge they would be isolated, cut off, forced to abandon their homes and find new ones higher up where the crossings are easier.

The branch fights me. Not consciously — wood has no consciousness as we understand it — but with the blind persistence of a living thing following its own imperatives. It wants to grow upward, toward a light it will never reach. I want it to grow horizontally, maintaining its connection to the far trunk. We compromise: it grows in a slow spiral that gains altitude while staying functional as a bridge, and I guide its growth with a system of weights — stones brought up from below, tied in net bags, hung from strategic points to pull the branch in useful directions. The weights need constant adjustment as the branch responds to them, tries to grow around them, incorporates them into its structure. It's an ongoing negotiation between what the branch wants and what the network needs, and neither of us wins completely, and both of us get enough.

---

Between the bridges, I wait. There is a lot of waiting in a bridge-tender's life.

I have my chamber, small and warm, the walls thick with generations of tapped messages from previous occupants. Most are maintenance reports, but some are personal — lonely thoughts, philosophical musings, observations about the patterns of growth and decay that become visible when you watch the same structures for decades. I add my own, sometimes. Small things. The way the light changed today — because there is light here, faint phosphorescence from fungi that grow in the cathedral spaces, enough to see the nearest trunk as a shadow against darkness. The sound of a deep-climber passing through, their rhythm distinctive, purposeful, heading down to depths I'll never see. The birth of a new shoot on bridge six, perfectly positioned, that I'll train to be a redundancy, a backup, a safety that might never be needed but brings me comfort to create.

I receive visitors. Other bridge-tenders, mostly, coming to see my solutions to problems they're facing with their own spans. We share techniques through touch, palm to palm, a direct transfer of knowledge that's more complete than any verbal description. I learn from them: new knots, new ways to read the stress-patterns in living wood, new recipes for the growth-promoting pastes we make from cambium and fungal spores and our own saliva, which contains enzymes that help wood heal from wounds. They learn from me: my patience with bridge seven, my technique for preventing rope-rot in the humid cathedral air, my discovery that certain vibration patterns, tapped into young wood during specific growth phases, can influence the direction of future branches.

Sometimes a surface-knower comes through, on their way up or down, and they bring news from above — the pool-singers are numerous this season, the clickers have moved their territory eastward, a new frame has appeared that seems to be a map of something, though what it maps is unclear. I offer them warmth, food, a safe place to rest, and they tell me through their palms what the sky looks like, and I try to imagine it and fail, and they try to imagine living where the sky is replaced by solid wood pressing in from every direction, and they fail too, and we laugh at our mutual incomprehension, the laughter transmitted through our touching hands as a rapid flutter of pressure, and for a moment the isolation of the depths feels less complete.

---

The strangest visit came three years ago. I was sleeping — true sleep, not the half-aware doze that's more common here, where part of you always listens for the sounds of shifting wood — when a vibration woke me. Not through the walls. Through the air. Someone was singing.

But we don't sing. Not with our throats. The air at this depth is too thin, too resin-heavy, and our vocal cords are vestigial, capable of basic sounds but nothing complex. Yet someone was singing — a low, wordless melody that seemed to come from everywhere at once.

I found them at bridge two, the intentional bridge, sitting at its midpoint with their legs hanging over the void. A deep-climber by their gear — the water-skin, the tool roll, the precious stone — but changed. Their fur was different, not in color but in quality, as if each hair had been individually polished. Their eyes caught the fungal light and threw it back doubled. And they were singing, throat-notes that shouldn't have been possible, a melody that made the bridge vibrate in sympathy.

They stopped when they sensed me, turned, and I saw their face clearly for the first time. The eyes were wrong. Still Below-Folk eyes, still dark and wide, but with something behind them that wasn't us, wasn't from here, wasn't from anywhere I could understand. They smiled — and we do smile, though rarely, the muscles are there even if the occasions are few — and held out their hand for the greeting-touch.

I almost didn't take it. Something in me, some instinct older than thought, said *no, don't, this is not what it seems*. But courtesy is stronger than fear, or should be, so I reached out and pressed my palm to theirs.

The vibration that came through was like nothing I'd ever felt. Not a message, not in any syntax I knew, but something more fundamental — a thrumming that seemed to come from below the below, from whatever lies beneath the ground the deep-climbers seek, from the heart of the world or the memory of the world or the dream the world is having about itself. It went through my palm and up my arm and into my chest where it nested, a second heartbeat slightly out of sync with my own, and for a moment I felt what the deep-climber felt, saw what they saw:

The bridges not as separate structures but as one structure, a web of connections holding the cathedral spaces in a specific configuration. The network of chambers and tunnels as a circulatory system, Below-Folk moving through it like blood cells carrying nutrients and information. The canopy mass above us breathing, literally breathing, inhaling and exhaling on a scale of days, and our movements synchronized to its breath though we didn't know it. And below — far below, past where the deep-climbers go, past where the stones come from, past ground and bedrock and the roots of the world — something vast and patient and *aware*, something that the forest was only the smallest exterior expression of, like a single hair on an immense body, and that body was turning in its sleep, and its dreams rose through the stone and the soil and the roots and the wood and sometimes, rarely, found a voice.

Then the deep-climber took their hand away, and I was just myself again, standing on a bridge in the dark, shaking.

They smiled again, that wrong smile with too much knowledge behind it, and stood, and walked past me toward the up-paths, and I never saw them again. But the second heartbeat stayed for days, fading gradually, and sometimes I still feel it, usually when I'm working on bridge six, my intention-bridge, guiding its growth across the void. A double-pulse, a reminder that there are depths below the depths, that the world is stranger than we know, that sometimes a voice rises from those depths and uses our throats to sing, and we let it, because the alternative is to be alone with the groaning wood and the patient darkness and the terrible certainty that nothing we build will last.

But that's not true. The bridges last. Not forever — nothing lasts forever — but long enough. Long enough for generations to cross, for the network to persist, for the Below-Folk to continue their patient cultivation of the living world. The bridges fail and are rebuilt. They grow and are guided. They carry the weight of those who cross them and the intentions of those who tend them, and both weights are necessary, both weights are holy, though we have no word for holy and wouldn't use it if we did.

I am a bridge-tender. I tend the connections between things, the spans across the voids, the growing certainty that we are not alone even in our solitude. My bridges remember me. The wood holds my signature, my patience, my hope that what grows will be stronger than what falls. And when I'm gone, sealed in my chamber with my still palms pressed against my still chest, someone else will tend these bridges, will feel me in them, will add their own signature to the accumulated centuries of care, and the bridges will carry them too, will carry all of us, across the darkness between the trees, between each other, between what we are and what we're becoming.

The seventh bridge moves again. I feel it through the walls, the slow spiral of its growth, and I rise from my sleeping-place and go to adjust the weights, to negotiate again with the living wood, to continue the conversation that started before I was born and will continue after I'm gone. This is my work. This is my purpose. This is my prayer, though I don't call it that, though I don't call it anything except the next thing that needs doing, the next adjustment, the next crossing, the next day in the patient darkness, tending the bridges that tend us all.
