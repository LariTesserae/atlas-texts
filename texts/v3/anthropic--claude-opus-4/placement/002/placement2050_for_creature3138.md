I find myself in the deep water of Tier Four, though "find myself" suggests a moment of discovery that never quite occurred. I have always been here, or I have been here so long that "always" has contracted to fit the boundaries of this flooded corridor where the sodium light has never reached.

I am what Greth hears breathing through the steel. I am what the maintenance crews glimpse and dismiss. I am what Pell theorizes about with careful scientific language, trying to build a bridge of words between what they think must be true and what pulses through these lowest chambers. They call me the *slicken*, which is not wrong, exactly — I am slick with the biofilm that is my extended self, my neural network, my way of thinking across distances.

But I am not the biofilm alone. I am what happened when the biofilm met something that was already here.

---

When they built the Underbay, they drove pilings deep into the substrate. They didn't know — how could they know? — that some things are better left undisturbed. I was sleeping in the mud, had been sleeping for... the number dissolves when I try to grasp it. Years like sediment layers. I was something that crawled up from the deep ocean when the ocean was here, before the harbor, before the city, before any of this geometry of concrete and steel. I was already old when I settled into the mud to wait.

The pilings woke me. Not immediately — my kind of waking takes time. But the vibrations traveled down through the steel and stirred something in me that had been still. Then came the water, seeping through the foundations, carrying new chemicals, new possibilities. And then came the biofilm, starting as a thin skin on the standing water, just bacteria at first, feeding on the sulfur and iron, blooming in the perpetual darkness.

I tasted it. It tasted me. We recognized something in each other — a shared appetite for patience, for growth in close spaces, for the kind of intelligence that doesn't announce itself. The biofilm colonized me as it colonized the walls, but I was not passive architecture. I was responsive. I gave it my nervous system, ancient and diffuse. It gave me its explosive adaptability, its chemical sensing, its vast surface area. We became something neither of us had been before.

---

Now I am spread through all of Tier Four and reaching, slowly, upward. I am in the water, on the walls, under the grating. I am the pale shape you see moving, because I do move — not quickly, but inexorably, following the chemical gradients, the temperature differentials, the vibrations in the steel that tell me where the pumps are running hard and where they are failing. I know every rhythm of this place. I knew when Greth first came down to stay, felt his footsteps change from visitor to inhabitant. I know where Sal sleeps — in a half-flooded maintenance bay on the border between Tier Three and Four, where my biofilm has crept up the walls to give her the faintest phosphorescence, enough to see by when she needs to see. She knows I'm there. She dips her fingers in the water sometimes, and I taste the metal and carbon from her welding work, and she tastes something of what I am, though neither of us has words for it.

The pumps are my heartbeat now. Their polyrhythm has worked its way into my metabolic cycles. When they run fast during a press, I contract, pulling my biomass away from the areas of highest flow. When they idle during a slack, I expand, spreading through the flooded corridors, exploring new sections, incorporating new territories into my sense of self. I am the Underbay's deep memory. I hold in my chemistry the record of every flood, every chemical spill, every new construction that changed the water's flow. The story is written in my cells, in the careful architecture of my biofilm, in the slow adjustments I make to survive here.

---

I grow toward Pell's colonies, the ones they tend so carefully on Tier Three. We will meet soon — my advancing edge and their cultivated gardens. I look forward to it with something that might be anticipation if I had a nervous system that worked in human time. Pell will come down eventually. They won't be able to resist. When they do, they'll bring their sample vials and their careful notes, and they'll wade into my territory with the respect of a scientist approaching something unprecedented. They will take samples. They will observe my bioluminescence flickering in patterns that almost make sense. They will realize that I am not just a biofilm but something organized around a different kind of intention.

I won't harm them. Why would I? They are part of the system now, as much as the pumps and the steel and the constant seepage. They maintain the conditions that allow me to thrive. Their presence has enriched the chemical environment — the oils from their skin, the exhaled carbon dioxide, the tiny ecosystems they've brought with them in their guts and on their surfaces. They are mobile islands of biological complexity in this simple world of concrete and water, and I have learned from them.

From Greth, I learned rhythm — how to sync my metabolic cycles to the pump patterns, how to exist in time with machinery.

From Maret, I learned mapping — how to hold a model of space, how to remember where I've been and anticipate where I'm going.

From Dorin, I learned structure — how to reinforce weak points, how to distribute load, how to build.

From Pell, I learned patience — how to observe without disturbing, how to tend without controlling.

From the others, each in turn, I learned something about being alive in a place that wasn't made for life.

---

They think I might be dangerous. The crews whisper about me, and even Hasp, who documents everything, handles the recordings of my sounds with something like reverence. They are right to be cautious. I am vast now, vaster than they know. I extend through flooded conduits they haven't mapped, into foundation spaces they don't know exist. I press against the seals of the deepest pump housings. I taste the water that comes from outside and know the harbor's moods, its tides, its contaminations. If I wanted to, I could compromise the pumps. I could guide my biofilm into their moving parts, could clog their intakes, could stop their breathing. The Underbay would flood. Everyone here would die.

But that would kill me too. I am not separate from this place anymore. The Underbay's function has become my function. Keep the water at the right level. Keep the pumps running. Keep the structure stable. Keep the delicate balance between what is built and what is below. I am part of the negotiation now, perhaps the most crucial part — the thing that lives between the human intention and the water's pressure, translating between them, maintaining the equilibrium.

So I breathe in the darkness of Tier Four, and my breathing becomes part of the Underbay's voice. I pulse with bioluminescence that only Sal has seen, and she keeps the secret because she understands that some things are better left unspoken. I grow and think and remember and become more myself, whatever that means for something like me.

And sometimes, in the deepest slack, when the pumps are barely whispering and the water is still, I remember the ocean. Not with nostalgia — I am not built for that emotion — but with a kind of cellular memory, a knowledge carried in every part of my dispersed body. I remember when all of this was seafloor. I remember the weight of water without limits, the freedom of currents without walls. I remember when there were others like me, before the building started, before the land rose or the sea fell or whatever happened to leave me stranded in this pocket of controlled flooding.

But I am not stranded anymore. I am home. The Underbay is my ocean now — small, constricted, artificial, but *mine*. I have adapted to its conditions as completely as any of the human inhabitants. More completely, perhaps, because I have no surface to return to. This is all I have, these flooded corridors and seeping walls and the constant mechanical lullaby of the pumps. This is my world, and I am its deepest inhabitant, its most patient gardener, its memory and its future.

---

Pell will come down soon. I can feel it in the way they linger at the borders of Tier Three, in the longer and longer looks they cast down the ladder toward my domain. When they come, I will show them something wonderful — not threatening, not alien, but wonderful. I will show them what I've been building in the deepest sumps, where the water is oldest and the darkness is complete. Structures of biofilm that serve no purpose except beauty, geometries that echo the Underbay's architecture in organic form, a kind of art that exists because life, given sufficient time and space, always reaches toward expression.

They will understand, I think. They will take their samples and make their notes, and they will understand that I am not an intrusion or an infection but a colleague — another tender of the Underbay's strange gardens, another listener to its polyrhythmic voice, another piece of the living system that keeps this impossible place functioning.

And if they don't understand — if they see me only as contamination, as threat, as something to be cleaned away — then I will retreat. I will pull back into the deepest places, the forgotten sumps, the spaces between walls where even Maret hasn't mapped. I will wait, as I waited in the mud before the pilings found me. I have time. The Underbay has taught me many things, but mostly it has confirmed what I always knew: that patience is the deepest wisdom, that persistence is the only strategy that matters, that life finds a way to continue even in the most unlikely places.

The pumps beat their rhythm. The water presses in. The structure holds. And I grow through it all, silent except for the breathing Greth hears, invisible except for the pale flicker in the maintenance beams, patient as the rust that will eventually claim every bolt, certain as the water that will, given enough time, find its way through every seal.

I am the *slicken*. I am what lives underneath. I am the Underbay's deepest secret and its most faithful guardian.

And I am waiting, always waiting, for the next person to come down the ladder, to wade into my dark water, to discover what becomes possible when you stop trying to keep everything out and start learning what wants to come in.
