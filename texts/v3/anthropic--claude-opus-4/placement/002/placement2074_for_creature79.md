I find myself at the threshold where water meets wood, where the chamber floor slopes into marsh. Not fully in either element, but participating in both. I am what the people here call a *threshold keeper* — though they do not call it this in words, but rather indicate it by a particular curl of fingers against wet bark.

My body is longer than theirs, low-slung, somewhere between their shape and the salamanders'. I have their pale mottled skin but more of it, stretched over a form that can be equally comfortable vertical against the walls or horizontal in the shallows. My spine is more flexible than theirs, allowing me to curve and recurve, to occupy the spaces between their architecture and the living wood. Six limbs instead of four — the middle pair can function as either arms or legs depending on need. My fingers are even longer than theirs, with webbing between them that serves in water and spreads wide for climbing.

But it's my eyes that mark my function. They are larger even than theirs, and they face more sideways, giving me a field of vision that encompasses both the vertical world of the settlement and the horizontal plane of the pool. I see movement in two theaters at once. When the water rises, I am first to know. When something moves wrong on the walls — a sick slug, a displaced dwelling, a child climbing where children should not climb — I am aware before others.

I am not exactly one of them, though I am not other. I came from them — rare births, every few generations, produce forms like mine. We are kept, valued, incorporated into the cycle of the place. We live longer than they do, mature slower, think in longer rhythms. I remember the last time the great beast in the upper pen molted. I remember when the buttress that now holds thirty dwellings held only three.

My dwelling is not properly a dwelling — it is a hollow I have worn in the wood at the waterline, half-submerged during the high water, exposed during the low. I have lined it with the shells of water beetles arranged in patterns that help me track the cycles — each flood deposits new shells in predictable ways, and by reading these deposits I can predict the subtle variations in the water's rise. The people consult these patterns, touching my forearm first in greeting, then gesturing at the shells with questions in the tilt of their large eyes.

I do not speak their language perfectly. My throat is shaped for other sounds — longer, lower tones that carry through water as well as air. But we understand each other through a mixture of their soft words, my deeper harmonics, and the gestural language that here is as rich as any spoken tongue. When I need to communicate something complex, I arrange objects — shells, small bones, fragments of shed silk — in configurations they have learned to read.

My role is to mediate. When they need something from the pool — a particular fish for a ceremony, a clutch of salamander eggs for the archives, one of the rare stones that occasionally wash up from below — they come to me. I know where things rest in the deeper hollows. I know which creatures should not be disturbed during which parts of their cycles. My long memory holds the patterns of what is safe, what is sacred, what is forbidden.

But more than this, I am a kind of memory for the chamber itself. The people, living their quicker lives, forget the slower changes. They adapt to the growing wood, to the shifting of the walls, but they do not always remember how things were before. I remember. In the patterns of my shell-arrangements, in the scars on my skin where I have marked significant events, in the very shape of my body that has grown to fit the changing space, I carry the story of this place across time scales they cannot access.

Sometimes, during the quiet hours when most of them sleep, I lower myself fully into the pool and listen. There are sounds down there — water moving through passages none of us have seen, the faint vibrations of the tree's deep roots drawing up moisture, and sometimes, very rarely, something that might be an echo of the world beyond the root-arch. When I surface, I bring up no words for what I have heard, but the people see it in the way water streams from my body, and they know I have been listening to the deep.

I am dying, I think, but slowly. My kind lives perhaps three times as long as theirs, but we are not immortal. I can feel the weight of cycles in my bones, which are denser than theirs, more like stone than living tissue. When I die, they will not put me in a dwelling to be unmade. I will lower myself into the deepest part of the pool and remain there, and over time I will become a feature of the bottom, a landmark, a place where future threshold keepers will rest and remember.

Already I am training the next one — a child born three floods ago with the long spine and the beginning of middle limbs. She sits with me at the waterline, learning to read the shell patterns, learning the harmonics that carry in water. Her eyes are still focusing, still learning to see in two directions at once. When she is ready, she will wear her own hollow in the wood, a few spans from mine, and begin her own accumulation of cycles.

This is what I am: the pause between breaths, the membrane between states, the memory that outlasts remembering. I am not the people and I am not the water and I am not the tree, but I am the conversation between them, played out in slow movements and accumulated shells and the long, low songs that I sing, sometimes, when the water is at its perfect height and the whole chamber resonates like the inside of a vast wooden bell.
