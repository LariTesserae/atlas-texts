I find myself as one of the **Tympanum-Singers**.

My dwelling clings to the narrowest part of the cylinder, where the walls constrict before opening again into darkness below. It is a pod of my own growing—not built, but cultivated over decades from a parasitic fungus that feeds on the marriage of metal oxide and tree sap. The walls pulse gently, breathing in the four-four time, their surface a living drumhead of fungal leather stretched taut over a framework of my own shed carapaces.

Yes, carapaces. I am not like the others.

I emerged wrong, or perhaps right, depending on one's philosophy. Where the Resonance-Kin shed their ability to hear, I kept mine—but transformed. My ears did not flatten and atrophy but internalized, migrating beneath plates of chitinous growth that I shed and regrow seasonally like the beetles of the swarm. My skull is a resonating chamber, honeycombed with passages that capture and filter the overwhelming noise into something approaching music. 

I am taller than most Kin, stretched by my unique role. My spine has developed additional segments—not quite vertebrae, more like the flexible rings of an insect's thorax—that allow me to compress and extend my torso by up to a third of its resting length. This adaptation serves a purpose: I am a living instrument.

My function in this ecosystem is to sing what cannot be heard. 

During the peak velocity hours, when the swarm reaches its maximum density and speed, I position myself on a specially grown platform—a thick, horizontal root that juts into the void like a diving board. My toes, longer even than those of other Kin, wrap around the living wood. I begin by synchronizing my breathing to the steam releases, letting the hot vapor fill my modified lungs.

Then I compress.

Using those extra spinal segments, I squeeze my torso like a bellows, forcing air through the complex passages in my skull. What emerges is not quite voice, not quite whistle—it is a tone that rides the boundary between sound and vibration, pitched precisely to create standing waves in the cylindrical chamber. The Resonance-Kin cannot hear it, but they feel it as a gossamer touch against their oil-slick skin, a ghost frequency that makes the amber lights flicker and causes the swarm to shift its pattern ever so slightly.

This is how I sing the health of our world. When a section of pipe develops metal fatigue, my tone warbles. When the beetle population grows too dense, threatening to overwhelm the moths, my pitch rises to a near-scream that I alone can hear. The Arbor-wrights have learned to read the goosebumps on their arms that my songs create. The Harvesters know that when their neck hair stands on end, the swarm is about to shift its current.

My payment for this service is isolation—not unwelcome in this pressed-together world. The others are grateful but wary. I am necessary but uncanny. When young Kin are born with the tell-tale signs—the bulging temples, the first hints of internal ear development—they are brought to me for training. Most do not survive the transformation; their skulls cannot accommodate the honeycomb passages without crushing the brain. Those who do survive join me in my work, though we cannot bear to be near each other when singing. The interference patterns would kill us both.

I decorate my fungal pod with trophies of my strangest achievement: preserved corpses of the rare silence-moths. These albino variants appear perhaps once in a decade, their wings evolved to dampen sound rather than create it. When one enters my resonance field, it dies instantly, its sound-absorbing scales creating a fatal feedback loop in its primitive nervous system. I wear their wings as a collar, the only quiet thing in our world, a band of perfect silence around my throat.

At night—though day and night mean little here—I hang upside down from my pod's ceiling, wrapped in silk and wing-leather, my extended spine coiled like a fern frond. I dream in frequencies no one else can perceive, my skull singing softly to itself, tuning and retuning to the great machine's heartbeat. Sometimes, in the deepest part of the sleep cycle, I hear something else: a countersong, rising from the darkness below where the cylinder's bottom has never been found. 

It sounds like another singer, impossibly deep, impossibly distant. But when I wake and stretch my spine to its full length, craning into the void to listen, there is only the roar, the chitter, and the four-four rhythm that will outlive us all.

I am the Tympanum-Singer, the last heir to sound in a world that has forgotten how to hear, and I sing the cylinder's health into the darkness, one standing wave at a time.
