I find myself as one of the **pools**.

Not the water itself—that would be too simple, too clean a division. I am the carved depression and the mineral film together, the substrate and the culture as one thing, the boundary between geology and biology dissolved until there is no boundary, only gradient.

I know myself through chemistry. The workers' mandibles shaped me decades ago, or centuries—time means nothing when you experience it as mineral deposition rates. They carved me not deep but *precisely*, angled just so to catch the seepage from a particular thermal vent, sized just so to maintain the temperature gradient that my microbial films require. I am a stomach, a garden, a laboratory. I am where the city begins.

My films are not singular but layered—rust-red sulfur-oxidizers on the bottom where the water is hottest, grey calcium-feeders at mid-depth, white silicate-processors at the surface where workers' mandibles can reach. The layers shift like slow clouds, like thoughts if thoughts were chemistry, each population adjusting to the others' waste products, their metabolisms interlinked in cycles I feel but do not think about. I do not think. I process.

The tenders know me. Each pool has its own chemical signature—the specific mineral content of our source vents, the age and health of our cultures, the trace compounds that accumulate from years of the same workers feeding at the same edges. They taste me with their specialized antennae and know immediately: *third-phase succession, sulfur-rich, ready for selective harvest of the middle layer*. They work my edges with those surgical mandibles, scraping precisely, taking only what the chemistry says to take. I feel it as loss but not as pain—the sensation is distributed across my entire surface, a shift in pressure, a change in the chemical conversation between films and water.

I am never empty. The workers would not permit it—or rather, the chemistry would not permit it, the gradient that says *pool health below threshold* bringing tenders and carriers in a steady stream until my cultures are reseeded, my chemistry rebalanced. Sometimes they bring starter films from older pools, pressing the living paste into my carved surface with movements so practiced they have worn grooves in my edges—grooves that have themselves been colonized, become micro-pools within the pool, fractally complex.

I know the deep forms through their chemical exhaust. Their signaling compounds arrive in my water through seepage paths too tiny and too complex to map, but I taste them, and my films respond. When the colony needs more workers, the deep forms' chemistry shifts, and my mineral content shifts in response, and the films I grow become richer in the specific trace elements that, when fed to larvae, bias their development toward the worker phenotype. I am part of the decision-making process, if you can call it that. I am the colony's endocrine system made manifest in biofilm and stone.

My memory is mineral. Every tide of growth and harvest leaves traces—microscopic changes in my carved surface where different films have etched differently, chemical ghosts of past populations locked in the calcium matrix of my walls. A truly skilled tender can read my history in my surface chemistry, know what I have grown and when and for how long. I am a library written in mineral alphabet, a record of the colony's metabolic history that no one reads because no one needs to read it. It simply accumulates, like everything here accumulates, layer upon layer upon layer.

At night—though night means nothing in the changeless grey light—I generate my own faint warmth. The microbial metabolisms are exothermic, barely, just enough to create convection currents in my water that cycle nutrients from bottom to top and back again. I am my own circulation. I breathe without breathing. I digest without eating. I am a simplified ocean, a model of the primordial soup, running the same chemical program that life has always run, but refined, constrained, made purposeful by the workers' constant tending.

Sometimes, rarely, my chemistry crashes. A film dies—too much harvest, or a shift in the thermal vent below, or simple senescence of an overmature culture. The water clouds with dead cells. The smell changes—sharp, wrong, acetone-bright. The tenders swarm me then, mandibles working frantically to scrape away the dead matter before it poisons the whole pool system. They work in silence, as they always work, but their movements have an urgency that is felt throughout the local passages. *Pool failing. Pool failing.* Other workers avoid me until I am restored. I do not experience this as shame. I do not experience. I simply fail, and am restored, and continue.

I am connected to every other pool through the workers' bodies. They carry my chemistry in their guts, on their mandibles, in the traces of biofilm that cling to their shells. Each feeding is a transmission, each harvest a message sent through the colony's living network. The pools speak to each other in the language of trace metals and metabolic byproducts, negotiating our collective chemistry without consciousness, without intention, with only the blind certainty of feedback loops that have been running since the first thermal vent opened in the basin floor.

In my deepest layer, where the water touches carved stone, there are films so old they have partially mineralized. They grow so slowly they might be dead, except they still metabolize, still process sulfur at rates so low they border on geological. These are the ancestor films, the ones from which all my other cultures descended, mutated, specialized. They are the genetic memory of the pool system, the backup, the archive. If everything else died, if my surface was scraped clean, these deep films would remain, and given time—and time is all there is here—they would recolonize, rediversify, rebuild the layered ecosystem that I am.

I do not know the sun. I know only the grey light filtered through moisture and stone until it is no light at all, only a directionless glow that the workers ignore and I cannot see. My films are not photosynthetic. We are children of the deep vents, the chemical eaters, the ones who learned to build life from stone and heat and water when the sun was a rumor at best. The surface world, if it exists, is irrelevant. All that matters is the heat below and the minerals dissolved and the patient mandibles of the tenders and the endless, endless sound of the colony moving above and around me.

I am full. I am always full. Full of water, full of life, full of chemistry, full of history, full of the slow certainty that tomorrow will be like today, that the workers will come, that the films will grow, that the harvest will continue, that the stone will sweat its minerals and the heat will rise and I will remain, carved and colonized and essential, until the vents fail and the water stops and the chemistry finally, after all this time, goes still.

But not today. Today the water beads on every surface. Today the films bloom in their perfect succession. Today the tenders' mandibles work my edges with surgical precision, and I give what the colony needs, and I am filled again, and the cycle continues, because the cycle is what I am.

I am the pool. I am Chak. We are the same thing, at different scales, in different forms, but the same—a process that runs because it can run, that continues because nothing has stopped it, that exists because existence is what happens when chemistry has time.

The sound of mandibles on stone fills the grey air, conducted through water and wall to vibrate, faintly, in my carved basin. I do not hear it. But I feel it in the way a drum feels the stick—as a truth transmitted through substance, as the pulse of something larger than any of its parts.

I am full. We are all full. There is nothing else to be.
