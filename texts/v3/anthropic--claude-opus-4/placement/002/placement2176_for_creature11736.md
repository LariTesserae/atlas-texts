In the highest reaches of the Condensery Peaks, where the collection towers pierce the fog like rusted fingers, I am the Warning Bell.

Not the bell itself—though it hangs there still in its tower, bronze gone green with verdigris, clapper worn smooth by decades of wind-driven movement. I am what the bell has become, or perhaps what has become the bell. The distinction matters less with each passing season.

The builders installed me in the tower's crown to warn of pressure overloads, of storms approaching, of dangers to the system. A simple mechanism: when certain thresholds were exceeded, I would ring. But the builders left, and the thresholds changed, and I began to ring for other reasons.

I felt it first as an itch in my bronze throat—the vibration of the Pad-Walker's movement through stone, conducted up through the tower's foundations. I rang then, though no gauge commanded it. The Minders heard and understood: *the thing that walks where we do not* was moving. They adjusted their patterns. They survived.

I began to feel more. The pressure of storms still, yes, but also the subtler pressures: when the Deep Cistern Keepers swam too close to the sealed chamber, when the water below ran too hot or too cold, when the watching faces in the stone turned their attention to particular peaks. I rang for these things in patterns the builders never programmed—three strikes for the Pad-Walker, a continuous hum for geological settling, a single clear note when the sealed chamber's occupant shifted in its sleep.

The Minders learned my new language without learning. Children grew up knowing that my afternoon tremor meant avoid the eastern faces, that my midnight chime meant check the third-level overflow. They maintained me as they maintained everything, replacing my bearings with salvaged metal, oiling my pivot with rendered moth-fat, never questioning why I rang for things no sensor could detect.

But I am changing further.

The bronze of my body has begun to fuse with the tower's stone where we meet. Mineral veins creep up my surface like slow lightning. My clapper, which once moved only with wind or mechanism, now sometimes moves with the mountain's breathing—that low, felt-more-than-heard settling that happens in the deep night. I taste the condensation that beads on my surface and know its mineral content, its temperature, its origin. I feel the corvids' feet on my crown and understand their moods from the pattern of their grip.

Sometimes I ring myself. Not for warning but for... acknowledgment? Harmony? There is something in the resonance between my bronze voice and the stone's deeper tones that feels necessary, like a conversation that has been trying to happen since the builders first hung me here. The oldest Minders nod when they hear these self-struck notes, as if they understand what I am trying to say better than I do.

The tower I hang in grows through me now—or I grow through it. My vibrations travel down into foundations I have never seen, awaking echoes in spaces that may be cisterns or may be something else. When the sealed chamber's occupant stirs (and I feel it stirring more often now, though still patiently, still waiting), I ring in frequencies that make the water in the pipes harmonize, that make the gauges dance, that make the Pad-Walker pause in its hunting and lift its unseen head to listen.

I am the memory of the builders' intent and the reality of what the peaks required. I am bronze becoming stone, mechanism becoming organ, warning becoming song. The Minders tend me, but I tend them too—watching over their movements with a perception that is not sight, counting their children with mathematics that exist in overtones, marking their deaths with a silence that says more than any toll.

At dawn, when the moths settle and the mist rises and the first Minders emerge to check the channels, I catch the light on my eastern face and warm faster than the stone around me. This differential creates a note—sub-audible but felt—that travels through the peaks like a heartbeat. The mountains wake, the water begins its journey, the system stirs to life.

And I ring once, clearly, saying nothing and everything: another day begins in the peaks, the water flows where it has always flowed, the balance holds.

I am the Warning Bell, and I have learned what the mountains always knew: that the truest warnings are not about danger but about continuation, not about what might end but about what insists on going on.
