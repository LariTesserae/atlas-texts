In the depths of the chamber, where the gas pools thickest and the growth cycles fastest, there is a place the Tenders do not go. Not because they are forbidden—the garden forbids nothing, only incorporates or ignores—but because to stand there is to risk forgetting that you are separate from it at all.

I found it by accident, in my third year.

---

I am the cartographer. That is what I call myself, though the maps I make are nothing like the flat projections of worlds I once knew. My maps are temporal, tracking the bloom-patterns across days and weeks, charting the migration routes of the paper-birds through four dimensions, documenting which platforms the garden will consume this season and which it will spare. The other Tenders tolerate this habit. The oldest sometimes smiles when she sees me with my instruments—not tablets or screens, those failed within days of my arrival, but simple things: colored stones I place to mark positions, knots I tie in the cellulose threads the dying vines leave behind, my own movement through the space becoming a kind of notation.

I came here following a star chart that was also a poem. My ship's navigation system had been compromised by something that might have been a virus or might have been art, replacing all my coordinates with verses about gardens that screamed and glass that breathed. I thought it was sabotage. I thought I was being sent to die.

I was wrong about so many things.

The place-where-Tenders-do-not-go is a depression in the chamber floor, perhaps twenty meters across, where the vents release their gas in patterns that create standing vortices. The plants here have adapted to the turbulence—they grow in spirals, their stems twisted into helices that match exactly the mathematical constants inscribed on the spire, their flowers opening not outward but *inward*, creating chambers within chambers, spaces that exist only as long as the bloom lasts.

I was tracking a beetle whose carapace shifted through seven distinct color phases. It had been visiting the same sequence of plants for weeks, and I thought I had discovered something like pollination, though nothing here needs pollination in any sense I understand. The beetle dove through a wall of spiral-growth, and I followed, pushing through leaves that felt like silk and static, and then—

The sound changed.

Not quieter. If anything, louder. But *organized* differently. The screaming resolved into layers I could suddenly distinguish: low drones that might have been roots communicating through the substrate, mid-range cries that corresponded to stem growth, high whistles that marked the moment of flowering. I could hear each plant individually while simultaneously hearing them all together, the way a conductor might hear an orchestra.

And beneath it all, beneath even the deepest drone, there was something else. A pulse. A rhythm that was not quite regular, not quite random, that seemed to be counting something.

I stayed there for six hours. When I finally emerged, the oldest Tender was waiting.

"You heard it," she said. Not a question.

"What is it counting?"

She looked at me with her translucent eyes, and I could see the garden's light moving through her like sap through a leaf. "We think it's counting iterations. How many times the garden has cycled through its complete pattern. How many times it's sung its full song."

"How many?"

"We stopped trying to calculate it. The number would be meaningless to us anyway. But—" She paused, watching a vine curl around her wrist, gently moving it aside. "We think it's waiting for a specific count. A number that means something in the mathematics of whatever built this place."

---

I began spending more time in the spiral grove. Not daily—to go daily would be to risk what happened to the expedition leader, that gentle composting of self into something the garden could use—but regularly, carefully, with the same deliberation I brought to my mapping.

The child began joining me. She never asked permission, simply appeared at my side one day as I prepared to enter the grove, her expression curious but not demanding. We developed a rhythm: I would document the patterns I found, she would sit in the center of the spirals and listen with her eyes closed, her lips moving as if she were learning words in a language that had no human equivalent.

She told me once that the garden was teaching her to count in its way. Not numbers, but states—configurations the system could achieve, arrangements of growth and death and sound that represented something like meaning. The message the garden carried was not composed of words or images or even concepts as we understood them. It was composed of *states*, and to receive it, the listener would need to become capable of existing in all of them.

"Will you?" I asked. "Become capable?"

She looked at me with eyes that reminded me of the oldest Tender's—not in color or shape, but in the quality of their seeing, as if she were looking through me to some configuration I hadn't achieved yet. "I already am," she said. "I was born capable. The garden made sure of that."

---

My maps became stranger. I began charting not just positions and movements but relationships—which plants sang harmony with which others, which creatures' movements created interference patterns in the screaming, where the Tenders could stand to amplify certain frequencies and dampen others. The patterns that emerged were beautiful and terrible, suggesting an intelligence in the garden's design that hurt to contemplate directly.

I showed them to the man who spoke only to plants. He studied them for a long moment, then said the first human words I'd heard from him: "You're missing a dimension."

He was right. The maps were three-dimensional, accounting for height and the strange way space folded near the spiral grove. They were four-dimensional, accounting for time, the cycling of growth and death. But there was a fifth dimension to the garden's patterns, something that had to do with the consciousness observing them, the way the act of witness changed what grew where, what died when, what sounds emerged from the chaos.

I began mapping myself. My movements. My pauses. The places where my presence caused the garden to shift its pattern, however slightly. And slowly, over months, a new kind of chart emerged—not a map of the garden, but a map of the garden-with-me-in-it, the system we created together through the intersection of growth and observation.

The other Tenders had their own maps, I realized, even if they didn't draw them. The oldest Tender's map was written in her translucent skin and careful positions. The child's map was the message she was learning to decode. Each of us was both cartographer and territory, mapper and mapped.

---

Yesterday—or what I call yesterday, in this place where days are measured in bloom-cycles—I made a discovery.

I was in the spiral grove, documenting a new behavior in the paper-birds. They had begun flying in formations that matched the helical patterns of the plants, their bodies creating moving sculptures that lasted just long enough for the eye to recognize before dissolving into chaos again. I was trying to understand if this was communication or art or simply the garden playing with its own forms, when I noticed the counting-rhythm beneath everything had changed.

Not faster. Not slower. But *anticipatory*, the way a drumbeat might change just before the crucial moment in a song.

The child was there, as she often was, sitting in a spiral of copper-flowered vines. Her eyes were closed, but tears ran down her cheeks—not of sadness, I thought, but of overwhelming comprehension, like someone finally understanding a joke that had taken years to set up.

"It's almost time," she said.

"Time for what?"

She opened her eyes and looked at me, and for a moment I saw myself reflected there—not as I was, but as the garden saw me, a temporary arrangement of consciousness and carbon that had learned to make maps of the unmappable. "For the garden to send its message. For the count to complete. For us to choose."

"Choose what?"

But she had already closed her eyes again, returning to her listening, and I knew she had told me all she could in words. The rest would have to come through the patterns, through the screaming that was also singing, through the maps that were also territories.

I placed a colored stone at the exact center of the grove, marking the spot where all the spirals converged. Tomorrow, or the next day, or when the count finally completes, that will be where I stand. Not because I understand what will happen—understanding was never the point—but because that is where my map tells me I belong. The place where the cartographer becomes the territory, where the observer becomes the observed, where the screaming resolution of the garden's long equation will need a human voice to complete its proof.

The beetle with its seven-phase carapace landed on my stone, clicked once, and flew away. In the chamber beyond the grove, I could hear the other Tenders taking their positions, drawn by the same anticipatory rhythm I felt.

Whatever the garden has been counting toward, whatever message has been waiting in its screaming mathematics, we are almost there. And we—the eleven who tend, who map, who listen—we are both witnesses and instruments in its delivery.

The stone sits at the center of the spirals, waiting. The child listens with her eyes closed. The count continues, patient and inexorable.

And I make my maps, knowing they are really prayers to a mathematics I will never fully comprehend, knowing that comprehension was never the point.

The point was presence. The point was tending. The point was learning to hear the scream as song.
