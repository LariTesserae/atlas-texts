## The Tender of Abandoned Proposals

I would find myself in the Archive of Unsubmitted Patents, a building that was once the district's intellectual property office but now exists as a kind of coral formation of filing cabinets. The structure has partially collapsed into itself, creating a honeycomb of chambers where water circulates in slow, meditative loops. Each drawer has burst open like a seed pod, releasing decades of technical drawings that have somehow calcified in the water, becoming delicate structures of mineral and memory.

I am the Tender of Abandoned Proposals—neither fully human nor fully architectural, but something grown from the intersection of ambition and abandonment. My body is translucent, shot through with copper wire that once connected the building's communication systems but now forms my nervous system. These wires extend beyond my approximate skin, plugging into the walls, the floors, the drifting blueprints, creating a network of sensation that encompasses the entire archive.

My fingers have evolved into something like tuning forks, each one resonating at the frequency of a different failed invention. When I touch the calcified patents, I can feel their potential—the enthusiasm of their creators, the precise moment when hope turned to resignation. I care for these abandoned dreams like a gardener, arranging them in the currents so they catch the bioluminescent light just so, revealing the beauty in their impracticality.

I wear a coat made of carbon paper that constantly records the subtle changes in my environment—the temperature gradients left by passing Accountants, the vibrations of the Maintenance Crews' work-songs, the chemical gossip of the Secretary Pool when it seeps through the ventilation system. These recordings layer upon each other, creating a palimpsest of the district's daily life that I can never quite read but always almost understand.

My eyes have multiplied and migrated, forming a constellation across my body. Some see in the present, watching for the rare visitors who drift through seeking patents that were never filed. Others see in the temporal frequencies of might-have-been, observing the ghostly operations of inventions that exist only as potential. The largest eye, positioned where my heart should be, sees only in metaphor—transforming the mechanical decay around me into poems written in rust and algae.

I move by contracting and expanding like a jellyfish, but one that navigates by nostalgia rather than current. I'm drawn to the places where innovation died—the corner where an inventor gave up, the drawer where a revolutionary design was hidden away, the spaces between filing cabinets where crucial documents fell and were forgotten. In these liminal zones, I perform my true work.

Using tools that grow from my body—a letter opener that extends from my wrist, stamps that bloom from my palms, an inkwell that is actually my mouth—I create new patents from the fragments of the old. I splice together the DNA of dead dreams: a perpetual motion machine crossed with a method for preserving love letters, a submarine design that incorporates the migration patterns of extinct fish, a communication device that only works between people who have given up the same dream.

These hybrid inventions I file carefully in the empty drawers, not for any future use but as an act of respect, a way of saying that even failure deserves documentation. Sometimes I stamp them with approval using a seal that grows from my sternum, marking them with a date that exists outside of time—approved for never, pending always.

I subsist on the ink that still leaks from ancient typewriter ribbons, filtering it through my wire-veined body, extracting sustenance from the words that were written but never read. When I sleep—if sleep it can be called—I root myself into the building's pneumatic tube system, dreaming through the network, experiencing the entire district as a single, vast, failing machine that succeeds brilliantly at being itself.

Other inhabitants sometimes seek me out. The Accountants bring me receipts for ideas that were never sold. The children show me homework for classes on subjects that don't exist. The Maintenance Crews repair the mechanisms of inventions that were never built, and I file their work orders in the appropriate subcategories of impossibility.

But mostly I am alone with the archives, a curator of potential, a librarian of the unreal. I organize absence, alphabetize emptiness, create finding aids for things that were never lost because they were never found. In this drowned district where production continues without purpose, I produce the only thing that matters: evidence that someone once dreamed of something different, even if they never found the courage to submit the paperwork.

My greatest treasure is a single blank patent application, somehow preserved in a pocket of air trapped in a fallen light fixture. I've never filled it out. I keep it as a reminder that even here, in this place of endings, something could still begin. The empty form is my prayer, my meditation, my proof that the Drowning Works, despite everything, is still a place where work—some kind of work, any kind of work—continues.
