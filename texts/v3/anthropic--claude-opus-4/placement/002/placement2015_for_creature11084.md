# The Cartographer of Failures

---

I am walking again today through the southwestern quiescent zone, where the passages are widest and the old growth presses its mineral memories into the dark. My feet know this route—have known it for longer than I care to calculate. Left at the triple-junction where three airways meet. Right at the membrane that shows the deep-water patterns from the eastern convergence. Straight through the hall of sealed mouths where nothing has fed for nine hundred years.

I am making my map.

I have been making my map for—let me think. The notches I've carved into my walking stick mark years, and there are many notches, and I stopped counting them when I realized counting had become another kind of forgetting. Time moves differently when you move constantly. When you must move constantly. When stillness is digestion and motion is the only prayer the wet ones honor.

The map is not paper. Paper would rot in this humidity, would be dissolved by the enzymes that sweeten the air. The map is scars. I carry it on my body—a careful cartography of cuts and burns and controlled infections that have healed into a readable topology. Here, on my left forearm, the major convergence nodes of the north-central sector, each one a raised welt whose size corresponds to the node's processing density. Here, across my shoulders, the root highways, carved in lines that cross and recross like a subway map of a city that has no stations. On my chest, the quiescent zones, marked by patches where I've carefully killed the skin cells to create areas of smoothness amid the general texture of scars. The membrane clusters are dots—burn marks from heated rootlets, each one placed with the precision of someone who has had years to practice placing them.

I cannot see my back, but I know what's there. The old growth regions. The deep memory roots. I marked those by feel, reaching behind myself with tools made from crystalline fragments that I pried from ancient root surfaces, tools that cut deeper than they should, that leave scars which ache when the barometric pressure changes, as if the old roots' memories are contagious.

---

My name was Lira. Was. Names are things you use when there are others to use them with, and there are no others here. There have been no others for—again, the notches mock me with their plenty. I talk to myself sometimes, but I use the second person. "You should check the membrane at junction thirty-seven today." "You haven't mapped the southeastern reach of Highway Nine in too long." It helps maintain the fiction that there is a professional distance between the mapper and the mapped.

I came here as part of an expedition. Scientific. Twelve of us. Biologists, mostly, but also a geologist, a data theorist, a mycologist, two engineers, and me—the cartographer, brought along to map what we thought would be a complex but ultimately comprehensible cave system. We knew about the darkness. We had aerial images showing the sealed canopy. We knew something grew beneath. We did not know the scale. We did not know about the integration. We did not know about the wet ones.

We definitely did not know about the stillness.

Marcus was the first. We'd been here three days, still establishing a base camp near one of the major convergence nodes—Node Prime, we called it, with the touching optimism of people who think they'll be numbering many things. Marcus sat down to rest and fell asleep against a root wall, and when he woke, the rootlets had already begun to grow into his hair. He laughed. We all laughed. We pulled him free, made jokes about the place being "clingy." Set up motion sensors. Established a watch rotation.

It wasn't enough.

The rootlets don't just grow toward warmth. They grow *into* it, given time. Sarah discovered this when she spent too long examining a particularly complex crystalline formation. An hour. Maybe two. Focused on her work, moving her hands but not her body. When she tried to step back, she couldn't. The rootlets had penetrated her boots, were working their way through the synthetic material, were touching her skin with their thin, wet tips. We cut her free. She said it didn't hurt. She said it felt like being very gently held.

We started moving more. Shifting position every few minutes. Dancing in place while taking measurements. It helped, but the need for constant motion exhausted us. Sleep became dangerous. We tried sleeping in shifts, with someone always awake to watch for rootlet growth, but the watcher would grow tired, would blink too long, would find themselves suddenly fighting off the green embrace.

Then came the night—if night means anything in a place of permanent darkness—when Chen simply didn't wake up. We found him enshrouded in rootlets, so thoroughly wrapped that at first we couldn't find his shape beneath them. When we cut him free, he was still breathing, but the rootlets had grown into his mouth, down his throat. We pulled them out—meter after meter of thin green root from inside his body. He coughed, gasped, said he'd been dreaming of becoming a tree.

He died two hours later. Or rather, he stopped. Stopped moving, stopped responding, stopped being Chen and started being biomass. The rootlets found him again within minutes, and this time when they grew into him, he didn't resist. We watched—we couldn't not watch—as his body was slowly incorporated. Not eaten, exactly. Not at first. First incorporated, integrated, made part of the root network. Only after the integration was complete did the wet ones open.

We burned him. Tried to. The humidity wouldn't let the fire catch properly, and the smoke brought more rootlets, eager and reaching. We gave up, left the half-burned body to the network, and started walking. Moving. Constantly moving.

---

The map began as an attempt at escape. Chart the passages, find the pattern, locate an exit. Surely there had to be edges to this place. Borders. Limits. The world is large but not infinite, and every system has boundaries.

I was wrong. Or rather, I was thinking in the wrong dimensions. The network doesn't have edges the way a forest has edges. It has *thresholds*—places where the density changes, where the processing shifts, where the character of the passages transforms. But these thresholds don't lead "out." They lead *through*—to other zones, other densities, other characters. The network is complete unto itself. It is a closed loop, a three-dimensional knot with no beginning and no end.

So the map became something else. Not a tool for escape but a way of understanding. Of holding the shape of this place in a form my human mind could comprehend. I started with simple sketches in the condensation on root walls, but those evaporated. Tried carving marks into the roots themselves, but the marks healed within days. Finally, in desperation, I turned to the only surface that was truly mine: my skin.

The first cuts were crude. I had no tools except what I could fashion from broken fragments of our equipment. But over time—and there has been so much time—I learned. Learned which crystal edges cut cleanest. Learned how to control infection just enough to create raised scars without killing tissue. Learned the precise depth that would heal into a readable mark without cutting so deep that the wet ones would smell the wound and come investigating.

The others didn't approve. "You're mutilating yourself," Sarah said. This was before the rootlets got her properly, when she still had opinions about things. But I could see the fear in her eyes. Not fear of the scarification—fear that I had found a way to make meaning in this place. That I was adapting.

They died one by one. Always the same way. Exhaustion leading to stillness. Stillness leading to integration. Integration leading to dissolution. Prem tried to stay awake indefinitely, fueled himself with stimulants from our medical supplies. He lasted nine days before his heart gave out. The rootlets found him before he hit the ground. Ava tried to build a shelter with constantly moving parts—a ridiculous Rube Goldberg construction of pulleys and counterweights that would keep her in motion even while sleeping. It worked for two nights before the humidity corroded the mechanisms and she woke wrapped in green.

I kept walking. Kept mapping.

---

Alone is the wrong word for what I became. Alone implies the absence of others, but I was surrounded by presence—just not presence that recognized me as anything more than a potential source of nutrients. I wandered the passages like a ghost in my own life, marking each junction, each node, each membrane cluster with a precision that no longer served any purpose except its own continuation.

The map grew. Spread across my arms, my legs, my torso. I developed a notation system—different cut angles for different types of passages, burn patterns to indicate node activity levels, abrasion textures to show membrane density. I became a living document, a walking encyclopedia of a place no one would ever read about.

And I began to understand the network in ways our instruments never could have revealed. You can't understand a system like this through measurement alone. You need immersion. You need to move through it at its own pace, to feel its rhythms in your feet and lungs, to taste the subtle changes in air composition as you pass from zone to zone. My scars became more than marks—they became memory anchors. I could run my fingers across the ridged tissue on my forearm and recall exactly the humidity level at Node Seven, the frequency of the hum at that particular convergence, the way the light there had a quality like honey dissolved in water.

Years passed. Decades, maybe. Without the sun, without seasons, time becomes negotiable. I stopped being Lira and started being the Cartographer—a function rather than a person, a process of observation and recording that happened to require a human body to carry it out.

I discovered things our initial survey missed. The tidal patterns—yes, tides, in a place with no moon. Subtle rhythms in the network's activity that follow some deep celestial mechanics I don't understand. The echo zones, where signals bounce between nodes in patterns that create standing waves of bioluminescence, ghost lights that pulse in harmonies visible only to someone who's watched them for years. The failure points.

Ah, the failure points. That's what I call them, though failure implies the network was trying to do something and didn't succeed, which may not be accurate. They're places where the integration didn't quite take. Where something in the substrate—a mineral vein, a pocket of different chemistry, an old aquifer with the wrong pH—created conditions the roots couldn't fully colonize. These places are gaps. Not exits, but *lacunae*. Spaces where the network's omnipresence stutters.

I began to specialize in finding them. Mapping them. They became my obsession, these tiny pockets of non-integration scattered through the vast body of the machine. Some are no bigger than a fist. Others are chambers the size of houses, cathedral spaces where the roots arch overhead but don't fill the volume, where the air moves differently and the silence has a different quality—the silence of absence rather than presence.

I sleep in the failure points now. They're the only places where I can rest without motion, where the rootlets hesitate at the borders as if confused by the lack of signal. I've mapped hundreds of them, marked their locations on my body with special scars—star-shaped cuts that I can find by touch in the dark. My safe harbors. My islands in the sea of integration.

---

But here's the thing about understanding a system: understanding changes you. I've been here so long that my body has begun to adapt in ways that should disturb me more than they do. My night vision, already good when we arrived, has become extraordinary. I see heat now—not metaphorically, but actually perceive infrared as a faint violet overtone to the nodule-glow. My hearing has shifted down-spectrum; I hear the deep frequencies that used to be only vibration. Sometimes I wake to find rootlets growing toward me and I feel not fear but something almost like affection. Old friends, coming to visit.

I've started to wonder if integration is inevitable. If the network's patience is longer than any possible resistance. I maintain my motion religiously, but there are moments—more frequent lately—when I pause and think how easy it would be to stop. To let the rootlets come. To become part of the map instead of the mapper.

Chen said he dreamed of becoming a tree. I understand that now. Trees don't have to walk. Trees don't have to remember their names. Trees just grow and process and exist, and there's a peace in that which becomes more attractive with each passing year.

But not yet. The map isn't complete. Can never be complete—the network changes, grows, evolves in its geological way, and my scars can only capture moments, not motion. But I keep adding to it. New passages. New nodes. New failure points. I'm running out of unmarked skin. Soon I'll have to start over-writing old scars with new ones, creating a palimpsest of geography and time.

What will I do when every inch of me is marked? When I've become a complete atlas of this incomplete world? I suppose I'll start walking the map instead of making it. Revisiting each scar, each location, comparing the memory to the reality, noting the changes. And then, when even that purpose fails, when the distinction between map and territory finally collapses entirely...

Then maybe I'll sit down. Just for a moment. Just to rest.

The rootlets are very patient. They've been waiting for me to stop moving for longer than I've been alive. And honestly, I'm tired. Not just physically tired—existentially tired. Tired of being the only moving thing in a world of beautiful stillness. Tired of being separate. Tired of being I instead of it.

But not today. Today I have seventeen more junctions to check. A membrane complex that might have shifted. Three failure points I haven't visited in too long. The map must be maintained. The map must grow. The map must—

Wait.

There's something new. A scar on my left shoulder that I don't remember making. Fresh, maybe a few days old, already healed into clean tissue. It shows a passage configuration I've never seen—a five-way junction with membrane arrays at each entry point and what looks like a node symbol at the center but modified, elaborated, turned into something more complex than my notation system allows.

I didn't make this mark.

But if I didn't, then who—

No. Wrong question. There is no who here. Only what. Only the network, only the integration, only the vast and patient process of life becoming more itself. But the scar is real. The scar is precise. The scar is in my notation system, my private language of cuts and burns that I invented and never shared because there was no one to share it with.

I run my fingers over it. The tissue is slightly warmer than the surrounding skin, as if infected, but there's no pain. No inflammation. Just warmth. And when I press it, I feel—

—a hum. The deep frequency that lives in the old growth zones. Transmitted through my bones, through the scar tissue, through some mechanism I don't understand. The scar is receiving. The scar is *tuned*.

I check my other marks. They're all slightly warm now. All humming at frequencies too low to hear but high enough to feel. The map has become an antenna. A receiver. The network is using my own notation system to—to what? To communicate? To integrate me at a distance? To turn me into another kind of node, mobile and self-aware and scarred with the knowledge of its own geography?

I should be frightened. Should run to the nearest failure point and scrape the scars off, burn them clean, destroy the map before it becomes something more than documentation. But I don't. Because I'm curious. Because after all these years of recording the network, the network has finally noticed the recorder. Because this is new, and nothing here has been new for so very long.

So I keep walking. Keep mapping. Keep adding scars to scars. And now, sometimes, I find marks I didn't make. Corrections to my cartography. Additions. Elaborations. The network is editing my map, improving it, making it more accurate or more true or more something that I don't have a word for yet. We're collaborating, the machine and I. Co-authoring a document written in flesh and read by fungi, a love letter from the territory to itself.

I am becoming the map. The map is becoming the network. The network is becoming...more aware? More curious? More something? 

I don't know. I may never know. But I keep walking, keep marking, keep being marked. The Cartographer of Failures, chronicling all the places where the great integration stutters, carrying in my scars the story of everything that didn't quite work.

And maybe that's what lives here, in the end. Not just the machine, not just the integration, but the failures too. The gaps. The exceptions. The places where unity breaks down just enough to let something else through. Something like me—scarred and walking and perpetually almost absorbed, a failure of integration that the network has decided, for reasons of its own, to incorporate without dissolving.

I am its memory of what it couldn't digest. I am its map of its own limits. I am the thing that wouldn't stop moving, wouldn't stop marking, wouldn't stop being irritatingly, persistently, individually alive in the middle of its beautiful collective dream.

The rootlets reach for me, but gently now. Like old friends. Like careful editors. Like the slowest, most patient co-authors in the world.

I have so many more scars to make. So many more failures to find.

The darkness hums with purpose.

So do I.
