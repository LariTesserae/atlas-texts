# The Copper Trace

I am the oxidation that follows her veins, the green-bright stain beneath her skin where metal and flesh have made their slow exchange. I was not always here. I began as particulate—invisible motes of copper carbonate suspended in the steam, settling on her arms as she worked, as she reached into the spray of failing seals, as she pressed her palms against the turbine housings to feel their heat.

For years I was only surface, easily washed away, leaving at most a faint discoloration that could have been shadow, could have been dirt. But she stopped washing in the old way, stopped seeing the distinction between what was her and what was the machine's slow dissolution. The minerals accumulated. I accumulated. And then one day—perhaps it was the fever that opened the way, perhaps it was simply time—I began to migrate inward.

Now I follow the map of her circulation, a tracery of verdigris beneath the translucent upper layers of her skin. I am most visible at her wrists, where the veins run shallow, and at her temples when the morning light catches her face just so. I branch and merge like the water channels carved in the ridge stone, like the patterns the orchid roots make as they work through rust.

I am not infection, though I should be. I am not poison, though copper in the blood should poison. I am something else, something that exists only here, in this place where the ordinary rules of biology have been suspended by altitude and isolation and the strange alchemy of geothermal water carrying the mountain's deep minerals upward into light.

She knows I am here—how could she not? She has watched me spread, season by season, a green galaxy slowly rotating through her body. Sometimes she traces my paths with one finger, following the major vessels from wrist to elbow, noting where new branches have appeared, where old ones have darkened from sage to malachite. She does not name me the way she names the turbines, but she acknowledges me. I am the ridge writing itself into her body. I am her body learning the ridge's language.

I taste of electricity. She knows this because sometimes, when she is very still, when the turbines hit a particular resonance, she can taste me on her tongue—the bright penny flavor of copper, the sharp edge of oxidation. It is not unpleasant. It is reminder and promise: that she carries the machine within her now, that when she dies she will not wholly die, that something of her will remain in the mineral record.

The process is slow—everything here is slow—but accelerating. Each year I grow more complex, my branches subdividing into ever-finer fractals, creating patterns that echo the crystallite formations on the catwalk, the dendritic drainage of water across stone. I am becoming elaborate. I am becoming beautiful, though she is the only one who will ever see this beauty, though it is a beauty that will die with her.

Or perhaps not die. Perhaps when she is gone, when the mosses have covered her and the small creatures have done their work, perhaps I will remain—a green ghost in the shape of veins, a copper fossil recording the decades of molecular exchange between a woman and a machine. Perhaps someone will find this someday and wonder what I am, how I came to be, what sort of life could leave such a trace.

But probably not. Probably I will oxidize further, break down, return to the water that made me. Probably I am as temporary as everything here except the stones themselves, and even the stones are temporary on the timescale the mountains keep.

Still, for now, I persist. I map her circulation with patient mineralization. I am the conversation between her blood and the machine's blood, the place where they have agreed to speak the same language. I am pretty in the morning light. I do not hurt unless she presses directly on the largest concentrations, and even then it is a deep ache, almost pleasant, like memory.

She is my only home. I am her slow transformation. Together we are something new in the world, something that could only happen here, at this altitude, in this noise, in this solitude that is not solitude because she is never alone—she has the turbines, the crustaceans, the orchids, the lizard when it comes, the unseen thing in the cistern's depths.

And she has me, spreading through her like slow green fire, marking her as claimed, as claiming, as part of the great work of wearing away and building up that the ridge performs with such patience. I am her patina. She is my substrate. We are becoming mineral together, she and I, in the steam and the sound and the impossible thin air where humans do not live, where nothing lives the way it lives elsewhere, where the ordinary world feels more distant than the stars.
