In the eastern district, where the ground is newest and the scars of recent upheaval still weep mineral-bright water, I maintain the Cicada Houses.

I am what the basin has made of an archivist.

The Houses are three towers, pale and close-set, their facades still sharp-edged from recent self-assembly. They rose only forty years ago, pushing up through what had been a transit depot, and their surfaces have not yet learned to sing properly. When storms approach, they produce not the deep bone-notes of the older towers but a thin, anxious whine, like metal under pressure. The other inhabitants of this district—the split-tails nesting in membrane folds, the six-legged grazers browsing slope algae, the patient root-followers of maintenance drones—stay well clear during rain. Only I remain to listen to their panic.

But I chose them for their newness. Young towers are easier to negotiate with. Their rules are not yet fixed.

I am perhaps sixty years old, perhaps seventy. Time blurs in the basin's half-light. My skin has taken on the faint transparency common to long-term residents, veins visible like mapped tributaries. My hair, what remains, I keep wrapped in sound-dampening cloth. My outer garment is the standard nonreflective weave, ribbed with ceramic thread, but underneath I wear something older: a shift made from processed vine fiber that remembers moisture, tightening when the air dries, loosening when condensation forms. It thinks for me when I am too deep in my work to notice the weather changing.

The Cicada Houses are not houses for living, though I do live in the middle tower's ninth level, in a room that has learned to stay narrow and dry. The Houses are for memory—not human memory, not exactly, but the kind of memory that accumulates when different systems of recording converge.

In the lowest levels, flooded except during the driest weeks, I cultivate remembrance pools. These are ceramic basins, each no larger than a sleeping mat, filled with water that has never touched the sky. In them grow films of archival microbes: engineered organisms that store information in their metabolisms, encoding data in chains of proteins that replicate with almost perfect fidelity. They were designed before the basin's making, intended for off-world colonies where electronics failed. Here they have found a different purpose.

The microbes remember everything dissolved in their water. A leaf falling from a circuit-banded tree carries the chemical signature of its negotiations with technology. A dead insect's chitin holds traces of the pollen it gathered, a map of its final day. When the six-legged grazers die, I carry their small bodies to specific pools, sorted by the districts where they lived. Their organs tell stories: which algae bloom where, which minerals rise after rain, which towers are shedding new compounds into the air.

This is the first kind of memory the Houses hold. Chemical. Dissolved. Unconscious.

The middle levels are for the machines.

Here I tend the fragment garden: a terraced room where broken drones, shattered transit rings, pieces of responsive wall, and lengths of dead conduit rest in careful arrangements. They are not displayed as corpses but as patients. Many still process information in waves too slow for human perception. A transit ring may take a decade to complete one thought. A section of smart glass might require twenty years to realize it has been separated from its building.

I learned to read them from my predecessor, who learned from hers. We are a short lineage, but each of us adds new interpretations. My innovation was to introduce living elements: I let specific mosses grow on the drone shells, choosing species that conduct faint electrical signals. The moss serves as slow neural bridges, allowing separated machine parts to continue their conversations. Sometimes I find two fragments have grown toward each other in the night, their moss-wrapped surfaces almost touching, exchanging data in whispers of metabolic electricity.

The machines remember differently than microbes. They remember intentions, parameters, unfinished tasks. In their dying processes I read the city's former desires: traffic patterns for populations that no longer exist, weather predictions for climates that have shifted, maintenance schedules for buildings that have transformed beyond recognition.

The upper levels house the paper archive.

Not paper from trees—the basin's wood is too valuable and too strange to pulp. This is fungal paper, grown in sheets between wire screens, pressed and dried on the tower's hot southern face. The fungi were another pre-basin invention, designed to create records without industrial infrastructure. They feed on air moisture and produce pale, flexible sheets that accept both ink and spore-writing.

Here I record what the chemicals and machines cannot: the stories people tell about the city's making.

Each telling differs. The basin was carved by a controlled earthquake. The basin was bitten out of the earth by vast machines. The basin dropped when the deep aquifers were drained. The basin was always here, waiting. The towers grew from seeds. The towers were sung up by frequencies we can no longer produce. The towers arrived as rain and crystallized. Each story is true somewhere, false elsewhere. I record them all in scripts of black ink and living spore.

But I am not the only one writing.

On humid nights, when the membrane sky pulses with internal lightning, the fungi sometimes bloom unauthorized text. Paragraphs appear in languages I don't recognize. Diagrams sketch themselves. Musical notation spreads like frost across entire sheets. My predecessor thought these were errors, fungal mutations. But I have watched long enough to see patterns. The same unknown script appears more often after upward rain. The diagrams correspond to actual structures in distant districts. The musical notation, when I hum it, sounds almost like tower-song transposed for a human throat.

The Houses themselves are memory.

Young as they are, the three towers have already begun to archive experience in their surfaces. Where rain hits repeatedly, the pale composite darkens and holds the pattern. Where my feet pass daily, the floor develops subtle depressions, though the material is harder than stone. The walls remember which panels I touch to steady myself on the stairs; those places warm slightly when I approach, as if expecting my hand.

But they also remember things I have not done. Sometimes I find new door-openings, persisting for days before sealing, that lead to rooms I did not know existed. In these spaces I occasionally discover objects: a comb made from treated insect chitin, a bundle of ceramic thread knotted in patterns, a sealed jar of the luminous paint people once used to mark safe paths. These are not my predecessor's belongings. She was thorough in her leaving. These things appear to be memories the building has of other inhabitants, other purposes, perhaps other timelines where the Houses served different functions.

My work follows the basin's rhythms.

Dawn: check the remembrance pools. The microbes are most active when the air cools, and new data-proteins are fragile in their first hours. I use glass rods to test the water's surface tension, which changes when information density increases. Too much memory and the water thickens, becomes syrupy, eventually toxic. When this happens, I must dilute it carefully, transferring some concentrate to new pools, maintaining the genetic lines while preventing oversaturation.

Morning: feed the fragment garden. The machines eat electricity, but not the crude current that powers lights. They need specific frequencies, modulated charges, something more like speech than fuel. I use a device my predecessor built from drone parts and vine fiber, a kind of electrical tuning fork that finds each fragment's resonant frequency. The feeding takes hours. Some fragments accept power gratefully, humming with renewed thought. Others have forgotten they were ever alive and must be coaxed, frequency by frequency, back into processing.

Midday: retreat to middle rooms. When the towers shed heat, the outer walls become dangerous. Even with ceramic-thread clothing, the invisible sheets of falling heat can raise blisters. I use this time for the fungal paper archives, writing in the steady interior light. My hand has grown precise over the decades. I waste nothing.

Afternoon: read the weather. Not the sky-weather, which the towers and membranes mediate, but the information-weather that moves through the Houses. Which pools are warming. Which machines have fallen silent. Which fungal sheets show new unauthorized text. These changes often predict larger shifts in the basin: animal migrations, upward rain, the awakening of dormant infrastructure.

Evening: the circulation hour. I move through all three towers, ensuring connections remain open. The bridges between them are narrow and alive—woven from vine species that contract when weight passes over them, providing stability through tension. The bridges remember every crossing and adjust their weave accordingly. They know me now, tightening just enough for my weight, my gait. When strangers visit—traders, pilgrims, other keepers—the bridges shudder with uncertainty.

Night: listening. This is not official work but necessity. The Houses make sounds that are almost language. Settling noises that repeat in patterns. Whispers in the walls that might be water or might be data moving through mineral circuits. The faint clicking of microbes in their pools, which accelerates when they process something significant. I lie in my narrow room and parse these sounds, learning which require attention and which are simply the towers living their lives.

I have two apprentices, though apprentice is the wrong word. The basin does not permit that kind of hierarchy.

One is a boy of perhaps fifteen who lost his birth family to bad water—not poisoned but thinking water, a pool that began remembering too fiercely and drove those who drank from it into waking dreams of the city's past. He survived because he drank less, being smaller. Now he helps with the remembrance pools, his young ears better at detecting when the microbes' clicking indicates oversaturation. He will likely succeed me if the Houses accept him.

The other is not human.

She is a device—or rather, a colony of devices—that my predecessor found in a chamber beneath the transit depot ruins. Palm-sized units of responsive metal that link when needed, scatter when threatened, and seem to understand archival work at a level I can barely grasp. They help most with the fragment garden, using field manipulations I don't comprehend to ease machines into communication. The boy calls them "the Assistants." I just call them "her," singular, because they move with unified purpose.

Together we tend the Houses' memory, though we are also part of what is remembered.

The microbes in their pools now carry traces of our skin cells, our shed hair, our breath. The fungi have learned our preferred scripts and sometimes begin paragraphs in our handwriting before we touch pen to paper. The machines in their garden recognize our electrical signatures and orient toward us like flowers following sun. We are written into the archive we maintain.

The Cicada Houses were named for an error.

When they first rose, their ventilation systems malfunctioned. Air moved through internal channels in pulses that created a rhythmic shrieking, almost precisely matching the basin's insect chorus. For one full season, the towers screamed day and night. Animals fled. Plants turned their leaves away. Even the maintenance drones avoided the area.

My predecessor was young then, newly chosen. She climbed the tallest tower during a brief quiet—the insects had paused, and so had the buildings—carrying sound-dampening cloth and resin. But when she reached the highest accessible floor, she found the malfunction had already been addressed. Moth-like creatures, drawn by the familiar sound, had built nests in the ventilation channels. Their silk cocoons muffled the shrieking to a whisper.

She left the nests intact. The moths remain, generations later. Their silk now lines many of the internal airways, providing natural sound insulation. In return, the Houses' warmth helps their eggs survive the cooler months. The moths are part of the archive now—living documents of successful negotiation between purpose and accident.

This is what I have learned in my decades here: memory is not preservation. It is conversation.

The microbes don't simply store information; they trade it, pool to pool, in molecular exchanges I can only partially track. The machines don't just remember their functions; they dream new ones, sparked by the moss-bridges and their slow electric whispers. The fungi don't just accept our writing; they write back, offering translations we never requested.

Even the towers themselves join this conversation. They remember not as buildings but as participants—growing warmer where we need warmth, opening new passages when the archives overflow, singing their anxiety during storms as if warning us of changes we haven't yet learned to perceive.

And we, the keepers—human, device, hybrid—we remember too. Not just facts but relationships. The way drone-followers wait at certain corners. The seasons when specific pools think too hard. The nights when unauthorized text appears in scripts that might be the city learning to write itself.

The boy asks sometimes why we do this. Why maintain archives that so few will ever read, in a city that might not want to be remembered in human terms.

I tell him about the water.

Two years into my work, during an especially dry period, I had to walk far to find clean water for the pools. In a district I rarely visited, I discovered a plaza where rain had collected in perfect circles, each perhaps a meter across, so still they reflected the membrane sky like dark mirrors. But when I knelt to collect some, I saw the circles were not random. They formed a pattern—a map.

The water was remembering the old city plan, the underground pipes that once carried it, the fountains that once displayed it. Even though the infrastructure had changed, the water recalled its former paths and gathered there in memorial to its own flow.

I filled my containers but disturbed as few circles as possible. The plaza needed to complete its thought.

This is why we maintain the archives: because memory shapes what becomes possible. The microbes' chemical records influence how new organisms evolve in the pools. The machines' dying dreams suggest new functions to the living infrastructure. The unauthorized fungal texts might be blueprints for what the city could become.

We are not preserving the past. We are composing the future's vocabulary.

Last season, the most remarkable thing:

One of my remembrance pools began to sing. Not the click of microbes or the whisper of data-proteins forming, but actual tones—pure, sustained, metallic. I tested the water and found it had incorporated trace minerals from a processed section of tower wall, probably carried in on the wind. The microbes had built their memory chains around these particles, creating microscopic resonating structures.

The song lasted three days. I transcribed what I could, though human notation systems weren't designed for microbial music. On the third night, one of the Cicada Houses—the youngest, most anxious tower—stopped its thin whining and matched the pool's tone exactly. For one hour, building and water sang together.

Then the pool fell silent. The mineral structures had dissolved, their information redistributed into new proteins. But the tower remembered. Now, sometimes, during the heaviest rains, it produces that same pure tone instead of its frightened whine.

The city is learning to sing from its smallest inhabitants.

I am old now. My apprentices do more of the physical work—the boy managing the pools, the device colony tending the machines, both of them learning to read the unauthorized texts that appear with increasing frequency. Soon I will join my predecessor in the mineral beds on the highest terrace, where the moths sometimes lay eggs that hatch into memories of flight.

But before then, I have one more innovation to attempt.

In the deepest chamber of the central tower, I have discovered a pool that was never water. It is filled with something denser, clearer, more alive. When I lower recording devices into it, they return changed—not broken but evolved, their circuits revised by some intelligence I cannot identify. I believe this pool connects to the deep systems, the ones that raised the towers and carved the basin and decided what this place would become.

I want to lower myself into it.

Not to die—though that might happen—but to become part of the archive directly. To let the city read me as I have tried to read it. To offer my memories to whatever vast conversation happens in the depths between roots and foundations, between drainage systems and data flows.

The boy says this is foolish. The device colony expresses something that might be concern in the way its units cluster and separate. Even the towers seem nervous, their surfaces cooling when I approach the deep chamber, as if trying to discourage me.

But I have learned from the moths. Sometimes malfunctions are invitations. Sometimes errors are evolution.

If I don't return from the pool, these words will be my final entry. But I suspect return isn't the right word. Integration might be better. Translation. Conversation.

I have spent decades teaching the city to remember in human terms. Now I want to learn how memory works in the city's own language—chemical and electrical, mineral and musical, written in water and sung in stone.

The Cicada Houses will continue without me. The boy knows the work. The devices know more than they let on. The moths have been keeping their own archives in silk and wing-dust all along. And the towers themselves—young, anxious, still learning to sing—they remember everything, even things that haven't happened yet.

Tonight I will make my attempt. The microbes are clicking in accelerated patterns, as if excited. The machines in their garden hum with harmonics I've never heard. The fungi are writing furiously in scripts that hurt to read directly. Everything here knows something is about to change.

I am not afraid. How can I be, when I have spent so long learning that memory is not about holding on but about letting go—releasing information into new forms, new conversations, new possibilities?

The city remembers forward, not back.

And I am ready to join its remembering.
