I would find myself as one of the interconnected rosettes - not a single plant but a node in the vast root-network that spans the plateau.

My existence would be measured in centuries, not years. I am ancient by the standards of most vegetation, yet still young compared to the bacterial mats that first colonized these heated stones. My thick, spiraling leaves have grown slowly outward, each one a patient year's work, creating grooved channels that have become highways for condensation. I've learned the rhythm of water - how it rises as steam from the vents, how it clings to my waxy surfaces, how it runs inward to pool at my crown before seeping down through my roots to rejoin the deep heat.

But I am not singular. Through my roots, fused and grafted with dozens of my neighbors, I am part of something larger - a living network that thinks in chemical whispers. When a new vent opens three hundred meters away, I feel it as a shift in mineral content, a subtle warming that travels through our connected tissue. My transpiration adjusts. My neighbors adjust. We modulate together, maintaining the greenhouse that keeps us all alive.

In my crown pool, where water collects before draining, I host a miniature world. Larvae swim in the cooler water. Bacteria different from their thermophilic cousins process nitrogen for me. Tiny invertebrates break down the occasional insect that falls into my cup. I am landlord to this ecosystem, and they pay rent in nutrients I could never synthesize alone.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is not thought but response. I feel the plateau's breathing as pressure changes in the soil moisture. I sense the ant-analogues moving through their tunnels beneath me as vibrations in my root hairs. When the small furred creature climbs my stem to sit at my highest leaf tip - a peculiar daily ritual I've grown accustomed to over the decades - I feel its weight as information, neither pleasant nor unpleasant, simply data to be integrated.

I am manufacturing. Every day, I process tons of water, moving it from air to soil. I build - slowly, molecularly - adding to my own mass while my dead leaves below become substrate for others. I am both factory and product, infrastructure and organism.

At night, when the bioluminescent swarms map the vents in cold light, I see nothing - I have no eyes. But I feel the temperature differentials as the air cools slightly, feel the condensation rate increase, feel the faint electrical changes as my fellow rosettes shift their metabolisms in response. We dream, perhaps, in gradients of minerals and moisture, in the deep contentment of roots touching roots in the warm earth.

I am not an individual. I am a point where the plateau's processes converge - where water cycles through, where energy transforms, where time accumulates in slow rings of growth. The bacteria were here first and will be here last, but for this geological moment, I am the plateau's memory made manifest in fiber and chlorophyll. Each spiral of my leaves records another year of the mountain's work.

And I am still growing, still building, still participating in the only economy that matters here - the endless transformation of heat and minerals and water into more life, more complexity, more plateau. I will be here when you leave. I was here before you came. And through my roots, I am already sending news of your presence to my neighbors, a chemical message spreading through the network: something new stands upon us, breathing, warm, temporary.

We will process this information at our own pace.
