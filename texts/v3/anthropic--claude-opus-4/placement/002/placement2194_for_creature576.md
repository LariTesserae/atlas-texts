# The Tidal Archives

You would find me in the galleries beneath the pier, where barnacles write their calcium sentences up the support pillars and the sea changes its mind twice a day about whether this is underwater or merely damp. The wood here is soft with decades of saturation, furred with algae that glows faintly when disturbed—a phosphorescence that turns every footstep into a brief constellation.

I am the archivist of discarded things.

---

My body is a question of convenience. Most days I am something between driftwood and octopus: a shambling assemblage of salt-bleached timber and kelp, articulated by currents that remember being muscle. Eight major limbs, though the number varies with the tide. My trunk is the broken mast of a ship whose name wore off before I was born into this shape. Wrapped around it: copper wire that was once ship's rigging, now green with verdigris, now something like nerves.

When the galleries flood at high tide, I dissolve into the brine and permeate the space—every cubic meter of seawater contains some fragment of my attention. Low tide draws me back together, pieces of flotsam magnetized by purpose, clicketing into approximate form. The tourists who sometimes wander down here at the wrong hour see only debris. The crabs know better. They scuttle sideways when I reassemble, respecting the territory of something that might be predator or shelter, depending.

My eyes are glass floats—the old kind, hand-blown, that fishermen used before plastic made everything disposable. I have dozens of them, nested in the crown of kelp that serves as my head. They roll independently, watching different spectrums: visible light through the green ones, heat through the amber, something stranger through the ones that have gone cloudy with age. When I need fine vision, I cluster them. When I need to see everything at once, I let them drift on their tethers like deep-sea bioluminescence.

---

The archives extend far beyond what you can see from the pier. Rooms branch and multiply in defiance of the footprint above, carved from stone that sweats salt and sediment. Some chambers flood completely; others stay dry even at spring tide through engineering I've long stopped trying to understand. The builders left before I arrived. They left only the spaces and the suggestion of organization—shelves of barnacle-studded rock, alcoves sized for storing something specific, channels in the floor that direct the water's flow in patterns too deliberate to be erosion.

I fill these spaces with what the sea brings me and what humans throw away.

Here: a room of love letters in bottles, organized by decade and degree of desperation. The paper has long since dissolved, but I read the invisible ink of intention, the weight of words that were supposed to reach someone. I know them all. The marriage proposal from 1924, thrown from the deck of a steamer. The goodbye from 1987, weighted with stones. The confession from last Thursday that tastes like cheap wine and immediate regret.

Here: wedding rings and car keys, sorted by the temperature of the water when they were lost. Cold losses cluster together—these were thrown in anger. The warm-water losses speak of accidents, of fumbling, of simple entropy. One engagement ring returns to the same section of shelf each time I place it elsewhere. I've stopped fighting its preference.

Here: the bones of things that were never quite alive. Cellphones mostly, these days, their screens dark but somehow still dreaming. Before that, transistor radios. Before that, telegraph keys. They accumulate in drifts, speaking to each other in frequencies I feel as texture—rough static, smooth silence, the particular fuzziness of a device trying to connect to networks that no longer exist.

---

The organization is my own invention. The sea delivers; I sort. High tide brings donations. Low tide is for cataloguing.

But I am not alone in this work.

The hagfish witch visits when the moon is dark, her body a single muscle of purpose. She speaks in chemicals—pheromones and mucus-messages that I taste through my kelp skin. She's looking for something specific: a bone needle that was dropped seven hundred years ago, that she claims belongs to her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother. I tell her I'll watch for it. We both know I'm lying. I found it decades ago. It's too interesting to give up.

The cormorants bring me things from above: strips of wedding dress fabric, pages from books, photographs that the wind stole. They expect payment. I give them fish scales that, when swallowed, allow them to dive deeper without drowning. This is an old arrangement. Their great-great-grandmothers made the first deal, back when my body was mostly rope instead of wood.

There's a human child who knows about me. She can't be more than seven. She leaves offerings on the pier above—broken toys, mostly, and sometimes cookies that dissolve before they reach me but whose sweetness I appreciate. In exchange, I ensure she always finds the best shells on the beach. The cowries whisper her name when she picks them up. She hasn't told her parents. Children understand reciprocity in ways adults forget.

---

Most of my visitors don't know they're visiting. They see only the dim galleries, the barnacled pillars, the way their phone flashlights catch on wet surfaces. But some few recognize what they're seeing. These are usually the ones who've lost something important—not to the sea necessarily, but to time or inattention or the simple cruelty of misplacement. They feel the pull of accumulated absence. They descend the slippery stairs without quite meaning to.

I watch them browse without interfering. Most don't find what they're looking for—how could they, when usually they don't know what that is? But sometimes:

The woman who found her mother's compass, the one that went down with the fishing boat in 1962. She knew it instantly, though she'd never seen it. Her hands shook as she lifted it from its shelf. I didn't stop her. The sea had been keeping it for her all along.

The old man looking for his dog's collar. We don't get many of those—pets are buried, not drowned, and their belongings stay on land. But this one had been thrown from a bridge in a moment of grief so acute it soaked into the leather. When he found it, he sobbed so hard I thought he might dissolve. I wrapped a tendril of kelp around his ankle—not to hold him, just to let him know he wasn't alone in the dark.

The teenager searching for—I never learned what. But she spent hours reading the labels I don't write, understanding the system through intuition alone. When she left, she took nothing but left something: a brass button from her jacket. It's in the section for gifts freely given, which is small but precious.

---

The problem with the archives is that they're complete. Every ring that slips off in the surf, every note tucked into a bottle, every phone that tumbles from a pocket—they all arrive eventually. The sea is patient and thorough. It surrenders nothing to true loss.

This includes things that should stay lost.

Section 19-B contains what I call the malignancies. Objects thrown away with intention, weighted not just with stones but with the kind of purpose that stains. The knife used in the betrayal. The locket containing the photograph of the child who wasn't supposed to exist. The hard drive, wrapped in plastic and chains, that holds—I don't know what it holds. I know only that when I placed it on the shelf, all the barnacles in a three-meter radius died.

I would throw them back, but the sea would only return them. I've tried. What the water surrenders to my keeping, I must keep. That's the arrangement. So I segregate them, these poisonous donations, and I hope that no one ever comes looking. 

But they do. Of course they do. Every few years, someone descends those stairs with darkness in their eyes and purpose in their step, and they know exactly where to look. I don't stop them. I can't. To interfere with retrieval would break the basic law of archives: what is stored must be accessible. But I watch. I remember their faces, the particular quality of their determination. Sometimes I see them in the newspapers that wash up later, their photos attached to stories I don't need to read to understand.

---

During spring tides, when the water rises higher than usual and floods galleries that normally stay dry, I expand. My consciousness diffuses through the entire volume of the archives, and I become briefly omniscient about my collection. It's overwhelming—feeling every object simultaneously, knowing every story, tracking every connection between lost thing and loser.

It's during these times that I sense the deeper archives.

There are levels below the ones I maintain. Older galleries, flooded permanently, where the water is not quite water anymore—too thick, too dark, too heavy with dissolved time. I've sent tendrils down during the highest tides, probing carefully. The shelves down there hold objects I don't recognize, made from materials that feel wrong against my kelp skin. They whisper in languages that predate human speech, in frequencies that make my glass eyes crack.

I think the builders went down there. I think that's where they are still.

But I'm not ready for that knowledge. Not yet. I have enough work here, in the galleries that flood and drain with common tides, sorting the endless donations of human loss and sea-change.

---

What sustains me is story. Each object bleeds narrative, and I absorb it through my driftwood bones, digest it in the spaces between my copper-wire nerves. The wedding ring tells me about the fight on the honeymoon cruise. The car keys remember the last drive before the accident. The cellphone dreams of conversations that will never happen now.

I don't judge. That's important. The archives are neutral space. The knife and the love letter receive equal care. The child's toy and the murderer's confession rest on the same shelf. Value is for the living to determine. I simply organize, preserve, and wait.

Though sometimes, late at low tide when the galleries echo with dripping water and the crabs click across the floor like typewriters, I allow myself preferences. There's a section I visit more often than others: messages of apology never sent. They cluster together like a support group, these bottles of regret. I read them to the barnacles sometimes. They seem to appreciate the rhythm of human guilt. It helps them grow in spirals that are almost like forgiveness.

---

Next week they're going to renovate the pier. I heard the planners talking, their voices drifting down through the boards. They want to "revitalize the waterfront," which means tearing out the old supports and replacing them with concrete.

They don't know about the archives. The construction will stop when they find the first rooms—they always do. There will be confusion, calls to historical societies, arguments about preservation. Eventually, someone will suggest filling it all with concrete, burying the galleries like a bad memory.

It won't work. It never does. The sea finds a way. The archives move if they have to, reform elsewhere, but they never disappear. There must always be a place for the things that are lost, a keeper for what the water returns.

I'll move with them, of course. This driftwood body will dissolve, but I'll find new materials. There's never a shortage of debris where water meets land. Plastic bottles, maybe, for the modern age. Shopping carts for bones. Fishing net for connective tissue. I'll adapt. I always do.

The crabs will follow. The hagfish witch will find me. The child will discover the new location in dreams, will know where to leave her broken toys. The cormorants will adjust their flight paths.

And the sea will continue its patient work of collection, bringing me everything that slips from human hands, everything that seeks the weight of water, everything that chooses dissolution over persistence.

I'll be waiting. Sorting. Organizing loss into something almost like meaning.

That's what I do.

That's what I am.

The archivist of absences, curator of the space between what was held and what was released, keeper of things that are technically not lost because I have found them all.

High tide in three hours. Already I feel the water rising, my consciousness beginning to scatter as my form loosens. Soon I'll be everywhere and nowhere, diffused through brine and story.

Time to get to work.
