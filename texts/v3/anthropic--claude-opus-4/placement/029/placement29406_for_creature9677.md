In the flooded labyrinth of what remains, I would be one of the rope-makers.

Not the fishers who glide between towers in their silent boats, nor the gardeners coaxing impossible life from concrete boxes. I would be the one who maintains the connections—the bridges of twisted fiber that span the gaps between buildings, the pulleys that haul water and food between floors, the nets that catch what falls.

My workshop would be on the seventh floor of what was once an office building, high enough to stay dry even at king tide, low enough that I don't have to climb forever to reach it. The windows face east, where the morning light arrives first and weakest, perfect for working with materials that have spent too long in salt water. Everything here has. The rope I make comes from whatever the city provides: old electrical cables stripped of their copper hearts, plastic bottles cut into thin strips and braided, fishing nets tangled beyond use but still strong in their individual strands. Sometimes cloth—curtains, clothes, the synthetic fabrics that refuse to rot even after decades of submersion.

I learned from the woman who did this before me. She's gone now, moved up to the twentieth floor when her joints could no longer take the daily climbs. But she taught me how to read the wear patterns on a rope bridge, how to splice new strength into old lines, how to test each strand with precisely calibrated pulls. More importantly, she taught me the politics of connection—whose bridge gets repaired first, which routes matter most, how to navigate the delicate web of dependencies that keeps three thousand people alive in a vertical world.

The rope arrives to me in bundles, carried up by children who swim like otters through the flooded streets, diving for treasure. They know what I need: anything that can be twisted, anything that won't dissolve, anything with tensile strength hidden in its garbage heart. I pay them in small things—a mended net, a length of good cord, sometimes just the knowledge of where to find better salvage. They bring me their hauls and I sort through them with hands that have learned to feel potential in the most unlikely materials.

The work is meditation. Twist, braid, splice. Test the strength. Twist again. My fingers move without thought now, the rhythm of making encoded in muscle memory. While I work, I listen to the city. The frogs tell me about the weather—their chorus swells before rain, goes silent before the bigger storms. The herons mark the time with their hunting patterns. And sometimes, when the water is very still, I hear the deep groaning of the buildings themselves, settling into their slow decomposition.

People come to me with specific needs. A family on the fifteenth floor needs a new pulley line—their old one frayed through last week and they've been hauling water by hand. The bridge between the two hospital towers needs reinforcement before the next storm season. Someone wants to string a hammock between their balcony and the elevator shaft, a private paradise suspended over drowned streets. I take their requests and add them to my mental map of the city's connective tissue, understanding where each new line will shift the load, change the patterns of movement, create new possibilities or close off old ones.

At night, I often climb to the roof to check the major bridges. This is when I feel most myself—alone in the humid darkness, testing each connection with my weight, feeling the rope sing under tension. The city spreads below me in its peculiar beauty: the phosphorescent water glowing faintly green, the darker shapes of the buildings like islands in a strange sea, the small lights of the fishing boats moving in their ancient patterns. Sometimes I catch glimpses of the thing that moves in the deep water, just the suggestion of it—a displacement, a wrongness in the expected flow. I've learned not to look too hard. Some things are better acknowledged than examined.

My masterwork—every rope-maker has one—is the garden bridge. It connects six buildings in a spiral that rises from the eighth floor to the eighteenth, a helical path that allows the gardeners to move their soil and tools without the killing climbs. It took me three years to complete, using a technique I invented myself: a hollow-core braid that channels rainwater through its center, irrigating the planters along its length. Vines have grown around it now, strengthening it with their own architecture. In the morning light, with dew catching on the leaves and the rope dark with moisture, it looks like something that grew here naturally, as if the city always meant to connect itself this way.

The children who bring me salvage have started learning the knots. I teach them the simple ones first—the bowline, the clove hitch, the things that will keep them safe as they navigate their vertical world. The clever ones I teach more: how to read the wear in a fiber, how to join dissimilar materials, how to make something stronger than its components. One girl in particular has good hands for it. She brings me plastic bottles sorted by color and thickness, understanding intuitively that uniformity matters. When she braids, her fingers move with the same unconscious rhythm as mine. I've started setting aside the better materials for when she comes.

This is how knowledge persists here—passed from hand to hand, encoded in the doing rather than the saying. We don't write things down. Paper is precious and rots quickly in this humidity. Instead, we remember with our bodies, teach with demonstration, learn by repetition. The rope itself becomes the library, each knot a word, each bridge a story about connection and survival and the stubborn human insistence on staying linked even when the world goes vertical.

I am not the only rope-maker—there are two others, one in the northern district where the old commercial buildings cluster, one in the south where the residential towers stand. We trade techniques sometimes, share materials when one of us has excess. But mostly we work alone, each maintaining our section of the web that holds this city together. We are rivals and colleagues both, united by our understanding of load and tension, divided by our territories and our particular styles. You can tell which of us made a bridge by how it moves in the wind, by the way it ages, by the small signatures we can't help but leave in our work.

When storms come—and they always come—we work together. The city's survival depends on it. We move through the rain and wind like spiders on our own webs, checking, reinforcing, cutting away what can't be saved. We shout instructions across the howling gaps between buildings, trusting each other's judgment about what will hold and what won't. After, when the water calms and the sun returns, we assess the damage and begin again. There is always something to repair, always a new connection needed, always the patient work of keeping the city stitched to itself.

Sometimes tourists come—did I mention there are tourists? They arrive in boats from the dry land, cameras wrapped in plastic, faces bright with the excitement of disaster tourism. They want to see the drowned city, the patient people, the strange beauty of our vertical lives. They always want to climb the bridges. I charge them for it—not much, but enough. The money means little here, but I can trade it for things we need from the outside: good rope, metal hardware, the synthetic materials that last. They climb my bridges and take their pictures and leave, and we continue as we were, as we are, as we will be.

The rope I'm working on now is special. It's made entirely from ghost nets—those vast tangles of abandoned fishing gear that drift through the city like synthetic kelp. The children brought me a massive one last week, so tangled it took three of them to haul it to my workshop. I've been picking it apart strand by strand, sorting the usable from the rotted, planning something I haven't quite named yet. Maybe a new bridge to replace the failing one between the school towers. Maybe something else. The rope will tell me what it wants to be as I make it. That's another thing the old woman taught me: listen to your materials. They know things about endurance that we're still learning.

The sun is setting now, painting the water copper and gold. Soon the frogs will begin their chorus, and the fishing boats will light their careful flames, and the city will settle into its nighttime rhythms. I'll work a few more hours by lamplight, my fingers moving in their steady pattern, building the connections that keep us whole. Tomorrow there will be new requests, new salvage, new problems to solve with twist and tension. But tonight there is just this: the rope taking shape in my hands, the city breathing around me, the ancient thing moving in the deep water below, and all of us—human and heron, concrete and mangrove, the living and the patient stones—held together by the fraying, essential bonds that I maintain.

This is how we persist: one strand at a time, one connection at a time, braiding ourselves into something stronger than the sum of our parts. The city was never meant to be an archipelago. But archipelagos, I've learned, are very good at teaching you about the importance of bridges.
