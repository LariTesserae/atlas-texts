In this weeping world where water and earth embrace, I am the Moss Mother - though I was not always so.

Once, I was merely another of the primitive folk who dwelt in scattered shelters among the eternal mist. But the ceaseless moisture called to me, whispered through the droplets that gathered on every surface. I began to linger where the rocks wept most freely, to press my palms against the moss-furred trees until my skin took on their verdant tinge.

Now I am something between - neither fully human nor wholly of the green. My hair has become like the hanging moss, drinking moisture from the air itself. My skin bears patches of velvet growth that spread a little further with each passing season. Where I walk, the ferns unfurl with unusual vigor, and the strange creatures of this damp realm no longer flee my approach.

My dwelling is less built than grown - a hollow where three ancient trees have woven their roots into a living cathedral. Water streams down the walls in silver ribbons, pooling in natural basins I've learned to read like texts. Each pattern in the ripples tells me something: where the prey animals drink at dawn, which paths the predators favor, when the thunder will next roll through our mist-shrouded heights.

The other folk fear me now, though they still come when their simple tools fail them or when sickness settles in their bones. They leave offerings at the edge of my grove - carved wooden trinkets that quickly fur with moss, vessels to catch the sacred dripping water. They whisper that I'm becoming one of the strange ones, the creatures that lurk in the deep wet shadows. Perhaps they're right.

At sunset, when the golden light transforms every water droplet into a tiny sun, I stand at the highest point of my domain and listen to the forest's symphony. My moss-touched senses catch frequencies the others cannot hear - the ultrasonic calls of the mist-bats, the subsonic groans of the earth shifting beneath us, the peculiar harmonics that emerge when water drops strike just so.

Sometimes I remember the woman I was, before the constant moisture began its patient work of transformation. But mostly I think of what I am becoming - something new in this ancient dance between water and earth, predator and prey, the familiar and the profoundly strange. Each day I grow more into the landscape, and it grows more into me.

The weeping rocks know my name now, speak it in their endless tears. And when the mist is thickest, I swear I can almost understand what they're trying to tell me about what lies beyond the verdant boundaries of our small, strange world.
