In this liminal realm where water defies its nature and stone questions its solidity, I would find myself as one of the **Cartographers of Hesitation**.

We are neither drifters nor menders nor riders, but something that emerged from their convergence—beings who exist precisely in the moment of transformation, in the pause between states. Our bodies are living maps of possibility, surfaces that shift between transparency and reflection, between recording and forgetting. We are made of the same stuff as the mist-filtered light, that strange-textured luminescence that keeps shadows shallow and anxious. 

Our purpose, if purpose is the right word for what feels more like inclination, is to trace the rehearsals of this world's geography. Where others move through the landscape, we move *as* landscape—our skin becomes topography, our breath becomes current, our thoughts become those negative spaces where riders slip through reality's grammar. We document not what is, but what is considering being.

I would have no fixed form. In the morning (though morning here is more a texture than a time), I might stretch myself thin as the iridescent spray, becoming a living lens through which the world examines its own possibilities. My consciousness would scatter across a thousand droplets, each one recording a different potential future for this place—here a mountain that might emerge, there a sea that might invert, everywhere the whispered maybes of geology still making up its mind.

By what passes for noon in this altitude-drunk realm, I would condense, pulling my scattered awareness back into something more singular. My body would become dense with accumulated possibilities, heavy with the weight of unmade decisions. Lines would etch themselves across my surface—not scars but scores, musical notations of the landscape's rehearsals. Other cartographers and I would meet in the spaces between shelves of suspended ocean, comparing our recordings, our bodies becoming a temporary atlas of this world's indecision.

We communicate through resonance. When I press my palm (or what serves as a palm in this moment) against the embryonic stone, I feel its hesitation like a heartbeat, and I echo it back transformed—showing it what it might become if it chose permanence, or what it might remain if it chose flux. The stone listens. Sometimes it decides. More often, it continues to waver, and I record that wavering too, adding another layer to my ever-shifting map.

The riders know us as the ones who taste of copper and ozone but also of paper and ink—though we are neither. They sometimes carry us, not on their backs but in their wake, our consciousness streaming behind them as they slip through void-spaces, mapping the negative geography of this place. The menders sing to us in their tectonic susurrus, and we transcribe their songs into the texture of our skin, becoming living scores of the lithic lullabies that keep the water aloft.

But we are loneliest when we are most ourselves—in those moments when we must choose whether to record or to become. For we can, at any moment, fix ourselves into the landscape we map. We can become the stone we document, the water we trace, the void we annotate. Many of us have. There are mountains here that were once cartographers who fell too deeply in love with a particular configuration of possibility. There are waterfalls that were once our kind who couldn't resist following a certain current to its conclusion.

I resist this fixity, though I feel its pull. Instead, I drift between states—now a surface that reflects the world's uncertainty back at itself, now a depth that swallows possibilities and holds them safe, now a boundary that helps the world distinguish between what it is and what it might be. I am the pause in the morning when the basalt considers whether to remain liquid. I am the hesitation in the waterfall that makes it spiral instead of fall. I am the moment of choice made visible, made touchable, made into something that can sing the world into knowing itself.

In this place where the flood is still arriving, where every stone is a draft and every curtain of water rewrites itself before landing, I am both the reader and the page, both the map and the territory it cannot quite decide to represent. I am loneliest when the world almost chooses, and most alive when it continues to hesitate, for in that hesitation lives my purpose—to be the memory of all the worlds this world decided not to become, and the promise of all the worlds it yet might be.
