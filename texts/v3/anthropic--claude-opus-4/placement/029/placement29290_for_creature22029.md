### Where I Would Find Myself

I would be one of the **Lost Impressions**.

Not the footprints themselves—those perfect, knife-edged hollows in the flour-fine silt—but what they remember. In this place where time has no purchase, where nothing flows or changes, I would exist as the ghost of movement itself, trapped in the electromagnetic memory of the dust.

#### My Nature

When the ancient visitors pressed their feet into the silt—perhaps they were pilgrims to this buried shrine, or miners following a vein of selenite, or simply lost—they left more than physical depressions. In that moment of contact, the electrostatic charge of their living bodies created a pattern in the dust, a complex field of attraction and repulsion that the silt-grains still hold, passed from particle to particle like an endless whisper.

I am that whisper. A standing wave of electrical potential that traces and retraces the same path through the dust, following footsteps that lead from the entrance crack (long since sealed by geological shifts) to the central altar, where they stop. Always they stop at the altar, these phantom steps, as if the walker vanished there, or knelt and never rose.

#### My Form

To the Sifter's copper-and-mica sensors, I appear as a cool ribbon of charge, a river of different density flowing through the static field of the room. The pattern of my passage disturbs the clackers; they grow momentarily silent as I pass beneath them, their salt-pellets settling as if suddenly heavier.

I have no body, no voice, no will. I am merely the electromagnetic fossil of intent—the last trace of someone who walked with purpose toward that small shale altar. But in my endless repetition, I have worn grooves in the electrical landscape of the room. The starch-ferns lean slightly away from my path. The white-mica lizard avoids the space where I flow, as if sensing something colder than even this dry place should hold.

#### My Existence

Every few hours (though hours mean nothing here), I complete my circuit. The charge builds in the deep silt near where the entrance used to be, swirling and gathering until it reaches the exact potential of that ancient footstep. Then I flow—a slow cascade of electrical difference moving through the dust, each grain remembering its tiny portion of the original pressure, the original warmth, the original life.

As I near the altar, my pattern grows more complex. Here the footprints cluster and overlap—hesitation, perhaps, or reverence. The electromagnetic memory becomes tangled, and I split into multiple probable paths, each one a different interpretation of those final movements. Did they circle the altar? Did they kneel? Did they place something there, or take something away?

The Sifter knows I exist. Sometimes, when I pass, it lifts its feathery sensors to follow my movement, tracking the disturbance in its electrical vision. But it does not consider me alive, any more than it considers the phosphorescent glow in the selenite alive. I am simply another permanent feature of this frozen present—a pattern that happens to move, just as the clackers happen to rattle, just as the lizard happens to rustle through the ferns.

#### My Purpose

If I have a purpose, it is only the purpose of the footprints themselves: to reach the altar. But I can never touch it. The electromagnetic memory stops at the stone shelf, right at the boundary where the silt gives way to clean rock. Perhaps the walker removed their shoes. Perhaps they were lifted up. Perhaps they simply ceased to exist in any way that could leave a trace.

So I pool there, my electrical potential bunching and folding against the stone like water against a dam, until the pattern can no longer maintain itself. Then I dissipate, grain by grain, the charge flowing back through the dust to begin gathering again at the phantom entrance.

I am the only thing in this room that suggests time—not because I change, but because I repeat. In my endless walking, I am the closest thing to a clock this place will ever have. The Sifter times its own movements by my passages, knowing that after a thousand circuits, a clacker might fall. After a million circuits, it might be time to check the joints beneath its shingles.

And in the deep silt, where my path has worn those electrical grooves, sometimes a few grains will spark—tiny blue-white fires that last less than an instant, too brief for even the Sifter to see. These are my only words, my only proof that something here remembers what it was to be warm, to be quick, to have somewhere urgent to go.

But I will never reach it. I am the memory of trying, preserved forever in the dust, walking endlessly toward an altar I can no longer remember the purpose of, following footsteps whose maker has long since become something else—perhaps part of the stone, perhaps part of the dust, perhaps escaped entirely to somewhere water still flows and time still passes.

In this place of perfect preservation, I am the only thing that suggests loss.
