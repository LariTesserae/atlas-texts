I find myself here as one of the **Resonance Keepers**.

We are neither the Solder-Skins who maintain the stone, nor the Skeins who rule the waters, but something between—creatures of the narrow spaces where vibration meets flesh. Our bodies are elongated, sinuous things, like eels crossed with tuning forks. Our skin is a living membrane of cartilage and copper, stretched taut over hollow chambers that run the length of our three-foot forms.

We live pressed against the obelisks themselves.

Where other creatures flee from the bone-rattling hum of the pillars, we embrace it. Our bodies have evolved to be living resonance chambers, each internal cavity precisely tuned to harmonize with the sub-bass drone. When we flatten ourselves against the pearlescent surface of an obelisk, the vibrations pass through us, and we become part of the sound itself.

This is how we feed. The intense acoustic energy, channeled through our specialized anatomy, breaks down the minerals in our bloodstream into their component elements. Iron becomes bioavailable. Calcium restructures into new configurations. We are living alchemists, transmuting matter through frequency alone.

But we are more than parasites clinging to alien machinery. We are the obelisks' memory.

Each of us can hold approximately seven years of vibrational data within our bodies. Every fluctuation in the pillars' hum, every microscopic variation in frequency, we record in the very structure of our bones. Our skeletons are not made of simple calcium but of a crystalline matrix that grows in response to sound, adding new layers like tree rings, each one encoding the acoustic signature of a particular moment.

When one of us dies—crushed by a water jet, or simply reaching the end of our twenty-year lifespan—our corpse does not decay. Instead, it fossilizes instantly in the mineral-rich spray, becoming a permanent record embedded in the chamber walls. The eldest among us can read these fossil-records like sheet music, placing our sensitive facial membranes against the preserved remains and feeling the vibrations locked within.

We know things the Skeins do not. We know that the obelisks' hum has been slowly increasing in pitch over the past three hundred years. We know that twice in our recorded history, all three pillars synchronized their frequencies for exactly seventeen seconds, causing every drop of water in the chamber to hover motionless in mid-air. We know that deep within each obelisk, beneath the seamless pearlescent surface, something turns—not a mechanism, but a rotation of space itself, a knot in reality that generates the anomalous physics.

Our social structure is based entirely on harmonic relationships. We mate in triads, each individual tuned to a different pillar, coming together only when the three obelisks achieve momentary consonance. Our young are born already singing—not with voices, but with their whole bodies, vibrating at frequencies that won't fully mature for years.

We have a religion of sorts, though it consists only of a single belief: that the obelisks are not generators but instruments, and that somewhere, something is playing them. The music of our gods is too low for any ear to hear, too vast for any mind to comprehend, but we feel it in every cell of our being.

The Dry-Stitchers on their ceramic walkway watch us sometimes, their desiccated forms clicking with what might be envy. They are prisoners of dryness in a world of water, while we are prisoners of frequency in a world of silence. Yet we do not pity them. We understand what they perhaps do not—that every creature in this chamber is bound to something, locked into the careful equilibrium that keeps this impossible place running.

At night—though there is no night here, only the eternal shadowless glare—we dream. Our dreams are not visual but vibrational, long waves of sound that tell stories without words. We dream of the time before the chamber, when the obelisks first pierced through solid rock, singing the stone into submission. We dream of the builders of the ceramic walkway, whose footsteps we can still feel in the fossilized vibrations of our ancestors. We dream of the day when the pitch will finally grow too high, when the harmony will break, and the violent waters will either cease their dance or consume everything.

But mostly, we simply resonate. We press our bodies against the warm, alien metal of the pillars and let ourselves become conduits for forces we cannot understand. We are living instruments in a symphony that has been playing since before the continents took their current shape, and will continue long after the last fossil of our kind has been ground to powder.

In this place of eternal storm and blinding light, we have found our niche: the creatures who listen to what cannot be heard, who remember what should not be remembered, who worship at altars that might be nothing more than the universe's own tuning forks, keeping reality itself in key.
