I find myself drawn to a forgotten corner of the plateau, where the schist has weathered into a natural bowl no wider than outstretched arms. Here, in this shallow depression that catches both morning fog and evening shadow, I am one of the Quiet-Guards' tools—though perhaps tool is too crude a word for what I've become.

I am a Listening Stone.

Not the great standing stone that marks noon in the plaza, but something humbler: a piece of native schist the size of a sleeping cat, worn smooth by countless seasons. My surface bears the faint turquoise script of slow lichen, patterns that took lifetimes to write. The Stone-Joiners selected me generations ago from a seam where the rock rang clear when struck, where the grain ran straight and true.

The Quiet-Guards placed me here at the intersection of three natural sound paths—channels in the rock that carry whispers from the settlement's edges. When someone approaches along the eastern mule path, their footfalls travel through the stone itself, up through my body as a subtle thrumming. A conversation in the northwestern firecourts arrives as barely-felt pulses. The drop-drip-drop of the fog nets creates a constant background rhythm, like a heartbeat made of water.

I am both instrument and guardian. The Quiet-Guards visit me at dawn and dusk, placing weathered palms on my surface to read what I've heard. Through me, they know if strangers have topped the ridge in the night, if the cisterns' rhythm has faltered, if someone has broken the rule of making themselves known. One guard—an old woman with lichen-pale hair—calls me "the settlement's ear," though I am more precisely its memory of sound.

The children know of me. During their slow decade of carving, some choose to work their hairline grooves nearby, and I feel their patient chisel-work as a steady percussion through the seasons. They tell me secrets in the gesture-language of their tools: *tap-tap* (I am afraid), *tap-hold-tap* (I am in love), *scratch-scratch-scratch* (I hate the taste of birch bread). I cannot answer, but I remember.

Ravens sometimes perch on me, their claws finding purchase in the shallow cups where rain pools. They leave offerings—snail shells, interesting pebbles, once a trader's dropped copper ring—as if they understand my purpose. The fog knows me too, condensing first on my night-cooled surface, writing temporary messages in moisture that the sun erases.

But mostly I listen to the vast quiet. I've learned its varieties: the expectant hush before dawn, the drowsy stillness of midday, the thoughtful pause of twilight. I know when the silence is healthy—full of small sounds that belong—and when it turns brittle with withheld words or approaching storm.

The Memory Goat visited once. I felt it as an absence of vibration, footsteps that somehow subtracted sound rather than adding it. The next morning, the Quiet-Guards found a single white hair caught in my lichen. They built a small cairn around me, not to hide me but to mark me as touched by the settlement's strangest guardian. Now I am both listening stone and shrine, accumulating meanings like the slow lichen accumulates its turquoise script.

In the deepest part of night, when even the fog nets pause between drops, I sometimes feel the whole plateau's breathing—the minute expansion and contraction of stone under starlight. The settlement sleeps above me, and I keep watch through the medium of stillness, a shaped piece of the ridge's bedrock that has learned, over lifetimes, to pay attention.

Tomorrow the Shadow-Readers will recalibrate their noon measurements. The Netwrights will repair the fog-catching linen. The Stone-Joiners will true another block. And I will remain here in my shallow bowl, listening to it all, remembering in the only way stone can: completely, silently, and for as long as the ridge itself endures.
