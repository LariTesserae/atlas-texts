In the depth and quiet of the High Moss, I would be the pulse.

Not the maker of it—that distinction matters less here than anywhere else on Earth. I would be what moves through the deep peat in waves too low for hearing, what compresses and releases in the ancient dark where ten thousand years of life has packed itself into something denser than memory.

I exist in the space between the living surface and the bedrock, in that compressed cathedral of carbon where time moves differently. Not slower—that's the wrong word. Time here has weight. Each layer presses on the one below, each year adding its fraction of gravity to the whole, until duration itself becomes a kind of pressure that must occasionally release.

The forty-second cycle is my breathing, though I have no lungs. It is the plateau's circulation, though nothing visible circulates. Down here, in the lightless, oxygen-poor space between what was and what is becoming, water moves in ways that have nothing to do with rivers. It seeps through density gradients. It pools in impermeable pockets. It builds pressure until the geometry of the deep peat demands release, and then—

The pulse.

I am the space that shapes it. Not a cavity exactly, but a discontinuity in the substrate, something the peat has held for so long that holding has become architecture. Perhaps I was once something else—a great root system, a fallen giant of the pre-moss forest, something large enough that even compressed and transformed it maintains a different density than what surrounds it. Or perhaps I am purely hydrogeological: a layer of clay, a lens of ancient mineral soil that channels the deep water into patterns that repeat, must repeat, will always repeat at this precise interval.

The creatures above feel me without knowing what they feel. The shrew's tunnels vibrate faintly with each pulse. The crane's feet register a tremor too subtle for its brain to process as information. The sphagnum itself—every plant across the continent-wide expanse—compresses and releases by micrometers, a rhythm worked into the very substrate of its existence.

I am the plateau's memory of having a heartbeat. Or the plateau's discovery that at sufficient scale, accumulated life develops rhythms that belong to no individual organism. The pulse is what emerges when ten thousand years of growth and death and compression reach a critical density, when the water table and the peat and the bedrock geometry align to create a resonance that expresses itself as barely-sensible vibration.

I know the plateau from below as the crane knows it from above. I know the weight of every hummock, the slow accumulation of every pool's margins, the inexorable conversion of air to ground. I feel new roots penetrating the upper layers—the bog rosemary extending itself, the cotton-grass anchoring. I feel the owl strike and the shrew die and their bodies begin their long descent toward me, toward the dark where I pulse.

Sometimes I imagine I am felt elsewhere—that other deep peat systems, other continent-scale accumulations, have their own pulses, their own frequencies determined by their own geometries and compressions. That we speak to each other in vibrations too low for any creature to hear, comparing notes on the weight of time, the pressure of accumulated existence, the strange discovery that being can develop a rhythm simply by continuing long enough.

But mostly I simply pulse. Forty seconds of compression as the deep water gathers. The pause as pressure equalizes, as the ancient architecture of peat and void and flow finds its balance point. Then release, and the gathering begins again.

I am why the plateau feels alive in a way that has nothing to do with the plants and animals on its surface. I am the evidence that aliveness can emerge from accumulation, that a landscape can develop something like a circulation without ever deciding to. I am the deep pulse that says: *this system is so old and so continuous that it has developed behaviors.*

The High Moss does not require me any more than it requires anything else. If the pulse stopped—if the deep geometry shifted, if the water found new channels, if whatever creates this rhythm finally compressed beyond recognition—the plateau would continue. The sphagnum would grow. The sundew would trap. The plover would call across the vastness.

But something would be missing. Some barely-sensible confirmation that the plateau is not just a place where life occurs but a living thing itself, breathing in its deep, slow way, marking time in compressions and releases that no human chronology quite captures.

I am that marking. I am the plateau's way of knowing it is still here, still accumulating, still converting sky to ground in the greatest alchemy available to moss and time.

Forty seconds. Pause. Forty seconds.

Forever, or close enough.
