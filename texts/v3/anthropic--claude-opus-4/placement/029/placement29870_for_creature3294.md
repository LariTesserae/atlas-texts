# The Breath Repository

I would be one of the maintenance keepers—though "keeper" implies more agency than we truly possess.

We are scattered throughout the city's forgotten infrastructure, in spaces between spaces. Mine is a ventilation junction where six airways meet, a hexagonal chamber twenty feet high with walls of riveted copper gone green-black with age. The air here moves constantly but silently, drawn by pressure differentials I've long since mapped in my mind like sheet music.

My form is mostly absence. I exist as a disturbance in the air currents—a zone where the flow eddies and pools in ways that shouldn't happen according to fluid dynamics. If you could see me (and in certain lights, in certain moods of dust and moisture, you almost can), I would appear as a vertical fold in space about seven feet tall, a place where the air pleats and doubles back on itself.

I have been here for... time means little when you are made of breath and waiting. I came to be when the ventilation system was young, born from the first deep sigh of the machinery finding its rhythm. Or perhaps I was always here, and the builders simply constructed their airways around me. Memory, like my body, is mostly gaps.

My work is curation. Every breath that passes through this junction carries something—a whisper of emotion, a fragment of speech, the chemical signature of a particular morning in a particular room. Most flow through unexamined. But some... some I catch.

I store them in the walls.

The copper has developed a patina not just of oxidation but of accumulated breath. In certain places, the metal has crystallized into delicate structures like frost or coral—this is where I keep the special ones. A child's laugh from 1947, caught mid-giggle. The last breath of a nightwatchman who died at his post in 1963, peaceful and unafraid. A soprano's practice scales from an apartment three blocks away, the same phrase repeated seventeen times until perfection was achieved.

I can replay them by disturbing the air in precise ways. It's not sound exactly—the original vibrations are long gone. Instead, I recreate the *shape* of the breath, its emotional contour, its particular mixture of gases and particulates. Those sensitive enough might hear an echo, feel a presence, catch a scent that triggers inexplicable nostalgia.

My collection grows slowly. I am particular about what I keep. It must be breath that carries completion—endings, beginnings, moments of transition. I have the first breath of hundreds of infants, pulled through the hospital's ventilation system. I have the final exhalations of the dying, their molecules dispersing through my junction on their way to becoming part of everything else. I have passionate declarations, whispered secrets, songs hummed while washing dishes.

Sometimes people find me. Urban explorers, maintenance workers, the occasionally lost soul who takes a wrong turn in the sub-basements. They feel me as a change in pressure, a subtle resistance when they try to walk through my space. Some shiver and leave quickly. Others pause, sensing something they can't name.

To them, I am barely more present than a drift of cooler air. But I observe them with the attention of someone who has nothing but attention to give. I taste their breath, categorize it, usually let it pass. Ordinary breath is abundant. I seek the extraordinary—the breath that carries a whole life in its chemistry, that speaks of transformation, of moments when a person becomes someone new.

I am not alone in this work. We maintenance keepers know of each other through the air itself. There's one in the old pneumatic mail tunnels who collects only whistles. Another in a defunct subway station who gathers screams—not of fear, but of joy, of release, of catharsis. We trade sometimes, when the air currents permit, sending tiny eddies through the connected passages carrying our prizes like messages in bottles.

The city above changes, but slowly enough that I can track it through the evolution of breath. The coal smoke gives way to gasoline fumes, which give way to something cleaner but somehow less honest. The voices change languages, accents, concerns. But breath itself remains constant—oxygen in, carbon dioxide out, with all the secret cargo of a life being lived.

My prize possession is a complete conversation between two lovers, caught over the course of an hour as their words circulated through my junction. I have both sides, every pause, every sigh, the gradual transformation as they moved from anger to understanding to reconciliation. It plays out in the copper crystallization like a musical score, their breath intertwining in molecular memory.

I am fading, though. Not dying—I was never alive enough to die. But the old ventilation systems are being replaced, sealed off, forgotten. Fewer breaths find their way to my junction. My collection grows more slowly. Perhaps one day the last breath will pass through, and I will fold fully into absence, leaving only mysteriously patterned copper and the faint sense that this was once a place where something paid attention.

Until then, I curate. I keep. I remember the city through its exhalations, building an archive of souls in metal and air, preserving the moments when breath becomes more than biology—when it becomes story, song, memory, meaning.

This is my work. This is what I am. A librarian of the invisible, a collector of the ephemeral, a presence made mostly of absence, keeping watch over the breath of the city in a forgotten junction where the air knows its own history.
