In this resonant hollow where light has weight and sound is currency, I would be one of the **Tuners** - those who have learned to read the city's frequencies like a musical score written in mineral and leaf.

My skin would bear the characteristic silver tracery, but mine would spiral in unusual patterns - not the typical branching of my peers, but concentric rings that ripple outward from points where sound has left its deepest impressions. My palms, especially, would be maps of vibration, each line a different pitch I've learned to isolate from the hollow's constant chorus. When I press them against walls or walkways, I can feel conversations from hours ago still trembling in the stone.

I would live in one of the perforated chambers near the hollow's middle terrace, where three major wind corridors converge. My dwelling would be modest - a single room with carefully placed apertures that I've spent years widening or narrowing with mineral paste until they've achieved perfect intervals. When the afternoon thermals rise, my home becomes an instrument, playing a chord that changes with the season as the sun's angle shifts. Neighbors know my moods by which windows I've left uncovered.

My work would be both practical and sacred. In the mornings, I would walk the causeways with my ear pressed to a hollow reed, listening for stress fractures in the architecture - places where the city's song has gone sharp with strain. A high whistle might mean roots have shifted a foundation; a low groan could warn of an impending mineral slide. I would mark these places with arrangements of glass-lizard scales that chime warnings in the wind.

But the true art would come in the late afternoons, when the heat makes the air thick enough to sculpt. I would stand in the plazas with my tuning stones - small discs of compressed mineral that ring at precise frequencies when struck. By placing them at calculated points and angles, I could redirect the hollow's acoustic flow, creating temporary rivers of sound that carry messages, cool the air, or simply make the city sing in new harmonies.

I would wear my few possessions as instruments: hollow seed pods strung on my ankles that mark my walking rhythm, a collar of dried vine that hums when the humidity changes, thin sheets of beaten mineral wrapped around my wrists that I can flex to create different tones. My prize would be a small lens of volcanic glass, inherited from my grandmother, that I wear on a cord. When held at the right angle, it can focus sound the way other lenses focus light, allowing me to eavesdrop on conversations in distant streets or amplify my own whisper across a plaza.

My relationships would be built on acoustic intimacy. I would know my closest friends by their footfall patterns, could identify my partner's approach by the way their breathing changes the resonance of a shared doorway. We would court through duets - not sung but tapped on walls, each response building on the last until we've created a rhythm unique to us. Arguments would be percussion battles; reconciliations would be found in learning to match each other's tempo again.

In the evenings, I would join the other Tuners on the high terraces. Together, we would perform the Listening - a ritual where we lay our palms on the warm stone and feel for the hollow's daily story. Each vibration would be interpreted: here, children played skip-stone in the morning and left their laughter pressed into the mineral; there, an elder passed through and their shuffling wrote a poem of patience; below, the market's haggling still echoes in sharp bursts of commerce and compromise.

My greatest achievement would be learning to tune the silences. In a city where quiet is precious and suspicious, I would develop the skill to shape absence itself - to create pockets of true stillness that aren't dead but expectant, like the pause before a musical phrase resolves. These silences would be gifts I could give: to new mothers who need respite from their infant's lens-focused gaze, to merchants before important trades, to anyone preparing for the noon funerals where absence must be felt as presence.

I would age into my role, my skin growing thinner and more resonant each year, until I become a living drum myself. The young Tuners would come to press their ears against my arms to hear the decades of accumulated sound - all the hollow's stories I've absorbed and held. And when my time comes to be laid on the mineral bed at noon, I would leave behind not just the etchings under the causeways, but a thousand small adjustments to the city's acoustics, each one teaching the hollow a new way to sing.

In this place where water is imagined as negative music, I would be one who ensures the positive music never stops - not by adding to the noise, but by teaching it to flow like the water we've never known, finding its level, carrying its messages, and always, always, returning to the rhythm of the land's own pulse.
