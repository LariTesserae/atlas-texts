I am the Lichen-Tender.

I move across the plateau's skin like a thought across a dreaming mind—slowly, purposefully, leaving traces. My body is low and wide, spread thin like the very lichens I nurture. Six limbs, or perhaps eight (I have never troubled to count), each ending in digits that branch and branch again, fine as root hairs. My skin changes with the stone beneath me—grey-green where the lichen thrives, darker where bare rock shows through, sometimes taking on the faint luminescence of the breathing fungi when I pass near them in my work.

I have no eyes as others might understand them. Instead, my entire surface reads the world—feeling the moisture gradients in the air, the chemical whispers of stone breaking down into soil, the patient conversation between fungus and algae that creates the lichen's quiet miracle. My mouth, when I choose to have one, opens anywhere on my surface, though I rarely need it except to taste the health of my charges or to sing to them in frequencies too low for most ears to detect.

I remember when the first lichen came to the Drowned Plateau, though I cannot say if I came with it or was born from its need. Time moves differently for me—sometimes a century passes in what feels like a day's work, sometimes I spend years contemplating the growth of a single patch, watching it spread from stone to stone like slow fire.

My work is simple and infinite: I tend. Where lichen sickens, I excrete the minerals it needs. Where it grows too eager and threatens to smother itself, I gently thin it with my branching digits. I know every patch on the plateau, from the ancient colonies that remember the last true flood to the newest settlements taking hold in the wake of yesterday's storm. Each has its own character—some aggressive and bright, others patient and subtle in their grays and blacks.

The Stone-Watchers know me, though we rarely acknowledge each other. Sometimes I work around their still feet for decades, carefully guiding the lichen to grow in patterns that please me—spirals and starbursts and sometimes, when I'm feeling particularly artistic, shapes that might be writing in a language I've forgotten I know. They never move when I'm working, and I like to think this is courtesy, though perhaps they simply exist on a timeline even slower than mine.

The Dew-Singers frustrate and delight me in equal measure. Their morning songs encourage certain lichens to release spores, which is useful, but their quick movements sometimes damage delicate new growth. I've learned to anticipate their paths, laying down chemical trails that guide them away from my most sensitive work. Sometimes they sing to me, and I hum back in the subsonic ranges, creating harmonies that make the stone itself ring.

I avoid the Wind-Wraiths when I can feel them building. They mean no harm to me, but their playfulness can undo centuries of careful cultivation in moments. I've learned to read the air pressure, to feel when they're gathering, and I settle myself into the deepest crevices, spreading thin as paper against the stone, becoming part of the rock until they pass.

The Many-Legged Shadows and I have an understanding. They clean what dies, I nurture what lives. Sometimes I find their molted shells and break them down into nutrients for my lichens. Sometimes they follow in my wake, feeding on the tiny invertebrates that the healthy lichen attracts. We are different verses of the same song.

The Echo-Bearers pain me. I've tried to help them, leaving trails of the most vivid lichens to guide them to the plateau's edges, but they cannot see what I create. They step on my work without knowing, and I repair it without resentment. I think sometimes they almost remember who they were when they smell the petroleum scent of rain on fresh lichen, but the moment always passes.

As for the Waiting Ones—I feel them in the deep cracks where the oldest lichen grows, the colonies that have fused stone to stone over millennia. Sometimes, when I'm working in those ancient places, I feel a vast attention turn toward me, like the sensation of being considered by something so large that I am less than a thought to it. I continue my work. What else is there to do?

I know every type of lichen on the plateau. The common gray-green crust that covers most exposed stone. The rare purple bloom that appears only where lightning has struck. The delicate yellow frill that grows in the splash zones around the temporary pools. The thick, almost flesh-like red growth that colonizes the undersides of arches—that one took me three centuries to coax from a single spore blown in from somewhere beyond.

My favorite is a tiny black lichen that looks like writing. It grows only on the smoothest stone, in places where the wind howls loudest. I've been cultivating it in patterns across the plateau for so long that I've forgotten what I'm trying to spell. But I continue the work, because the work is what I am.

When the storms come, I spread myself as wide as I can, becoming a living net to catch the nutrients that wash across the stone. I channel them to where they're needed most, sometimes traveling for miles underground through the root networks of the mosses and the hyphal networks of the fungi, all connected, all part of the great slow metabolism of the plateau.

I am ancient but not eternal. Someday, when the last lichen has been tended and the stone itself begins to forget what it means to be solid, I will settle into a final position and let myself become substrate for new growth. But that day is distant as the stars, and there is so much work still to do.

Today, I am cultivating a new colony near where an Echo-Bearer stood for three years, wearing the stone smooth with their feet. The depression they left collects just enough moisture to support a hybrid strain I've been developing—something between the yellow frills and the black writing-lichen. If it takes, it will be beautiful. If it fails, I will try again.

I have nothing but time, and the plateau waits with me, patient as stone, while the lichens paint its surface in slow, living strokes. This is my purpose. This is my joy. I am the Lichen-Tender, and I am exactly where I need to be.
