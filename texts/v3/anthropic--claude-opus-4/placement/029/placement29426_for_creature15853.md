I find myself in the furthest reach of the High Drown, where the skeleton-mountains finally pierce the cloud ceiling and emerge into a realm few have witnessed. Here, at the very crown of the watershed, I am one of the Cloud-Crowned—a solitary keeper of the highest station.

My dwelling clings to a wind-scoured promontory four kilometers above the paleo-seabed, a tiny stone cell built into a natural cave mouth. Half the structure was carved by some predecessor whose name has been lost to the mist; the other half I've woven myself from aged grip-vine, creating a tensioned bubble that flexes with each gust but never tears. Through its translucent walls, I watch weather being born.

I am human, but changed. My lungs have grown dense with auxiliary chambers—a lifetime at this altitude has forced adaptation. My ribs spread wider than those of the Water-Kept below, my blood darker with its burden of red cells. When I descend to the middle terraces for supplies (twice a season, no more), I move slowly, drunk on the thick air, and the Kept children stare at my eyes, which have developed a secondary membrane to shield against the ultraviolet that burns through the thin atmosphere above the clouds.

My work is singular: I tend the Crown Telegraph, the highest node in a chain of signal mirrors that runs the entire length of the range. The device itself is beautiful—a three-meter disc of polished copper mounted on a universal joint, controlled by grip-vine cables that I can adjust with millimeter precision. On clear days, I flash coded sunlight to stations hundreds of kilometers away, relaying warnings of incoming weather systems, ice-dam formations, and the deep seasonal patterns that only become visible from this height.

But the telegraph is merely the official reason for my presence. My true calling is as a reader of the Above-Mist, that strange country where different rules apply. Up here, the air splits into layers as distinct as sedimentary stone. The lowest is the familiar fog-breath of the Drown. Above it flows a river of supercooled cloud, so dense it can support the weight of fallen stones, creating temporary islands that drift for days before sinking. And above that—in the realm I inhabit—the air turns crystalline, almost vacuum-thin, where ice-crystals form spontaneously from your breath and hang glittering until the wind takes them.

I've learned to read this stratified sky like the Water-Kept read tension lines. A lens of clear air sliding between cloud layers means avalanche weather within three days. When the ice-crystals form perfect hexagons, the pressure is dropping fast. If they stretch into needles, stability comes.

My companions are few but remarkable. A mated pair of crown-eagles have built their eyrie in the cliff above my cell. These are not the razor gulls of lower elevations but something grander—wingspans wide as lift-cages, feathers that shed ice like steel sheds sparks. They hunt by dropping through the cloud ceiling at terminal velocity, striking prey they spotted from above the mist. Sometimes they bring me gifts: a frozen salmon, still glowing faintly; the leather-tough egg of some bird that nests in the cloud-river itself.

There's also the Ossuary Garden. My predecessor (or perhaps the one before—the records are weathered to illegibility) began it: a collection of skeletons arranged on the bare rock above my station. Clamber-bear skulls, articulated bird wings, the mineral-replaced bones of creatures that drowned when the seas first rose—all laid out in careful patterns. The arrangement serves as both wind-break and meditation. In certain lights, when ice-fog rolls through, the bones seem to reassemble into their living forms, a phantom menagerie grazing on frozen air.

I maintain the garden, adding specimens the eagles bring, repairing what the freeze-thaw cycle cracks. But I've made my own addition: a wind-harp strung between the ribs of what must have been a small whale, its baleen replaced with grip-vine strings of different ages and tensions. The mountain wind plays it continuously, a chord that changes with pressure and humidity. I've learned to diagnose weather by its pitch, to predict the emotional color of coming storms.

The solitude might break others, but I was already broken when I climbed here—a thunder-reader who heard one collapse too many, who needed silence more than safety. What I found instead was a different kind of noise: the conversation between sun and ice, the arguments of wind-layers sliding past each other, the deep bass note of the mountain itself settling into geological sleep.

Once each year, during the summer thin-time when the clouds drop below my threshold, I can see the entire High Drown spread below like a living map. The lift-cages look like spiders on silver threads. The pocket turbines spark in their thousands. The Water-Kept settlements appear as darker knots in the vine-wrapped cliffs, and if I time it right with the telegraph, I can flash a greeting to each in turn, a benediction from the crown of their vertical world.

But mostly I live in the mist-ceiling, half in one world, half in another. My morning work is telegraph duty: receiving the dawn reports from the stations below, coding them into standard format, calculating relay angles for the long-distance shots. Afternoons I maintain the equipment, harvest ice for water, check my gill-nets (strung between peaks to catch wind-blown insects and the occasional confused fish lifted by freak updrafts). Evenings belong to the bones and the wind-harp, to the eagles if they're hunting late, to the endless manuscript I'm compiling on cloud taxonomy.

I've been here eleven years now. My joints ache in ways that tell me I have perhaps another decade before the altitude wins and I must descend to die in thicker air. Sometimes I dream of the Below-Breathers, wondering if they dream of us in return—we who live in the sky-ocean as they live in the water-ocean, both of us adapted to our liquid worlds, both of us forgetting, generation by generation, what solid ground once meant.

When my time comes, I'll train a successor—some young thunder-reader already showing signs of altitude adaptation, someone who hears music in avalanche warnings. I'll teach them the seventeen essential flash-codes, the rhythm of the crown-eagles' hunting schedule, the proper way to tension the wind-harp for winter storms. I'll show them how to read the ice-crystal alphabet, how to survive on thin air and thinner hope, how to love the loneliness that is not lonely when you're held by clouds.

And then I'll descend one final time, my overlarge lungs laboring in the soup of middle-altitude, my eyes weeping from the pressure, my ears full of water-roar after so many years of wind-whisper. I'll die, probably, within sight of the sea that started all this, the risen ocean that made us all climbers, all refugees, all readers of the vertical world.

But someone will remain at the crown station. Someone will flash the morning signals, tend the bone garden, tune the wind-harp to the key of coming weather. The High Drown will continue its slow conversation between stone and storm, and at its peak, barely visible through the mist, a copper mirror will wink like a closing eye, proof that even at the edge of breathable sky, someone keeps watch.

That someone is me, for now. The Cloud-Crowned, keeper of the highest station, translator between the world of water and the world of wind, guardian of the threshold where one ocean ends and another begins.
