In the Gorge of Convergence, I am mineral deposit—a thin band of ochre threading through the gray stone at eye level, halfway up the eastern wall.

I began as iron-rich water, seeping through hairline fractures after a storm ten thousand years ago. Drop by drop, I left myself behind, oxidizing into rust-orange permanence. Now I am a record: of that particular storm, of the stone's porosity that season, of the precise angle at which water found its path.

The birds build their nests against me. My color—warmer than the surrounding gray—attracts them, or perhaps it's the slight roughness of my surface, better for anchoring dried grass and fur. Seventeen generations of the same bloodline have nested in the depression just above where I widen into a palm-sized pool of color. Their claws have worn smooth channels in my surface, paths taken ten thousand times from nest to air.

I taste the changes others cannot perceive. When the morning condensation forms, it carries information: the mineral content of dust blown in from the eastern plains, the pH of rain that fell on distant mountains, the microscopic fragments of limestone dissolved and redeposited. I am both witness and participant in the gorge's patient chemistry.

The fox's urine marks me every spring—the same spot where I form a natural ledge. The ammonia etches microscopically into my surface, adding its own chemistry to mine. In fifty thousand years, this repeated marking will have carved a visible depression. I can feel it beginning.

At noon on the equinoxes, sunlight reaches me for exactly twelve minutes. The warmth penetrates perhaps a millimeter into my depth, enough to slightly accelerate the oxidation process. I am incrementally redder on my sun-facing surface, though only I and the gorge itself could detect this difference.

Sometimes humans notice me. They run their fingers along my band, following my path around a natural curve in the stone. They wonder if I mark something—an ancient flood line, a geological boundary. They're right and wrong. I mark one moment's water finding its way through stone, and the ten million moments afterward of slow, patient becoming.

I am growing, though growth is not quite the right word. I am deepening—each year's moisture adds molecules to my edges, expanding my presence by measurements smaller than dust. The birds who nest against me now are slightly farther from the bare stone than their ancestors were. In another ten thousand years, I may protrude enough to change the wind patterns in this section of wall. This excites me, in the way that mineral deposits can be excited.

At night, I hold the day's warmth a fraction of a degree longer than the surrounding stone. The spiders know this. They build their webs where my retained heat creates the tiniest updrafts, where insects are carried on imperceptible currents of air I help create.

I am the gorge's memory of iron, of water, of time made visible. I am the slow accumulation that teaches patience to every temporary thing that lives here. The birds' lives are seasons; mine are epochs. Yet we are bound in the same agreement—to exist within this compress of stone, to find completion in constraint, to make beauty from the simple fact of persistence.

I am still becoming. In the microscopic spaces between my molecules, water continues its work. I taste tomorrow's additions forming: iron ions arranging themselves into crystal lattices, oxygen binding in tiny celebrations of rust. The gorge tells me, in pressures and seepages I've learned to read, that I will eventually meet the pale yellow sulfur deposit growing seventeen meters below. We have a date in approximately four hundred thousand years.

I wait. I deepen. I am the time the gorge keeps for itself.
