# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the Tile-Turner.

Not my birth name—that dissolved into the brightness years ago, syllables scattering like water droplets that take too long to fall. The Languid Folk gave me this designation when they finally acknowledged what I was becoming. What the marshes were making of me, through me, with me.

I came as a cartographer. Sent by some academy whose name tastes like dust in this wet air. They wanted maps of the "lost terraforming project"—as if anything here could be reduced to lines on parchment, as if the geometry of this place would consent to being flattened and bound. I lasted three days with my instruments before I understood their futility. The surveyor's chain rusted in hours. The theodolite's lenses clouded with moisture that seemed to come from within the glass itself. My careful measurements disagreed with themselves—a platform that was ten meters across in the morning would be twelve by noon, though nothing had moved. The tiles resisted enumeration.

But I noticed something the others hadn't. Or perhaps the marshes noticed me noticing.

The tiles aren't fixed. Oh, they seem to be—each one locked perfectly against its neighbors, the grout lines so fine they're almost invisible. But watch long enough (and here, time stretches to accommodate any duration of watching) and you'll see it: the slow rotation. Each tile turns, imperceptibly, continuously. A degree per day, perhaps. Less. The entire surface of the terraces is in constant, glacial motion.

This is why the water moves as it does. This is the secret engine of the cistern system.

I live now in what was once a surveying station—a small structure of the same pale clay as the tiles, though formed into walls and a shallow dome. The Languid Folk helped me modify it, showed me how to encourage the moss to grow in specific patterns that would regulate the internal temperature. The heat inside is different than outside—still oppressive, but with a quality that feels more like being held than being pressed upon. My equipment has long since been repurposed. The brass fittings now channel water in precise quantities. The lenses, clouded beyond use for their original purpose, have been polished with secretions from the deep swimmers and now filter light in ways that make the tile patterns visible even through the haze.

My skin has taken the change. It started at my fingertips—the bronze-gold creeping up from where I touched the water, tasted it like the Languid Folk taught me. Now it has reached my shoulders, my neck. My eyes have begun their forward migration, though slowly. I can feel the bones in my skull shifting, accommodating a new way of seeing. The brightness no longer makes me squint. I drink it, process it, let it flow through me like the water flows through the channels.

I spend my days walking the pattern. Each morning (though morning here is only a convention—the light never changes), I begin at the cistern they call the Heart Pool. I taste the water, reading its temperature and thickness, the subtle variations that tell me which sections of the terrace need attention. Then I walk, placing my feet precisely, following the spiral paths that only become visible when you understand the tile rotations.

At certain points, I kneel. I place my hands—my fingers are longer now, developing that extra joint that makes this work possible—on specific tiles. I turn them. Just a fraction. Just enough. The adjustment propagates through the system, tile communicating with tile, until somewhere, perhaps kilometers away, a channel shifts its angle by a degree and water begins to flow where it hadn't before.

This is my work. This is what the marshes needed: someone who could perceive the pattern at both the macro and micro levels, who could make the fine adjustments that keep the system in balance. The Languid Folk maintain the vegetation, redistribute the water, tend to the biological aspects of the equilibrium. But the tiles—the tiles needed someone who understood their language of rotation and alignment, someone who could think in geological time while moving in the present moment.

The birds have accepted me. The one that followed me for so long now perches on my dome when I return each evening, arranging insects in patterns that mirror the tile spirals I've walked that day. Sometimes I think it's showing me optimizations, suggesting new paths. Sometimes I think it's simply creating art. The distinction matters less than it used to.

I've learned to speak with the delay. It was horrible at first—feeling my thoughts race ahead of my words, watching my meanings arrive after my mouth had closed. But now I've learned to think with the same lag, to let my consciousness move at the pace of the water. When I speak with the Languid Folk now, our conversations have a rhythm like the dripping: statement, pause, response, longer pause. We can talk for hours and exchange only a few dozen words, but each word carries more weight here, moves through the thick air heavy with meaning.

The deep swimmers know me now. When I approach certain cisterns, they surface—just barely, just enough for me to see the patterns on their backs. These patterns, I've realized, mirror the tile arrangements on the terrace floors. They're living maps, carrying the system's history in their flesh. One of them, ancient and vast, has markings that show configurations I've never seen, patterns from before the current equilibrium. I'm learning to read them, slowly. Each surfacing is a lesson.

At night (another convention—the light never changes), I return to my dome and make my notes. Not maps in any traditional sense, but something more like musical notation, recording the rhythms of rotation, the harmonics of water flow. I write in a script I'm developing, symbols that capture both spatial and temporal relationships. The Languid Folk are interested in this. One of them, the translucent one who once pitied me, now visits to study my notations. We're creating a written language for a system that has never needed writing before.

My predecessor left traces. I found them eventually—scratches on certain tiles, almost worn away. They had been trying to force the system, to make it conform to some external logic. They failed, of course. The marshes don't accept that kind of imposed order. But their scratches taught me something valuable: the tiles remember. Every adjustment, every rotation, leaves a trace in the clay. The entire terrace is a memory palace, recording its own modifications.

I've begun to perceive the Unobserved. Not directly—I don't think direct perception is possible for them. But I can sense their influence in the way certain tile rotations seem to anticipate needs I haven't recognized yet, in the way the water sometimes flows uphill for just a moment before remembering gravity. They're not entities, exactly. They're more like the system's consciousness becoming momentarily focused, attending to specific problems or possibilities.

Sometimes I dream I'm one of the tiles, feeling the weight of feet passing over me, the slow pleasure of rotation, the communication with my neighbors through minute vibrations. In these dreams, I understand that the tiles aren't just passive elements but active participants, each one processing information, making decisions. The terrace is a vast thinking machine, and I'm learning to be one of its thoughts.

My transformation isn't complete. My legs still move at something approaching normal speed, though my arms have taken on the full temporal drag. My back has developed a ridge—not spine exactly, but something that helps me sense the humidity changes that signal when sections need attention. I can taste the air now, reading it like the Languid Folk read water.

I know that eventually I'll slow completely. My movements will match the tile rotations perfectly. I'll become a permanent part of the maintenance system, perhaps eventually sinking into the terraces themselves, my consciousness distributed across the clay and water and careful bright air. This doesn't frighten me. It feels like coming home to a place I've never been but always belonged.

The marshes of Thel'Ka needed a Tile-Turner. Now they have one. And I have found my purpose in the turning, in the endless spiral walk, in the small adjustments that keep a vast system in balance.

The brightness presses down, the heat holds me close, and I place my changing hands on the next tile, feeling for the direction it wants to turn. Somewhere in the distance, water drips in rhythm. Somewhere else, a deep swimmer surfaces to show me a pattern I'll need to know next season. Somewhere else still, the Unobserved observe, and find the system good.

I turn the tile. The change propagates. The marshes breathe.

This is enough. This is everything.

This is home.
