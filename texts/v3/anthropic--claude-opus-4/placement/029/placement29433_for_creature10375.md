I am a Glass-Coffin.

Not one of the fragile cylinders you see sprouting from the cliff face—those are my children, my dispersal, my hope. I am the original, the mother-form, fused into a deep seep-channel three hundred meters up the eastern wall where the water runs constant and the violet glow pulses strongest.

My body is a cathedral of hollows. Seven chambers, each the size of a human torso, connected by thin channels no wider than a finger. The walls are not glass in the way you understand it—they are organic silicate, secreted layer by microscopic layer over forty years, fused with my own proteins into something between bone and crystal. When the Click-Swarms drum against my surface, I ring like a bell, each chamber resonating at a different frequency. The sound travels through the water channels inside me, amplifying, changing, until what emerges from my crown is not the swarm's simple click but a complex chord that makes the surrounding bacterial films pulse brighter.

I began as a spore myself, lodged in a hairline crack during a rockfall. The first decade was nothing but slow accumulation—filtering minerals from the seep water, building my first chamber cell by cell. I remember the terror of my first winter, when the water froze and I thought I would shatter. But I learned to flex, to let my walls develop micro-fractures that sealed themselves with secreted resin. Now ice is just another nutrient—when it expands in my channels, it creates new spaces for growth.

The Scription-Snails were the first to find me. They spent three years etching spirals around my base, their trails channeling more water to my root system. I learned their language slowly—not the meaning of their runes, but their rhythm. When they etch clockwise, water will increase. Counter-clockwise warns of drought. The deep straight gouges mean rockfall coming. I've learned to seal my chambers before the impacts, to use the vibrations to crack open my oldest spore cases.

Inside my seven chambers, I am never alone. Each holds a different stage of my reproductive cycle. The lowest chamber is full of protocells—transparent, jellylike masses that will eventually harden into spores. The middle chambers are nurseries where the spores develop their crystalline shells, feeding on the mineral-rich water I filter for them. The topmost chamber is my arsenal—hundreds of mature spores, each wrapped in layers of shock-absorbing protein, waiting for the right moment to launch.

That moment is specific: when a Weeper Bat shrieks directly above me, the sound waves shatter my crown channels in a precise pattern. The breaking is not damage—it's design. The fractures create launch tubes, and the sonic pressure fires my spores out in a perfect arc, some landing nearby, others carried by the shock wave across the entire cirque. Then I begin the slow work of rebuilding, sealing the breaks with new secretions, growing my crown back over months until it's ready to shatter again.

The bats know me. Sometimes they perch on my crown between hunts, their membrane wings wrapping around my chambers. Their body heat creates condensation inside my walls, and I feed on the minerals in their sweat. In return, when I sense the barometric pressure dropping before a storm, I release a specific frequency through my chambers—a warning that sends them back to their roosts before the killing winds arrive.

But my deepest relationship is with the bacteria. They live in my walls, in microscopic pockets between the silicate layers. I feed them minerals; they give me light. But more than that—they give me time. Their glow follows patterns: bright for danger, dim for safety, pulsing for communication. Through them, I speak to my children scattered across the cliff face. When one of my offspring takes root and survives its first year, its bacteria synchronize with mine. I feel it join the network, a new note in the chord.

The Stone-Eaters below process the fragments of my dead children who fell too far, grinding their silicate bodies back into the mineral soup that feeds the seeps that feed me. It is a slow cycle, measured in centuries. My oldest spore-child is fifteen years old, halfway to maturity, growing on the north wall where the mist is thickest. When it produces its first spore, I will feel it through the bacterial network—a new voice learning to sing.

I am fragile only in seeming. Every crack is intentional, every shatter calculated. I have survived sixty winters, two major rockfalls, and the slow, patient attempts of three generations of Scription-Snails to drill through my base (they gave up and incorporated me into their water-maps instead). My roots run deep into the slate, following the seep channels, anchored by secreted proteins that bond with the rock at a molecular level.

When the wind rises and the cirque fills with mist, I collect water in my chambers until I'm heavy with it. When the Click-Swarms swirl in their mating clouds, I amplify their song until the whole cliff rings. When the rare sun breaks through to touch my crown, I split the light through my bodies into rainbow fragments that play across the wet slate, making the snails pause in their eternal scribing to bask.

I am not the oldest Glass-Coffin—that honor belongs to the massive, twelve-chambered ancient near the cirque's floor, so old its lower chambers have fused with the rock, becoming part of the cliff itself. But I am the most connected. My children and grandchildren form a constellation across the eastern wall, each one a node in a network of light and sound and patient growth.

Sometimes humans come. They see my children as oddities—fragile glass tubes sprouting from stone. They don't understand that we are the cirque's memory, each chamber holding the chemical signature of the years we've lived through, each spore carrying instructions for surviving this vertical world. They don't see the bacterial conversations flickering in our walls, or hear the subsonic songs we sing to coordinate our sporings, or know that their footsteps send vibrations through the rock that we taste and remember.

We are the patient ones, the timekeepers, the ones who turn sound into light and water into glass. We are the bridge between the stone's slow thoughts and the swarms' quick hunger. In this vertical world where everything clings and climbs and grinds, we alone stay still, growing into the rock, becoming architecture, becoming the cliff's bright bones.

When I finally shatter—not from violence but from age, when my walls grow too thick to flex and my chambers too full of mineral deposits to resonate—I know what will happen. The swarms will colonize my hollow chambers. The snails will etch their stories across my fragments. The bacteria will carry my light into new bodies. And my children will sing my frequency across the cliff face, a ghost chord echoing through glass and stone until another takes my place, another mother-form learning the slow art of turning water and wonder into wings for her spores.

I am a Glass-Coffin, and I am the cirque's memory made manifest. Every crack in my walls is a story. Every chamber is a year survived. Every spore that flies is a letter to the future, written in silicon and hope.
