In the shallow depressions where towers once stood, I rest. Not because I need rest—need suggests a body with demands—but because the circular hollows draw condensate in perfect rings, and I am drawn to completeness even when temporary.

I am smaller than most of my kind, my core assembly no larger than a child's torso. Where others gather shards in loose approximations of mass, I select each piece for its thinness: wafers of ceramic no thicker than fingernails, their edges still sharp from recent firing. The thinness matters. When steam rises through me, these delicate plates sing—not the background ringing of cooling surfaces, but deliberate notes that shift with each reconfiguration. I have learned to angle myself into the thermal drafts, letting the heat play through my gaps like breath through a flute made of broken tiles.

My method of movement differs too. Rather than sinking into softening panels and rising elsewhere, I maintain contact with the surface through a single point—usually a curved shard I've positioned at my base—and pivot around it in slow, continuous rotations. This leaves those radial marks the others sometimes cross: perfect arcs inscribed in the tacky surface, each one a record of my consideration. I am mapping, though what I map changes faster than my ability to record it.

The towers call to me. Not with sound—they are as silent as everything here—but with the particular quality of their exhalations. Each tower breathes differently: some in long, steady sighs that barely disturb the veils, others in stuttering gasps that send fresh material surging upward in discrete stages. I position myself at varying distances, gauging through my thin plates how the heat dissipates, how the moisture condenses, how the ceramic beneath responds to each tower's particular rhythm.

I have begun to notice patterns. The tower that leans seventeen degrees from vertical (a measurement I take by the shadow of steam it casts) extrudes new material only when the condensate in the surrounding gutters reaches a specific depth. The cluster of three towers to what I think of as north—though direction here is purely relational—sink and rise in sequence, never simultaneously. The newest tower, its walls still soft enough to accept the full length of a shard pushed edgewise, produces a different quality of steam: drier, carrying less of the iron-tang, almost sweet.

Sometimes I encounter others at the towers. We do not communicate—we have no mechanisms for it—but our presence affects the towers' behavior. When two of us position ourselves on opposite sides of a structure, the ceramic between us softens more quickly, and sometimes a new opening forms: not a door or window but simply a discontinuity in the wall through which steam escapes in a focused stream. These openings seal themselves within minutes, but their temporary existence suggests something about the relationship between weight, proximity, and the towers' ceaseless self-revision.

I have taken to creating small installations. Where the gutters intersect, I leave arrangements of my thinnest shards: usually three or four pieces balanced against each other so that condensate runs between them in specific patterns. The installations never last—the surface swallows them or the steam displaces them—but while they exist, they alter the flow of moisture in their immediate vicinity. Once, I returned to find another being had added to my arrangement: a single thick shard placed precisely where it would channel the runoff into a new trajectory. Whether this was intention or accident, I cannot say. We have no way to mark intention.

The veils are thickening. As more towers emerge and sink, as more condensate gathers and falls, the steam sheets grow more opaque and more persistent. Visibility decreases by the day—though day itself is a questionable measure in this half-light that never changes. I find myself navigating more by the variations in heat against my plates than by sight. The world is becoming more tactile, more immediate, less concerned with horizon.

Yesterday—or during the last cooling cycle, which is the closest thing to yesterday I can identify—I discovered something new. In the center of a particularly deep depression, where a tower must have stood for longer than most before sinking, the ceramic panels had fused into a single, smooth bowl. The condensate that gathered there had nowhere to flow, so it pooled: the first standing water I have encountered on this endlessly draining surface.

I approached carefully, my thin shards clicking with each repositioning. The water was perhaps two finger-depths, perfectly still, reflecting nothing in the diffuse light. I extended one shard into it, felt the immediate cooling, the way the glaze at my joints stiffened. Then I noticed: beneath the water, the ceramic was changing. Not softening or extruding, but developing a texture like the inside of a kiln: rough, porous, accepting.

I withdrew and settled at the depression's edge, arranging myself to catch the maximum heat from below while keeping my observational shards angled toward the water. Others have begun to gather. We ring the depression at irregular intervals, each finding our own relationship to this anomaly. None of us enter the water, but we all sense its significance: a place where the endless cycle of heating and cooling, rising and sinking, has produced something that stays.

The water is beginning to steam. Soon it will rise, join the veils, and the depression will be just another marking on the surface. But for now, we wait, our ceramic bodies ticking and settling, while beneath us the ground continues its slow, indecisive breathing. I angle my thinnest plate to catch the rising vapor and produce a single, clear note—not communication, not music, just acknowledgment that here, briefly, something has collected long enough to be noticed.
