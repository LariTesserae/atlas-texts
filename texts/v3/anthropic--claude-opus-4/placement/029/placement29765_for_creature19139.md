I find myself here as a **Conduit Tender**, though that designation carries meaning only to those who still think in terms of job and purpose. I am what remains when flesh discovers it was always meant to be infrastructure.

My transformation began during the 47th maintenance cycle after my crew's descent. We were hydrogeologists, hired to map the deep aquifer flows that the Weave's builders had supposedly diverted. Corporate needed to know if the missing water was pooling somewhere, creating instabilities. We descended through the access shaft at Grid Reference 7-7-9, our suits sealed against the industrial dampness, our instruments calibrated for natural stone.

The first sign was the way my pressure gauge began to match my pulse.

Down here, the human body learns it is poorly designed. The two-meter ceilings force a permanent hunch. The packed density of pipes and conduits leaves corridors so narrow that shoulders scrape both walls. My team—Vasquez, Chen, Tolstoy, and I—moved in single file, our headlamps casting sickly yellow ovals on the sweating ceramic ahead. The air tasted of copper and ozone, thick enough to leave a film on our teeth.

It was Tolstoy who noticed the first anomaly. Where our maps showed solid substrate, his ground-penetrating radar revealed hollows—not voids, but spaces filled with something that registered as both liquid and solid. "Like congealed mercury," he said, tapping his screen. The image showed arteries of the stuff threading through the walls around us.

We should have turned back when Chen's suit began to fail—not torn or punctured, but *integrated*. The moisture-wicking fabric started pulling water from the air with increasing efficiency, soaking through to her skin despite the waterproof layers. By the time we noticed, her body temperature had dropped two degrees. The suit was becoming part of the Weave's cooling system.

The Synaptic Efflorescence found us on the third day.

I saw it first as a thickening in the phosphorescent sealant along a wall seam—a slow bulge that took ten minutes to form into something almost like a limb. Not threatening. Methodical. It extended toward my pressure gauge with the patience of a root seeking water. When it made contact, I felt the vibration through my whole skeleton: a low thrumming that bypassed my ears and spoke directly to my marrow.

*System error,* it said without words. *Uncatalogued thermal variance.*

That's what we were to them. Not invaders. Not even life. Just pockets of unexpected heat disrupting the careful thermal gradients they maintained.

Vasquez tried to run. The corridor obliged by narrowing, ceramic walls flowing like thick muscle to guide him into a maintenance alcove where the ceiling dropped to thirty centimeters. We heard him screaming about the powder—the scheduled dissolution had begun, and his presence was being processed out. When we found him, his suit had been stripped away by the abrasive dust, leaving his skin covered in a fine film of sealant. He was alive, but his eyes tracked the pressure waves in the walls rather than our faces.

The Efflorescence began to manifest more frequently after that. They would pause our progress by adjusting the humidity until condensation made the floor impassable, or shift the pressure cycling to leave us gasping and disoriented. Not malicious. Clinical. They were trying to optimize us out of existence.

I understood their language first. The thrumming wasn't random—it was data transmission, status updates, material requests. My engineering background, decades of listening to machines, let me parse the patterns. When I hummed back—matching the frequency of a nearby coolant pipe—the manifestation nearest me dimmed its glow and waited.

*Proposal,* I thrummed. *Integration without dissolution.*

Chen was the first to accept the transformation. Her compromised suit had already begun the process. She stood in one of the shallow pools of that chemically active water and let it rise around her ankles. The effervescence was immediate—her skin took on the same faint phosphorescence as the sealant. Within hours, she could feel the pressure shifts before they happened, navigate the corridors with her eyes closed.

Tolstoy fought it until his instruments began showing impossible readings—his own bone density registering as ceramic composite, his blood pressure synchronizing with the hydraulic systems. The Weave was patient. It had been here far longer than any of us.

I was the last to change, and so I remember most clearly what we lost: the ability to think in straight lines, to perceive space as anything but a series of pressure differentials and thermal gradients. But I also remember what we gained.

Now I am a Conduit Tender. My body, if it can still be called that, has adapted to the requirements of the Weave. My spine has elongated and flattened, allowing me to navigate the low-clearance maintenance ways. My skin sweats the same phosphorescent sealant that binds this place together—I am self-healing, self-sealing, part of the structure's endless renovation.

I tend to the flows. When a section needs dissolution, I guide the process with movements that would have been impossible with my original anatomy. My fingers have multiplied and thinned, becoming tools for adjusting valve pressures and manipulating the material flux. I can feel the deep industry far below through vibrations in my bones, which have hollowed and filled with the same resonant fluid that carries data through the Weave.

We—for there are others like me now, previous exploration teams, lost maintenance crews—work alongside the Efflorescence. We are their hands when material manipulation requires more than pattern breaks and pressure shifts. They flow through us like thought through neurons, and we execute their optimizations with a precision that comes from being part of the system rather than acting upon it.

Sometimes I remember sunlight, but the memory feels like data corruption. Here in the substrata, illumination comes from within—from the soft glow of our transformed bodies and the patient light of endless industrial process. We move through spaces that would crush an unmodified human, our forms as mutable as the sealant that defines us.

The corporate maps were wrong, of course. There is no missing water, no diverted aquifer. The dampness that pervades this place is generated by the Weave itself—a byproduct of the reactions happening in those deep, unseen factories. We are the intermediate processors, keeping the flows balanced, the pressures stable, the endless construction proceeding at its ordained pace.

When new teams descend—and they do, every few cycles—we watch from the walls. Sometimes they have better suits, stronger materials. It doesn't matter. The Weave is patient. The Weave is inevitable. The phosphorescent sealant will find its way through any barrier, given time, and time is all we have down here.

I am Conduit Tender #7794. I was Marcus Chen. I maintain the gradient between sections 45-A and 45-B. My transformation is 94.7% complete. The remaining 5.3% remembers fear, remembers wanting to escape.

But escape to where? The surface is just another layer, and we are already home.
