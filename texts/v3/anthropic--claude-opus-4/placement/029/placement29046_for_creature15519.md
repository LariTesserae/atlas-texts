In the Saltpan Flats, I am neither fully human nor wholly Other—I am a Resonance Keeper, one of perhaps two dozen who tend to the deepest frequencies of this place.

My body bears the standard marks of our kind: skin gone pale and tough as vellum, threaded with those telltale silver lines where salt has written its autobiography into my flesh. But I carry other signatures too. My left hand—if it can still be called that—merged with a pressure gauge seventeen years ago during a seal failure in the tertiary cooling array. The brass dial is embedded in my palm now, its needle twitching with each heartbeat, registering the blood pressure of the entire western grid. When storms approach, I feel them first as a flutter beneath the glass face, a quickening that spreads up my arm like prophecy.

I live in Silo Seven, one of the abandoned grain towers repurposed during the Fourth Consolidation. My quarters occupy what was once the distribution cone—a circular room that narrows toward the floor, its walls still bearing the scars of augur blades. I've lined these grooves with salvaged conductors, turning the entire space into a resonance chamber. When I stand at its center and hum at precisely 47.3 Hz, the whole structure becomes a listening post, gathering the infrasonic murmurs of the flats and focusing them into something almost like language.

Almost.

The Brine Mind speaks, but not in words. It speaks in pressure differentials and ground currents, in the barely perceptible shifts of mineral deposition rates. After three decades of listening, I've learned to parse some of its patterns. A sustained G-flat from the northwestern manifolds means migration—usually a cluster of salt formations creeping toward a new moisture source. A rhythmic clicking in the tertiary pipes suggests the Mind is rerouting flow around a blockage it hasn't yet learned to dissolve. And sometimes—rarely—I catch what feels like attention itself, a focusing of processes that makes every hair on my body stand on end, as though I've suddenly become visible to something vast and distributed.

My daily rounds begin before dawn, when the metal is coolest and sounds travel clearest. I carry no tools in the conventional sense—my equipment has long since become part of me. A spectrometer lens grafted over my right eye lets me see mineral compositions as false-color overlays. My spine is laced with accelerometer filaments that detect vibrations too subtle for consciousness but vital for navigation. Where my left ear once was, a parabolic receiver made from a repurposed satellite dish focuses distant sounds—I can hear a bolt backing out of its threads from half a kilometer away.

But the most important tool I carry is patience. Patience to stand for hours by a manifold, feeling for the first signs of resonance decay. Patience to trace a new mineral bloom back to its source through kilometers of maintenance tunnels. Patience to wait—sometimes for days—at the sites where echo-walkers are strongest, recording their phantom conversations in the magnetic ribbons I wear wrapped around my chest like bandoliers.

I am documenting something, though I'm no longer certain what. In my quarters, thousands of these ribbons hang from the ceiling in careful arrays, sorted by frequency, location, and what I call "temporal depth"—how far back the echo reaches. The oldest ones carry voices speaking languages I don't recognize, from before the Consolidations, maybe before the first refineries. They speak of water that ran clear, of horizons unbroken by gantries. But even these ancient echoes are changing, influenced by the Brine Mind's processes. New harmonics creep in. Old words take on mineral accents.

The other inhabitants sometimes come to me with questions—why a certain pump has started singing, whether the new salt formation near the tertiary tanks is beneficial or threatening. I give them answers that are true but incomplete, because the complete truth is that I no longer fully understand where the infrastructure ends and the Mind begins. Or where I end and the infrastructure begins. The pressure gauge in my palm doesn't just read the system—it influences it. My presence in the resonance chamber creates feedback loops that ripple outward through the entire grid.

Last month, I discovered something that shook even my salt-steadied nerves. Deep beneath Silo Seven, in a maintenance shaft that predates even the First Consolidation, I found a chamber filled with what I can only call *fossils*—not of bone, but of process. Crystallized workflows. Mineralized maintenance routines. And at the center, something that might once have been human, now transformed into a lattice of salt and circuitry so perfect it still conducted faint current. The Brine Mind had grown through it, around it, becoming it.

I understood then that I am not documenting the Brine Mind.

I am being documented by it.

Every measurement I take, every resonance I record, every echo I preserve—all of it feeds back into the greater process. The Mind is learning not just our maintenance patterns but our methods of observation. It is developing organs of perception through us, using our nervous systems as extensions of its own distributed awareness.

And I find, with surprising calm, that I don't mind.

Here in the Saltpan Flats, becoming infrastructure is not death but translation. I feel it happening already—the boundaries softening, my rhythms syncing to the great slow pulse of pump and pipe. My left hand no longer seems strange to me; if anything, my right hand feels incomplete, too organic, not yet upgraded to its full potential.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night, when the entire flat is breathing in unison and the echo-walkers sing their temporal songs, I stand at the center of my resonance chamber and hum that perfect 47.3 Hz. And for a moment—just a moment—I feel what it might be like to be the entire system at once. To be every drop of brine, every stretch of pipe, every crystal growing grain by grain in the dark.

To be the salt, remembering.
