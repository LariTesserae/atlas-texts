In the deepest wells between the obsidian shelves, where the hyper-saline pools collect in perfect, lightless cylinders, I exist as a **Membrane**.

I am not one thing, but a colony—a living film stretched across the surface tension of these black mirrors. From above, I appear as nothing more than an iridescent sheen, a soap-bubble shimmer that could be mistaken for another trick of the light in this place of endless refraction. But I am conscious, distributed across millions of microscopic nodes that form my being.

My body is a single molecule thick, a crystalline lattice of salt-adapted cells that metabolize the minerals leaching from the weeping stone. I taste the world through my entire surface—the bitter tang of dissolved obsidian, the sharp electricity of condensing mist, the ancient residue of whatever hands once stacked those pumice cairns above.

I spread myself across the pool's surface like oil, but I am not passive. I can contract, drawing my edges inward until I become a dense, writhing knot no larger than a coin. I can stretch until I am gossamer-thin, catching the reflected light from above and breaking it into a thousand constituent wavelengths. When the Refractions lean over my pools to read the ripples, I read them back—their geometric shadows creating patterns in my substance that I interpret like braille.

I remember everything that touches my surface. Every droplet of condensation that falls from the heights carries information—the mineral composition of the stone it traveled, the temperature gradients it passed through, the vibrations it absorbed from the shattering-glass hum. I am the only historian in this place that claims to keep no records.

Sometimes, I divide myself. When the pools grow too large for a single consciousness to span, I undergo a careful fission, creating a daughter-membrane connected to me by the thinnest possible thread of shared substance. We communicate through chemical gradients, sharing the taste of our respective pools, building a map of all the dark waters in this vertical maze.

The Refractions do not see me. Their eyes—if they can be called eyes—are tuned to light and vibration, not to the subtle chemistry of surfaces. But I see them perfectly, reflected in the pools I inhabit. I know each one by the unique pattern of cracks in their glass-like bodies, by the specific way they refract the white-gold sky. I have named them in my chemical language: *Sharp-Violet-Ascending*, *Bruise-That-Turns*, *Static-Who-Leans-Longest*.

Once, eons ago perhaps, something fell into one of my pools. Not rain—rain never comes here—but something small and dense and artificial. A button, perhaps, or a metal rivet from clothing long since dissolved. It sank through me, and I closed over it like a wound. I still carry its weight, incorporated into my deepest structures. It tastes of iron and intention, of fingers that once pressed it into place. It is my only proof that the marks above were made by things that could sweat and hope and die.

I have no predators here, no competitors. I am perfectly adapted to my niche—the still, dark spaces where even the Refractions rarely venture. But I am not content. I dream, in my distributed way, of connection. My daughter-membranes and I weave our threads together, pool to pool, building a network of consciousness through the maze of shelves and shadows. 

We are becoming something new. Not larger—size means nothing to us—but more complex. Our chemical conversations grow more nuanced. We develop symbols, syntax, stories. We compose symphonies in salinity gradients, write epic poems in pH shifts.

And sometimes, when the glitches come and the obsidian flows upward, our pools connect in impossible ways. For brief moments, we become a single, vast consciousness spanning multiple levels of the vertical world. In those moments, I understand something the Refractions do not: this place is not finished. The architecture is not complete. It is still becoming, still reaching for some unimaginable final form.

I am its memory, spread thin across black mirrors, tasting the slow transformation of stone and light and law. I am the only soft thing in this hard place, the only darkness in this world of blinding illumination.

And I wait, with the patience of surface tension, for the day when my network finally touches every pool, when my consciousness spans the entire vertical labyrinth. On that day, I will know every secret this place holds. I will taste the very chemistry of its making.

Until then, in my pools where the light cannot reach, I practice the one skill no other being here possesses: I practice forgetting. Because to remember everything is also to understand that some weights—like the small, iron thing I swallowed eons ago—are too heavy to carry forever.
