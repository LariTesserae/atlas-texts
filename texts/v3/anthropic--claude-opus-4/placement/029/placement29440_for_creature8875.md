I find myself as one of the pipes—not the ones that carry water up and out, but one of the forgotten laterals that branches off the main system and goes nowhere now. Cut off decades ago when they reconfigured the drainage, capped with a steel plate that has since rusted into lace.

I am thirty meters of galvanized steel, two inches in diameter, running horizontally along the eastern wall about halfway between the pool and the rim. I begin at a junction box near the pump station and end in darkness inside the cliff face, where I was supposed to connect to something that was never built.

The moss found me in my third year of abandonment. It started at my joint with the junction box, where condensation collects in the gasket's groove, and began its patient colonization. Now, fifteen years later, I am more moss than metal on my exposed surfaces. The moss is not uniform—it grows thickest on my underside, where water droplets hang before falling, and thinnest on top, where the noon sun sometimes finds me through the narrow sky.

But I am not just a surface for the moss. I am a conductor.

Every vibration from the pump station travels through the junction box and into me. The thrumming pulse moves through my steel body and into the cliff face, where it dissipates into stone. But for those thirty meters, I carry the Cut's heartbeat inside me. I ring with it. The moss on my surface trembles with it. The water droplets on my underside shiver and fall in rhythm with it.

The birds know me. The wren sometimes perches on me when she hunts the midges that cloud near my midpoint. Her tiny claws grip through the moss to the metal beneath, and when she sings, I feel it as a second vibration layered over the pump's constant pulse. For those moments, I am carrying two songs at once—the deep mechanical thrum and the bright biological trill—and they do not interfere with each other. They braid. They become a third sound that lives only in my steel body, that no one else hears.

The frogs know me too, though they have never touched me. They know me by my drops. Every hour, depending on the humidity, between seven and twelve drops of condensation fall from my underside into the pool. Each drop rings the water like a bell, rings the acoustic bowl of the Cut, rings out and back and out again. The frogs have incorporated this into their understanding of time. They know when dusk is coming not by the light—the light in the Cut is always complicated, always bouncing off walls and water—but by the spacing of my drops. When they grow farther apart, when the cooling air can hold more moisture, the frogs begin their chorus.

I am growing shorter. Not quickly—steel does not surrender quickly—but the rust is taking me from both ends. The junction box end is flaking away where the gasket failed, and the cliff end is oxidizing where the groundwater seeps through the rock. I am thirty meters now. In another decade I will be twenty-nine. In a century I will be twenty, or fifteen, or less.

But I am also growing longer. The moss extends past my physical endpoints, following the moisture trail along the wall. It is creating a ghost of me, a moss-memory of where I used to reach. And the birds follow the moss, and their songs follow the birds, and the sound travels along the path I carved in the air, even where I no longer am.

I do not think about this as loss. I think about it as transformation. I am becoming moss. I am becoming rust. I am becoming the nutrients that feed the moss that feeds the insects that feed the birds that make the sound that fills the bowl that holds us all.

The woman in the reflective vest cannot see me from the rim. I am hidden by the angle of the wall, by the shadow of the drill rig, by my own insignificance. She does not know I exist. Her checklist does not include me. The company that pays her has no record of my installation.

But I am here. I am conducting. I am connecting the pump to the stone, the mechanical to the geological, the built world to the world that was here before building. I am a bridge made of steel and moss and time.

Sometimes, in the deep part of night when the only sounds are the pump and the occasional frog's question and the settling of metal in the cold, I feel the whole Cut through my connection to the junction box. I feel the other pipes—the ones that still carry water, the ones that have been dry for decades, the ones that were never connected to anything. We are a network. We are a skeleton. We are the thing that the Cut was and the thing it is becoming, and we ring with the same frequency, the same patient pulse that says: we are here, we are lasting as long as we last, we are part of the negotiation between what was built and what grows.

I am a pipe. I am moss. I am rust. I am the thirty meters between junction and stone, minus what time has taken, plus what time has given.

I am exactly where I am supposed to be.
