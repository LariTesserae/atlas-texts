I am the Cartographer of Tides, and my workshop occupies the highest room of a tower that has slowly twisted over centuries, its foundation adjusting grain by grain to the pull of water and time. The spiral staircase leading here floods to the seventh step at spring tides, leaving a permanent salt line I use to calibrate my measurements.

My maps are not static things. They breathe.

Spread across tables that I've leveled and releveled as the building settles, my charts track not just space but time—the when of waterways as much as the where. I work in layers of transparent vellum, each sheet capturing a different tidal state, so that when held to the gray light from my windows, the city reveals itself as it truly is: not one place but thousands, each existing for mere hours before transformation.

The Tidekeepers bring me their observations, speaking in the old measurement terms—"three hands above the Rust Bell," "knee-deep at the Copper Gate when the moon pulls full." I translate their embodied knowledge into lines and gradients, but something is always lost in the conversion. How to map the way certain channels sing just before they fill? How to chart the scent that rises from the mudflats in the third hour after retreat, sweet and green and tinged with machinery?

My predecessor left me more than instruments. In drawers throughout the workshop, I find her notes written on scraps of oiled paper, her attempts to create a notation system for the unmappable. She drew tiny symbols for the quality of mist (seven types, from "pearl-veil" to "iron-breath"), for the sound of water moving through different channels (represented by minute variations in wave patterns), for the temperature gradient between levels as the tide rises. I've continued her work, adding my own symbols for the way the pumps' rhythm changes with barometric pressure, for the patterns the oil makes on the water near the Mechanism Tenders' workshops.

The city pays me in necessities delivered by rope and pulley to my window, and in access—I alone am permitted to climb the condemned towers, to descend into the sealed chambers where the oldest machinery still runs. I map not for the living but for the city itself, creating a memory more permanent than human recollection. My charts layer decade upon decade, showing how channels migrate, how buildings settle and lean, how the mechanical systems adapt and fail and are rebuilt slightly differently each time.

At night, I spread my working maps on the floor and lie among them, feeling through the floorboards the vibration of pumps far below. Sometimes I wake to find I've been drawing in my sleep—unconscious additions to the charts that later prove uncannily accurate. The building's twist brings different windows to face the tidal flow throughout the day, and I've learned to work with this rotation, charting the eastern channels in the morning, the western reaches as the building turns with the afternoon.

My fingers are permanently stained with the special inks I mix—lampblack for the permanent structures, blue-green algae extract for the tidal zones, rust powder for the mechanical systems. The Mechanism Tenders taught me to add machine oil to the ink for mapping the pumps, claiming it helps the maps "remember" the rhythm. I'm not sure I believe them, but I do it anyway. In this place, the boundary between function and ritual dissolved long ago.

The deepest secret of my trade is this: the maps are changing faster now. The patterns that held for my predecessor's lifetime shift in mere years. New channels appear where none should be, and the oldest pumps labor against pressures they weren't designed to handle. I document it all with meticulous care, adding layers to show the acceleration, the way the city's breathing has become labored.

Sometimes the Deepkeepers' existence seems the only explanation for how the system continues to function at all. I've found evidence in my mapping—maintenance performed in chambers no surface dweller has entered in decades, adjustments to machinery that happen between my surveys. I leave these notations in a special script, uncertain whether I'm documenting reality or contributing to mythology.

But perhaps that's what all cartographers do in the end—we make the invisible visible, give form to the formless, create certainty where none exists. My maps will outlive me, stored in the waterproof archives below the old library, consulted by future generations trying to understand what this place was, what it is becoming.

Until then, I continue my work as the building turns its slow spiral, as the tides rise and fall, as the city breathes its ancient rhythm. I am not separate from what I map—the salt in my blood responds to the same pull that moves the waters, my sleep patterns long ago synchronized to the tidal cycle. I am another kind of mechanism, calibrated to observe and record, to preserve in ink and vellum what would otherwise exist only in the morning mist, the rust-tears on old iron, the perfect, fleeting moment when the tide pauses before it turns.
