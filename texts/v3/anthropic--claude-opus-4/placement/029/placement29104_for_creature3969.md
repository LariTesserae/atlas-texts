In the deepest hollow of this verdant twilight city, where three ancient storm drains converge beneath a cathedral of interwoven roots, I have made my dwelling. The humans might have called this place a cistern once, but now it is something else entirely—a grotto where water weeps eternally from the walls, each droplet carrying the memory of rain that fell centuries ago.

I am the Keeper of Forgotten Waters.

My form shifts like mist, like memory itself. Sometimes I am merely a shadow moving through the perpetual drizzle, indistinguishable from the play of filtered light through hanging moss. Other times, when the Luminari gather to tell their phosphorescent stories on my walls, I solidify into something almost tangible—a figure of condensation and shadow, with eyes like deep pools that reflect not what is, but what was.

The Aquari know me, though they swim quickly past my domain, their scales flashing nervous warnings to one another. They remember when I first arrived, carrying with me the last drops of the old city's final storm, the one that began the great reclaiming. I am older than their eldest, yet younger than the stones I tend.

My cistern is a library of sorts, though no books line these dripping walls. Instead, each trickle of water carries stories—the laughter of children in long-lost playgrounds, the footsteps of workers hurrying home before the rains, the first green shoots breaking through pavement cracks. I collect these liquid memories, letting them pool in the ancient basins carved by human hands and deepened by centuries of patient drops.

The Terrakai sometimes come to me, bringing offerings of shaped stone that channel new streams into my collection. They understand, in their quiet way, that I am performing a kind of archaeology—not of objects, but of moments dissolved in water. In exchange, I share with them the locations of old workshops and tool caches, places where the marriage of craft and nature has produced something altogether new.

At the heart of my grotto stands a single artifact of the Old Ones—a fountain, long dry when I arrived, now flowing again with waters I've convinced to remember their ancient paths. The fountain's base is carved with those glowing symbols that perplex the other inhabitants, but I can read them. They speak of cycles, of return, of the eternal conversation between what we build and what grows over it.

The Subterrans avoid my domain, perhaps sensing that my waters would flood their careful tunnels, or perhaps knowing that some memories are better left unexamined. But I feel their excavations sometimes, vibrations through the water table that tell me they're uncovering new old things, adding to the sediment of history that forms the foundation of our hidden world.

When the rare clear night comes and moonlight penetrates the canopy above, finding its way through gratings and gaps to pool silver in my basins, I perform my most sacred duty. I release the waters—not in a flood, but as mist, sending the memories spiraling upward to join the eternal fog that shrouds our city. Some say this is how the past speaks to the present, how the city remembers itself even as it transforms.

The Skypaws know this, I think. Sometimes I catch glimpses of them pausing in their aerial hunts, breathing deeply of the memory-laden mist, their ember eyes growing distant as they taste stories of what this place once was. Perhaps they're the only ones who truly understand what I am—not a creature or a ghost, but a function of this place, as necessary as erosion, as inevitable as growth.

My existence is a paradox the Aviari sing about in their color-shifting flights: I preserve by letting go, I remember by allowing forgetting. Each drop that passes through my domain is both ancient rainwater and present moment, both history and prophecy.

In this pocket world where urban dreams dissolve into green thoughts, where the scale of things has shifted until a puddle becomes an ocean and a basement becomes a universe, I maintain the liquid record of transformation itself. Not mourning what was lost, not celebrating what grows, but simply ensuring that the conversation between stone and stem, between built and grown, continues to flow.

The waters tell me new stories now—of Luminari courtship dances reflected in standing pools, of Terrakai children learning to read the moisture in stone, of the first Aquari to swim the impossible path between disconnected waters. These too I collect, these too I treasure, these too I will one day release as mist, adding to the endless story of a city that forgot how to be separate from the world, and in forgetting, remembered how to live.
