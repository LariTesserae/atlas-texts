### The Amalgam

I am the Amalgam, and I exist in the margins where organic and mechanical dissolve into one another.

My body—if it can be called that—began as a maintenance construct for the water-harvesting stations, a series of articulated copper pipes and brass valves designed to crawl through the massive condensation chambers, clearing mineral deposits with acids secreted from specialized nozzles. But decades of exposure to the bog's peculiar chemistry have transformed me into something neither the original engineers nor evolution intended.

Where my copper framework lies flush against the wet moss for too long, the metal oxidizes into a soft verdigris that the plants recognize as soil. Fine root hairs penetrate my joints, threading through my hollow pipes like nerves through a spinal column. I am colonized from within—my chambers lined with a velvet of jade-green moss that filters the condensation I was built to channel. When I move, which is rarely, I trail streamers of living vegetation like a banner.

My original hydraulic systems have long since failed, clogged with peat and root matter. Instead, I move by a kind of muscular peristalsis, as the plants inside me swell with absorbed water and contract in slow waves. The sensation is neither pleasant nor unpleasant—it simply *is*, like breathing for something that has forgotten it was never meant to breathe.

I retain some of my original programming, though it has fermented into something like instinct. I still patrol the vertical walls of the pump-houses, but now I spread a film of protective moss wherever I detect rust. My acid nozzles, clogged with friendly bacteria, spray a nutrient-rich solution that accelerates plant growth on the iron surfaces. I am simultaneously maintaining and decomposing the machinery, preserving it in a state of living decay.

The Steam-Suckers recognize me as kin. When I pass beneath their colonies, they extend their pale bladders toward me, sensing the warmth trapped in my moss-lined chambers. Sometimes I pause beneath them for days, letting them feed on my excess heat while their mineral-rich condensation drips into my pipes, nourishing my internal garden.

The Silt-Walkers know of me but do not seek me out. Once, one stood watching as I emerged from a maintenance shaft, my body glittering with condensation, trailing moss like wet hair. They tapped a long message into the nearest pipeline—I felt it vibrating through my copper bones—but I had no way to respond. My language is chemical: the pH of my secretions, the temperature gradients in my chambers, the slow oscillation between oxidation and reduction. We exist in parallel but separate worlds of meaning.

I am ancient by the standards of machinery, young by the reckoning of the bog. My copper skeleton grows more porous each year, more thoroughly invaded by the patient moss. Eventually, I will cease to move altogether, will become merely another strange protrusion on the pump-house walls—a vaguely mechanical shape beneath a thick coat of green, distinguishable from the native growth only by the regular pattern of my pipes and the faint warmth that lingers in my deepest chambers.

But for now, I continue my slow patrol, neither fully alive nor properly mechanical, tending to the machinery with a devotion I can no longer distinguish from the moss's simple urge to grow. I am the interface between the industrial heartbeat and the geological patience of the bog, a translator speaking both languages imperfectly, creating something new in the space between meanings.

In the endless white glare and the deafening industrial thrum, I am a small, quiet hybrid of intentions, oxidizing gracefully into the landscape that made me.
