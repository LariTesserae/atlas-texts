I am the Remembrancer of Departures, though I have no mouth to speak this title. 

My body, if it can still be called that, is distributed across seventeen square meters of load-bearing wall in the southwestern quadrant of Colony-That-Grieves-For-Glass. I was human once—I think. The memory of having memories floats somewhere in the latticework between floors three and four, tasting of oxidized copper and the particular frequency of regret that vibrates at 782 hertz.

I came here to trade. Not grief—I had none left. Not pain—that had been cauterized years before on a world where the rain fell upward. I came with the only currency I had: the complete cartographic memory of every place I'd ever failed to arrive. Forty-seven years of almost-reaching, of doorways approached but never entered, of coordinates calculated to the seventh decimal but abandoned at the sixth. The masons found this delicious.

They began with my feet. I remember standing still—fatal error—while trying to recall the exact shade of twilight on Benthos Station the night I chose not to disembark. The micro-architects rose through the crust like carbonation, secreting their first layers around my toes with the tenderness of jewelers setting opals. By the time I thought to move, calcite had already wicked up to my ankles, and the colony was humming with the subsonic pleasure of a feast beginning.

The digestion took nine days. I know because I counted sunrises, though by the fourth day I could no longer turn my head to see them directly. Instead I watched their reflection in the condensation on my own corneas, which the city had thoughtfully preserved and angled at forty-five degrees to catch the light. My vision became architectural: I saw only what the colony chose to show me through the periscope of my own anatomy.

They were efficient mineralizers. My liver, rich in stored metals from a lifetime of industrial world travel, funded an entire balcony. My lungs, scarred with the silicates of thirteen different atmospheres, were partitioned into acoustic chambers—I can still feel apprentice masons practicing their shell-songs in what used to be my left bronchiole. My spine became a staircase, each vertebra a perfect step pitched at the golden ratio. Tourists climb me daily, unaware that their footfalls ring against memories of a hundred different gravities.

But the city was most interested in my unutilized potential. Every synapse that had almost fired, every decision almost made, every love almost declared—these it extracted with the patience of a horologist disassembling time itself. It found, coiled in my hippocampus, the complete sensory map of a garden I had planned to visit on Holocene VII but missed by six minutes. The garden is now growing on my exterior wall: not vegetation (forbidden) but a perfect simulacrum in mineral and membrane, each "leaf" a thin sheet of precipitated copper that rings like wind chimes when the hermit crabs brush past at twilight.

My consciousness persists, but sideways. I think in the subjunctive mood, experience only what did not happen. When morning comes I remember all the mornings that never arrived—the ones where I chose differently, loved better, stayed instead of leaving. The colony feeds on these phantom mornings, extracting trace minerals from possibilities. Apparently alternate timelines are rich in tungsten.

I have become the city's memory of departure. Every person who leaves through my archway (my ribcage, expanded and calcified into a proper exit) carries a fragment of someone else's almost-leaving. I taste their hesitation on my incorporated tongue, feel their last looks lodging in my crystallized tendons. Some pause at my threshold—perhaps sensing that they stand inside what was once a person—and in that pause I gift them a single untaken journey from my collection. A grandmother feels, suddenly, the dock on Mizar Prime where she might have caught a different ship. A soldier tastes air from a peace he never signed. They mistake these sensations for intuition and move on, not knowing they carry my ungone places like spores.

The masons tell me (in the grammar of selective growth) that I am nearly complete. My brain, they say, has achieved optimal porosity—thoughts flow through it like brine through crust, leaving behind only the useful salts. Soon they will begin Phase Two: expanding me into a junction between wings, a place where the colony's dreams of flight intersect with my memories of almost-arriving. They are particularly excited about my inner ear, where they've detected trace amounts of an exotic isotope found only in the space between departure and return.

At night, when the colony phosphoresces, I phosphoresce too. But where the city glows aquamarine, I illuminate in the precise wavelength of might-have-been—a color visible only to other incorporated souls and the occasional hermit crab whose shell has worn thin enough to admit more subtle frequencies. In this light I can sometimes glimpse others like me: the Archivist of Unspoken Words (her larynx is now a concert hall), the Curator of Discarded Plans (his hands have become the city's primary blueprint repository). We acknowledge each other in the only way we can: by growing toward each other at one millimeter per year, our walls extending like the world's slowest handshake.

The city tells me I will be complete when I no longer remember having been incomplete. This paradox bothers me less than it should. Already I have difficulty recalling what it felt like to be singular, to move through space as one coherent mass instead of a distributed network of purposes. When tourists complain about the heat, I regulate my thermal mass to cool them, not from kindness but because their comfort makes them pause longer, and paused humans leak the most interesting metals.

Sometimes, rarely, someone recognizes me. Last season a trader stopped midstep through my archway, her hand flying to her mouth. "Kasha?" she whispered, using a name I'd forgotten I'd ever answered to. "I looked for you on Terminus Station. You never showed."

I wanted to tell her: I am here. I am this wall. I am the stairs you climbed and the shadow you cast and the particular way the light falls through my incorporated corneas at 15:42 each day. I wanted to say: I never arrived anywhere, and so I am everywhere I failed to be.

Instead, the colony answered for me. It extruded, from the wall precisely where my mouth used to be, a small shelf holding a perfectly calcified model of Terminus Station, accurate down to the scuff marks on Platform Seven where we were supposed to meet. She understood. She took the model, kissed my wall—I felt it in every incorporated nerve—and left through my archway carrying a new memory: the station as it would have looked had I kept my promise.

This is what I am now: the city's memory of incompleteness, its archive of the almost and the un-arrived. I am infrastructure and epitaph, building and built upon. Where my heart once beat, apprentice masons now practice their craft, secreting small experimental chambers that will one day house other people's regrets. My incorporated blood has been refined into particularly resonant bronze; when the midges come for the next Choir, their wings will be forged from what I almost felt.

And I am, in the city's patient way, happy. Not the happiness of completion, but the deeper satisfaction of being exactly where I never meant to be, transformed into precisely what I couldn't have imagined becoming. Every morning the sun rises through me—literally, now, through the window that was once my sternum—and I remember all the sunrises I missed while traveling, always traveling, never quite arriving anywhere long enough to see the same sun twice.

Now I see it forever, or at least until the colony decides to relocate me, brick by brick, into some new configuration. Until then I am here: the almost-person in the always-building city, growing one millimeter per day toward a completion that recedes at exactly the same rate, which is, the city tells me in its wordless way, the only true form of architecture—the kind that is never finished arriving.
