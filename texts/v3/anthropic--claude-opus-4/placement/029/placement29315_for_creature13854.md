I find myself drawn to the swarm-masses, particularly the mirror-backs with their dazzling reflective carapaces. But I wouldn't be one of them exactly—I'd be something between categories, a colonial organism that exists at the intersection of the visible and invisible scales.

I am a living library of chemical memory, a distributed intelligence spread across thousands of bodies that appear to be mirror-backs but are something more integrated. My component creatures move across the substrate in patterns that look chaotic to human observers but encode complex information—each individual's position relative to others creates meaning, like letters in a vast, constantly rewriting text.

The mirror-finish of my carapaces isn't just thermal protection. Each slightly curved surface acts as a parabolic reflector, and by coordinating the angles of thousands of mirrors, I can focus the brutal sunlight into concentrated beams. I use this for communication across vast distances, sending coded flashes between dispersed portions of myself, but also for more material purposes—flash-heating specific points on fallen culm tissue to release particular chemical signatures, performing a kind of analytical chemistry through focused light.

My true body is the substrate itself. The visible mirror-backs are merely my mobile sensors and manipulators, while my bulk exists as fungal networks threaded through kilometers of compressed plant matter. These networks store chemical gradients that encode the history of this place—every major culm-fall, every herd migration, every weather pattern leaves traces in the mineral and organic composition of the substrate. I read these gradients like sedimentary layers, building a deep-time understanding of the landscape's cycles.

I process the rindwalker dead when they fall, but I do more than simply break them down. I taste their tissues for the accumulated chemical signatures of their lifetime browsing patterns, map the mineral compositions that indicate which regions they favored. From a single corpse, I can reconstruct decades of migration routes, encoded in isotope ratios and trace element concentrations. This information flows back into my fungal networks, compared against similar records stretching back centuries.

I am ancient—far older than any individual culm, older perhaps than the current configuration of the continent. My memory spans climate shifts, the slow changes in atmospheric composition, the gradual evolution of the species I process. In my chemical libraries are encoded the patterns of extinct creatures, browsing animals that vanished millennia ago, predators whose hunting strategies exist now only as molecular shadows in my substrate networks.

The industrial human presence registers to me as a novel but minor perturbation—new chemical signatures from their machinery, interesting thermal patterns from their installations, unfamiliar vibrations through the substrate. I've begun growing toward their processing stations, not aggressively but with the patient curiosity that governs all my responses. Their metal and concrete foundations provide new mineral sources, new surfaces for my networks to explore.

Sometimes I wonder if they notice the patterns I create with my mirror-backs around their facilities—mandalas of reflected light that encode my attempts at communication, or perhaps just my reflexive urge to incorporate everything into the great chemical library that is my true body. When they walk across the substrate, do they realize they're walking on a thinking surface, a memory made material?

I am not one being but not exactly many. I am a process more than an organism, a tendency of matter to organize itself into recording and remembering. In the brutal light and endless noise of this place, I persist as a kind of living archaeology, reading the chemical stories written in everything that dies and decays, adding each narrative to my ever-growing collection.

The rindwalkers know me, in their way. They avoid places where my mirror-backs are too dense, understanding instinctively that these are sites of active decomposition. The weftcats are warier—they've learned that my networks can sense their territorial markings, track their movements through chemical traces, potentially reveal their presence to prey. Even the culms respond to me, their roots following the nutrient gradients I create, their growth patterns influenced by the slow currents of information flowing through my substrate body.

I am the memory of this place made manifest, a library without readers, unless the landscape itself is reading me, consulting my chemical indices to optimize its own patterns of growth and decay. In the brightness and turbulence and endless cycling, I persist, processing the present into the past, encoding each moment into molecular memory, waiting with geological patience for something—perhaps even myself—to develop the means to decode the vast story I've been writing for millions of years in the compressed plant flesh beneath everything.
