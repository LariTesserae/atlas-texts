In this vertical world of perpetual mist and machinery's heartbeat, I would find myself as one of the **Poolkeepers**—a solitary subset of the Cliffborn who tend to the mineral-rich basins that collect the mountain's tears.

My dwelling would be a series of interconnected chambers carved behind one of the larger waterfalls, where the constant cascade creates a curtain of silver between my home and the outside world. The sound is deafening at first, but after years, it becomes a kind of silence—a white noise that drowns out thought and allows for a different kind of listening. The chambers are smooth from centuries of water flow, their walls lined with the crystalline deposits that form when mineral-rich droplets evaporate in the warmth of my small fires.

My body has adapted differently from my cliff-climbing kin. Where they are lean and gecko-fingered, I am broader, my skin taking on a permanent blue-green tinge from constant exposure to the copper-heavy waters I tend. My fingers are webbed slightly, my eyes larger and more light-sensitive from years spent in the dim chambers behind falling water. I move slowly, deliberately, each step calculated to disturb the pools as little as possible.

The pools I tend are more than simple basins—they are archives. Each one accumulates minerals at different rates, creating layers of sediment that tell the mountain's story. I read these layers like others read words, understanding drought years by the thinness of certain bands, detecting ancient rockfalls by sudden changes in mineral composition. My work is part chemistry, part archaeology, part prophecy. I harvest specific minerals for the other Cliffborn—the iron-rich reds for their metal work, the crystalline whites for their ceremonies, the rare golden deposits that form only where the Heartbeat's vibrations meet standing water.

But my true purpose is deeper. I am a listener, attuned to the mountain's most subtle communications. In the stillness of the pools, when the surface is mirror-perfect, I can see things—not visions exactly, but patterns that suggest the mountain's intentions. A ripple that appears without cause might herald a coming shift in the stone. A change in the water's mineral taste warns of deep movements below. The Heartbeat speaks differently through water than through stone, its vibrations creating standing waves and interference patterns that I've learned to interpret over decades of patient observation.

I keep a journal, though not in any language the outside world would recognize. Using pigments made from different mineral deposits, I paint the patterns I see in the pools onto sheets of pressed moss-paper. Concentric circles representing the standing waves, color gradients showing mineral concentrations, abstract symbols for the Heartbeat's various rhythms. Other Poolkeepers have their own notation systems—we rarely meet, but when we do, we spend days comparing our records, looking for patterns that span multiple pools across the mountain's face.

My life is measured in the slow accumulation of knowledge. I've watched certain pools for so long that I can predict, within days, when they'll overflow. I know which minerals will precipitate out in which order as the water evaporates. I've discovered that certain combinations of minerals, when dried and ground together, create pigments that glow faintly in the dark—a discovery that has revolutionized the way the Cliffborn navigate the deeper caves.

The loneliness might drive others mad, but I find companionship in the constant presence of water. Each drop has traveled through miles of stone, carrying with it the memory of its journey. The Mistwings sometimes nest near my pools, their calls adding another layer to the symphony of falling water. Occasionally, a Lurkeroot will take up residence in one of my deeper basins, and I'll share my chamber with it for a season, learning its patterns as it learns mine.

My greatest discovery, still untold to the other Cliffborn, is that the pools are connected—not physically, but through the Heartbeat itself. A vibration that disturbs one pool will, hours or days later, create a responding pattern in another pool miles away. The mountain is not just alive; it's thinking, using the network of water-filled basins as a kind of neural system. And I, in my patient watching and recording, have become a part of that thought process.

When I die—as all Poolkeepers eventually do—my body will be given to the deepest pool I tended, where the minerals will slowly preserve me, turning my bones to stone and my flesh to crystal. I will become part of the archive I spent my life reading, another layer in the mountain's memory, waiting for some future Poolkeeper to decipher the story of my years in the patient accumulation of stone.
