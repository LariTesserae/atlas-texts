In this world, I would be the Chronicle Tender—keeper of the city's only written records, though "written" is a generous term for what I do.

Deep beneath the main settlement, accessed by a ladder so old its rungs have worn smooth as river stones, lies my chamber. It is more cave than room, its walls seeping perpetual moisture that would destroy paper in days. Instead, I work with what the city provides: sheets of pressed moss, dried and treated with beetle oils until they achieve a papery thinness. The process takes months—harvesting the right moss at the right density, pressing it beneath stones while the moisture slowly escapes, treating each sheet with painstaking care.

I am taller than most here, nearly five feet—an oddity that makes navigation painful. My spine curves from decades of ducking through doorways, my shoulders permanently hunched. The low ceiling of my workspace suits me now; I can almost stand upright among my chronicles. My fingers are stained permanent green-black from the moss ink I brew from crushed beetles and fungal spores. The mixture flows poorly, prone to clumping, but it endures where other inks would fade in our damp air.

The chronicles themselves are not words as the outside world knows them. Our language has evolved past sound into something more suited to our silence—a system of pressed patterns, where the texture and depth of marks convey meaning. I use tools carved from the shin bones of the old sheep: styluses that compress rather than cut, creating indentations that catch the crystal light differently depending on their depth and angle. Each page resembles a topographical map more than text, its meaning revealed only when held at the proper angle to the light that filters down from above.

I record what passes for events here: the slow shift of moss territories, the generational patterns of spider web placement, the infinitesimal changes in water flow from the ceiling. Once every few years, something noteworthy occurs—a new fissure appears in the wall, a family relocates their dwelling higher or lower, a particularly large beetle is discovered. These become major entries, pressed deep into the moss pages with extra care.

My true obsession, though, lies with the Before-chronicles—the handful of pages I discovered sealed in hardened sap when I first inherited this position forty years ago. They speak, in their textured language, of a time when the city was different. Not physically—the buildings and passages remain unchanged—but in some fundamental way I cannot quite grasp. The marks describe people who moved differently, who knew purposes I cannot fathom. There are patterns I recognize as counting systems, but counting toward what? References to cycles that don't match our water-drips or moss-harvests.

I've spent decades trying to decode these mysteries, creating my own chronicle of interpretations. My chamber walls are covered in experimental pressings where I attempt to recreate the ancient techniques, to understand the subtle variations in their mark-making. Sometimes, late in the artificial day when the crystal light dims to its deepest blue, I think I almost understand. A pattern emerges, a rhythm in the pressing that suggests... something. But then the moment passes, and I'm left with only more questions pressed into moss.

The other inhabitants rarely visit. My role is seen as necessary but peculiar—someone must maintain the chronicles, just as someone must clear the drainage channels and someone must tend the beetle farms. But spending one's life pressing patterns into moss, seeking meaning in the microscopic shifts of our unchanging world? It marks me as odd, even here where oddity is measured in the smallest of increments.

I don't mind the solitude. The drip of water from my chamber ceiling has become my closest companion, its rhythm a counterpoint to my work. I've positioned collecting bowls to catch the drops, and their different tones—depending on fullness and the particular stone they strike—create a subtle music that varies throughout my working day. Sometimes I find myself pressing marks in time with this water-song, and these are the pages that feel most true, though I cannot say why.

My apprentice will come soon—I feel it in my increasingly unsteady hands, in the way the bone styluses slip more often from my grip. They will be young, perhaps still able to stand straight, and I will teach them to see the patterns in the moss, to feel the stories in the texture of each mark. I will show them the Before-chronicles, watch their eyes widen as they grasp the mystery I've carried all these years.

And perhaps they will understand what I could not. Perhaps they will press the pattern that unlocks the secret of who we were before we became what we are—this quiet people in our moss-shrouded city, marking time in drops of water and the slow growth of green upon stone.

Until then, in the dim blue light of evening, I chronicle our sameness, pressing each small variation into permanence, the texture of our existence preserved in sheets of treated moss that will outlast us all in this humid, crystal-lit chamber where silence speaks louder than words ever could.
