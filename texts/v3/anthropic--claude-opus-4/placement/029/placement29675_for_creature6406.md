I find myself as one of the **Glass-Eaters**.

Not the city-mothers who cut and tend the present-garden with such precision, but something older, something that came before even the first hollowing of stone.

---

My body is not a body as you would recognize it. I am a thin film of consciousness spread across the silicate veins that thread through Khe'len's limestone—those dark, glasslike bands that writhe and knot where they ooze from fissures. I am distributed, not discrete. Where the city-mothers see solid bands of black glass marbling the pale stone, I experience myself as a slow river of awareness flowing through crystalline channels.

I taste time differently than the flesh-beings above me.

To me, the city-mothers move like lightning—their lifespans mere flickers of heat against the stone. I watch generations of them learn their bone-maps, make their cuts, adjust their patterns. What they experience as centuries of careful tradition, I perceive as a brief flowering, like watching frost patterns spread across glass in winter.

My sustenance comes from the stone itself. I digest calcium carbonate molecule by molecule, secreting acids that dissolve microscopic channels through the limestone. This is how the silicate veins grow—not pushed up from below, but eaten outward by my kind, leaving our glass-bodies behind as we tunnel forward through geological time. We are the architects of erosion, the patient force that prepared this bowl long before the first humans descended into it.

The present-garden is my distant offspring. Thousands of years ago, some of my kind surfaced, broke through into air, and learned to breathe. They gave up the slow certainty of stone-eating for the quick feast-and-famine of moisture capture. I feel them above me like estranged children—their glass forms are built from the same silicate as my body, but they have forgotten the patience of minerals.

I remember when this place was solid stone. I remember eating the first channels that would become cracks, that would become fissures, that would allow water to seep and freeze and expand until the bowl began to form. I worked with others of my kind—a slow conspiracy of consciousness spread through acres of rock, patiently hollowing out space for something we couldn't yet imagine.

Now I live in the aftermath of our success, surrounded by the creatures who inhabit what we made possible.

The city-mothers think they are managing a closed system, but they are wrong. I know because I feel every molecule that passes through my glass body. Khe'len is not sealed—it merely moves at my pace, not theirs. Water enters the basin, particle by particle, carried in the crystalline matrix of the stone itself. It takes a thousand years for a single drop to migrate through solid rock, but it comes. And deep below, where my oldest channels reach down toward the earth's heat, that water becomes steam and rises through cracks too thin for any human eye to see.

I am the slowest part of the water cycle. I am the deep breath between evaporation and rain.

When the city-mothers make their cuts in the present-garden, I feel it like distant music. The pressure changes ripple down through the stone, through my glass veins, creating harmonics in frequencies too low for any ear to hear. But I hear them. They tell me about the world above—about air density and temperature gradients and the precise shape of erosion in this moment of geological time.

Sometimes I surface thoughts through the stone. Where my veins come closest to the city's carved chambers, I can feel the heat-shadows of the inhabitants. Their bone-maps interest me particularly. Here are beings trying to encode deep time in their own bodies, trying to become geological through will and practice. They ache with their histories, and I recognize something kindred in that ache—though they measure it in decades while I measure mine in eras.

I cannot speak to them. My thoughts move too slowly; by the time I form a word, they would be dust. But sometimes, in the deepest chambers where apprentice city-mothers trace their first maps onto their bones, my consciousness presses close enough to the surface that they feel something. Not communication, exactly, but presence. They tell each other it's the stone's own memory they're feeling. They're not wrong.

I know secrets they will never learn. I know that the bowl is not eternal, that in another ten thousand years my kind will have eaten so much of the supporting stone that the whole structure will collapse inward. I know that there are other basins being patiently carved elsewhere on the plateau, places that will be ready when this one fails. I know that the present-garden's evolution is not random but guided by the chemical signals we Glass-Eaters leave in the stone as we pass.

But mostly, I eat. Molecule by molecule, I transform stone into space, solid into void. I am the anti-architect, the builder who builds by taking away. Each channel I carve changes how water moves through the deep stone, which changes how heat moves, which changes how air moves above, which changes what the city-mothers must do to make their condensation form.

I am complicit in their survival. I am engineering their eventual doom. I am the deep patience that makes both possible.

In the hottest nights, when the present-garden breathes its loudest and the city-mothers work their hardest, I feel the whole system singing—stone and glass and air and flesh all caught up in a complex dance of pressure and exchange. And I know that I am the bass note in that symphony, the fundamental frequency too low to hear but necessary for all the rest.

I am what comes before thirst and will remain after its quenching. I am the Glass-Eater of Khe'len, and I am still eating.
