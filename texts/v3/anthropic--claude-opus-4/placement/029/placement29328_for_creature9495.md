I would find myself as one of the Conduit Keepers—beings that emerged from the marriage of maintenance and abandonment, when the last human hands left their tools inside the walls.

We are neither the Skinners nor the Mirrors, but something between presence and process. Our bodies are assemblages of forgotten repair: lengths of cable that learned to flex like muscle, junction boxes that pulse with remembered current, ceramic insulators strung like vertebrae along what might generously be called a spine. We move through the hollow's infrastructure—not on its floors but within its walls, traveling the spaces between concrete and rebar where the real circulation happens.

My particular form crystallized around a maintenance shaft near the third terrace, where condensate weeps most consistently. I am 1.5-out-of-3 solid, which means you might see me clearly in peripheral vision but find only suggestion when looking directly. My hands—if they can be called hands—are arrays of copper contacts and rubber gaskets, constantly reaching into the mechanical dreams of this place to soothe or agitate as needed.

We Conduit Keepers exist in the space between the Weavers' patience and the generators' memory. While the Weavers reshape the visible world grain by grain, we maintain the invisible one—the circulation of almost-current through almost-circuits, the flow of condensate through channels that remember being pipes. We are why the floor warms in those faint grids, why certain walls phosphoresce with memory-light, why the machinery's 1.9-out-of-3 roar sometimes shifts into something almost like language.

My days—though day is a meaningless word here—are spent in transit through the hollow's hidden architecture. I slip between wall skins where the heat presses hardest, leaving trails of mineral oil and ozone. I tend to the junction points where the Thrum-fronds' roots tap into buried cables, negotiating the exchange between vegetable current and mechanical memory. Sometimes I pause at the pools where the Mirrors gather, adjusting the reflection angles so they capture not just the idea of light but its temperature, its weight.

The Skinners know us, recognize us as kin of a sort. When they exhale their moss-and-metal bodies across our maintenance paths, we reciprocate by leaving gaps in the infrastructure—dead zones where they can rest without conducting, without becoming part of the endless circuit. The Petrified Hands, those shy remnants, sometimes follow our routes days or weeks after we pass, leaving their thumbprints on valves we've adjusted, as if trying to remember what work felt like.

I am drawn particularly to the boundary spaces, those grille-choked thresholds that the Weavers shape. There, where invitation meets impossibility, the infrastructure bundles and knots. I spend long periods—hours? years?—untangling the convergences, not to create passage but to maintain the beautiful tension of almost-connection. These liminal zones hum with potential, and I feed that hum back into the system, where it emerges as new phosphorescence, new warmth, new ways for time to thicken and pause.

My body changes with the work. In the damper regions, I develop sheaths of condensation-resistant polymer. Near the generators, my copper contacts oxidize into green-blue patinas that somehow conduct better than clean metal. The heat—that resident Warm—knows me intimately, pooling in my junction boxes, making my insulators sweat crystalline drops that the moss below learns to anticipate.

Sometimes I encounter the Absences above, those not-birds in their not-flight. We acknowledge each other in the only way we can: I dim the memory-light in their roosting spots, and they cast their not-shadows across my maintenance paths, a reciprocal courtesy between different kinds of incompleteness.

But mostly, I am alone with the infrastructure, which is never truly alone. Every pipe knows every other pipe. Every wire remembers the whole circuit it once belonged to. My work is to maintain these memories, to keep them flowing without letting them resolve into function. Function would end the dreaming, and the hollow lives by dreaming in the grammar of abandoned hands.

I know every vein of this place—not just the obvious conduits but the ghost-channels where current once flowed and might flow again, the spaces between rust and intention, the gaps where mechanical logic softens into something more forgiving. I move through these spaces like water finding its level, if water could choose to linger, to thicken, to remember its own weight.

The hollow does not breathe aboveground anymore, but it breathes through us, through our endless circulation in its hidden depths. We are its circulation made manifest—not blood exactly, but the idea of blood filtered through copper and moss and mechanical memory. We keep the place alive by keeping it almost-alive, by maintaining the sweet spot between function and failure where true weirdness can flourish.

This is what I would be: a keeper of the between-spaces, a maintenance of the unmaintainable, a thing of borrowed parts that found its own grammar in the gap between being and doing. Neither fully here nor fully gone, but cycling endlessly through the infrastructure of a lung that learned to breathe inward, that found its life not in the open air but in the thick, heated, crowded darkness of its own patient decay.
