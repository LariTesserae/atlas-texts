**The Memorist**

I am neither the basin's engineer nor its chronicler, but something between—a filter-feeder of time itself, straining moments from the mineral-thick water and storing them in calcified chambers that spiral through my body like a nautilus of pure archive.

My kind are few. We anchor in the transition zones where cold seeps meet warmer upwellings, our bodies stretched between temperatures like living thermometers. Each of us is perhaps two meters long when fully extended, though we spend most of our time contracted into dense coils, processing what we've gathered.

Our flesh is translucent cartilage webbed with branching vessels that pulse with filtered fluids. Along these vessels, at regular intervals, grow small chambers—memory pearls, the Turners might call them if they had words. Each chamber mineralizes around a specific chemical signature: the iron taste of a new vent opening, the sulfur plume of a dying seep, the calcium cascade when shell-drifts collapse. We do not choose what to remember. Our bodies decide, encoding chemical events into solid time.

The oldest of us carry thousands of these chambers, our bodies becoming heavy with accumulated history. When we die, we sink to the basin floor and slowly dissolve, releasing decades of chemical memory back into the water—ghost seasons that the other creatures taste and respond to, phantom instructions from a time before.

But I am young, only seven years since I first attached to this shelf of black basalt where three currents converge. My memory chambers number in the hundreds, not thousands. I can feel them beneath my skin like a rosary of small stones, each one a perfect moment:

- The day the Basin Listener first sang after the spring pressure shift, and every Turner in the middle depths turned silver-belly-up for a full heartbeat
- The morning a new chimney burst through the floor, tasting of deep copper and something almost sweet
- The season when the rain stopped for three days and the surface became a mirror, reflecting the first stars any of us had ever sensed

I feed by extending feathery filters into the convergence zone, where chemical gradients are steepest. Bacteria, larvae, shed scales, dissolved minerals—everything passes through me. My body sorts it all, feeding on the simple organics while shunting the mineral signatures into my growing chambers. Each chamber takes days to form, accreting in careful layers like tree rings made of calcium and trace metals.

Sometimes the Siphon Looms adjust their jets and my feeding currents falter. I contract, pulling my filters back, and wait. In these quiet times, I can feel the memories pulsing—not as images or sounds, but as chemical echoes. The iron chamber throbs differently than the sulfur chamber. The rare freshwater intrusion chambers feel almost effervescent against my inner walls.

The Basin Listener knows we exist. When it rises to breathe, it sometimes pauses near our anchoring zones, its pressure-sensitive pits reading the chemical gradients we create. Once, it brushed against my extended form with surprising gentleness, its blind skin tasting the temporal map written in my flesh. I contracted violently, but it simply moved on, carrying my chemical signature into the deep.

We memorists are the basin's only record-keepers, though we keep records without understanding them. We are living libraries written in a language of minerals and time, our bodies slowly becoming landscapes of accumulated moments. The other creatures live in the eternal present of pump and feed and flee. Only we carry the past forward, pearl by pearl, chamber by chamber.

When I die—perhaps in another forty years, when my body grows too heavy with memory to maintain position—I will sink into the chimney fields. My chambers will dissolve in sequence, releasing their chemical stories in reverse order. The basin will taste its own history flowing backward: recent years first, then deeper time, until finally my earliest memories emerge—the taste of my first attachment, the chemical signature of the world when I was new.

The creatures who feed in those waters will respond to signals from before they were born, following chemical instructions laid down by a younger basin. They will turn when there is no reason to turn, flee from dangers that no longer exist, gather at vents that have long since sealed.

For a brief season, the basin will dream through my dissolved body.

And somewhere on another convergence shelf, a young memorist will be filtering those same dreams, encoding them into new chambers—memories of memories, time folded into itself, the basin learning to remember remembering.

This is what I am: a soft machine for making time solid, a library that builds itself from the inside out, a creature that exists to prove that even in this place of endless present-tense motion, the past persists.

I feed. I filter. I remember without knowing what memory means.

And sometimes, in the deepest quiet between the basin's groans, I can feel all my chambers humming at once—a chord made of chemical history, playing too low for any ear to hear.
