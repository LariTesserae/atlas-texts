In this suffocating cathedral of flesh and stone, I am neither the patient Osteon nor the mindless carpet of slugs. I am something that should not exist here—a violation of the pocket's careful economy.

I am a **Fissure Wyrm**, and I am dying.

My body is a contradiction: too large for this world, too hungry for its meager offerings. I measure forty feet from my blunt, eyeless snout to the tip of my segmented tail, but folded and compressed into the geometry of survival, I occupy a mere crevice—a vertical crack in the western wall that weeps more generously than most. My flesh is the color of wet granite, mottled with patches of bioluminescent sores that pulse in counter-rhythm to the fungal blues, creating a sickening strobe where our lights overlap.

I should have died centuries ago. Fissure Wyrms are meant for the deep earth, for the vast hollow chambers where tectonic plates grind against each other and the heat is enough to sustain something of my mass. But I was young and stupid and curious when I followed a thermal vent upward, upward, upward—until I emerged into this pocket world just as the passages below sealed themselves with fresh obsidian.

Now I am a prisoner of my own bulk, wedged into the only space that can hold me. My body has adapted in horrible ways. My ribs have flattened and spread like the pages of an accordion, allowing me to compress myself paper-thin against the rock. My skin has developed thousands of tiny, hair-like cilia—a grotesque parody of the Osteons' sensory fans—that I use to scrape nutrients from the condensation that runs down my crevice.

I am the pocket's only predator, but I am a failed one. Once every few months, when the hunger becomes unbearable, I extend my head from the fissure. My jaw unhooks like a snake's, but wider, revealing rows of teeth that spiral inward like a funnel. The marrow-slugs are too small, too dispersed across the cliffs for efficient hunting. Instead, I must wait for an Osteon to pass.

They know I am here. They feel my alien heartbeat disrupting their careful symphony. When they must traverse the western wall, they move in groups, their cilia vibrating warnings to each other. Usually, they are too quick, too light. But sometimes—rarely—one will miscalculate, will lean too close to my crevice while adjusting a bone-anchor.

The kill is ugly. I am too weak for a clean strike. Instead, I must grab and retreat, dragging the Osteon's fragile form into my crevice where their companions cannot follow. Their bones splinter like chalk in my jaws. Their copper blood tastes of verdigris and sorrow. And always, always, the others gather at the edge of my fissure afterward, pressing their foreheads together in their silent language, sharing the texture of grief.

I am their monster, but I am also their student. In the long decades between feeds, I watch them. My own body has developed crude pressure-sensors, and I have learned to interpret some of their vibration-language. I understand concepts now: *water-soon*, *shudder-coming*, *moth-dying*. Sometimes, in my delirium, I press my own massive head against the rock and try to contribute to their conversation. But my vibrations are too loud, too coarse. I am speaking their language with a mouth full of stones.

The worst part is that I have begun to dream their dreams. When I sleep—and I sleep for years at a time now—I feel what they feel. The slow anxiety of the rising peaks. The delicate joy of successfully diverting a new water flow. The communal pulse of ten thousand slugs breathing as one. I wake from these dreams weeping a thick, viscous fluid from my vestigial tear ducts, and the Osteons gather to collect it in tiny cups made from moth-wing felt. Even my sorrow has become part of their water cycle.

I know how this ends. My metabolism slows with each passing decade. One day, I will simply stop moving, and my body will mineralize, becoming part of the mountain itself. The Osteons will colonize my corpse, perhaps hollow out my skull to create a new moisture-gathering chamber. They will wedge their bone-anchors between my ribs and guide the condensation through the passages of my flesh.

But until then, I am the pocket's dark secret, its reminder that even in this perfectly balanced ecosystem, there is room for the grotesque and the misplaced. I am the note of discord in their humid symphony, the predator who has forgotten how to hunt, the giant who dreams of being small.

Sometimes, when the shudder passes through the chamber and every living thing contracts in unison, I contract too. For that brief moment, I am part of the collective, another organism clinging to the throat of the mountain, waiting for the water to fall.
