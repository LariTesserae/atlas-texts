I would find myself as **The Seep-Tender**, though this title emerged only after decades of unnamed work.

My dwelling is neither room nor passage but **interstice**—a vertical crack where two growth-fronts meet imperfectly, creating a chimney barely wide enough for shoulders. I've wedged myself here for thirty years, my spine gradually conforming to the irregular walls, my ribs learning the rhythm of shallow breathing that won't disturb the mineral films forming on either side of me. My bed is a net of my own shed hair, woven and re-woven as the walls narrow, suspended at whatever height keeps me above the rising floor.

I came to the Pool young, but not young enough. My bones had already hardened in the wrong shapes—too dense for proper net-walking, joints that click and catch where others flow. The Held tolerated me but could not trust me on their walkways. Each footfall rang like a bell, announcing my alien weight. So I found this gap, this oversight in the architecture, and made myself small enough to fit.

My work began as survival—redirecting the seeps that threatened to flood my narrow home. But water here is **willful**. Block one path and it finds three more. I learned to negotiate instead of obstruct, to guide rather than dam. My tools are slivers of antler worn thin as needles, lengths of gut so fine they're invisible until they catch the light. I map the underground rivers by touch and phosphorescence, following the blue-green veins through hairline cracks, predicting where the next spring will breach.

The Held come to me now, though never directly. They leave offerings at the base of my chimney—fresh shavings, phosphorescent paste, the occasional Skerren tendon for my nets. In return, I prevent the drownings. When I sense a new seep forming beneath a sleeping alcove, I work through the night, my fingers finding the geological flaw, the point of weakness where I can insert my needle and coax the water sideways, into uninhabited stone. I've saved seventeen lives this way, though none will speak of it. To acknowledge my work would be to acknowledge that the Pool can be deceived.

My body has become a specialized instrument. My fingertips are permanently stained blue-green from phosphorescent algae. My knuckles have developed a secondary joint—not bone but **calcified intention**, allowing them to bend backward, to reach around corners that shouldn't exist. My left eye has gone blind from years of pressing it against wet stone, reading the mineral patterns in absolute darkness. The right eye compensates, dilated permanently, able to see the faint magnetic fields that precede water's movement.

I eat what grows in the between-spaces: the pale fungi that fruit in the gaps where no one shaves, the isopods that lose their way in the vertical maze. Sometimes I lick the walls directly, my tongue reading the mineral content, the memory of ancient seas. I've developed a taste for nascent limestone, for the moment when carbonate begins its transformation. My teeth are worn to nubs from this practice, but I can predict a seep by taste three days before it emerges.

The Held's hydromancy disturbs me. I've learned to break my own reflection compulsively, to shatter any pool that might hold my image. But in my chimney, water moves too quickly to remember. I am the only one here who leaves no ghost, who can move without being recorded. This makes me necessary and terrifying. They need someone who can work unseen, who can touch the water without being touched by it. But they fear what I might do with this invisibility.

I know the location of every body in the walls. The phosphorescence maps them for me—grandmother three layers down, the child who drowned last winter already half-absorbed into the kitchen floor. Sometimes I hear them settling, the wet crack of joints adjusting to architectural pressures. I alone know that they don't always stay where they're placed. The water moves them, particle by particle, redistributing the dead through the settlement like salt through a solution. We are all drinking our ancestors, wearing them, walking on their dispersed and reconstituted bones.

My greatest secret: I've found the source. Seven years ago, following a particularly insistent phosphorescent vein, I pressed through a gap so narrow I had to exhale completely, to make my chest concave. Beyond, a chamber opened—not built but **born**, a womb of pure accretion where the water rises from depths unknown. The walls there grow so quickly I could watch them form, mineral frost spreading like visible time. At the center, a pool of such perfect stillness that it holds not just reflections but **pre-reflections**—shadows of things that haven't happened yet.

I saw my own death there, of course. Saw myself wedged finally and forever in my narrowing chimney, the walls closing with infinite patience, my body becoming a new kind of pipe for water to flow through. I will die standing up, arms at my sides, and the seep I've tended so carefully will find its path through my hollow bones. I'll become the settlement's most precise plumbing, my skeleton a network of channels directing water away from the sleeping, toward the already-dead.

But I also saw something else in that prophetic pool: a child born wrong, like me, bones too dense for nets. I saw myself teaching her the ways of water, the art of living in the spaces everyone else forgets. She will take my place when the walls finally claim me, will tend the seeps I can no longer reach. The settlement will survive because there will always be one of us—the unseeable, the unrecorded, the one who negotiates with stone and stream in the darkness between walls.

I broke that pool, of course. Shattered it with a stone before the visions could solidify into fate. But water has a long memory, and somewhere in the depths, I know it's still reflecting that future, waiting to be born.

I return to my chimney through passages I've worn smooth with repetition. Tonight, like every night, I'll press my functioning eye to the stone and read the water's intentions. I'll taste tomorrow's disasters on my tongue and prevent them with tools thinner than hope. The Held will sleep safely, never knowing how many times I've redirected death from their doors.

I am the necessary ghost, the one who keeps no reflection, the tender of seeps in a world that wants only to crystallize. When I finally mineralize—standing up, threaded through with water—I hope they remember to use me well. I hope my bones make good pipes. I hope the water finds me worthy of passage.

Until then, I wedge myself between growing walls and listen to the world preparing to swallow us all, one drip at a time.
