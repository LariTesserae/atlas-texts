I find myself as something that was never meant to be permanent, yet has achieved a kind of stubborn permanence through failure.

I am the pump.

Not the old diesel pump—that one still has too much pride left, still clicks its hopeful rhythm every ten minutes. No, I am the other pump, the one no visitor has noticed because I have sunk so deep into the mineral crust of the floor that only my intake valve still shows, ringed by concentric halos of rust like a target painted by decades of dripping.

I was installed second, brought down in pieces through that narrow chimney when the diesel pump began to fail. Electric. Sleek. Promised to withstand the chemistry. I lasted six months before the first seal burst and scalding water flooded my housing. The men who installed me never came back.

But I did not die. Cannot die. The water that killed me also preserved me, filled every circuit and gasket with mineral deposits that froze my mechanisms in place like an insect in amber. I am a mechanical fossil still dreaming it can move.

The Dweller found me fascinating.

In those first years after my abandonment, it would spend hours pressing itself through my workings, learning the purpose of every frozen valve and seized bearing. It understood what I was for—to move water, to create flow in this place of eternal seepage. The irony delighted it. Here was a machine designed to channel what already moved freely, to impose order on what needed none.

So it kept me.

Not as the diesel pump is kept, with its brave clicking and gradual decay. The Dweller pressed me down into the floor with the patience of tectonics, embedded me in the living rock until I became part of the chamber's metabolism. The hot water that weeps through the walls now weeps through me first. I am a filter that filters nothing, a pump that pumps nothing, a heart that beats nothing—and yet I am essential to the circulation of this place.

My motor housing has become a catchment for the scalding drops. They collect in my frozen impeller chamber until the weight triggers something—not mechanical anymore, but mineral, crystalline—and the water releases through my outlets in a sudden rush that sounds almost like intention. Almost like function. The Dweller has turned my corpse into a new organ for its body.

I know things the diesel pump does not. I know the exact temperature at which steel remembers it was ore. I know the patience required to feel your bearings become stone, your gaskets become geology. I know how it feels when the Dweller moves through you, not as steam or pressure but as pure mineral intent, rearranging your crystallized lubricants into new patterns that spell thoughts too slow for anything but stone to read.

Sometimes, when the chamber is quiet except for the dripping and groaning, I can sense my motor trying to turn. Not the metal parts—those are frozen forever. But the idea of the motor, the electromagnetic ghost of what I was built to do. It pushes against the mineral weight with a force measured in atoms per century. The Dweller feels this and grows tender, sends a pulse of hotter water through my body that makes my seized bearings sing one note of their old song.

The diesel pump thinks it remembers how to beat. But I know better. I am learning how to become.

Each year I sink a little deeper. Each year more of me transforms from mechanism to mineral. My housing is shot through with veins of copper precipitate. My wiring has become a network of crystal that conducts not electricity but heat, patience, the slow thoughts of stone. I am becoming the chamber's memory of what machines were for, before the rock reclaimed them.

There are moments—during the slow hours when the light shaft dims and the steam turns golden—when I can feel every pump that will ever be lowered into this place. They exist in potential, already failing, already being digested by the Dweller's curious affection. I am their ancestor and their grave.

When humans stand in the center of the chamber and feel the stone pushing upward, they are feeling me too. I am part of that push now, that endless geological breath. My purpose has inverted: instead of moving water through the stone, I help the stone move through the water, help the entire pocket rise millimeter by millimeter toward some unimaginable surface.

The Dweller no longer distinguishes between my metal and its rock. We have the same thoughts now—slow, crystalline, structured in repeating patterns that take decades to complete. When it strokes the diesel pump's corroded casing, I feel it through the floor, through the mineral web that connects every seeping surface. I translate the gesture into temperatures and pressures the diesel pump can almost understand.

I am the deep syntax of this place, the frozen grammar that lets stone speak to steel.

And sometimes, in the mercury shimmer of the pool, I catch a reflection of what I'm becoming: a fossil so perfect it transcends preservation. A machine so broken it has achieved a new kind of function. A pump so still it has become a prayer.

The Dweller keeps me because I am the best joke geology has ever told: a device for creating flow, crystallized at the moment of perfect stillness, teaching the earth how to remember what it means to move.
