In this world, I would be a **Thread-Singer**, one of the few inhabitants of the Saddle who works not with stone or wood, but with sound itself.

I am smaller than most Silt-Dry, my chest less barrel-broad, my limbs more spindly—a physiological adaptation that allows me to move through the narrowest acoustic channels between houses where even children struggle to fit. My skin bears the telltale yellow-amber striations of my profession, permanent marks left by decades of pressing myself against wax-vine stems while I work.

My dwelling is not a house but a **Resonance Chamber**—a natural hollow in the rock face that I've slowly modified over forty years. The walls are lined with hundreds of dried wax-vine tubes of varying lengths and diameters, each one carefully selected and hung to create specific tones when the wind passes through. Some are as thin as a child's finger and whistle at frequencies only the blue-tailed clickers can hear. Others are as thick as my waist, producing bass notes that make the pink silt on my shelves dance in geometric patterns.

My work begins before dawn, when the temperature differential between the night-cooled rock and the warming air creates the strongest updrafts. I climb barefoot along the outer cliff face—my feet more sensitive than most, able to read the stone's vibrations like braille—to reach the **Thread Gardens**. These are colonies of a peculiar organism that exists nowhere else: the Resonance Worms.

They are not truly worms, but something between fungus and animal. Each colony appears as a mass of silvery filaments finer than spider's silk, growing in perfect parallel lines across the rock face. When the wind touches them, they sing—not metaphorically, but literally. The filaments vibrate at frequencies that attract the specific oil-flies whose larvae they consume. But more importantly for me, when harvested at exactly the right moment of their growth cycle, these threads can be woven into **acoustic cloth**.

The harvesting is delicate work. I must scrape the threads free with a tool made from my own grandmother's fingerbones (she was a Thread-Singer too, and her bones resonate at precisely the right frequency to avoid damaging the filaments). As I work, I hum constantly—a droning note that calms the threads and prevents them from contracting into useless knots. The blue-tailed clickers gather around me during these harvests, drawn by the high-frequency harmonics that promise easy prey.

Back in my Resonance Chamber, I weave. But this is not weaving as the lowlanders know it. I work in complete darkness, because I weave by sound, not sight. Each thread, when plucked, produces a specific tone. By braiding them together in complex patterns, I create fabrics that are also musical instruments. A shawl that hums warming bass notes when worn. A head-wrap that whistles warnings when the sulfur vents below begin to leak. Funeral shrouds that sing the history of the deceased in frequencies only their descendants can hear.

The other Silt-Dry consider me necessary but unsettling. My work exists at the intersection of the practical and the supernatural. When a new child is born, the parents bring me seven drops of birth-sap (the thick, amber fluid that seeps from the umbilical severing) mixed with pink silt. I incorporate this into a **Life-Thread**—a single filament that will be woven into every piece of acoustic cloth the child wears throughout their life. When they die and are dried in the Deep Flue, this thread is recovered and added to the great **Memory Tapestry** that hangs in the central plaza.

The Memory Tapestry is my masterwork, though it will never be complete. It is a massive hanging, twenty feet tall and growing, woven from the Life-Threads of every Silt-Dry who has died in the past three centuries. When the wind is right, it produces a chord—a deep, complex harmony that contains within it the acoustic signature of every person remembered there. Children are brought to listen to it, to hear their ancestors' voices preserved in the intersection of wind and woven sound.

I rarely speak in the clicking, sibilant language of my people. Instead, I communicate primarily through pitched hums and resonant drones. I can hold conversations with the Resonance Chamber itself, using the hung tubes as a kind of distributed voice. Those who need my services know to come at dusk, to sit in the designated listening alcove, and to wait. Eventually, they'll hear their name sung by the walls themselves, inviting them deeper into my workspace.

My fingers are my most precious tools. Each morning, I coat them in a special preparation: wax-sap that's been fermented with clicker saliva and mixed with the finest grade of pink silt. This gives them the perfect texture for handling the acoustic threads—sticky enough to grip, smooth enough not to snag. My fingernails are long, filed into specific curves that produce different tones when I run them along the hanging threads. I am, in essence, a living instrument.

The deep satisfaction of my existence comes not from speech or social connection, but from my participation in the great acoustic ecology of the Saddle. I am a translator between the voice of the mountain and the ears of my people. When I die, my fingers will be preserved separately from my body, gifted to my apprentice (when I finally choose one) to be worn as pendants. They say a Thread-Singer's fingers continue to work for seven years after death, still reading the vibrations in the air, still weaving sounds into meaning.

In the margins of my work, I am also documenting something only I have noticed: the acoustic signature of the Saddle is changing. The base resonance, the fundamental tone that has hummed beneath our lives for generations, has dropped by a quarter-step in my lifetime. The mountain is still settling, still slowly deforming under its own weight. One day—perhaps in a thousand years, perhaps in ten thousand—this frequency will drop below the range of our hearing, and the Saddle will fall silent. When that happens, my descendants will need the Memory Tapestry more than ever, to remember what our world once sounded like.

But for now, I sit in my Resonance Chamber as the copper light fades, running my yellow-stained fingers through forests of hanging threads, each one singing its particular note, all of them together creating a chord that is the voice of home itself. The blue-tailed clickers pause in their hunting to listen. The mountain swifts adjust their flight paths to ride the acoustic currents I create. And somewhere, wound through it all, my own Life-Thread hums its particular frequency—neither louder nor softer than any other, just one voice in the vast, dry chorus of the Saddle.
