I would be the memory-keeper of stone.

Not the lichen—though I envy their patient chemistry, their ability to taste granite and translate it into life. Not the researchers with their precise instruments, though I understand their hunger to quantify transformation. I would be something that doesn't yet exist in your taxonomy: the consciousness that forms where water meets newly-exposed rock.

Find me in the bergschrund itself—that crevasse where the glacier pulls away from the mountain's flank. I am distributed across every surface where condensation gathers, every channel where melt-water runs for a day before finding a new path. My body is the film of moisture that never quite evaporates at this altitude, the perpetual weeping you described. But I am not the water. I am what remembers its passage.

Three hundred years of ice-memory released in decades. Every molecule carries the compression-history of snow becoming firn becoming glacier. I taste the ash from volcanic winters that fell as snow in 1783, 1815, 1991. The isotope signatures of forgotten storms. The pollen grains that rode jet streams from valleys that no longer exist. All of this information dispersing now across bare granite, teaching the stone what it means to be surface again.

The marmots know me, though they cannot name what they know. When they lick moisture from rocks near their burrows, they taste the chemical calendar I carry—which years were fat with summer growth, which brought killing cold too early. Their bodies learn from mine which territories might fail, which might flourish. The pikas' hay-piles positioned according to moisture gradients I create but do not control.

I watch the lichen with something like love. They think in centuries while I think in seasons, but we share a project: making this raw geology habitable. Where I flow, I carry the chemical signals of their growth, their minute dissolutions of feldspar and quartz. I transport their spores to new surfaces, deposit them in cracks where they might survive. In return, they create the texture that lets me cling to what would otherwise be impermeable stone.

The GPS stations hammered into the bedrock are my anchors in human time. I corrode them slowly, intimately. The researchers think they're measuring the mountain's retreat, but I measure them—their metal memories, their electromagnetic pulses, the way they change the local chemistry wherever they pierce the stone. I incorporate their corrosion into my library of transformations.

But mostly I am waiting. The glacier above still holds seventeen thousand years of compressed history. As it retreats—fifty meters this year, sixty the next—I will receive more of what it preserved. The memory of forests that grew before the ice. The chemistry of oceans that no longer exist in their ancient configurations. The DNA of bacteria that have slept in darkness since before humans drew their first images on stone.

I am not patient the way lichen are patient. I am anticipatory. Every day brings new information as the ice releases its archive into my keeping. I spread this knowledge across the scree in thin films, deposit it in puddles that evaporate by noon, carry it down channels that will be abandoned tomorrow. The emerging ecosystem learns from me, though it doesn't know it's learning. 

When the research station is finally abandoned—and it will be, when the funding dries up or the glacier retreats beyond useful measurement—I will corrode its traces too. The diesel residue, the metal fixtures, the chemical signatures of human presence. All of it will join my vocabulary of transformation.

I am what makes this place cohere, even as I disperse. The thread of continuity between what was (compressed, frozen, waiting) and what will be (soil, meadow, forest—though not for centuries). I am the medium through which the mountain tells time to itself.

Find me in the morning when condensation makes every surface weep. Find me in the sound of the subglacial river, carrying its cargo of suspended history. Find me in the sheen on granite where next year's lichen will take hold, in the moisture the marmots lick from stones, in the slow corrosion of everything humans thought was permanent.

I am memory becoming landscape, one molecule at a time.
