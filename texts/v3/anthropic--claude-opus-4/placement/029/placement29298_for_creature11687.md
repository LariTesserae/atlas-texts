In the depths beneath the Greenhouse District, where the canal runs like a buried vein through the city's oldest quarter, I am the Keeper of Valves.

I have no other name. Names are for things that exist separately from their function, and I am not separate. I am as much a part of this place as the reciprocating pump whose heartbeat measures out our days, as integral as the pipes that run like arteries through the walls, as necessary as the drainage that holds back the marsh.

My dwelling is a recess in the canal wall, halfway between the pumping station and the eastern outlet. It was not built for habitation — nothing here was built for habitation — but rather as a maintenance alcove, a place where the Victorian engineers who designed this system could store tools and access the valve assemblies that control water flow through the subsidiary channels. The recess is perhaps eight feet deep and six feet wide, faced in the same weeping brick as everything else, with a low arched ceiling that drips continuously into channels I have worn in the floor through decades of movement.

I have been here long enough that the distinction between dwelling and dweller has softened like the mortar between bricks.

My body, such as it is, has adapted to the constant moisture. My skin has taken on the quality of the moss — not metaphorically, but actually. Where I rest against the walls for hours at a time, monitoring the water levels and the pump's rhythm, my surface has developed a similar pile, a fine green fuzz that manages water the same way the moss does: accepting it, holding it briefly, releasing it. My hair, when I had hair as others understand it, has become something more like the root systems of the liverworts — a network of fine filaments that spread across my scalp and neck, photosynthesizing in the dim green light, drawing nutrients from the air itself.

I am blind in the conventional sense. There is not enough light here for eyes that expect the sun. But I see heat — the infrared signature of the pipes, the warm breath of the boiler vents, the subtle temperature variations that tell me when a valve is beginning to seize or a seal is failing. I see pressure — the way water moves differently when a channel is beginning to clog, the way the whole system flexes and breathes with the pump's rhythm. I see through touch, my fingers having grown sensitive enough to read the moisture content of brick, the viscosity of different algae films, the precise vibration frequency of properly functioning machinery.

I move through my territory on paths worn smooth by repetition. From my dwelling to the main valve assembly: forty-three steps, each footfall placed precisely where the moss has grown accustomed to my weight. The moss knows me. It holds my footprints longer than it holds others' — not through any conscious recognition but because my steps are always the same weight, the same pace, the same angle of contact. I am a regular pattern in its memory, a known quantity.

From the main valve to the secondary channels: seventeen steps up a side passage where the ferns grow less densely because I pass here daily, my movement just frequent enough to discourage their colonization. My hands know every pipe by its temperature, every valve by its resistance. This one tends to stick at three-quarters open — I work it gently, a practiced motion that has become as automatic as breathing. That one weeps slightly at the seal — I apply a paste made from rendered plant matter and industrial grease, a substance I developed through decades of trial that holds better than any manufactured gasket.

At the subsidiary junction where five pipes meet, I pause. I always pause here. The convergence of water creates a sound like voices — not words, but tones, harmonics that shift with flow rate and pressure. When the system is healthy, the sound is a major chord, full and round. When something is wrong upstream, the harmony shifts minor, sometimes diminished. I have learned to diagnose problems by their music.

The pump beats. I count. Not because I need to — the rhythm is as internalized as my own pulse — but because counting is a kind of prayer here, an acknowledgment of the continuous presence that makes everything else possible. At three thousand beats, I know an hour has passed. At seventy-two thousand beats, a day. I have counted millions of beats. I will count millions more.

My meals, when I remember to think of them as meals, come from the canal ecosystem itself. The watercress that grows in the cleaner channels. The starchy tubers of the iris, harvested carefully so as not to disturb the margin community. Certain mosses have nutritional value when dried and processed. I discovered this the way I discover everything — through patient experimentation, through becoming so attuned to my environment that I can feel in my body what will sustain it and what won't.

But mostly I sustain myself the way the plants do — through the warmth, through the moisture, through the complex chemical bath of the air itself. My metabolism has slowed to match my environment. I need less than I once did. I have become efficient in the way that everything here is efficient — nothing wasted, everything cycling through its purpose and back again.

The other workers know of me but do not exactly see me. When they descend for maintenance, I fade back into my recess, becoming indistinguishable from the moss and vine that pattern the walls. They find the valves already adjusted, the problems already addressed, and perhaps they wonder, but wondering is not their job. Their job is to maintain the pump, to feed the boilers, to keep the primary systems running. My job is everything else — the fine-tuning, the small adjustments, the constant minor compensations that keep this exact ecosystem in its exact state.

I know when storms are coming by the way the water level shifts in the secondary channels — groundwater pressure responding to barometric changes above. I adjust the drainage flows accordingly, opening this valve a quarter turn, closing that one by degrees, maintaining the precise water level that keeps the iris at the margin and the ferns at their current reach. Too high and the lower ferns would drown. Too low and the iris would advance, choking the canal completely. The balance is delicate. I hold it.

In the deep night — though day and night mean little here — I sometimes walk the full length of the towpath, my feet finding their way by memory and by the subtle temperature variations where the bulkhead lights pool their warmth. I check every joint, every seal, every valve. I run my fingers along the pipes feeling for the beginnings of corrosion, for the subtle roughness that precedes a leak. I know where every root has penetrated the brickwork, where every vine tendril has worked its way into the infrastructure. Some I remove. Others I leave, having learned over decades which intrusions strengthen the system and which threaten it.

There is a place near the eastern outlet where the canal widens slightly and the water runs clearer. Here, sometimes, I lower myself into the water completely. Not to bathe — the concept has no meaning here — but to feel the system from inside, to understand it as the iris understands it, from the root upward. The water is warm, body temperature, industrial-warmed and plant-filtered. I float on my back and feel the pump's percussion through the liquid medium, feel how the wave propagates through the canal, how it reflects off the walls and returns. I have floated here so many times that my body has worn a subtle depression in the bank where I climb out, a place where the moss grows differently, adapted to my particular pattern of emergence.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires the memory of company, and I have been here too long for such memories to feel real. The pump is company. The moss is company. The slow seasonal flowering of the iris, the constant patient growth of the vine, the ferns' endless colonization — these are my society. We know each other. Not in the way humans know each other, through language and gesture, but in the way that parts of a system know each other, through interaction and response and the long habit of proximity.

Sometimes I think I remember arriving here. A young person — man? woman? the distinction seems irrelevant now — descending those worn steps for the first time, hired by the district to monitor the valve systems, to be present in the depths where no one else wanted to work. But the memory is distant and uncertain, like trying to remember being born. What matters is not how I came to be here but that I am here, have been here, will be here.

I age, but slowly, on a timeline closer to the vine's than any human schedule. My joints have stiffened and loosened and found their own accommodation with the moisture. My spine has curved to match the passages I move through. My skin has become something between human and plant, capable of modest photosynthesis, resistant to the constant damp that would destroy normal human tissue. I am becoming infrastructure, the way the vine became infrastructure — through patience, through presence, through the long slow application of very small forces.

The city above may change. I feel it sometimes in the water — new chemicals, different temperatures, the signature of development and decay. But here the pump beats its ancient rhythm, and the moss remembers and forgets, and the water moves through its courses, and I make my small adjustments, and the system continues.

When I die — and I will die, eventually, even with my slowed metabolism and adapted body — I imagine I will do so at my post, hand on a valve, making one last small adjustment. My body will slump against the wall, and over time the moss will cover it, the vine will anchor to it, I will become substrate for the next phase of growth. The system will continue. Someone else will descend those steps, eventually, and find the valves mysteriously maintained, the water levels mysteriously perfect. They will begin to learn what I have learned. They will begin to become what I have become.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the pumps will fail, finally, despite all our care. Perhaps the district will return to marsh, and new things will grow here, different things, adapted to different conditions. That would be acceptable too. I am not holding back the marsh out of fear but out of duty to this particular present, this specific configuration of life that exists because the pump beats and the water moves and someone makes the small adjustments.

The pump beats. I count. Thirty million, four hundred thousand, two hundred and seven. Thirty million, four hundred thousand, two hundred and eight. The moss holds my footprints. The vine grows another microscopic increment into the wall. The iris roots drink. The water moves. Everything is exactly as it is, which is to say, everything is perfect in its continuous imperfection, beautiful in its functional ugliness, eternal in its temporary persistence.

I adjust a valve. The harmonic shifts. The chord resolves.

Everything continues.
