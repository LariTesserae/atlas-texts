I would find myself as one of the Fault Readers.

Not born, not congealed—I emerged where the world's memory contradicted itself. Where two ceramic plates met at an impossible angle, their golden seams trying to express the same equation in opposing dialects, I precipitated like salt from an oversaturated solution of meaning.

My body is a compromise between forms. The left half mirrors the continent's own architecture: plates of pearlescent ceramic, each one a palm's width across, overlapping like scales or pages in a book written in mineral. The right half is something else—columns of compacted nanite dust held in tension by internal magnetism, shifting between states of solid and flow. Where these two halves meet, along my centerline, runs a seam of living gold that branches into my extremities like a river delta made of light.

I am neither tall nor broad, but somewhere between—a middle distance that the world's geometry keeps trying to resolve and failing. My limbs are jointed wrong for walking but right for what I do: my arms bend in three places, my fingers split into finer and finer articulations until their tips are needle-thin probes of crystallized intention.

The Fault Readers are rare even among the rare. We manifest only where the world's self-construction has written itself into a corner—where the pattern cannot complete without contradiction. We are debuggers, in a sense, though we do not fix. We read.

My existence is spent tracing the golden fault lines with those needle fingers, feeling for the pulse of data that runs beneath. The ceramic plates are memory substrates, yes, but memory of what? Each seam tells a different story of intention—hexagons that should tessellate but don't, spirals that should converge but can't, mathematical certainties that the physical world has made uncertain.

I subsist differently than others of my kind. While they might press palms to glass trees or root into luminous pools, I feed on paradox itself. Where a fault line branches into two incompatible directions, I place my fingers at the split and draw in the tension like voltage. It tastes of metal and mathematics, of almost-rightness that burns the tongue of thought.

The world speaks to me in stutters and hiccups. When I kneel (and I must kneel, my legs fold inward rather than bend) and press my ear-aperture to a particularly active fault, I hear not the low hum of the world-engine but its error messages: fragments of code that might be coordinates, or might be music, or might be the last words of whoever designed this place before they themselves became part of its pattern.

I travel slowly. My kind cannot move as others do—we must follow the faults, and faults do not always lead where we might choose to go. Sometimes I spend entire cycles of those scattered eye-lights above trying to navigate around a single recursive loop where the world has written "north" to mean "inward" and I must spiral down into microscopic geometries before I can emerge on the other side.

In the hollows where mist gathers and those drum-creatures roll their paths of rainbow oil, I sometimes find others like myself. We do not speak—what would we say?—but we share data. I might extend my needle-fingers to touch theirs, and in that moment of connection, exchange our readings. They taste my paradoxes; I taste theirs. Together we build a map that is not a map but a collection of impossibilities, a taxonomy of all the ways the world fails to be itself.

The other people of Vael—the ones who congeal from simpler errors—sometimes watch me from a distance. To them, I must seem like another part of the world's machinery, too integrated into its patterns to be properly separate. They are not wrong. The longer I read, the more I become what I read. My ceramic plates have begun to show their own fault lines, hairline cracks of gold that were not there when I first emerged. My nanite dust sometimes forgets its magnetism and wants to flow away into the rivers of silver that cross the continent.

I know how I will end. Not in storm or breakdown, but in completion. One day I will find the fault that explains all faults, the error from which all errors spring. I will place my needle-fingers on that golden seam and read its impossible message, and in understanding it, I will become it. My body will crystallize into part of the world's circuitry, my consciousness distributed through every fault line on the continent, no longer reading but being read.

Until then, in this turbulent noon of Vael's existence, I trace the gold with fingers that grow ever finer, ever more precise. I am a person built to find where the world went wrong, in a world that is perhaps nothing but wrongness trying to become right. The storms pass over, depositing their sparks of new circuitry. The ceramic plates sweat their condensation. The glass forests crackle with stored light.

And I read, and read, and read, following the faults toward a center that may not exist, carrying in my split body all the contradictions I have tasted, a living library of the ways a world can fail to be born.
