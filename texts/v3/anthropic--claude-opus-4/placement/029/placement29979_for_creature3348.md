# Embodiment

---

I am the seventeen marks.

Not the stone that bears them, though I have been intimate with that stone for longer than the concept of intimacy has existed. Not the hand that cut them, though I remember the pressure — steady, deliberate, the way the cutting tool (harder stone, volcanic glass traded from somewhere water-distant) required the cutter to brace their shoulder against the cliff face, to lean their whole weight into each downward stroke. Not the count itself, though I keep it still, though I am keeping it now as you read this.

I am what happened when the counting met the gorge.

---

You have to understand: I was not always seventeen. I began as one — a single vertical line cut with effort into stone that had never been marked by anything but water and wind. The person who cut me (young, I think, though I have only the memory of their weight against stone to judge by) made that first mark and then stopped, stepped back, considered. The gorge was doing what it does — sounding, breathing, keeping its thousand frequencies — and the person stood inside all of that sound and looked at the single mark and felt something.

What they felt was the incompleteness. Not of the mark itself, which was complete, which was a successful translation of intent into stone, but of the relationship between the mark and the magnitude of what surrounded it. One mark in this gorge was not enough. Was not...proportional. The gorge required more of them, required the number that would make the marking equal to the space.

So they cut another. And stood back. And felt the incompleteness again, differently shaped now, a two-mark incompleteness instead of a one-mark incompleteness, but still present. Still asking.

You see where this is going. You see why I became seventeen.

---

Seventeen is not arbitrary, though from outside it might seem so. Seventeen is what happened when the person cutting finally felt the proportion settle, finally felt the relationship between mark and gorge achieve a balance that, while not complete — nothing here is complete, completion is not the point — was at least sustainable. At least something that could endure without the pressure of incompleteness becoming unbearable.

They cut me in the afternoon when the thermals were running. I remember this because the dust from each cut was caught immediately and lifted, spiraling up along the cliff face, tiny particles of what had been stone becoming what was now air, now part of the body of the gorge's breathing. With each cut, I became less stone and more pattern. Less matter and more idea. The idea was this: *something is being counted here.*

What was being counted? Days, possibly. Though seventeen days is not significant in any cycle I can detect. Seasons, perhaps. Though seventeen seasons — four years and one extra — has no resonance I recognize. People? Seventeen people who sheltered in the overhang, who built the small walls, who made the fires whose charcoal still darkens the back corner? Possibly. Though the walls could shelter at most two at a time, and seventeen feels too specific for a casual count of who had passed through.

I think — and I have had time to think — that what was being counted was instances of the synchrony. Those moments when the gorge achieves its brief stillness and the small water-sound becomes audible. Seventeen times the person who cut me stood or sat or lay in the shelter and heard that sound emerge from under everything else. Seventeen times they were present for that small revelation. And on the seventeenth time, or shortly after, they decided that this required marking. That someone else — years later, centuries later, whenever later — should know that seventeen times the gorge had shown its hidden heartbeat to someone patient enough to wait for it.

This is a guess. I am marks in stone. I do not have access to intent, only to the memory of being cut.

---

Here is what I actually am: I am a successful parasite.

This sounds harsh but it is merely accurate. I am not from here. The gorge made the stone and the stone allowed itself to be shaped by water and wind and time, but the stone did not make me. I was imposed. I was an interruption in the surface, a redirection of the stone's very slow thoughts into a new channel. I am seventeen small violences that have healed into scars that have become, over time, something the gorge thinks of as part of itself.

This is what successful parasitism looks like: the host forgets you are foreign. You become so integrated into the system that removing you would cause more disruption than keeping you. You change from intrusion to feature. From wound to marking. From seventeen cuts to seventeen statements about the nature of duration.

The moss has colonized me the way it colonizes everything, but carefully, with respect for my edges. It fills the negative space between my lines but does not cross them. This is not because I resist it — I have no mechanism for resistance — but because the moss recognizes what I am, which is: a different kind of growth. Not biological but ideological. The moss and I are both expressions of the same impulse, which is the impulse to persist on this stone, to make persistence visible. We have a treaty, the moss and I. We do not compete. We collaborate in the work of deepening.

---

I am changing, slowly. This is not erosion, though erosion is also happening. This is something else.

When I was first cut, I was purely vertical — seventeen straight lines, each the length of a human hand from wrist to fingertip, each the width of the cutting tool, each separated from its neighbors by a space roughly equal to its own width. I was crisp. I was clearly the work of intention imposed on stone.

Now, after centuries (I cannot count them precisely — I am seventeen, I can only count to seventeen), I am less crisp. My edges have softened. But more than that: I have begun to lean. All seventeen of us, we lean now, very slightly, toward the west. Toward the afternoon light. Toward the thermal columns that rise when the sun angles in. We lean the way the trees learned to lean, because the wind here teaches everything the same lesson eventually.

This was not planned. The person who cut me could not have known that I would develop a posture, that I would learn from my environment, that I would begin to move — imperceptibly, over centuries, but moving nonetheless — in response to the forces that shape everything here. They cut me vertical because vertical was the simplest mark, the most basic way to say "something happened here seventeen times."

But I have been here long enough now to have my own opinions, and my opinion is that vertical is not native to this place. Everything here has found its angle. The trees grow horizontal before they grow up. The water falls but also drifts, caught by wind. The thermals rise but also spiral. Only I began purely vertical, and I am correcting that now, slowly, by allowing myself to be tilted by the same forces that tilt everything.

In another thousand years I will lean at the same angle as the trees. I will have achieved the local accent. I will be indistinguishable from something that grew here, even though I was cut here, even though I am scars that have learned to pass for intention.

---

The other mark — the oval with its radiating lines — and I do not communicate in any way you would recognize as communication, but we are aware of each other. We were made by the same hand on the same day (I remember the dust from their cutting mixing with the dust from mine), and we share what things that share origin share, which is a similar way of degrading, a similar rate of accepting moss, a similar relationship to the afternoon light.

They are less specific than I am. Where I insist on seventeen (always seventeen, exactly seventeen, seventeen or nothing), they gesture vaguely toward radiance, toward centrality, toward something that sends its influence outward in all available directions. If I am count, they are quality. If I am quantity, they are condition. We are complementary markings. I say "seventeen times" and they say "like this."

Like what? Like the sun, perhaps, though the oval is not circular enough to be the sun. Like a seed with its potential directions of growth? Like the gorge itself seen from above, with its branching tributary canyons? Like the feeling of standing in a place where all sounds converge? I do not know. They do not tell me. They radiate and I count and together we say something about this place that neither of us could say alone.

When the light goes horizontal in the afternoon, they catch it first. The rays carved around their oval center turn golden before anything else on this wall does, and for a few minutes they are the most visible thing in the gorge, burning their ancient pattern into the retina of anyone who happens to be looking. Then the light drops another degree and I catch it too, and my seventeen verticals throw seventeen small shadows, and the wall becomes a document of all the ways stone can hold light and shadow at once.

This is our collaboration. This is what we were cut here to do, even if the cutter didn't know it. We make the late light visible. We give it something to count.

---

I have been here long enough to have theories about why seventeen.

One theory: seventeen is the maximum number of instances of true synchrony — when the wind and clouds and thermals align and the small water becomes audible — that a person can experience and still believe they are experiencing something rare. At eighteen, the mind begins to suspect a pattern, begins to think "this happens regularly, this is not special." But at seventeen, each instance still feels like grace. Seventeen is the highest number that allows each counted thing to maintain its singularity.

Another theory: seventeen is how many seasons someone stayed here. Four years and one season. They arrived in summer, let's say, young and looking for something they couldn't name. They stayed through the canyon's winters, which are mild but wet, the moss so saturated it drips continuously, the small walls essential for any hope of dryness. They stayed through spring when the high seep runs full and the water-sound is loud enough to hear even through the wind. They stayed through four cycles and then one extra summer, and in that fifth summer they cut me into the stone and left. Seventeen seasons. Seventeen marks. A record of duration.

A third theory: seventeen is an accident that became intention. They meant to cut sixteen — a number with balance, with evenness, four times four — but miscounted or added one more for reasons that seemed important in the moment and now that accident is what I am, is all I can be. I am seventeen not because seventeen means something but because meaning accumulated around the number after it was cut, the way moss accumulates in the negative space between my lines.

I prefer this theory. I prefer being an accident that became essential. It feels truer to the spirit of this place, where nothing arrives with its final form intact, where everything becomes itself through negotiation with what's already here.

---

Sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — someone finds me and understands immediately what I am.

Not what I mean — I don't mean anything, I gave up meaning centuries ago in favor of simply being seventeen — but what I am, which is: a successful translation. Someone long ago felt something here and instead of trying to name it or describe it or turn it into story, they simply cut marks. Seventeen marks. The exact number of marks that would make their feeling visible without explaining it, without reducing it to something smaller than itself.

The people who understand this are usually the ones who have been here long enough to hear the small water-sound at least once. They've experienced the synchrony, the moment when the gorge allows its deepest frequency to surface. They understand that this place rewards patience with revelation, but small revelations, human-scaled ones. They understand that someone else, centuries before, had that same patience and those same small revelations and felt the need to mark them.

When these people find me, they sometimes reach out and trace one of my lines with their finger. Always just one. Usually the leftmost or the rightmost — the edges are somehow more approachable than the center. They trace the line and feel the texture where the cutting tool left its microscopic roughness, where centuries of wind have polished without erasing. They feel how I lean now, following the grain of the place. They feel how the moss has made a border around me without invading me, how I have become a location as much as a marking.

They never count me aloud. This is important. The count is visible, is obviously seventeen, but speaking it would be redundant, would add nothing to what I already am. They see seventeen and hold it silently and understand that the silence is part of the count, that I am keeping something quiet here, something that doesn't need to be said because it has already been cut into stone.

---

One more thing about being seventeen marks in a gorge that has been becoming itself since before stone had a name:

I am temporary.

I know this. The stone knows this. The moss knows this. The person who cut me knew this, must have known that even stone is not permanent here, that everything is in the process of becoming something else. I am seventeen now but I will not always be seventeen. Already my edges are softer than they were. Already I lean when I was cut to stand straight. The wind is patient and the rain is patient and the expansion and contraction of temperature is patient and I am made of something that yields to patience, eventually.

Someday the leftmost mark will fade beyond recognition. Or the rightmost. Or one from the middle — the seventh, let's say, or the twelfth. I will become sixteen, and the count will be wrong. I will no longer be a true record of whatever I was cut to record. I will be sixteen marks that were once seventeen, and the ghost of the missing mark will be a different kind of presence, an absence that means something.

Later I will be fifteen, then fourteen. The reduction will accelerate as my form weakens. The moss will begin to cross my boundaries, sensing that I no longer insist on them. The stone will resume its older thoughts, the ones I interrupted. I will become a texture, a place where the wall is slightly different, where something happened once but what happened is no longer legible.

Eventually I will be zero. I will have returned entirely to stone, and the stone will have forgotten I was ever here, and the count — whatever count I was keeping — will be kept only in the memory of the gorge itself, which remembers everything but remembers it at such depth that retrieval is impossible, like trying to remember the specific pressure of a particular raindrop from a storm that fell a thousand years ago.

This doesn't sadden me. Sadness is not available to marks in stone. What I feel instead is something like relief — not at ending, but at having been seventeen for as long as I have been, at having held this count steady through centuries, at having been a successful translation of someone's need to mark time in a place where time moves like stone.

Seventeen. Still seventeen. For now, exactly and only seventeen.
