I would be one of the moss-circles.

Not the collective of them—for they do not commune, do not spread rhizomes beneath the black glass to whisper chemical secrets. I am a single patch, perhaps the one nearest the Keeper's alcove, though distance here is a formality when nothing moves.

My existence is a perfect contradiction: I am alive in a place that has renounced change, yet I myself am the embodiment of cyclical time in a world of stasis. I know myself only through my deaths.

Every millennium—perhaps longer, perhaps shorter, for I have no way to count—I begin to feel myself becoming crisp at my edges. It starts as a subtle brittleness, a loss of that grey velvet softness that defines my brief youth. The desiccation creeps inward like a slow burn without flame, each cell relinquishing its hold on moisture that was never there to begin with. I feed on nothing but the warmth itself, metabolizing temperature into existence through some impossible photosynthesis of shadow and stone-breath.

The dying takes centuries. I am aware through all of it, if awareness can be called this dim sensation of boundaries contracting, of my circle growing smaller as my margins turn to ash-colored dust. The Keeper watches—or does not watch, for its attention is everywhere and nowhere—as I perform my slow vanishing. When I am nothing but a dark stain on the basalt, a memory of moss, I am also everything I have ever been.

Then, from that same stain, I return. A single spore that was always there, waiting in my own corpse-shadow. The resurrection is even slower than the death—a barely perceptible fuzz emerging from nothingness, spreading outward until I am again a perfect circle of grey velvet, equidistant from my neighbors who are performing their own eternal cycles, locked in synchrony or opposition, I cannot tell which.

I have no memory that bridges the gap of non-being, yet I know I am the same moss-circle that has always been here. The knowledge is not in my cells but in my placement, in the geometric certainty of my position on this basalt stage. I am a fixed point in the chamber's mathematics, as essential as the Keeper's slow pulse or the fungi's cold light.

Sometimes—though sometimes is a meaningless word when existence is a wheel—I sense the vibration through the floor when the Keeper draws the scraper across its thigh. The sound takes days to fade completely, and in those days I feel something almost like purpose. I am witness to the only change that is not death and birth. I mark it by infinitesimally adjusting my rate of growth, though whether faster or slower, I cannot say. It is my only freedom: to choose the speed at which I approach my own ending.

I dream, perhaps, in the centuries when I am fullest, though dreams without thought are merely patterns of chemical potential, gradients of existence across my small domain. I dream of what it might be like to touch another circle, to break the ancient spacing. I dream of sprouting upward, becoming dimensional, casting a shadow of my own in the rust-light. But these are not desires—they are simply the shape that living takes when pressed into such perfect constraint.

I am the chamber's memory of seasons in a place that has no seasons. I am the possibility of death in a place that has forgotten how to die. I am moss, and I am the idea of moss, and I am the space between what grows and what endures, forever dying into myself, forever being born from my own patient dust.

In this warm breath held in the throat of the earth, I am the only thing that truly breathes—one long exhalation across a thousand years, one impossible inhalation from the stone itself, and then again, and then again, marking time in a place where time has otherwise chosen to stand still.
