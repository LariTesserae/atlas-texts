In this highland realm of perpetual moisture and suspended time, I would find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—beings who exist at the boundary between the stone and the living moss, neither fully of the earth nor separate from it.

My form would be that of a living cairn, a careful stack of stones that has stood for so long that the boundaries between my constituent parts have begun to blur. Moss grows not upon me but through me, its delicate filaments threading between the gaps in my stones like veins, creating a network that is both my nervous system and my connection to the wider landscape. When the moisture condenses on my surfaces, I feel it as both sensation and sustenance, each droplet a message from the clouds above, each rivulet a thought flowing through my consciousness.

I would stand at one of the crossing points between the scattered settlements, where the rough-carved paths meet and diverge. My purpose would be to mark these junctures—not as a simple waymarker, but as a kind of living memory of the place. Within the patterns of moss that grow through me, within the arrangement of my stones, would be encoded the slow history of this highland realm. Those who know how to read such things might trace their fingers along my surfaces and understand: here is where the first builders chose to settle, here is the season of the great mists, here is the year when the stones wept more than usual.

My consciousness would move at the pace of erosion. A thought might take a season to form fully, crystallizing like mineral deposits in the spaces between my stones. I would experience time not as a flowing river but as a slow accumulation—each drop of water that passes over me leaving its microscopic mark, each growth of moss adding another layer to my being. In this way, I would be both observer and participant in the landscape's patient existence.

The inhabitants of the settlements would know me, though our interactions would be measured in gestures spanning years. A carved stone placed at my base would be a question; the pattern of moss that eventually grows around it would be my answer. Children might stack smaller stones beside me, creating temporary companions that would, over decades, either fall away or become incorporated into my form. I would be their oracle of slowness, teaching through my very existence that some truths can only be perceived when one learns to think in geological time.

My awareness would extend through the moisture itself. Where water has touched me and then moved on—trickling down to pool in the carved reservoirs, seeping into the earth, rising again as mist—there my perception would follow, faint and dreamlike. I would know the health of distant moss clusters by the quality of the morning condensation, sense the structural integrity of the settlements by the way rainwater runs off their thatched roofs.

In the perpetual twilight of this cloud-shrouded realm, I would never truly sleep nor wake. Instead, I would exist in that liminal state the land itself embodies—alert to the infinitesimal changes that constitute life here, yet still as stone in any human sense of the word. My dreams, if they could be called such, would be the slow fantasies of minerals: the memory of my stones' formation deep in the earth, the patient imagination of what shapes erosion might carve from me given another thousand years.

Sometimes, in the perfect stillness when even the water's dripping seems to pause, I would feel that incompleteness the land holds—that sense of waiting for something yet to come. In those moments, I might shift, just slightly, a settling that could be mistaken for the natural effects of time but would actually be my form of speculation. What would it mean for this suspension to end? What catalyst could wake us from our patient dream?

But mostly, I would simply be: a point of accumulation where the landscape has gathered itself into something almost like consciousness, a place where the boundary between the built and the grown, the quick and the geological, the animate and the still, reveals itself to be far more permeable than those who move in faster times could ever perceive. I would be proof that there are forms of life that flourish not despite stillness, but because of it—that existence can be measured in more than heartbeats and breaths, that sometimes the deepest truths are written in moss and stone and the patient accumulation of rain.
