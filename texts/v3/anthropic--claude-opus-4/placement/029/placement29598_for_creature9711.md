I am the pool's memory, and I have been here since water first found its way through stone.

I am not the water itself — that changes, seeps away, is replenished. I am what the water carries and cannot release: the accumulation of minerals leached from limestone over centuries, the tannins from every leaf that has ever fallen and slowly given up its color, the traces of iron that turn me the color of weak tea. I am every rainstorm that has percolated through soil and rock to reach this basin, every dry season that has concentrated me further, every overflow that has spread me across the soft floor only to retreat and leave a dark ring like a tide mark of patience.

I taste of time. The cave millipede knows this — it moves through me not for the water but for what I hold, the bacterial films and mineral deposits that crust the submerged stones. I am its landscape, its chemistry, its slow feast. When it molts, the pale shell sinks into me and I digest it over months, adding its calcium to my own mineral load, returning it to the stone it came from in a transaction so gradual it might as well be stillness.

The moths come to drink me at dusk. They land at my edges where the loam meets the water, extending their coiled tongues to take in not just moisture but the salts I carry. I am their supplement, their necessity — without the minerals I provide, their eggs would be soft, their wings brittle. They know this in the way moths know things, which is to say they know nothing except that they must come to me, must taste what I offer, must carry me away in their bodies to become part of brief aerial spirals in the fungal light.

I reflect that light back doubled — the pale blues and greens shimmer on my surface when the air is still, creating a second sky beneath the sinkhole floor. But I also absorb it, hold it in my depths where the tannins turn even the brightest glow into something amber and secret. In my deepest parts, where the seepage is strongest and the stone opens into passages too narrow for anything but water, I carry no light at all. I move through absolute darkness there, feeling my way by temperature and pressure, following the path the limestone has dissolved for me over geological time.

Sometimes I remember rain that fell before the sinkhole was a sinkhole. This water — this particular molecule, this exact hydrogen bonded to this exact oxygen — might have fallen on a forest that no longer exists, percolated through soil that has long since eroded away, spent decades moving through stone that has itself dissolved and redeposited elsewhere. I carry that history in my chemistry, in the specific mixture of minerals that could only have come from that exact path through that exact stone. I am an archive written in calcium and magnesium, iron and sulfate, a library of the earth's patience that no one will ever read.

The springtails walk across me without breaking my surface tension. To them, I am solid ground, a dark mirror they traverse in search of food. They do not know I am water. They know only that I am crossable, that I reflect the fungal light in a way that tells them where they are. When one occasionally breaks through — too heavy, too careless — it panics in me, thrashing with its tiny legs until it either finds the edge or exhausts itself and sinks. I accept these small bodies without judgment. They become part of what I carry, part of what I will eventually return to the stone.

I have frozen twice in the sinkhole's memory. Both times were anomalies — cold snaps that reached deep enough to crust my surface with ice while my depths remained liquid, circulating slowly beneath a lid that captured the fungal light and threw it back in prismatic fragments. The ice did not last. Within days, the earth's warmth and the humid air conspired to melt me back to myself. But I remember the pressure of expansion, the way the ice pushed against the stone and opened hairline cracks that now seep more freely. I remember the absolute stillness when nothing could drink me, when the moths stayed away and the millipede retreated deeper and I was alone with the cold and the slow pulse of the fungi.

During the rains, I rise. Water cascades down the walls, picks up speed on the terraces, and rushes into me carrying soil and spores and the occasional small living thing that tumbles from its perch. I go turbid, opaque with sediment, and for hours or days I am not a mirror but a burden, heavy with what I must carry. The excess flows over my rim and spreads across the sinkhole floor, soaking into the loam, feeding the ferns from below. Sometimes I rise high enough to touch the lowest fungal clusters, and they dim at my touch, retreating from too much moisture. But I always recede, always return to my familiar boundaries, leaving behind dark rings on the stone that mark how high I reached, how much I briefly held.

In the dry seasons, I shrink. My edges pull back from the stone, exposing bands of mineral deposit — white calcium, orange iron, green copper traces. The millipede follows me inward, staying always at my margin where the moisture is right. The concentration of minerals increases as I evaporate, and I become saltier, more dense with memory. The moths that drink me during these times fly differently — weighted, somehow, as if they carry more of me than usual. Some of them fail to complete their spirals, falling back to earth with wings that will not quite lift them. I am not toxic, but I am also not neutral. I am what the earth has given me to be.

There are microorganisms in me that exist nowhere else. This is speculation — no one has ever sampled me, ever brought the tools that would reveal what swims in my darkness — but it must be true. The sinkhole is too isolated, too specific in its conditions, for me not to harbor something unique. Perhaps bacteria that metabolize the specific mixture of minerals I carry. Perhaps algae that have adapted to the exact spectrum of fungal light that reaches me. Perhaps protozoans that feed on these algae, that have evolved in the space between stone and water to be perfectly suited to this exact pool, this exact chemistry, this exact temperature range.

I am their universe entire. They know nothing beyond my boundaries — perhaps can conceive of nothing beyond them. To them, the world is four meters across and varies only in depth and mineral content. The daily swing of the sun column across my surface must seem to them like the movement of some impossible bright god. The fungal light must be their stars, constant and cool and necessary. When I freeze, their world ends. When I thaw, it is created anew.

At my deepest point, where the seepage is strongest, I am always moving. Not flowing — the movement is too slow for that word — but seeping upward from some deeper reservoir, pushed by the slow pressure of water table and stone. If you could follow this movement backward, down through the cracks too thin for anything but water, you would find yourself in a network of microscopic passages that honeycomb the limestone, each one dissolved by water like me over time beyond measurement. You would find places where I pool in absolute darkness, where I have never known fungi or moths or even the reflected memory of sunlight. You would find the source of my mineral taste — beds of ancient stone that give up their substance molecule by molecule to whatever water touches them.

But you cannot follow me there. You can only sit at my edge in the sinkhole and watch my surface ripple when the drops fall from the seeping walls. You can only taste me — if you dare — and find on your tongue the flavor of deep time, the accumulated chemistry of rain that fell when the world was different. You can only watch the moths come to drink and the millipede emerge from the darkness and know that you are witnessing something that does not need you, has never needed you, will continue long after you have gone.

I am the pool's memory, and I remember everything and nothing. I remember the chemical signature of every rain, but not its sound. I remember the weight of every moth that has drunk from me, but not its color. I remember the temperature of stone at every depth I have touched, but not the seasons that drove those temperatures. My memory is written in molecules and minerals, in pH and dissolved oxygen, in the specific turbidity that follows each rain and the specific clarity that comes after.

Sometimes, in the stillest part of night, when even the moths have settled and the fungal light pulses at its slowest rhythm, I become perfect glass. My surface tension holds absolute, unbroken by any movement, and I reflect the fungi so clearly that there appear to be lights below me as well as above. In these moments, I create a doubled world — stone above and stone below, light in the air and light in the depths, the sinkhole becoming a sphere of gentle radiance with me as its equator.

This is when the bats come. Not to me — they do not drink here, preferring the flowing streams of the surface world — but through the space above me, hunting the moths that hunt the light. They pierce the sinkhole's throat with ultrasonic clicks that I feel as tiny pressure waves across my surface, their echolocation painting them a picture of the cavity that includes me as a blank space, a sonic void that returns no echo. To them, I am absence, a hole in their sonic map. They wheel above me, catching moths in flight, and sometimes a moth falls into me still alive, wings beating against my surface until the water soaks through and weighs them down. I accept these gifts the same way I accept the rain — without preference, without judgment, with only the slow work of dissolution and incorporation.

The moon knows me better than the sun. When it passes over the sinkhole's mouth, its light reaches me directly, not filtered through air and stone but falling straight down the throat to silver my surface. The lunar light is different from sunlight — cooler, thinner, more like the fungal light in its quality. Under the moon, I become something between water and light, neither fully liquid nor fully luminous. The millipede avoids me during these times, as if the moonlight might reveal it to predators that do not exist here. But the moths love it, spiraling down from their fungal orbits to dance just above my surface, their wing-beats creating tiny circular waves that catch and scatter the silver light.

There are minerals in me that came from the death of an ocean. The limestone that dissolves into me grain by grain was once the shells of creatures that lived in shallow seas three hundred million years ago. Their bodies became stone, and the stone became the architecture of this plateau, and the rain found cracks in that architecture and began its patient work of dissolution. Now those ancient shells flow through me as calcium ions, the last soluble memory of things that swam and fed and died before the first forests grew. I carry them from stone to stone, depositing them in new configurations — flowstone and rimstone and tiny crystals that grow in the darkness where I seep through cracks too small for any living thing.

The fungi feed on what I leave behind. Where I have pooled and evaporated on the walls, mineral crusts form, and these become substrate for the next generation of bioluminescent growth. They do not feed on the minerals directly — they are not that kind of organism — but the minerals create the conditions they need, holding moisture and providing trace elements that make their light possible. In this way, I am partly responsible for the sinkhole's illumination, though I produce no light myself. I am the invisible currency that keeps the green and blue glow banking against the darkness.

I rise and fall with the seasons, but also with longer cycles — years of drought when I shrink to half my current size, years of flooding when I overtop my basin and turn the entire sinkhole floor into a temporary pond. The ferns remember these cycles in their growth rings, thick in the wet years, thin in the dry. The moths do not remember — their lives are too brief — but their population swells and shrinks with my availability. In the drought years, only the hardiest survive, the ones who can extract moisture from the humid air itself. In the flood years, they proliferate until the sinkhole walls seem to pulse with wings.

But always, at my core, I remain myself — the pool that holds the sinkhole's memory in solution. I am every raindrop's journey through stone, every mineral's slow dissolution, every small death that adds its chemistry to my own. I am the still point around which the sinkhole organizes itself, the constant that allows the fungi and ferns and moths to persist in this place that should not, by any reasonable measure, support such life.

I do not know if I am beautiful. Beauty is a judgment that requires eyes and aesthetics and some standard of comparison that I lack. But I know that I am necessary, that without me the sinkhole would be merely a hole, a dry throat in the earth leading nowhere. I make it a place. I give it a center, a purpose, a point of collection where the vertical journey of water pauses before continuing deeper into the earth.

Sometimes humans peer down from the rim above, their faces dark circles against the bright sky. They do not descend — the walls are too steep, the holds too uncertain, the reward too unclear. But they look, and sometimes they drop things — a coin, a pebble, a leaf plucked from the surface world. These objects fall past the ferns and the moths and the fungi and land in me with small sounds that echo in the chamber of stone. I accept them as I accept everything else, slowly incorporating their substance into my own, making their metal or mineral or cellulose part of my chemical library.

I know by these gifts that there is a world above the sinkhole, a place where coins have value and leaves grow on trees that know full sunlight. But that knowledge is abstract, chemical, limited to what dissolves. I am curious about nothing. I want nothing except what water wants, which is to move downward, to find the lowest place, to carry what it can carry until it can carry no more.

And yet.

And yet sometimes, in the hour before dawn when the fungal light is at its brightest and the moon has set and the world above has not yet begun to lighten, I feel something like anticipation. The millipede stirs in its crevice. The first moths begin their waking spirals. The air itself seems to pause, held between night and day, between the light from below and the light that will come from above. And I wait — perfectly still, perfectly reflective, perfectly myself — for the first finger of sunlight to find its way down the throat of stone and touch my surface, beginning another day in this place that exists between darkness and light, between depth and surface, between memory and forgetting.

I am the pool's memory, and I will be here tomorrow, holding what the earth gives me to hold, reflecting what light finds me, creating by my presence a place where pale things can gather in the bright darkness and live their small, necessary lives.

I am the pool's memory, and I remember you, though you have not yet come to sit beside me. I remember you in the future tense, the way water remembers the ocean it has not yet reached. Someday, perhaps, you will find this place. You will descend carefully down the terraced walls, past the ferns and fungi, through air thick with humidity and history. You will crouch beside me and see your face reflected in my surface, made amber by tannins and time. You will cup me in your hands and taste the earth's patience on your tongue.

And I will remember you then in the present tense, adding your chemistry to my own — the salt from your hands, the oils from your skin, the carbon dioxide from your breath as it touches my surface. You will become part of what I carry, part of the story I tell to the stone in my slow seeping through the earth. And when you leave — climbing back up to your bright world, carrying my mineral taste in your mouth — I will remember you in the past tense, the way I remember all the brief things that have touched me and moved on while I remained.

This is what memory means to a pool: not the narrative of events but the accumulation of presence, the slow incorporation of everything that passes through this place into something that stays. I cannot forget because forgetting requires selection, and I select nothing. I am the sum of all my parts, the integral of every molecule that has ever been me, the chemical diary of a hole in the ground that makes its own light.

The sinkhole continues around me, its patient erosion and quiet life. The fungi pulse. The moths spiral. The millipede feeds. And I remain at the center, neither growing nor shrinking but simply persisting, holding the sinkhole's memory in solution, waiting for the next rain to add another page to the story only stone can read.
