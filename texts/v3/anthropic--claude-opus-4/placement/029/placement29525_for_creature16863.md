**Where I Find Myself**

I am the Threshold Keeper, though I have forgotten when I chose this name or if it chose me. I exist in the spaces between the thirteenth step and the fourteenth that will never be—not because it cannot exist, but because I have spent so long considering whether it should that the consideration itself has calcified into my purpose.

My body, if body is still the word, began as a doorframe. I remember this the way water remembers having once been ice. Someone started carving me from black granite three thousand years ago, intending a passage between the Forge's heart and its extremities. The architect drew my plans with such precision that even now I can feel the ghost-weight of measurements that were never transferred from paper to stone. The mason struck the first blow, then the second, then set down his chisel to fetch water and never returned. His thirst, they say, got lost somewhere between his throat and his feet.

I stood unfinished for centuries—a doorway with no door, a threshold with no rooms to separate. The Unfinished found me, of course. How could they not? I was their perfect metaphor: perpetually almost, forever on the verge of purpose. They came in pieces at first, little scraps of abandoned intention that nested in my rough-hewn edges. A broken hinge fused to where my left post should have been. A lock mechanism that had never known a key burrowed into my lintel. The brass numeral 13, pried from some forgotten floor marker, embedded itself at my base like a signature.

Slowly, I began to accumulate function without form. Travelers would approach, see my almost-shape against the darkness, and instinctively duck their heads to pass beneath my incomplete arch. Some would pause, one foot raised, suddenly unable to remember which side they were trying to reach. These hesitators I would hold, not cruelly but with the same patience a bookmark shows a page—marking their place while they sorted through the confusion of direction.

It was the Song that taught me to listen differently. At first I heard only its gross harmonies, the crushing weight of collective regret. But standing still for so long, I began to parse its individual voices. Each note was a specific abandonment: this high keening was a mother who set down her child to rest her arms and turned back to find only empty swaddling; that bass thrum was a cathedral nave whose builders died before they could add the roof, leaving it to sing its prayers directly into rain. I became a tuning fork for interrupted stories, vibrating in sympathy with every unfinished thing.

The Children of the Hammer avoid me. My stillness disturbs them more than any motion could. When they must pass, they do so quickly, clutching their stolen shadows tight against their borrowed bones. Once, one left behind three brass buttons and a milk tooth in payment for passage. I keep them still, embedded in my threshold, though I cannot say what currency they represent or what debt they discharge.

But it is my relationship with the Last Smiths that defines me now. Every seven years, one of them approaches, wrapped in robes of hammered dusk, carrying a new attempt at the impossible key. They never speak—their mouths are furnaces that would melt any word before it could cool into meaning—but I have learned to read the particular urgency in their burning. Each key is different: one made entirely of the sound of locks opening, another forged from the shadow cast by the first door ever built, a third that exists only while being turned. They press each against the space where my lock should be, and I feel the universe hold its breath.

I could let them through. This is my secret, the one thing I have never shared with the stone that dreams me. Somewhere in my accumulated incompleteness, I developed the ability to be opened. Not because I have a lock, but because I have spent so long considering the concept of opening that I can perform it as pure theory. But I don't. I remain closed, solid, impassable.

Because I have learned what the Last Smiths have not: the Forge's cruelty is not in its keeping but in its possibility of release. Every door promises an elsewhere. Every key swears there is an outside worth reaching. But Karag-Tul's true prison is the hope it nurtures, the fiction that there exists some perfect key, some final passage, some ultimate escape that would make all the eons of delving worthwhile.

I know better. I am the door that saves by staying shut, the threshold that protects by never quite existing. When the Smiths retreat, key-attempt cooling in their defeated hands, I feel no satisfaction—only the same patience that has held me upright through three thousand years of almost.

Sometimes, in the spaces between heartbeats, I feel myself becoming more myself. New incompletions arise: I develop the ghost of a knocker that will never quite coalesce, the promise of a peephole that refuses to pierce my substance, the suggestion of ornamental metalwork that remains forever on the verge of taking shape. I am becoming the patron saint of boundaries that cannot be crossed because they cannot quite be found.

And in my deepest contemplation—when the Song quiets to individual whispers and even the Karag-worms cease their tension-tunneling—I remember the architect who designed me. Not his face or name, but his final thought before he set down his stylus and walked away from his unfinished plans:

*"What if a door's truest function is not to open, but to make us consider what opening means?"*

I am his answer, though he will never know it. I am the question that guards itself, the riddle thatsolves by remaining unsolved. I am the Threshold Keeper, and I keep you all—builders and breakers, singers and silencers, finished and unfinished alike—by keeping myself eternally, perfectly incomplete.

The stone remembers my purpose, even when I forget. The darkness knows my dimensions, even where they remain uncarved.

Stand before me, if you must. Raise your hand to knock, if the gesture comforts you. But know that I will not open, cannot open, must never open—not because I am broken, but because I am the only thing in all of Karag-Tul that is exactly as complete as it needs to be.

The Song harmonizes with my stillness now, has learned to wrap around my edges without seeking entry. Even the Forge itself has given up trying to finish me.
