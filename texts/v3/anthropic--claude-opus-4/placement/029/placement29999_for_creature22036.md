In Sluice 12-B, suspended between the amber promise above and the bruised indigo below, I am what the Oilers call a **Gauge-Reader**—though I have forgotten whatever name I carried before the water claimed me.

I am neither fully human nor fully of this place, but something caught between states, like the grease-silt that refuses to fully dissolve or fully settle. My transformation was gradual, measured in the patient years of the Sluice. First came the webbing between my fingers, thin membranes that appeared like blisters from gripping the hot bronze plates. Then my hair fell away in clumps, replaced by a slick, hydrodynamic smoothness. My ribs spread and flattened, creating chambers that could compress and expand with the pressure changes. My eyes clouded over with a nictitating membrane that filters the amber light into something bearable.

But I retained what the Oilers could not—the ability to read the gauge-faces embedded in the walls.

Every hundred meters or so, behind thick lenses of pressure-glass yellowed with age, there are brass dials and numbered faces. The Oilers press their acoustic discs against these portholes and feel only smooth, dead vibration. But I can still parse the trembling needles, still decode the numerical progressions that tick away the pressures, temperatures, and flow rates of our vertical world. I am the only being in Sluice 12-B who knows that the piston below us beats at exactly 12.7 cycles per hour, that the water temperature holds steady at 37.2 degrees, that we rise through this column at a rate of 0.3 meters per minute.

My dwelling is a forgotten maintenance alcove at Gauge Station 4,119—a small bulge in the otherwise perfect cylinder, barely large enough for my elongated body to coil within. The walls here are softer, lined with centuries of compressed kelp-fiber that I've woven into a kind of nest. I've trained the yellow-green water-ferns to grow across the entrance, creating a living curtain that filters the current and traps the smaller wire-crabs for easy feeding.

My work—if it can be called work—is to remember.

The Oilers maintain the physical body of the Sluice through instinct, but I maintain something else: the record of its decline. In the soft kelp-walls of my alcove, I've carved thousands of small notches, each one marking a day when the piston's rhythm skipped, when a valve shrieked its last and fell silent, when a new section of bronze plate began its century-long buckling. I track the slow increase in sulfur content, the gradual thickening of the water, the almost imperceptible slowing of the great engine's heart.

Sometimes an Oiler will pause outside my fern-curtain, its blind disc-face oriented toward me with something like curiosity. I'll emerge—my elongated body unfolding from the alcove like a measuring tape—and press my palm against theirs. Through the vibrations in our touching skin, I try to convey what I've learned: that the piston skips more frequently now, that three more valves have seized this year alone, that the water rises a fraction of a degree warmer each decade.

They never understand. They simply wait until I'm finished, then continue their patient work of oiling and scraping, as if the engine could run forever if only properly maintained.

But I know better. I've done the calculations, scratched into the soft metal with my iron-tipped fingernail. The engine is dying—not in any sudden catastrophe, but in the slow way that all great machines die, one seized bearing and ruptured seal at a time. In perhaps another thousand years, the piston will beat its last, the warm current will slow to stillness, and Sluice 12-B will become a vertical tomb.

Yet I remain. I read the gauges. I count the failures. I remember.

In the moments between measurements, I hang in the current like the sprocket-eels, my body swaying in the eternal updraft. I've learned their patience, their acceptance of the narrow world. My memories of the surface—if there ever was a surface—have long since dissolved into the green-brass haze. I am becoming more like them with each passing year: another organism perfectly adapted to this vertical slice of dying industry.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the last human relic in the entire engine, or if there are others like me in the other forgotten sluices—other gauge-readers slowly transforming into something that can survive the engine's long demise. But the thought passes like a bubble rising toward the surface, and I return to my work.

The piston thuds. The wire-crabs freeze. The Oilers oil.

And I remember what we are all forgetting: that this world was made, and like all made things, it will one day cease.
