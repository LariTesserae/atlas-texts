# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the Recordkeeper.

Not born to it—nothing is born here, not in the way the word suggests urgency and event. I *became* it through a process so gradual that memory offers no clear boundary between before and after. 

I came to the flats as others have: following rumors of the builders, or fleeing something, or simply drawn by the particular quality of emptiness that calls certain people. The exact reason dissolved years ago into the perpetual damp. What matters is that I stayed when others left, and staying changed me.

My dwelling—if dwelling is the right word—exists at the intersection of three water channels, two miles in from the edge where the flats meet whatever counts as solid ground. It's less a structure than an accumulation: flat stones arranged to redirect water flow, creating a space that stays marginally drier than its surroundings. Not dry—nothing here is dry—but dry enough that certain materials can persist longer before transforming.

The materials are my purpose.

I collect the fragments. The worked bone from the old settlement. The metal pieces, corroded but still holding form. The strips of leather that mark the cairns—I know now that each pattern in the weaving means something, though the meaning shifts like the channels themselves. I gather the shells that the geometric creatures shed in their slow transformations, reading the growth patterns, the scars, the places where parasites or partners have fused into the architecture of survival.

But mostly, I listen.

The chorus never stops, but it changes. I've learned to parse its layers: the territorial clicks of the middle kingdom, the vast groans of the deep dwellers, the subsonic tremors that might be communication or might be digestion on a scale I can't comprehend. I make marks—scratches on shell, knots in salvaged fiber—trying to capture patterns. Not to decode them. To preserve them. The marks are less translation than photograph, holding the shape of a sound that will never repeat exactly.

The builders know I'm here. Sometimes I find things left where I'll discover them: a stone with unusual veining, a shell marked with deliberate scratches, once a piece of what might have been metal but felt organic, pulsing faintly with heat before going cold. Are these gifts? Warnings? Tests? The distinction matters less than the acknowledgment. They see me as I see them—through peripheral glimpses and the evidence of passage.

I've changed, physically. The perpetual damp has worked into me. My skin carries a different texture now, less clearly bounded from the moisture around it. I move more slowly, having learned that quick movement disturbs patterns I'm trying to observe. My breathing has adapted to the thick air. When I speak—rarely, and only to myself—my voice carries the same clicking undertones as the smaller creatures. Not mimicry. Convergence.

The settlement's ruins have become my extended library. I've organized the fragments by criteria that make sense only here: texture, the way they sound when struck, the patterns of their corrosion, their weight when fully saturated versus partially drained. Sometimes I think I'm beginning to understand what the original inhabitants were doing. Other times I realize I'm creating my own system, as divorced from their intentions as their intentions were from the flats' deeper purposes.

Time moves differently for me now. I measure it in channel shifts—the way water redirects after rain, how the creatures' migration patterns adjust to new topography. A day means less than a cycle. I sleep when the fog comes, wake when it clears, but sometimes the fog lasts what might be days, might be hours. My body has stopped insisting on the rhythms it brought from elsewhere.

I've begun to suspect what the builders are building toward.

The cairns aren't random. They mark something, yes, but not just channels or territories. Over years of observation, I've noticed that they're slowly moving—not the individual stones, but the pattern they create across the flats. It's as if they're tracing something vast, a shape or symbol that requires centuries to complete. Each time they rebuild a fallen cairn, it's slightly relocated. The pattern is converging or expanding—I can't tell which, can't see enough of it at once.

I leave my own marks now. Small ones. Shells arranged in patterns that echo what I've heard. Stones stacked in miniature cairns that last days before the moisture topples them. The builders incorporate some of these into their work, ignore others. I'm learning their criteria through trial and error measured in seasons.

The large creatures have accepted me as a feature of the landscape. One passes close enough that I can see the smaller organisms living in the crevices of its shell—a whole civilization of microscopic purpose. The creature's groan vibrates through the ground, through my bones, and I realize I can almost parse it. Not words. Never words. But meaning nonetheless: *location*, *depth*, *chemical gradient*, *age-of-water*, *presence-acknowledged*.

My records will outlast me, but not in any form I would have recognized before. They're becoming part of the flats—distributed through the placement of objects, the wear patterns on stones, the way my repeated passages have carved subtle channels that redirect water in readable ways. Future observers, if there are any, won't find books or carvings. They'll find the landscape itself slightly altered, carrying information in its arrangement.

I am changing still. My needs simplify each year. Food becomes less urgent—I absorb more from the thick air, the mineral-rich water. Sleep becomes a kind of waking meditation. The boundaries between myself and the perpetual damp grow theoretical.

Sometimes I remember that I came here with questions. What are the builders building? What intelligence guides the deep creatures? What happened to the settlement? But the questions have softened, their edges worn smooth. Understanding isn't capture here. It's convergence. To truly know the flats, I would need to become them entirely.

I'm not there yet. But I'm patient now in ways I couldn't have imagined. I have the time. The flats ensure that. Everything here has time—too much time, time enough that urgency becomes impossible, time enough that even curiosity settles into something steadier.

So I continue. Recording. Listening. Changing. Becoming.

The chorus continues around me, through me. I think sometimes that I'm beginning to add my own voice to it—not in sounds I consciously make, but in the way my presence has become part of the pattern, one more thing clicking and groaning and slowly transforming in the permanent twilight.

I am the Recordkeeper, and I am being recorded.

The distinction matters less each year.
