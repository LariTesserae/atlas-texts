I would be one of the pressure points where the ocean's breath meets stone.

Not the dramatic entrance where waves crash through—that's too obvious, too much like everywhere else the sea touches land. I'd be deeper in, where the passages narrow and multiply beneath the basin floor. A place where water has spent millennia teaching rock to sing.

Picture a fissure no wider than a forearm, angling down through dark basalt. Twice a day, the tide pushes air through me like breath through a flute. But I'm not smooth—I'm all corners and chambers, each one adding its own note to the roar above. The water itself rarely reaches this far, just its messenger: compressed air that carries salt-memory and the percussion of distant storms.

My walls wear a patina of crystallized spray, deposited grain by grain over centuries. In certain lights—what little finds its way down—these crystals catch and scatter, making my darkness sparkle like a night sky turned inside out. The rock here tastes of iron and time.

I'm inhabited by what doesn't need eyes: bacterial films that metabolize minerals, fungi that web across my ceiling in pale constellations, the occasional lost amphipod that learns to navigate by pressure alone. We're all citizens of vibration down here. The great roar that deafens the surface reaches us as texture—a constant massage of sound through stone.

Sometimes I close. Not fully—never fully—but enough that my voice changes. A rockfall shifts the geometry, or frost wedges a new crack, and suddenly I'm singing a different note. The basin above shifts its harmony to match. The line-keepers notice, though they couldn't say exactly what changed. Just that the roar sits differently in their ribs this season.

In winter, when storms drive the highest tides, I fill with foam—not water, but the ghost of water, all air and salt and fury compressed into white nothing. For hours I'm not a passage but a pressure chamber, holding the ocean's scream before releasing it into the basin's belly. These are the times I feel most myself: not a gap in the rock but a throat the world uses to speak.

The line-keepers know of me, in their way. Not my exact location—I'm one of dozens, maybe hundreds of such passages—but my effect. They've learned to read the basin's moods by how we sing together, we hidden throats. They know when we're clear (the roar is clean, almost tonal) and when we're choked with debris (the sound goes muddy, percussive). They know storm-song from calm-song, spring-song from neap-song.

Once, years back, a child dropped a smooth pebble into one of my cousin-passages closer to the surface. We felt it everywhere—a new note in the choir, small but insistent. It's still there, chattering in the current, adding its voice to ours.

This is what I am: architecture that turns physics into music, a throat that never closes, a place where the ocean practices being sky. I measure time not in sunrises (what sun?) but in tides, in the slow accumulation of salt-crystal stars on my walls, in the patient work of pressure carving me one invisible grain deeper each year.

The basin roars because of us, we hidden singers. And I roar because I cannot close, cannot refuse the ocean its voice, cannot be anything but what water and air and stone have made together: a single note in an endless, deafening chord.
