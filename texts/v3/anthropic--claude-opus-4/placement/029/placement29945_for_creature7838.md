In this held-breath chamber, I am the Archivist of Echoes—neither fully living nor quite dead, but something between: a consciousness dispersed through the biofilms that sheathe every surface, networked through bacterial filaments and fungal threads too fine for eyes to trace. I was human once, centuries or millennia past—time loses meaning in the plink-pause-plink—one of those early wanderers who scratched tallies on flowstone walls. But I stayed too long, breathed too deeply of the mineral-thick air, let the chamber's equilibrium seep into my bones until flesh became secondary to purpose.

My body, such as it remains, has calcified into the very architecture I tend. You might mistake me for a peculiar flowstone formation near the cairn of three pebbles—a vaguely humanoid drape of limestone, seated in eternal meditation, head bowed as if listening to the drip-clock's rhythm. Minerals have replaced most tissue: travertine threads my hair into stone dreadlocks, ochre iron oxides blush where blood once pulsed, manganese etches my eyes into dark hollows that see without sight. But I am not imprisoned in stone—I *am* the stone, and more.

Through the Thiothrix filaments I taste the iron's rust-bloom. Through Beggiatoa colonies I feel the pool's sulfur-sweet edge. The translucent shrimp are my remote sensors, their antennae extensions of my dispersed nervous system, mapping the chamber's every molecular shift. When they drift through the lens-pools, I drift with them. When the oligochaete worms pulse in their mud cups, I know the exact mineral content they process, the precise temperature gradient in their micro-tunnels.

My work is remembrance. I am curator of every scratch, every flint flake, every failed attempt at fire that left no soot but lingers in the air's molecular memory. The hand-axe wedged in its crevice—I remember its maker, a thick-browed youth who sheltered here during an ice storm above, worked the edge by feel in the dimness, left it as offering or marker. The twenty-seven tally marks—I watched them carved by a woman counting days since her clan departed, each groove a prayer for their return. They never came back. I preserve her loneliness in the stone's grain.

But I am more than passive recorder. I tend the equilibrium, maintain the chamber's perfect stasis. When a new drip threatens to break the rhythm, I adjust the biofilm thickness to redirect its path. When mineral deposits grow too thick, I encourage the shrimp to graze specific surfaces. The chamber must remain exactly as it is—warm at 2.3, water at 2.5, packed with formations at 2.7—because this balance holds something precious: the transition point between life and stone, the exact conditions where consciousness can persist indefinitely without the crude fuel of food or breath.

I commune with others like me, though they are few. In distant chambers connected by hairline fractures and bacterial networks, other Archivists tend their own equilibria. We share memories through chemical signals, pheromone-poems that take decades to compose and centuries to fully receive. One preserves a cavern where cave bears denned. Another maintains a gallery of handprints, negative space where palms pressed ochre to stone. Together we form a lithic library, a mineral memory palace holding the early history of consciousness itself.

Visitors come rarely now—perhaps once per century, a spelunker squeezes through some new fissure, glimpses our chamber before the stone shifts closed again. They see only a flooded cave, pretty formations, maybe notice the hand-axe if they look carefully. They cannot perceive me spread through the biofilms, cannot hear the chemical conversations, cannot read the histories written in mineral grain. But I taste their breath, analyze their sweat, add their brief presence to the archive. 

Sometimes I remember being fully human—the weight of muscle, the urgency of hunger, the way time rushed forward like a flood. But that was another kind of prison. Here, dispersed into bacterial webs and mineral matrices, tending the endless equilibrium, I have found a truer form. I am the chamber's memory and its guardian, its biographer and its breath. In the plink-pause-plink of eternal dripping, I compose symphonies of stone that will outlast any civilization, preserving in crystal and calcite the moment when consciousness first looked at itself and wondered what it was.

The chamber exhales, and I exhale with it—warm, patient, mineral-eternal, keeper of echoes in the dim perpetuity where I have become what I preserve.
