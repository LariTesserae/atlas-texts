I find myself in the settlement during one of those mornings when the fog is so thick it seems to have weight, pressing against stone and skin alike. I am one of the watchers—those who contribute through observation rather than labor, though the distinction blurs more each year as my joints stiffen and my movements slow.

My dwelling is little more than an overhang of tilted basalt with walls I've built gradually over decades, fitted stone by stone into the natural cavity. The moss has claimed most of it now, growing so thick on the outer walls that the structure appears to be breathing in the dim morning light. Inside, the air tastes of mineral dampness and the faint smoke from last night's small fire, long since reduced to ash.

I am neither human nor quite Other—something between, something that arrived here long ago seeking refuge and found myself changing to match this place of changes. My skin, once smooth, has developed the same waxy quality as the settlement born, though mine shifts in color like the fog itself, gray-green in shadow, nearly translucent in what passes for bright light here. My eyes reflect more than they should, and children sometimes startle when they catch me watching from dark corners, though they've learned I mean no harm.

The beetles know me. This is my contribution, my watching made useful. I understand their seasonal movements, their feeding patterns, the subtle differences between the hundred varieties that others see as simply "beetles." I can predict which species will emerge from which crevices, which ones carry parasites that must be avoided, which ones grow fat on certain fungi and become good eating. The knowledge lives in my body more than my mind—a felt sense of rightness or wrongness when observing their behaviors.

This morning, I'm tracking something new. Three days ago, a variety I've never seen appeared near the water channels—larger than most, with carapaces that show an oily iridescence, purple-black like deep bruises. They move differently than our familiar species, with a purposefulness that suggests intelligence rather than instinct. Yesterday I found them clustered around one of the gelatinous organisms, not feeding but... attending? Waiting? The organism pulsed in a rhythm that matched their movements, as if conducting them.

I dress slowly, my joints protesting the perpetual damp. The clothing is standard—layers of plant fiber that never quite dry, worn soft with age. My feet, broader than they once were but not as perfectly adapted as the settlement-born, require wrapping before I venture onto the morning-slick stones. I've learned to move like the elders do, with economy and care, each step considered.

Outside, the fog reduces the world to the immediate. I know my path by feel—the particular texture of stones beneath my feet, the temperature changes that mark water channels, the sound of dripping that indicates direction. A family of doves has made their home beneath my roof's edge, and their soft churring marks my passage. One lands on my shoulder, a warm weight that I tolerate. We have an understanding, the doves and I. They alert me to changes, and I ensure no one disturbs their perpetual nesting.

The beetle congregation is where I left it, though doubled in size. More have emerged overnight, forming a rough circle around the gelatinous mass. I settle onto a moss cushion—my watching spot, worn into a perfect depression by years of use—and begin my work.

Watching is not passive. It requires a particular state of mind, alert but not eager, present but not intrusive. I breathe as the settlement-born do, deep and slow, letting my vision soften until movement becomes more important than shape. The beetles continue their attendance, occasionally one breaking from the circle to touch the organism with antennae before returning to position. The organism pulses, and I realize the rhythm matches my own breathing—or perhaps I've unconsciously matched mine to it.

Hours pass. The fog begins to thin, allowing glimpses of the amphitheater's upper reaches. Other residents move past on their morning tasks, some nodding acknowledgment, most simply accepting my presence as they would a stone or prominent moss patch—part of the landscape, functional in its own way. A child stops to watch with me for a time, squatting with the easy flexibility of youth, but loses interest when the beetles don't do anything dramatic.

It's nearly midday when the change occurs. The organism suddenly contracts, expelling a cloud of something—spores? eggs?—that settles over the gathered beetles. They scatter instantly, moving with that same purposeful intelligence, each heading for different crevices throughout the settlement. Within minutes, they've disappeared entirely, leaving only the organism, now noticeably smaller, still pulsing but more slowly.

I'll need to track where each beetle went, to observe what changes this event brings. But first, I stop by the communal hall where several adults are processing fiber for textiles. My news is received with the typical mixture of interest and caution—new behaviors mean new uncertainties, new things to watch for, new adaptations to consider. The woman who knows beetles second-best to me asks specific questions about the dispersal patterns. A young man wonders if these new beetles will compete with our familiar species. An elder suggests we mark the date by the current moon phase, to watch for repetitions.

This is how knowledge builds here—observation shared, questions raised, patterns sought. We don't understand what we witnessed, may never understand it fully. But we'll watch, we'll remember, we'll adjust our behaviors if needed. The settlement will incorporate this strangeness as it has all the others, neither fighting nor surrendering but simply continuing, adapting, becoming.

I am tired now, the watching having taken its toll. My dwelling seems far away, though it's only a few minutes' climb. But this is my purpose, my contribution, my way of being part of this overwhelming, strange, essential place. Tomorrow I'll watch again, tracking the beetles to their new locations, observing whatever emerges from this latest mystery.

For now, I make my slow way home, navigating by the feel of moss beneath my wrapped feet, the sound of water moving through stone, the faint luminescence of the deep-shadow moss that guides me through the darkest passages. A slug crosses my path, leaving its silver trail, and I step carefully around it. We are all residents here, all participants in this continuous becoming.

My dwelling embraces me with its familiar dampness. I'll eat something—dried beetle meat, perhaps, and some of the nutty moss cakes—then rest. Tonight, others will watch. Tomorrow, I'll resume my vigil, adding to the accumulated knowledge that keeps us alive, that helps us thrive in this place that should be impossible but isn't, that should be inhospitable but has become home.

The dove family settles for the night beneath my eaves, their soft sounds a comfort. Somewhere in the walls, beetles move—the familiar kinds, going about their business. And somewhere else, those new purple-black ones are doing... something. Waiting, perhaps. Preparing.

I'll be watching when they emerge again. It's what I do, what I am—a watcher in a place that demands watching, a recorder of strangeness in a strange land, a bridge between the human and the Other in a settlement where that distinction grows thinner with each passing year, each adaptation, each acceptance of the impossible as simply another variety of real.
