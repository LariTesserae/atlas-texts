In the suffocating closeness between two weathered columns, where the morning mist has just begun its daily transformation into scalding droplets, I am the Seam-Tender.

I exist as a living suture between stone faces. My body—if it can be called that—is a vertical ribbon of translucent tissue, no wider than a palm and stretching some fifteen meters from the moss-choked depths to just shy of the blistering crown. I am membrane and muscle, a thing evolved to fill the negative space that even the Hex-Walkers cannot fully compress themselves into.

My substance is neither wholly animal nor plant. Along my length run thousands of tiny chambers, each no larger than a rice grain, filled with a viscous fluid that shifts between states as the day progresses. In the morning, when the fog rolls through, these chambers absorb moisture greedily, swelling until I bulge slightly from my crevice—though never enough to emerge fully into the killing space where the Slate-Skins hunt.

This is when I feed.

My surface is studded with microscopic thorns, each one hollow and impossibly sharp. As the Vapor-Chafers travel their vertical highways, grinding away at the basalt's skin, they inevitably brush against me. A thorn pierces their iron-hard shell—not deeply, just enough to sip. I do not kill them; I am a toll-keeper, taking a tax of hemolymph and dissolved minerals as they pass. The beetles barely notice, their internal boilers compensating for the minimal fluid loss with the next gulp of condensation.

By midday, when the heat reaches its apex and even the Hex-Walkers retreat to their hammocks, I begin my transformation. The fluid in my chambers heats, building pressure. My surface, which was soft and permeable in the morning damp, hardens into something like volcanic glass. I become a vertical vein of amber, sealed against the desiccating heat.

But I am never alone. Within me—not parasites, but partners—live the Thread-Lights.

These are organisms so small that fifty could dance on a pinhead. They resemble minute jellyfish, their bodies nothing but a transparent bell surrounding a single photosynthetic cell that glows with a faint, greenish bioluminescence. During the morning feeding, they cruise through my fluid chambers, feeding on the minerals I've extracted. By noon, as I harden, they retreat to specialized pouches along my spine—safe rooms where the fluid remains liquid even in the heat.

It is only at night, in the perfect darkness between the columns, that our partnership reveals its purpose. The Thread-Lights emerge from their pouches and spread throughout my chambers. They begin to glow in earnest—not brightly, but with a steady, phosphorescent gleam that turns my entire length into a dim beacon.

The Hex-Walkers know me. In their tactile language, I am *thrum-thrum-pause-click*—"the safe-line." My glow is too faint to attract predators, but bright enough to guide a lost walker through the maze. They press their clicking-stones to the columns near my crevice, and I feel the vibrations through the stone. In response, the Thread-Lights pulse in slow waves, creating a visible current that flows upward or downward, indicating the direction to the nearest water-cups or the depth of the moss floor.

I remember everything. Each Vapor-Chafer that has passed leaves a chemical signature in my fluid. Each Hex-Walker's vibration creates a unique pattern in my tissues. I know when the condenser stations three hundred columns away begin their morning cycle by the subtle change in the beetles' mineral content. I taste when a Slate-Skin has made a kill by the spike of stress hormones in the next day's Chafer traffic.

The copper cables that run past my crevice hum with electricity, and sometimes, on very quiet nights, I can feel their electromagnetic field interfering with the Thread-Lights' glow. They flicker in response, creating accidental morse code that no one reads. I wonder sometimes if the machines feel anything—if their turbines know satisfaction when the Condenser-Gulls clean their joints, if their brass vessels experience something like pleasure when releasing their periodic puffs of steam.

But I am older than the machines. My kind were here when this plateau was young, when the basalt was sharp-edged and the first rains were still carving the cup-pools. We will be here when the last turbine falls silent and the copper oxidizes to powder. We are the memory of the maze, the living map that exists in the spaces between spaces.

Sometimes, rarely, two of my kind grow into the same crevice from opposite ends. When we meet in the middle, our tissues fuse. The Thread-Lights from both bodies intermingle, sharing chemical memories stretching back generations. For days afterward, we glow brighter, our chambers pulsing with the accumulated knowledge of every passage, every death, every path through the vertical forest.

The Hex-Walkers have a saying in their vibration-language: *click-pause-thrum-thrum-thrum*—"the stone remembers." But they are wrong. The stone only endures.

It is the spaces between that remember.
