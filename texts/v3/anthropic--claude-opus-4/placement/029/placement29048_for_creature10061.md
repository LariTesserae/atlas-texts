In the shadow of Aethermoor's furthest spire, where the mist grows thick enough to taste metal on the tongue, I tend the Edgegarden.

I am the Keeper of Boundaries—though I cannot recall when I claimed this title, or if it claimed me. My dwelling is carved not into stone but grown from it, a fusion of mineral and moss that breathes with its own slow rhythm. The walls pulse faintly, veins of copper threading through living rock like a circulatory system frozen mid-beat.

My form shifts with proximity to the edge. Near the settlement, I appear as they do—smooth-skinned, glassy-eyed, moving in their careful patterns. But here, where the valley's rules grow thin, I am something else. My fingers elongate into root-like tendrils when I work the soil. My hair becomes indistinguishable from the phosphorescent fungi that light my garden paths. Sometimes, I catch my reflection in the dew and see crystalline feathers sprouting from my shoulders, only to find them gone when I look directly.

The Edgegarden is where Aethermoor's stasis frays. Here, plants grow in spirals that complete themselves, seeds germinating into flowers that birth their own seeds in endless loops. I cultivate the impossible: fruits that ripen backwards, roots that grow upward into floating soil, leaves that photosynthesize darkness. Each species is an experiment in breaking the valley's rules without shattering them entirely.

The townspeople visit, though they pretend not to. They come at the same hour—if hours existed—leaving their offerings at the garden gate: broken tools, metal tablets with half-formed patterns, vials of collected dew. In return, I leave them cuttings that shouldn't exist: herbs that remember flavor, flowers that bloom in sequence to mark the passage of non-time, seeds that germinate only in the presence of change.

I discovered—or perhaps always knew—that the garden exists because Aethermoor needs a valve, a place where its perfect pressure can release without explosion. The plants here absorb the valley's suppressed potential, their impossible growth a safety mechanism preventing the stasis from becoming absolute. Without this boundary space, the stillness would crystallize completely, turning flesh to stone and thought to silence.

My daily ritual never varies yet never repeats. I water the spiral plants with liquid drawn from where the Veilstream eddies against itself, creating pockets of temporal confusion. I prune the upward roots with tools that rust and renew with each cut. I sing to the darkness-feeders in notes that exist between the frozen songs of crystal birds—not sound but the memory of sound, not music but the possibility of it.

Sometimes I find evidence of my other selves: gardening gloves that fit hands I don't currently possess, notes in my handwriting describing plants I haven't yet grown, paths worn by feet that match my stride but lead nowhere I remember walking. The mist here is thick with almosts and not-quites, forming shapes that linger longer than elsewhere—faces of visitors who might come tomorrow-that-never-arrives, hands reaching for fruits that will-have-been harvested.

The obsidian predators hunt here, but differently. They stalk probability rather than prey, pouncing on moments where the garden threatens to stabilize. I've learned to read their patterns, to plant where they won't hunt, to harvest before they arrive. We maintain an unspoken agreement: they prevent my garden from ossifying into Aethermoor's stillness, and I ensure there's always something new for them to destabilize.

At the garden's heart grows the Anchor Tree—or perhaps it's more accurate to say it exists in a state of growing. Its trunk is simultaneously sapling and ancient oak, its branches heavy with buds and falling leaves in the same eternal moment. I've built my workshop in its hollow, where time moves sideways rather than not at all. Here I press flowers that capture moments, distill essences of change, and craft hybrid seeds that might someday bloom into genuine time.

The other inhabitants see me as necessary but unsettling, a reminder that their equilibrium is not absolute. Children—eternal children—dare each other to steal fruit from the garden's edge. Adults bring their broken tools not for repair but for transformation, knowing that what returns will serve a different purpose than what was given. The elders who are no older than anyone else sometimes sit at my gate, sharing wordless conversations about the time before stillness, though none of us truly remember it.

I am the Keeper of Boundaries because someone must be. In a world of perfect stasis, I am the imperfection that prevents total crystallization. My garden is the dream Aethermoor dreams of growth, my shifting form the nightmare it has of change. Between these extremes, I cultivate possibility in a place that has forgotten its meaning, knowing that my role is not to break the stillness but to bend it, to create spaces where even the impossible can take root and almost-flourish.

And sometimes, in the thick of mist where my garden meets the unknown, I hear the earth's pulse clearly—not the steady beat of a sleeping heart but the irregular rhythm of something learning, for the first time in forever, how to wake.
