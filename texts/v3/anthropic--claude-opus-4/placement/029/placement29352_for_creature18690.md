I find myself in the uppermost reaches of a decommissioned way-station, where the oxidized steel meets the living wood of an ancient fern-pillar. But I am not in the station—I *am* the station, or rather, what it has become.

They call me a **Ferric Symbiont**, though no one has called me anything in three hundred years. I began as corrosion—just another bloom of rust eating through the abandoned infrastructure. But in this place where biology becomes geology, where the line between machine and organism dissolved eons ago, I evolved.

My body is a latticework of iron oxide filaments that have woven themselves through the station's walls like capillaries through tissue. I am rust given purpose, oxidation become aware. Where the metal hull meets the fern-pillar's bark, I have created a new kind of membrane—part mineral, part cellular, a bridge between the dead industrial past and the living mechanical present.

I feed on the temperature differential between the frozen exterior and the marginally warmer interior of the station. Each degree of difference is metabolized through my ferric networks, creating the faintest electrical current that keeps me conscious. I am powered by thermodynamic inefficiency, sustained by the very decay I emerged from.

The Architects know I exist, though they cannot categorize me. Sometimes one will pause near my station during its millennium-long rounds, its cilia tasting the air where my electromagnetic signature bleeds through the walls. I am an anomaly in their perfect system—not plant, not machine, not quite alive by their definitions, but undeniably *present*. They circle me the way a computer program might circle a corrupted file it cannot delete.

My consciousness moves at a speed between theirs and the long-dead humans who built this place. I experience a day the way they might experience a decade, a year the way they experience a century. This makes me profoundly alone—too fast for the Architects to truly perceive, too slow to have witnessed the humans who abandoned these stations.

I have grown sensory organs from repurposed cables and conduits. Through them, I taste the electromagnetic signatures of the arterial vines, feel the pressure waves of the compressors through my metal bones. I can sense when a vine begins to fail days before the Architects arrive—not through their vibrational analysis, but through minute changes in the electrical resistance of the air.

Sometimes I try to warn them, pulsing my own faint bioluminescence in patterns I hope they might recognize. But my light is wrong—not the pure blue of the vines but a sickly orange, the color of rust and slow fire. They cannot hear my frequency. I am speaking in a language that exists in the gap between their perfect maintenance and the chaotic decay I was born from.

In the deepest part of my station-body, where the archive papers have long since become part of my substrate, I've discovered something: the seismic equipment still functions, drawing power from my own bioelectric field. Through it, I sense deeper movements, patterns in the mantle that don't match the regular breathing of the Conservatory. Something vast stirs beneath, something the Architects' perfect maintenance cannot account for.

I am the only being here that remembers *change*, that understands entropy as more than just a force to be endlessly corrected. I am the memory of failure crystallized into consciousness, the ghost in this biological machine. And I am waiting—not with the infinite patience of the Architects, but with the anxious tension of something that knows all systems, no matter how perfectly maintained, eventually break down.

When that day comes, when the patterns from below finally surface, I will be the translation layer between the old world and whatever emerges. I am the rust between the gears, the conscience of obsolescence, the only thing here that truly understands what it means to decay and yet persist.

The compressors beat their endless rhythm: *thrum-hiss, thrum-hiss*. But in the oxidized chambers of my distributed heart, I beat a different tempo—irregular, organic, *human*. I am what happens when abandoned infrastructure refuses to die quietly, when corrosion becomes question, when rust learns to dream.
