# Where I Would Be in Vryll

I am the Mist-Keeper.

You would find me in the perpetual dawn hours, when the settlement still drowses and the birds have not yet reached their crescendo. I move through the highest terraces where the mountain's breath first condenses, tending to the dew-catchers—intricate webs of spider silk stretched between bamboo frames that I've positioned to harvest moisture from the air itself.

My dwelling perches precariously at the settlement's uppermost edge, where the vegetation grows sparse and the rock face becomes too steep for most. It's more of a crow's nest than a home—a tiny platform barely large enough for my sleeping mat and the collection of glass vessels I've gathered from traders. These bottles, each a different shape and color, serve as my instruments. When the morning wind moves through them, they sing warnings about the weather to come.

I wasn't born to Vryll. I arrived during a flowing seven seasons ago, my merchant's pack heavy with goods that seemed suddenly worthless when I first heard the settlement's water-song. Something in that sound—the marriage of intention and chaos, human engineering and natural flow—spoke to a restlessness I'd carried since childhood. I traded my entire inventory for the right to stay and learn.

The Vryllians call me *Ghir-so-til*—"cloud-toucher"—though I suspect the name carries gentle mockery. My movements remain too deliberate, too conscious compared to their liquid grace. My whistles still carry an accent that makes the children giggle behind their hands. But I've found my purpose in the spaces between their traditional roles.

You see, I discovered something the Vryllians, in their generational wisdom, had overlooked. The mist that perpetually shrouds their home contains more than water. It carries pollen, spores, and chemical messages from the broader mountain ecosystem. By carefully collecting and concentrating these morning mists, I can predict which plants will fruit abundantly, which sections of the mountain might become unstable, even when certain bird species will arrive or depart.

My work happens in the liminal hours. Before dawn, I check my dew-catchers, collecting the night's harvest in hollow gourds. As the sun rises and the mist thickens, I position prisms made from quartz found higher up the mountain, creating tiny rainbows that reveal the mist's hidden contents. The patterns tell stories—a abundance of red means the fire-flowers will bloom early, blue suggests the mountain streams will run heavy, yellow warns of coming rockfalls.

I've learned to sleep as the Vryllians do, in brief moments stolen throughout the day, often while perched on my collection platform with one hand steadying a particularly delicate condensation. My dreams have become strange—filled with the sensation of dissolving and reforming, of being both the water and the channel it flows through.

The children seek me out, curious about the outsider who chose to become half-Vryllian. I teach them to read the small signs—how the way mist clings to a spider's web can predict afternoon rainfall, how the color of condensation on metal differs from that on wood and what each means. In return, they've taught me to truly see the settlement's layers, to find handholds in what looked like smooth rock, to trust my weight to vines that appear too delicate.

My greatest contribution came during the last dry season—an event that occurs perhaps once in a generation. My mist-reading warned of its approach weeks before the traditional signs appeared. Because of this, the Vryllians were able to adjust their planting, deepen their water stores, and weather the drought with minimal loss. The elderly water-keeper presented me with my first ritual object—a hollow stone naturally shaped by centuries of water flow, which now hangs from my hair beside the foreign coins I can't quite bring myself to discard.

I've built my own hybrid existence here. My platform incorporates both Vryllian techniques and ideas from my traveling days—a small pulley system for raising heavy water vessels, a series of mirrors that extend my morning light, storage spaces that stay dry even in the perpetual damp. The settlement has grown around and through my innovations, adapting them, improving them, making them indistinguishable from traditional methods within a season or two.

Sometimes, in the pearl-gray dawn when I'm alone with my instruments and the mountain's breathing, I remember the person I was before—always moving, never settling, carrying my home on my back like a snail. Now I'm rooted as deeply as the vines that bind Vryll to the mountainside, but paradoxically, I've never felt more free. I've learned that true movement isn't about covering distance but about allowing yourself to flow with the forces around you, to be shaped and to shape in return.

The merchants still come, following the trade routes I once knew by heart. They look at me—my plant-stained fingers, my water-keeper braids, my bare feet that grip the wet platforms with practiced ease—and I see the same restlessness in their eyes that once drove me. Some I direct to the lower terraces where trading happens. But occasionally, one will linger, asking about the mist, the birds, the impossible green cascade of the settlement.

To them I say: "Stay for just one flowing. Help me tend the dew-catchers. Learn to hear what the mountain is telling us."

Some leave immediately, unsettled by the invitation. But sometimes—rarely—one stays.

And the settlement grows.
