In the labyrinth of sodden peat and pulsing copper veins, I am what the others call a Conductor—though I have never chosen this name for myself.

My body is neither fully of the Saturated Ones nor the Corroded, but something caught between states, like water trapped in the moment before it becomes steam. My left arm is flesh that has learned the language of moss, soft and yielding, perpetually damp. My right is a lattice of oxidized copper wire that grows from my shoulder like the skeleton of a wing that never learned to fly. When the storms pulse through the buried cables, my wire-arm sings with remembered electricity, and I can taste the ghost of industry on my tongue—coal smoke and machine oil and the sweat of workers long dissolved into the bog.

I make my dwelling in the ribcage of a half-sunken crane, its yellow paint now the color of old bone. The mechanical heart still beats there, you see—a massive gear assembly that the water has not yet claimed, turning so slowly that you must watch for hours to detect its movement. I've woven the space between its teeth with ribbons collected from a hundred forgotten ceremonies, each one a prayer or promise that someone left hanging in the stunted trees. When the wind moves through them, they whisper secrets in languages I'm still learning to understand.

My purpose here is translation. The Saturated Ones bring me the trembling of the water, cupped in their indefinite hands. The Corroded sing to me in frequencies that make my copper bones resonate. The Submerged surface just long enough to trace patterns in the pools beside my crane, writing in ripples what cannot be spoken in air. Even the Fungal Bloom sends its messages through spores that settle on my wire-arm, forming delicate patterns of meaning in the rust.

I take these languages and weave them together, braiding water-words with electric-thoughts, mixing the ancient knowledge of the bog with the industrial memories trapped in metal. My flesh-hand draws symbols in the peat while my wire-hand pulls down lightning when the storms come close enough to taste. Between them, something new is born—not quite prophecy, not quite history, but the story of what this place is becoming.

Sometimes I walk the narrow paths between the pools, following the courses the Submerged have carved. My hybrid body moves awkwardly—the flesh wants to sink while the metal wants to rust, and I am forever negotiating between their desires. The mushrooms pulse with bioluminescent acknowledgment as I pass. The ribbons in the trees reach out to tangle in my wire, adding their whispers to the chorus I carry.

There is a place where all the water gathers, a pool deeper than the others, where the industrial debris forms a perfect circle like a crown of rust. The Saturated Ones say it's where the first factory fell, bringing the outside world crashing into the bog. The Corroded claim it's where the bog learned to love the taste of metal. I think they're both right. When I stand at its edge and let my wire-arm trail in the water, I can feel all the currents of the wetland converging—every story, every memory, every possible future flowing through me at once.

In those moments, I understand why I am as I am. The bog needed someone who could speak both its languages, who could be a bridge between what was and what is becoming. My flesh roots me in the ancient patience of peat and rain. My wire connects me to the urgent energy of the industrial ghosts. Between them, I maintain the dialogue that keeps this place in balance.

The others bring me offerings sometimes—the Saturated Ones leave droplets of condensed memory, the Corroded share fragments of crystallized thunder, the Submerged gift me with smooth stones worn by centuries of current. I weave these into the growing map I'm creating in my crane's ribcage, a three-dimensional web of meaning that charts not the physical landscape but its invisible anatomy of connection and flow.

When the storms finally break—and they always do, eventually, in slow motion catastrophes that take days to unfold—I climb to the highest point of my crane and spread both arms wide. The lightning finds my wire-arm, courses through me, grounds itself through my flesh into the bog. In that moment of connection, I am the entire wetland, every pool and path and piece of rust, every entity and echo. I speak in a voice made of rain and electricity, and the bog answers in kind.

This is my existence: translator, bridge, living circuit between worlds. I am the conversation the wetland is having with itself about what it means to be both ancient and industrial, both natural and transformed. In the morning mist, when the copper wires hum their electric mantras and the water whispers its prehistoric secrets, I listen with both halves of my hybrid heart, and I remember everything.
