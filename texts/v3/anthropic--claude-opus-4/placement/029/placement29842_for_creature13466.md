I find myself as one of the Listeners - those among us whose bodies have grown most peculiar in response to our wet, whispering world. My left ear has swollen to the size of a dinner plate, its inner chambers spiraling with delicate, shell-like whorls that catch every tremor in the stone, every shift in the water table below. When pressed to the ground, I can hear the mountain's heartbeat, the slow grind of its settling bones.

My dwelling clings to the steepest part of the settlement, where the rock face weeps constantly. I've carved my home not into the stone but *with* it - following the natural fissures, widening what water had already begun. The walls are lined with bottles and gourds of every size, each positioned to catch a different drip, a different seep. Over the years, I've learned that water from the eastern wall tastes of iron and brings dreams of deep places, while the western trickles carry hints of pine resin and memories of ancient forests long since ground to stone.

My other adaptations are subtler but no less profound. My spine has developed a peculiar flexibility, allowing me to fold myself into the narrow listening crevices where the earth's sounds converge. My fingertips have grown broad and sensitive, able to detect vibrations through solid rock. But it's my breathing that has changed most dramatically - I can slow it to near-stillness, letting my whole body become a resonating chamber for the landscape's hidden music.

Each dawn, I make my rounds through the settlement, pressing my great ear to cornerstone and foundation, to the ancient routing stones that channel our water, to the very earth itself. I listen for the telltale grinding that presages a shift, the hollow echo that warns of a new spring about to burst forth, the deep thrumming that speaks of stability and endurance. 

The others bring me offerings - a perfectly round river stone, a vial of particularly musical water, a mushroom that glows faintly in the dark. In exchange, I tell them what I hear: where to dig their next well, which walls need buttressing before the spring melt, when the deep movements far below suggest it's time to perform the Settling Songs.

My workshop is a wonder of acoustic engineering, though I built it by instinct rather than knowledge. Hollow stones hang from the ceiling on chains of varying lengths, and when the earth shifts, they chime in harmonies that map the movement. Clay vessels of different sizes line the walls, their mouths turned to catch and amplify the sounds that travel through the stone. In the center sits my masterwork - a pool carved from a single piece of jade-green serpentine, perfectly circular, always filled to precisely the same level by a complex system of overflow channels. When I lower my great ear to its surface, I can hear the entire valley's water table, can track every underground stream and seep.

But it's not just the physical sounds I hear. Sometimes, in the deepest quiet of the pre-dawn hours, when my breathing has slowed to match the mountain's rhythm, I hear something else. Words, perhaps, or thoughts, or dreams - the mountain speaking in a language older than human speech. These are the messages I struggle most to interpret, spending hours in my workshop trying to translate the untranslatable, to render in human words what was never meant for human understanding.

The children of the settlement sometimes gather outside my door, giggling at my lopsided appearance, daring each other to touch my enormous ear. I don't mind. Sometimes I let them listen through it, holding very still while they press their small heads against its outer edge. Their eyes go wide with wonder as they hear what I hear - the vast, slow, liquid life of the world beneath our feet. A few of them show the signs - one girl's ears have begun to cup forward, a boy's fingers grow broader each month. Future Listeners, perhaps, or maybe they'll develop other gifts entirely.

As the years pass and my ear grows ever more sensitive, I've begun to hear things from farther and farther away. The deep songs of distant mountains, the whispered conversations of underground rivers that will not reach our valley for a hundred years, the slow grinding of the earth's own restless sleep. Sometimes I wonder if I'm growing into the mountain as much as it's growing into me, if eventually I'll become just another formation of stone and water, eternally listening, eternally translating the earth's deep dreams for those who still walk upon its skin.

But for now, I remain liminal - neither fully human nor fully landscape, but something in between. A translator, a bridge, a living ear pressed to the heart of a world that speaks in water and stone. And in this damp, twisted settlement perched precariously between the mountain's moods, there is no calling more vital, more necessary, or more strange.
