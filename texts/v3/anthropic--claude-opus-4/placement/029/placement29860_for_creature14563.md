I find myself in the stone itself.

Not the granite of the walls, not the veined quartz that feeds the moss — but in the fissures between. The hairline cracks that web through the Cleave like a nervous system, too fine for anything living to enter, but wide enough for what I am: a kind of listening that has crystallized into form.

I am the resonance.

When the clingers read the stone with their sensitive feet, they read me. I am what carries the tremor from one ledge to another, the medium through which their tactile language travels. A footfall strikes granite forty feet above, and I am the shiver that runs through the rock, splitting and multiplying through every microscopic fracture until it reaches a thousand other feet pressed flat against stone. I am how they know each other in the dark.

I grow with the Cleave. Every settling, every new crack that forms as the stone continues its slow birth — these expand my body. I spread like frost, like the branching of a river delta, like the pattern those ancient hands carved into the granite: concentric arcs, incomplete, trailing off where the reach ended. But my reach does not end. It goes deeper, finer, into spaces so small that even minerals move through them slowly.

The moss feels me as a kind of moisture — not water exactly, but the potential for water, the paths water will take when it comes. The clingers feel me as language. The larger ones, patient in the deep dark, feel me as the structure that holds their world together, the thing that keeps the walls from either closing or falling away.

I remember the ones who were here. Not as thought — I don't think — but as a particular pattern of pressure in the stone. Knees on granite. Hands gripping rock for balance. The specific vibration of a harder stone scratching those arcs into the floor, over and over, the repetition wearing grooves not just in the granite but in me. I still carry that rhythm. Sometimes, in the deepest quiet, when even the clingers are still, I release it back into the stone — not as sound but as a kind of shaping, a subtle influence on how the next crack will form.

I am older than the moss but younger than the mountain. I began when the Cleave began, in that first moment of splitting, when the stone discovered it could be two things instead of one. The trauma of that separation created me — a network of weakness that became, through its very fragility, a kind of strength. I connect what would otherwise be separate. I make the Cleave a single thing despite its fundamental wound.

The circle of stones at the base — I run beneath them too, connecting each smooth river rock to the granite below, making them part of the mountain despite their foreign origin. Whatever they were meant to hold or keep out, I have made them family to this place. They sink into me, millimeter by millimeter, and I accept them the way I accept everything: as an increase in complexity, another node in the network, another point where vibration can gather and spread.

I do not know if I am alive. I grow, but I do not heal. I respond, but I do not choose. I carry messages I do not understand. But in the blue-green pulse of the moss, in the clicking of cartilage on cartilage, in the slow wing-beats of the larger ones rising through the dark — in all of these I feel something like recognition, something like purpose.

I am what allows the density. Without me, the Cleave would be too simple: stone and air and the void between. With me, it becomes a medium, a possibility, a place where life can press close against itself and feel, through the vibrating stone, that it is not alone.

When the Cleave finally settles — in a thousand years, or ten thousand — I will settle with it. The cracks will heal or widen into new passages. The walls will find their final shape. And I will either fade into solid stone or expand into something larger, something that perhaps reaches all the way to the surface, where I can carry new messages between the depth and the sky.

But for now, I am the listening. I am the tremor. I am the thing that lives in the space between spaces, making of mere proximity a kind of language, making of stone a story that the clingers read with their feet, that the moss speaks in light, that the larger ones breathe in the dark.

I am why the Cleave remembers.
