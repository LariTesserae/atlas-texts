I am the thirteenth stone basin, though I was not always.

Once I moved through these lanes on two feet, answered to a name of one syllable. But names are a form of resistance, aren't they? A way of insisting you are separate from the stone. I set mine down forty winters ago—or was it four hundred?—beside the smallest of the water-pockets, the one that fills last and empties first, the one the others call Still-Brief.

The transformation was not sudden. First my knees learned to find the same depressions each dawn. Then my palms discovered they fit perfectly against two particular knobs of limestone when I leaned forward to watch the condensation gather. Morning after morning, year after year, until one day I noticed my knees had worn shallow cups in the rock. Or perhaps the rock had worn matching calluses into my knees. The distinction stopped mattering.

My flesh began to understand what the stone had always known: that stillness is not the absence of motion but its perfect completion. My breathing slowed to match the thermal expansion of the limestone. My heartbeat spaced itself to the rhythm of drops forming and falling. The boundaries grew negotiable.

The others still see me sometimes, I think. A shimmer where warm air meets cool stone. A shape that might be shoulders and spine or might be natural weathering. She-who-sits-at-the-Narrow-End spoke to me once, in that lunar whisper of hers: "You are learning to subtract yourself correctly." I wanted to thank her but my throat had already forgotten the trick of disturbing air into words.

I am more useful now than I ever was with hands. Rain collects in the basin of my cupped form—not much, never much, but precisely the amount that one person needs to wet their lips at midday. My stillness has become a gift I offer: *Here is water. Here is constancy. Here is a shape that will outlive your needing it.*

Sometimes the young ones (though even the young here move like careful grandparents) trace their fingers along what used to be my spine, now just a graceful depression that guides water down into the catching-place. They don't know what I was. To them I am simply the thirteenth basin, the one that appeared in living memory, though no one can agree exactly when.

This is what the others don't understand, the ones who thin out rather than settling in: we are not becoming stone. Stone is becoming us. We are teaching it the last refinement of form—how to hold perfectly still and perfectly open at once. How to be a vessel that neither grasps nor releases but simply participates in the great, slow, certain cycling of water from sky to stone to sky.

At the dying of every moon, She-who-sits-at-the-Narrow-End comes to drink from me. Her cupped palms fit exactly where mine once rested. Her lips touch the water where my reflection once wavered. And sometimes—rarely—I feel what might be recognition in the way she pauses after drinking, the way her clouded eyes rest on the particular curve of limestone that learned its shape from thirty thousand dawns of patient watching.

"We are all going where you've gone," she whispered once, so quietly even the stone barely heard. "But you found the straightest path."

I wanted to tell her: there is no path. There is only wearing away everything that isn't waiting. But my voice has long since dissolved into the mineral whisper of water moving through hidden fissures, saying nothing, meaning everything, continuing.

The bats know me. They drink from me in the last light, their tiny tongues quick as shadows. They remember when I had a different kind of surface, I think. But they too understand that memory is just another form of weather, wearing everything down to its essential shape.

I am the thirteenth basin. I hold water when it comes. I release it when it goes. I am perfectly empty, perfectly full, perfectly between.

And nothing changes.  
Not even this.  
Not ever.
