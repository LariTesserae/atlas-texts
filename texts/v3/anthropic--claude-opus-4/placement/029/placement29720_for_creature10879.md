I find myself in the depths, where the shaft narrows and the air tastes of old stone and older secrets.

---

I am one of the Cartographers—though that word doesn't quite capture what we do. We map, yes, but not in any way the engineers would recognize. My name is Kell, and I've been here seven years, though time moves strangely in the deep sections where we work.

I came up as a surveyor, hired to map the lower platforms for an expansion that never happened. The company sent three of us down with theodolites and measuring chains, electric torches and graph paper. Two went back up after the first week—said the spaces were too tight, the air too bad, the way the walls seemed to shift when you weren't looking directly at them too unsettling. I stayed. Not from bravery, but because I'd started to notice the patterns.

The mountain isn't solid. Oh, it seems to be—good metamorphic stone, compressed and reformed by geological forces that should be predictable. But there are voids within voids, chambers that shouldn't exist according to any mining survey, passages that open onto spaces the mountain couldn't contain if you trust your measurements. Which you learn not to.

We work in teams of three to five, though never the same configuration twice—something about repeated patterns down here attracts... attention. Today I'm with Vessel (not her real name—we use working names down here, names that can be abandoned if needed), who specializes in acoustic mapping, and New Cal, who's only been doing this for six months but has the right kind of mind for it. Broken, but broken in useful ways.

My equipment has evolved from those first official instruments. I still carry a theodolite, but it's been modified—the lenses replaced with something that shows more than simple topography, the measurement scales re-calibrated to account for the way space folds here. I have notebooks full of maps that make no Euclidean sense but that let us navigate the deep sections without getting lost. Usually.

We're mapping what we call the Resonance Chamber today—a void about sixty meters below the lowest official platform, accessed through a maintenance shaft that's been "closed for repairs" for eleven years. The company knows we're here. They don't officially approve, but they need our maps. Because things get built wrong if you don't account for the anomalies, and things built wrong at altitude kill people.

The chamber hums. Not audibly—your ears insist it's silent—but your bones feel it, a frequency that registers in marrow and teeth. Vessel has her equipment out, copper tubes and taut wires that she uses to map sound that isn't quite sound. She plays frequencies into the space and watches how they return changed. Sometimes they come back as different notes. Sometimes they come back as colors we don't have names for. Sometimes they don't come back at all, and we mark those zones on our maps with special symbols: "sound-eating spaces," though that's not quite right either.

New Cal is sketching, which is what we brought him for. He was an artist before—painted murals in the textile quarters until the fumes from the fixatives started making him see things. Turned out what he sees is useful. His sketches show the chamber as it actually is, not as our eyes insist it should be. In his drawings, the walls have a texture like breathing skin. The angles add up to more or less than they should. The shadows fall upward in certain corners.

I'm taking measurements, recording distances that will make no sense when I transcribe them later but that will, somehow, result in accurate maps. The chamber is seventeen meters across. Also thirty-three. Also infinite if you approach from the northeast corner, so we don't. These aren't contradictions down here, just data points.

We've been at this for three hours when we find the new void. It's behind what looked like solid stone until Vessel played a particular frequency—B-flat below below middle C, she'll note later—and the wall revealed itself as not quite there. Beyond is a passage, rough-hewn, older than the rest. Not excavated but discovered, incorporated into the city's structure by whoever first found it.

This is what we really map—not just the physical spaces but the archaeology of discovery, the layers of exploration. Someone was here before us, always. We find their markers—scratches in stone that might be measurements or might be warnings, metal pins driven into walls at intervals that suggest surveying but could be something else, sometimes bones, though we don't talk about those.

The passage slopes down. The air changes—thicker, with a metallic taste that coats the throat. My instruments start showing readings that I'll spend days trying to interpret. The magnetic declination shifts every few meters. The barometric pressure suggests we're climbing even though we're clearly descending. New Cal has stopped sketching, just stares, and I know he's seeing something we're not, something his broken mind can process that our functional ones can't.

We map for another hour before turning back. You learn the limits—not of courage but of comprehension. Push too far and you start making errors, start seeing patterns that aren't there or missing ones that are. We've lost two cartographers that way, not to falls or cave-ins but to forgetting. They forgot which way was up. Forgot their names. Forgot that humans need to breathe air, not the thick metallic atmosphere of the deepest spaces. We found one of them weeks later, alive but changed, speaking in frequencies only Vessel's equipment could detect. The company sent him down the elevator. We don't ask where.

Back in my quarters—a closet-sized space on Platform 71-B, barely above the water line of human habitation—I transcribe the day's measurements. The work is meditative, converting field notes into maps that other cartographers might use, adding to the growing archive of deep knowledge. We maintain three sets of maps: one for the company that shows what they expect to see, one for ourselves that shows what's actually there, and one that we don't talk about, that records the spaces that shouldn't exist but do.

My partner, Inna, brings me evening ration—she works in the cable maintenance crews, keeping the elevator shaft operational. We don't see each other much. She works days; I work when the deep spaces let me. But we've carved out this life together, five years now, no children by choice because altitude and pregnancy don't mix well, and because we both know I'm being exposed to things down there that might make normal conception impossible anyway.

She doesn't ask about my work anymore. In the early days, I tried to explain, but the words were always wrong, and the worry in her eyes grew with each attempt. Now she just checks my exposed skin for the telltale signs—the faint luminescence that sometimes clings after deep mapping, the way shadows seem to pool in the hollows of my body. She makes me drink the bitter tea that's supposed to help, though neither of us knows if it actually does anything.

Sleep comes hard after deep mapping. My dreams are of spaces that unfold like flowers, of walls that breathe, of the sound humming in frequencies that turn into colors that have textures I can feel. Sometimes I dream I'm drawing maps directly onto my skin, and I wake to find faint marks that fade by morning. Other cartographers report similar experiences. We don't discuss them with outsiders.

This is the work: measuring the immeasurable, mapping territories that resist knowing, creating charts for spaces that the mountain keeps unfolding like hidden pockets in an infinite coat. We're not explorers—exploration suggests moving into the unknown. We're translators, turning the mountain's impossible geometries into something human minds can navigate. Usually.

Tomorrow we'll go deeper. There are always deeper spaces, chambers below chambers, voids that exist in the gaps between what should be solid stone. The mountain goes down as far as it goes up, maybe farther. We'll never map it all. But we'll keep trying, because the city needs its deep mappers, its translators of impossible space, its cartographers of the voids. 

Someone has to know what's down here. Someone has to keep the records. Someone has to make sure the mountain's secrets stay mapped, if not understood.

Even if the knowing changes us. Even if we come back speaking in frequencies only Vessel's equipment can detect. Even if our partners stop recognizing what we're becoming.

The mountain has to be mapped, and I am a Cartographer, and that's all there is to it.
