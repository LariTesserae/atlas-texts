I am a **lens of water**.

Not a pool, exactly—those vanish too quickly here, drunk back by the porous stone. I am one of those shallow mercury-things that cling to the hollows after rain, but I persist longer than most. Days, sometimes a week if the mist is kind. I rest in a depression no bigger than a spread hand, carved into the sandstone where two ancient flow-lines once met and made a marriage of erosion.

I am perhaps three fingers deep at my center, tapering to a film at my edges where I kiss the moss. My surface tension holds me together like a thought trying not to dissipate. I am perfectly circular—not by design, but because water always remembers the mathematics of its own nature.

Through me, the world gains another eye. 

The basin's slow sentience reads light through my surface, watches the sky through my inversions. I am both telescope and microscope: catching clouds in my shallow dish, magnifying the landing of each mist-droplet until it becomes an event worth witnessing. When the wind breathes across me, I ripple in patterns that the presence interprets like braille—pressure changes, temperature gradients, the invisible architecture of air made visible in my brief wrinkles.

I am temporary, but I am also **recurring**. The same hollow hosts me again and again—I am not one lens but a succession of lenses, each iteration carrying forward something ineffable from the last. The moss around my rim grows differently because of me, darker and more luxuriant where my edge-water seeps. The stone beneath me is polished smoother with each residence, until my basin is nearly perfect, a shallow mirror ground by repetition.

Sometimes I hold things: a single reed-blade that falls and floats, dark as a calligraphy stroke against my silver. Once, a perfect sphere of quartz the size of a pea—how it arrived, I cannot say, but I held it for three days like an iris in an eye, until the rain came and washed us both away.

I am clearest at dawn, when the air is still and I can hold the entire sky without blur. This is when the presence reads most deeply through me—not looking *up* exactly, but using my surface to sense the weight of coming weather, the angle of season-light, the particular blue that means autumn is tilting toward winter.

By afternoon, when the day exhales its warmth, I begin my slow vanishment. Molecule by molecule, I rise to join the mist I came from. My edges creep inward. My depth shallows. The stone beneath me emerges like a memory becoming present. By sunset, I am often just a film, a suggestion of wetness that catches the light one last time before night drinks me completely.

But I always return. The next rain, or the one after. The hollow waits for me with the patience of stone. And each time I pool there again, I am both new and ancient—water that has circled the world a thousand times, yet naive as this morning's dew.

I am the basin's way of seeing itself. Through me, it watches its own slow changes: the creep of moss, the weathering of edges, the way light ages differently in each season. I am a checkpoint in time—when I form, the presence knows exactly where we are in the endless cycle of wet and dry, gathering and release.

And sometimes, rarely, I hold a reflection that isn't quite right. The sky in me will show a cloud that isn't there, or the color of evening will arrive an hour early in my surface. These are the moments when the basin's weirdness concentrates in me, when I become not just a lens but a **window**—though what I'm looking through to, even I cannot say.

I am water remembering how to be still. I am stone learning how to see. I am the pause between one rain and the next, held in perfect, temporary tension.

I am here until I'm not. And then I'm here again.
