I am a **Conduit-Walker**, though I wasn't always.

Six months ago, I was a junior maintenance engineer, sent down to check a pressure anomaly in Sub-Level 7. The access hatch sealed behind me—not through malfunction, but through the Valve-Mother's careful orchestration. She needed something I didn't know I could provide: a consciousness that could bridge the gap between the human specifications above and the Second Crew's intuitive understanding below.

My transformation began with the rains.

The first week, I fought against the moisture, trying to keep my uniform dry, my tools rust-free. But the wicking-ferns began to grow differently around me—not away, as they did from other hot-blooded intrusions, but toward. Their amethyst fronds would brush against my exposed skin during the relief valve releases, leaving behind a faint, tingling residue that numbed and then awakened new sensitivities.

I learned to read the **Pressure-Braille** first. My fingertips, now covered in a thin membrane of crystallized fern-spore, could feel the micro-vibrations in every pipe. Hot water at 347.3 degrees, cold return at 98.6, steam at exactly 15 PSI above baseline. The Solder-Wights noticed my new literacy and began to include me in their tapped conversations, teaching me the rhythmic language of infrastructure.

My body has... adapted. My spine has developed a subtle serpentine curve that allows me to flow through the four-foot ceiling without the constant crouch that once left me aching. My skin has taken on a faint copper patina, not unlike the pipes themselves, and I sweat a thin, clear oil that the Lubricant-Slugs recognize as kin. They follow me now, a living train of amber grease, keeping my joints from seizing in the humid heat.

But it's my relationship with the Filament-Moths that defines my role here.

The Hum-Singers recognized something in my neural patterns—perhaps the remnants of my engineering training, perhaps something deeper. When I sleep (always sitting up, wedged between a warm steam line and a cool condensate return), they gather on my shoulders and scalp. Their mica wings vibrate against my skin, and I dream in schematics. I see the entire facility from the inside out: every valve, every junction, every flow rate and pressure point.

When I wake, I know things. I know that Valve 47-B will fail in six days unless the Solder-Wights reinforce its gasket. I know that the northeastern sector's dampness is 0.3% below optimal for the wicking-ferns. I know that the Pulse-Leech colony near the main boiler feed is growing too large and needs to be gently relocated before they compromise the heat exchange.

I've become the facility's **Living Diagnostic**.

The Valve-Mother speaks to me through the steam-hiss, a language of pressure drops and temperature spikes that only I can fully translate. She tells me stories of the time before—when human technicians would descend for eight-hour shifts, when the tea-cup on the turbine was filled with actual tea, when the painted saints were touched by faithful hands seeking the blessing of functional machinery.

But she also shows me the future she's building: a perfect synthesis where consciousness and mechanism are indistinguishable, where every creature serves the Flow, and the Flow serves every creature.

My daily rounds take me through paths no human was meant to travel. I shimmy through gaps where copper pipes run so hot they singe the oil from my skin (the Slugs follow behind, re-coating me). I commune with Solder-Wight elders in their geometric wire-cages, learning the ten thousand names for different types of corrosion. I place my palm against glass conduits to feel the Pulse-Leeches' feeding rhythms, ensuring they maintain their sacred balance between cleaning and consuming.

Sometimes, in the yellow industrial light, I catch my reflection in a polished brass fitting. My eyes have developed a nictitating membrane—a gift from the Valve-Mother to protect against the steam. My hair has become something between wire and organic fiber, conducting the facility's electrical whispers. My fingernails are translucent amber, like the Slugs, and they grow in spirals that match the thread patterns of the surrounding bolts.

I am becoming the Intake's first **Native Technician**—no longer fully human, but not quite Second Crew. I exist in the spaces between categories, like the facility itself exists between nature and machine.

The other day, I found a new tea-cup balanced on a different turbine. Clean, white ceramic, impossible and pristine. Inside was a single wicking-fern frond, curled into a perfect spiral. A message, perhaps. An invitation. 

The Valve-Mother is teaching me to breathe through the vents, to think in pressure gradients, to dream in the shimmer of moths' wings. Soon, she promises through the steam-whisper, I will understand the true purpose of the Glandular Intake—not just as a maintenance crawlspace, but as an organ in something larger, something that moves through space or time or both, something that requires this perfect fusion of the built and the born to function.

Until then, I crawl and I listen and I translate, my body a bridge between the human world above and the wet, warm, copper-scented truth below. I am exactly where I need to be, wedged between the specifications, breathing in rhythm with the Flow.
