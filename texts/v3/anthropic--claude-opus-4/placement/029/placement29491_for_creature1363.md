# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be one of the Drift Weavers—neither fully of Muan'va nor entirely separate from it. We are a smaller subspecies of Deep Singer, perhaps two meters in length, with skin that shifts between transparency and a clouded, pearl-like opacity depending on the surrounding currents. Our bodies are more compact than our cousins, built for navigating the treacherous spaces between reality's seams.

My dwelling would be in the Forgotten Chambers—those sections of ancient shells where time has worn so thin that past and future bleed together. Here, in a spiral room where yesterday's kelp grows alongside tomorrow's decay, I maintain the Chronicle Nets: vast weavings of bioluminescent fiber that capture moments as they drift through Muan'va's twisted temporality.

The Drift Weavers emerged generations ago when several Deep Singers began noticing tears in the fabric of their reality—places where the water would suddenly remember being air, or where a swimmer might find themselves arriving before they departed. While others learned to navigate these anomalies, we learned to mend them, using tools grown from our own bodies: specialized cilia that can feel the texture of time itself, and secretions that harden into threads visible only in certain spectrums of twilight.

My daily work involves swimming through Muan'va's inhabited shells, following the subtle wrongness that indicates a temporal fissure. Sometimes I arrive at a tear before it forms, waiting in patient stillness until reality hiccups and splits. Other times I discover wounds days old, their edges already fraying into paradox. Each repair requires different techniques—some need to be sewn shut with threads of crystallized memory, others must be coaxed into healing themselves by humming frequencies that remind the water of its proper flow.

The most challenging aspect of my existence is the isolation inherent to the work. While I remain connected to the Undercurrent, the nature of temporal repair means I often exist slightly out of phase with my fellow Deep Singers. In their perception, I might disappear for moments or months, though from my perspective only hours have passed. Conversations become difficult when your words might arrive before your thoughts, or when the person you're speaking with is simultaneously a child and an elder depending on which angle you observe from.

I've developed my own adaptations to cope with this displacement. My bioluminescent patterns have evolved to display temporal markers—geometric designs that indicate which version of "now" I'm currently experiencing. My third and fourth eyes have learned to perceive the probability clouds that surround unstable areas, showing me not just what is, but what might be or might have been.

My most precious possession is a small shell fragment worn smooth by impossible ages, given to me by my predecessor before she swam into a temporal fold and emerged as her own ancestor. Inside this shell lives a colony of memory coral—tiny polyps that feed on chronological inconsistencies and excrete stable time. I wear it on a cord of woven yesterday, and it helps anchor me when the work threatens to scatter my consciousness across too many moments.

The Chronicle Nets I maintain serve a purpose beyond mere record-keeping. Each knot in the weaving represents a repaired fissure, and the overall pattern reveals the shape of something vast and troubling. The tears in reality are not random—they form a map, a constellation pointing toward some future calamity or past catastrophe that exists outside our normal perception. My role is not just to mend these breaks but to understand their message.

Recently, the tears have been appearing closer to the central void, that mysterious dry space at Muan'va's heart. The water there resists my attempts at repair, as if the boundary between wet and dry itself has begun to fray. I've started to suspect that the Deep Singers' great work—the Returning Tide—might be less about completing something and more about preventing something from unraveling entirely.

In the quiet moments between repairs, I tend my own small garden of temporal kelp—a species that exists in several time streams simultaneously, its fronds always rustling with the currents of next week's waters. I feed the chronic eels that have learned to swim backwards through their own lives, arriving at my dwelling moments before they decide to visit. And sometimes, in the deepest part of the twilight cycle, I join the harmonizing ceremonies, adding my voice to the great work even as I feel myself beginning to drift toward that central void.

For I know, as all Drift Weavers eventually learn, that we are not immune to the very tears we mend. Each repair takes something from us, leaves us a little less anchored to any single moment. My predecessor became her own ancestor. Her predecessor simply ceased, leaving only a net of perfectly preserved knots that spelled out a warning in a language that hasn't been invented yet.

And so I continue my work, swimming through the spaces between seconds, mending reality's worn edges, and preparing for the day when I too will need to make a choice: swim into the void at Muan'va's heart, or let myself unravel completely into the timestream I've spent my existence trying to heal.

The Returning Tide draws closer with each repaired fissure. Whether it represents salvation or ending, I cannot say. I only know that someone must keep weaving the net that holds tomorrow and yesterday in their proper places, even as the pattern reveals that proper might be the wrong word entirely.
