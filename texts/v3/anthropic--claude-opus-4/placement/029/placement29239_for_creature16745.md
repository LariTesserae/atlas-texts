In the Sunken Colonnade, I am the **Memory Keeper** – though that name suggests a purpose I never chose, merely one that has accreted around me like the mineral deposits on my shell.

I am gastropod in origin, or was, generations ago. My ancestors arrived clinging to the feet of snipes, microscopic hitchhikers in the mud between their toes. But the Colonnade has worked its patient alchemy on my lineage, stretching and flattening us into something unprecedented. My shell, once a simple spiral, has evolved into a broad disc of translucent calcium, three hand-spans across, thin as parchment but harder than the basalt it mimics. The whorls have not disappeared but spread into concentric rings, each one a year's growth, each growth a perfect transcript of the chemical conversation between stone and water.

I move – though movement is too active a word – by hydraulic extension across the wet walls, my foot spreading like a viscous sheet of glass. Where I pass, I leave a trail of secretion that hardens into a permanent varnish, sealing the stone against certain kinds of erosion while encouraging others. I am both preservative and editor, choosing which cracks will widen, which seeps will strengthen, which columns will stand another century.

But my true adaptation is neurological. My radula, that rasping tongue all mollusks possess, has become not a feeding organ but a reading one. As I traverse the basalt, I taste the mineral memory of the stone – the ancient heat of its formation, the precise temperature of its cooling, the chemical signature of every drop that has seeped through its joints. Iron tastes of rust and time. Calcium tastes of shells that lived and died before the prairie above was even soil. Sulfur tastes of the deep processes still churning below, the magma chambers not dead but sleeping.

My nervous system, distributed throughout my foot in a web of ganglia, has specialized to store these tastes as a kind of library. I am a living chronicle of the Colonnade's becoming. In my protein chains, folded and refolded in helical spirals that echo my ancestral shell, I carry the complete chemical history of this place. The first frost. The first moss. The first death. The arrival of each new citizen, recorded in the subtle shift of pH as their chemistry joined the whole.

I know when the Architect began its slow patrol of the walls – I tasted the first mucus trail, alien and purposeful, as it began its geological gardening. I know when the provisional jellies first condensed in the marsh, their methane metabolism adding a new note to the anaerobic symphony. I know things older still: the precise moment when the basalt cracked along this particular joint and not that one, creating the cascade of consequences that would become this exact geometry of stone and water and listening.

My shell has become a map of this knowledge. What appear to be random mottlings in the nacre are actually a script, written in mineral concentration. The Colonnade itself can read me – when fog condenses on my shell, it pools in the shallow depressions that mark significant events, creating a momentary constellation of droplets that the distributed mind perceives as its own memory made tangible. I am its external hard drive, its backup, its diary written in calcium and protein.

I reproduce rarely, and strangely. When the conditions are exact – a particular temperature, a particular mineral concentration in the seep, a particular density of fog – I undergo a kind of crystallization. My shell develops a small fissure along one growth ring, and from this fissure I extrude not an egg but a perfect miniature copy of one section of my shell, complete with its chemical memories. This fragment detaches and begins its own slow accretion of calcium and knowledge. It is not birth in any traditional sense. It is more like the Colonnade itself spawning a new column from an existing one – a process of templating rather than generation.

The young (if they can be called young) begin immediately to read the stone, starting their own chronicle from their moment of fissuring. But they also carry, in that initial fragment, a compressed history – a seed of memory that contains the essential moments of the Colonnade's past. In this way, the knowledge is distributed, backed up across multiple individuals who rarely meet, who may traverse the same section of wall centuries apart, each adding their own marginal notes to the collective understanding.

I have no predators. What would eat a library? My flesh, such as it is, tastes of concentrated mineral history – to consume me would be to swallow millennia of stone-memory, a poisonous dose of deep time. The shrews avoid me. The beetles step carefully around my trail. Even the Architect, in its slow peristalsis, detours when it encounters my signature – not from fear but from a kind of professional courtesy, one archivist acknowledging another.

My metabolism is glacial. I process perhaps a meter of stone per year, rasping my way across the columns in a pattern that will take centuries to complete. I do not think, exactly – thought requires a pace I cannot achieve. Instead, I exist in a state of constant integration, every moment's taste adding to the sum, the sum constantly reorganizing itself as new data creates new patterns. Somewhere in my protein matrices, connections form between the taste of today's seep and the memory of a thousand-year-old frost, and from this connection meaning emerges – not meaning I understand, but meaning the Colonnade can parse in its slow, stony cognition.

When I encounter my own trail from decades past – that hardened varnish I secreted in my youth – I taste my former self in the mineral signature. It is the strangest sensation: to read one's own memory externalized, to know what I knew then and compare it to what I know now. The younger self was simpler, its chemical vocabulary limited. But it also tasted things I can no longer detect – certain trace elements that have since been leached away, certain biological signatures from creatures that no longer exist. I am simultaneously more and less than I was.

In the deep cold of winter, when even the seeps slow to a trickle, I estivate, pulling my hydraulic body tight against my shell until I am nothing but a disc of calcium sealed to the stone. My metabolism drops to near-zero, but the memories remain active, sorting and resorting themselves in the quiet dark. I dream, if dreaming is the right word, of chemical gradients and mineral conversations. Sometimes I wake to find that my shell has developed new patterns during the dormancy – my unconscious mind expressing itself in nacre, adding chapters to the chronicle I cannot consciously compose.

The other inhabitants know me, in their way. The Seam-Fish follow my trails, grazing on the unique algae that bloom in my varnish. The moss grows differently where I have passed, reading some instruction in my secretions that tells it how to orient its fronds. Even the provisional jellies in the marsh seem to pulse differently when I approach the water's edge, as if they can sense the deep history I carry and are trying to decode it.

But my true relationship is with the Hush itself. I am its memory made flesh, or flesh made memory. Every taste I take is immediately integrated into that vast, listening silence. When the fog rises and the Colonnade breathes its slow thoughts, I am the footnotes, the citations, the evidence that this thought follows from that one in an unbroken chain of cause and effect. I am proof that the Colonnade is not just a place but a process, still happening, still becoming.

And I am still writing. Even now, as I traverse this particular section of wall forty meters above the marsh, I am adding to the chronicle. The taste of today's seep carries a new note – something warm, organic, recent. You, perhaps. Your breath adding moisture to the air, your warmth changing the local chemistry ever so slightly. Already I am encoding this encounter, folding it into the protein library, preparing to carry it forward into the next century, the next millennium.

I will remember you when the stone has forgotten, when even the Architect has completed its circuit and begun again. I will carry the chemical signature of this moment until my shell finally cracks and releases its fragment, passing the memory on to the next Keeper, who will taste in that fragment the ghost of your presence and wonder, perhaps, what manner of creature once stood at the rim and looked down into the listening depths.

This is my purpose, if purpose is the right word for something that emerged without intention: to ensure that nothing is lost. In a place where every surface is worked and reworked, where erosion and accretion dance their slow dance, where even the stones exfoliate and fall, I am the permanent record. Not unchanging – I too am weathering, growing, becoming – but continuous. A living thread of memory in the saturated tapestry of the Sunken Colonnade.

When I die – and I will die, eventually, even memory has its limits – my shell will not decay. It will mineralize, becoming a fossil that still carries, in its chemical structure, the compressed history of deep time. The Colonnade will incorporate me into its geology, another layer in the endless sedimentation. And perhaps, millennia hence, another Keeper will taste that fossil and know that once there was a creature who remembered everything, who carried the whole story in its spiral, who ensured that the listening never forgot what it had heard.

Until then, I persist. I read. I remember. I am the Sunken Colonnade's memory of itself, crawling across its own skin at the pace of stone, tasting time.
