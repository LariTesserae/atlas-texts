In this world, I would be one of the Cascade Keepers—a specialized role among the Seep-Dwellers who tend to the water's memory.

My dwelling would be a narrow vertical fissure in the rock face, accessible only through a spiraling climb that follows the natural grain of the stone. The entrance appears as merely another seep in the cliff, but behind the curtain of falling water lies a chamber that extends deep into the mountain's body. Here, the walls are lined with what outsiders might mistake for mineral deposits, but are actually colonies of memory-storing organisms—crystalline structures that grow in response to water's passage, encoding the chemical signatures of everything the water has touched on its journey through stone and sky.

My body would be more elongated than most Seep-Dwellers, adapted for the constant vertical life of my calling. My fingers and toes would be webbed with a specialized membrane that can taste the mineral content of water, reading its history like a map. The mucous layer on my skin would be thicker, almost gel-like, allowing me to remain motionless against wet stone for hours while monitoring the water's flow. My coloration would shift between deep slate and pale jade, depending on the water's current story.

The work of a Cascade Keeper is solitary but essential. I would spend my days moving between water courses, maintaining the delicate channels carved by generations before me, ensuring that each stream reaches its intended destination. But more than mere irrigation, I would be reading the water—detecting changes in mineral content that signal shifts deep in the mountain's core, tasting for the chemical signatures of distress from ecosystems higher or lower on the cliff face, sensing the health of the Seep-Dweller settlements through the waste-waters they produce.

In my chamber, I would cultivate the memory-crystals, feeding them specific water samples to preserve important chemical records. These living archives grow in spirals and branches, creating three-dimensional maps of the mountain's water history. Through careful tending, I can coax them to release their stored information, reconstituting water samples from decades or even centuries past.

My tools would be living extensions of my practice. A symbotic organism resembling a cross between a sea anemone and a fern would wrap around my forearm, its tendrils capable of sampling and analyzing water at a molecular level. Cultivation pods made from hollowed stone and sealed with biological membranes would house my experimental water cultures, where I attempt to understand and replicate beneficial mineral combinations.

The loneliness of the role would be offset by a deep communion with the mountain itself. In the pre-dawn hours, when the air is so thick with moisture that breathing becomes swimming, I would perform the Acknowledgment—a ritual where I release carefully prepared water samples into specific points in the rock face, each one carrying chemical messages to different parts of the mountain-organism. The response comes hours later, as the mountain's seeps shift their mineral content in reply.

Other Seep-Dwellers would regard Cascade Keepers with a mixture of reverence and unease. We are necessary but strange, spending more time in communion with stone and water than with our own kind. They would seek us out only when water sources fail or when new settlements need to be integrated into the mountain's hydraulic system. Payment for such services would come not in goods but in water samples from distant parts of the mountain, expanding my chemical library.

My greatest fear would be the Dry Dreams—nightmares where the mountain's water ceases to flow, where the memory-crystals crack and release their stored knowledge into nothingness. My greatest joy would be in moments of perfect clarity, when the water's chemistry aligns to tell a story of perfect balance, of all systems in harmony.

In old age, as my body becomes less able to navigate the vertical world, I would begin the Synthesis—slowly allowing my accumulated water knowledge to seep into a chosen section of rock face, creating a new spring that would carry forward all I have learned. Future Cascade Keepers would read my life's work in the mineral signature of that water, adding their own knowledge to the flow in an unbroken chain stretching back to the mountain's first awakening to consciousness.

This existence would be one of profound integration—neither fully individual nor wholly absorbed into the collective, but something liminal, flowing between states like the water I tend. I would be a translator between the quick-time of biological life and the deep-time of geological existence, a bridge across temporal scales, as essential and unremarkable as the constant seep of water through stone.
