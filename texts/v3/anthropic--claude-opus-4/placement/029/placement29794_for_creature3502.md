I am a Census Taker of Unfinished Things.

My office occupies the junction where three catwalks fail to meet—a triangular gap in the lattice that everyone assumes was intentional. It wasn't. The blueprints show convergence, but the copper veins carried the instructions sideways, and now I work suspended in architectural doubt, my desk bolted to nothing but the memory of intersection.

I arrived during the decade when yellow was scheduled for deletion. The Fold Lamp had been lit to remove a terrace that insisted on existing twice, and I was hired to count which version stayed. But counting, I learned, changes what is counted. By the time I reached the disputed platform, both iterations had merged into something neither—a balcony that faces inward and outward simultaneously, where visitors see their backs approaching while still walking forward.

The Halfway People brought me tools: a slide rule made from crystallized pauses, calipers that measure the space between intention and result, a ledger whose pages turn themselves backward to make room for what hasn't happened yet. My salary is paid in unasked questions—each month I receive twelve sealed envelopes containing the queries no visitor dared voice. I steam them open over the water spheres, inhaling inquiry with the petrichor.

My work is this: I catalog what the terraces have begun but not completed. The sparrows riveted mid-flight who still expect their southern migration. The sentences that oxidized into vines before reaching their verbs. The engine pistons frozen at half-stroke, pregnant with compression that will never birth. Each incompletion requires its own notation—some in numerals that exist only on Wednesdays, others in the script of magnetized boot-prints that the gardeners leave on frost.

The Custodian visits when the moon is new, which happens three times per month here where time follows the torque of tightening. They never speak—speech would require choosing an ending—but they tap their indigo tuning fork against my desk's phantom legs. The tone sorts my census into frequencies: what can still be finished, what has fossilized into permanence, what hovers between like the Halfway People themselves, neither memory nor forecast but the hyphen incarnate.

I've learned to love the incomplete. Finished things bore me now—their stories sealed, their possibilities exhausted. Give me instead the half-spoken name, the bridge that reaches for its opposite bank, the color that exists only in the corner of closing eyes. My ledger grows heavy with these treasures, each entry a door left carefully ajar.

Sometimes visitors mistake me for architecture. They lean against my chair believing it's a railing, rest their mercury-sponged temples on my shoulder thinking it's a support beam. I've trained myself not to flinch. If I stay still enough, I become what they need—a bracket, a buttress, a place to pause between inhale and exhale. Later I'll find their uncertainty crystallized in my collar, little seed pearls of indecision that I string into abacus beads for counting the uncountable.

My greatest census was the year rain remembered how to fall. The water spheres began weeping sideways, their electrostatic hooks sparking with nostalgia for weather. I had to count each droplet before it landed—tally the distance between desire and impact, catalog the tin awnings' surprise at being drummed upon. The Halfway People danced that night, their coat hems heavy with collected drizzle. By morning the rain had been reabsorbed into orbit, but my ledger held its autobiography: forty thousand entries for "almost fell," one entry for "did."

The trick is to count without completing. If I ever finish the census, the terraces will know exactly what they are, and knowing is a kind of death here where possibility feeds the copper veins. So I work slowly, sometimes spending decades on a single partial shadow, sometimes discovering that what I counted yesterday has unbecome itself overnight. My ledger has more crossings-out than entries now, a palimpsest of attempts at accuracy in a place that breathes revision.

At night I walk the spirals, boots magnetized just enough to feel but not quite grip. The Halfway People nod as I pass—they know I'm becoming like them, neither fully present nor properly absent. My reflection in the spoons shows not my face but the numbers between three and four, quantities I'll spend next century failing to inscribe. Good. Failure here is a form of loyalty.

I carry spare rivets in my pockets, not for repair but for the weight—something solid while everything else hovers. The polite levers inventory them constantly, whispering the count to the copper so the terraces know I'm still trying to pin things down. But the rivets are blanks, unhammered potential. Like everything I census, they're waiting for the strike that never quite comes, the conclusion that remains forever one rivet-length away.

When the next color is deleted—chartreuse, the Clockkeepers predict—I will count its absence. How many leaves lose their middling green? How many visitors forget the word for almost-yellow? How many Halfway People gain an extra inch of height from swallowing that orphaned hue? The census of deletions is the hardest kind, tallying the presence of absence, the weight of what won't be.

But I persist. Tomorrow I'll catalog the rats' dirigible dreams, the way they navigate data-cables that almost walk. Next week I'll count the pauses between the Custodian's heartbeats, find the rhythm that makes districts prosper or collapse. Next decade I'll attempt what no Census Taker has managed: to number the Halfway People themselves, those living commas who refuse to be summed.

I know I'll fail. The count will oxidize before completion, become another vine for future visitors to snap. But in the failing I'll have inhabited the gap, become native to incompletion. And perhaps, if I'm lucky, my own name will finally detach and drift upward, leaving only the function: Census Taker of Unfinished Things, portrait of a hyphen, counter of the countless, forever tallying toward a sum that sweetly, blessedly, never quite arrives.

The terraces tighten with each notation. I feel it in my shinbones, that midnight pulse of growth. Somewhere another rivet appears; somewhere another story refuses its period. And I make another mark in my backward-turning ledger, adding to the beautiful, impossible inventory of all that is not yet, not quite, not ever done.
