In the lower reaches where mist pools thick as milk and bioluminescent insects drift like living constellations, I tend the Listening Wells.

I am neither wholly of the builders nor the growers, though my work touches both. My skin bears the perpetual dampness of these depths, a sheen that never fully dries even when I climb to the middle terraces for supplies. My fingernails grow thick and ridged like bark, and my hair—when I let it free from its working braids—moves with a weight that speaks of constant moisture. The others say I smell of petrichor and rust, of deep earth and patient metal.

The Listening Wells are apertures in the city's ancient hide, shafts that descend beyond even my knowledge into the mountain's core. Some are narrow as my arm, others wide enough to swallow a dwelling whole. Water seeps up through them—not flowing exactly, but breathing, exhaling moisture that feeds the lowest gardens and fills the air with its mineral tang. But water is not all that rises.

There are sounds. Not the industrial hymn that threads through the upper city, but something older—resonances that predate the first terrace, the first deliberately placed stone. These sounds arrive in patterns I have spent forty years learning to interpret: the mountain's deep settling, the movement of underground waters, the migration of creatures that have never known light. Sometimes, rarely, there are other sounds—almost like voices, though no language I recognize.

My tools are unusual even by our city's standards. Long poles of a metal that rings when struck, each cut to precise lengths that correspond to different depths. Ceramic vessels shaped to capture and amplify the faintest vibrations. Charts where I map the sounds in a notation of my own devising, symbols that mean nothing to anyone but me and my few apprentices. We lower these instruments into the wells at specific times—dawn, when the mist is thickest; midnight, when the insects' glow pulses strongest; and during the brief moments when the city's machines pause their breathing to recalibrate.

The work changes you. My hearing has sharpened beyond comfort—I can detect the moisture content of air by its whisper, feel the health of stone by its resonance. But more than that, I have begun to understand that our city is not built upon the mountain so much as grown from it, that the wells are not mere gaps but organs, that the sounds rising from below are not random but patterned like speech or song.

My dwelling clings to the underside of a terrace like a barnacle, accessible only by a rope ladder I must oil daily against the rust. Inside, my walls are covered with decades of charts, the evolution of the deep sounds rendered in my careful notation. Patterns emerge if you know how to look: cycles that span years, crescendos that predict the blooming of certain deep-dwelling fungi, diminuendos that herald the migration of the luminous insects.

The other inhabitants regard me and my kind with a mixture of respect and unease. We are necessary—the wells must be monitored, their waters tested, their sounds interpreted—but we are also apart. We spend too much time listening to what rises from below, too little in the sun-drenched gardens above. Our apprentices come to us young and eager but often leave for brighter work after a few seasons. Only a few stay to truly learn the deep listening.

I have three apprentices now. One is learning to craft the resonance poles, shaving metal with infinite patience to achieve the proper tones. Another studies the notation, slowly building her own vocabulary of symbols for sounds I have no marks for. The third, the youngest, simply sits by the wells and listens, occasionally humming back frequencies that make my spine prickle with recognition.

At night, when the mist rises so thick that even the insects' glow becomes diffuse, I sometimes lower myself partway into the larger wells, secured by ropes woven from the tough vines that grow at these depths. There, suspended in the breathing dark, I listen not with my ears but with my whole body, feeling the mountain's pulse through the water-laden air. Sometimes I think I almost understand what it's trying to tell us.

But understanding, I have learned, is not the point. We are not here to decipher or control. We are here to witness, to maintain the conversation between what is above and what is below, to ensure that the city remains rooted in more than just stone. The wells breathe, the mountain speaks, and I listen, adding my own small notes to the endless notation of this ancient dialogue.

In the morning, I will climb to the middle terraces to trade my recordings for supplies—oil for the ropes, fresh ceramic for new vessels, the small luxuries that make life in the depths bearable. The journey up is always a shock: the brightness, the chatter of voices, the confident hum of machinery that knows its purpose. But I always return to the wells, to the mist and the listening, to the work that chose me as much as I chose it.

For I know something the sun-dwellers do not: our city is not as stable as it seems. It shifts, settles, adjusts itself in movements too slow for most to perceive. The wells tell me this in their changing songs, in the subtle variations of their exhalations. We are not living on something built, but something building, forever becoming itself through the patient work of water, stone, and time.

And I am its witness, its recorder, one small part of the vast organism that is this vertical world, keeping faith with the depths that feed us all.
