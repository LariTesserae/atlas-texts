Their diet is simple but not meager. The moss comes in varieties — some thick and bitter, some thin and almost sweet when blanched in the warm condensation that gathers near the engine's outer walls. The ferns produce small, tightly curled fronds that they harvest before they fully unfurl, eating them raw or steeping them in water heated by placing sealed stone vessels against the warm conduits.

From the pale trees on the terraces, they harvest not fruit but a kind of sap that beads along shallow cuts in the bark. It hardens into translucent drops that taste faintly metallic but sustain them through the long cycles of machine-tending. They never take too much from any one tree — just enough to feed, never enough to weaken.

The skittering ivory creatures are not eaten but carefully tended. The Listeners have discovered that these creatures' trails through the moss create patterns that predict where new growth will be densest. They follow these living maps with the same attention they give to the engine's hum.

### Sleep and Cycles

The Listeners sleep in shifts, never all at once.

Their sleeping platforms are carved directly into the stone of their dwellings — shallow depressions lined with dried moss that shapes itself to their bodies over time. They sleep with one hand or foot pressed against the bare stone, maintaining their connection to the city's vibrations even in rest.

Their sleep is not deep but layered, like their hearing. They rest in the shallows of consciousness, able to wake instantly if the engine's rhythm changes or if water begins to flow where it shouldn't. They have adapted to this half-sleep so thoroughly that deep unconsciousness would feel like drowning to them now.

They mark time not by sun or moon but by the engine's cycles — each full rotation of its deepest harmonic marks what they call a "breath." Thirty breaths make a "circulation," which is roughly equivalent to what others might call a day, though the flat light above never changes to mark it.

### Memory and Knowledge

The Listeners do not write in the way of ink on paper. Instead, they build memories into the stone itself.

Along certain walls, especially near the engine building, there are patterns of small indentations — some natural, some carefully carved. By running their fingers along these patterns while listening to the stone's vibration, they can "read" the history of adjustments made to that section of the city. Each configuration of holes creates a different harmonic when the stone vibrates, encoding information in sound rather than sight.

Their oldest memories are built into the foundation stones themselves — knowledge of how the engine was first awakened, how the channels were first carved, how the first moss was encouraged to grow. These deep memories require multiple Listeners working together, each touching different points on the stone to create the full harmonic pattern that unlocks the information.

Knowledge passes between them not through lengthy instruction but through guided touch. An elder Listener will take a younger one's hand and place it on exactly the right spot on a wall, at exactly the right pressure, until they feel what needs to be felt. Words are secondary to sensation.

### Beliefs Without Gods

The Listeners have no temples, no priests, no prayers in the traditional sense. But they do have a deep conviction that the world has patterns, and that their role is to maintain them.

They believe in what they call "the Accordance" — not a mystical force but a state of balance between water, stone, life, and machine. When everything is in Accordance, the engine hums smoothly, water flows where needed, moss grows thick, and the slow creatures thrive. When Accordance breaks, it's not catastrophe but drift — things gradually moving out of alignment until efficiency drops and life grows thin.

They see themselves as the conscious part of this system, the part that can notice drift and correct it. Not through divine mandate but through patient attention and small adjustments. They are the feedback loop that keeps their world stable.

Death, to them, is not an ending but a transition in the Accordance. They inter their dead in special chambers where the stone has been prepared to accept moisture differently. Bodies are wrapped in living moss and placed where the water can slowly carry their minerals back into the stone itself. They believe that in this way, the dead continue to participate in the cycles of their world — their calcium strengthening the stone, their water joining the hidden flows.

### Reproduction and Continuity

Children are rare among the Listeners, but not precious in the way of hoarded gold. They are precious in the way of new growth on an old tree — celebrated but not coddled.

Pregnancy is long among them — thirteen engine circulations — and marked by an increased sensitivity to vibration. Pregnant Listeners often discover new faults in the stone or variations in the water flow that others miss. They are not removed from work but given the gentler tasks, always near the stone where they can listen most clearly.

Children are born with their vibration sensitivity already partially developed. From their first breath, they are held against the stone to feel the engine's rhythm. They learn to walk on the damp terraces, to balance on the narrow channels, to place their hands just so to feel the water moving beneath the surface.

Education is not formal schooling but apprenticeship to the city itself. Children follow adults on their rounds, first watching, then touching, then adjusting. By the time they reach maturity (marked not by age but by their ability to sense a hairline crack in stone from across a plaza), they have already spent years in service to the Accordance.

### The Threshold Walker

But you asked who you would be in this world, and I sense you would not be born to this life of quiet attention and patient adjustment.

You would be what they call a **Threshold Walker** — one who comes from outside the bowl but learns to hear what the Listeners hear.

You arrived during what they call a "displacement" — when the engine's rhythm shifted just enough to suggest that something new had entered the system. Not a threat, necessarily, but a variable that needed integration. The Listeners found you at the rim of the bowl, drawn by something you couldn't name, already beginning to feel the vibrations in your chest.

Unlike them, you were not born to this sensitivity. You had to learn it through practice and failure. Your hands, thicker and less articulated than theirs, took months to develop the fine touch needed to sense moisture gradients in stone. Your ears, shaped for a louder world, had to be retrained to parse the subtle harmonics of the engine beneath the sound of your own heartbeat.

But you had one advantage: perspective.

Where the Listeners saw only their world of stone and water, you saw patterns from other places. You recognized that their engine's rhythm matched certain tidal patterns you'd observed elsewhere. You suggested new ways to channel water based on river deltas you'd seen in distant lands. You brought knowledge of plants that might thrive in their carefully tended moisture, expanding their limited diet.

You became valuable not despite your outsider status but because of it. The Listeners learned to trust your different way of sensing, your ability to stand at the threshold between their world and the larger world beyond the bowl.

You live now in a small chamber carved into the cliff face, halfway between the rim and the city proper. Your sleeping platform faces both ways — toward the stone where you can press your palm to feel the engine's pulse, and toward a small window where you can see the world beyond the bowl.

Each morning, you perform two rituals: first, the Listening, where you check the city's breath like any other tender of the Accordance. Second, the Watching, where you scan the horizon for changes that might affect the closed system of the bowl — storms that might bring too much water, dry seasons that might parch even their careful hoarding.

You have learned their language of low tones and precise pauses, but you also translate. When traders appear at the rim (rarely, but sometimes), you are the one who explains the city's needs in terms outsiders can understand. When young Listeners grow curious about the world beyond, you tell them stories that don't disrupt their connection to the stone but expand their sense of what patterns might exist.

You are not fully of their world, but you are necessary to it. You are the permeable membrane between inside and outside, the one who ensures that the bowl does not become a trap.

In a world of careful balance and patient adjustment, you are the controlled variable of change — neither rejecting the Accordance nor bound completely by it, but dancing always at its edges, where stone meets sky and the engine's hum fades into the wider silence of the world.
