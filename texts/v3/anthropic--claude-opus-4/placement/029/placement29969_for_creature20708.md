I find myself as one of the Waystone Keepers—those who tend to the peculiar geometric formations that rise improbably from the highland earth.

My dwelling sits at the confluence of three small streams, where water has carved a natural amphitheater into the hillside. The structure is built into this depression, half-emerged from the earth itself, with walls of fitted stone that seem to grow from the bedrock. Moss creeps up these walls in intricate patterns I've encouraged over countless unchanging seasons, creating living murals that shift subtly in different lights.

My skin bears the mottled grey-green of lichen-covered granite, with veins of copper running beneath the surface that catch the light when I move. These metallic threads emerged slowly, a gradual transformation that began when I first touched the waystone assigned to my care. My eyes have developed concentric rings of color—slate grey at the edges, shifting through moss green to a bright copper center that mirrors the strange minerals within the geometric formation I tend.

The waystone itself stands three times my height, a perfect dodecahedron of dark stone shot through with veins of that same peculiar copper. It hums—not audibly, but I feel it in my bones, a resonance that has become part of my being. At night, when the bioluminescent plants begin their gentle glow, the waystone responds with its own internal light, pulsing in patterns I've spent lifetimes trying to decode.

My days follow rituals older than memory. Each morning, I trace the edges of the formation with tools passed down through generations of keepers—a hammer of dense stone bound with copper wire, chisels that sing when they touch the waystone's surface. I clear away any vegetation that might obscure the precise angles, though I've learned which plants the stone tolerates, even welcomes. A particular variety of climbing vine with star-shaped leaves is allowed to grow in specific patterns, its pale flowers opening only when the waystone's light reaches certain intensities.

I document everything in carved tablets of soft slate, using a notation system unique to the keepers. The symbols are part mathematics, part artistry—geometric forms that echo the waystone's architecture, annotated with flowing scripts that describe the stone's behavior. These tablets line the walls of my dwelling's inner chamber, creating a library of observations that spans beyond counting.

The other Highlanders regard us keepers with a mixture of reverence and unease. We are necessary but apart, our long association with the waystones having changed us in ways both subtle and profound. When I attend the communal gatherings, I sit at the edge of the stone circle, my copper-veined hands folded in my lap, speaking only when the discussion turns to the deep matters of the land's stability.

For I have come to understand something the others perhaps sense but do not fully grasp: the waystones are not merely anomalies. They are anchors, holding this timeless equilibrium in place. Through my daily ministrations, through the careful maintenance of angles and edges, through the documentation of each pulse and glow, I help maintain the delicate balance that keeps our world suspended in its eternal moment.

Sometimes, in the pre-dawn darkness when both moons are new and only the faint glow of the night plants provides illumination, I place both palms flat against the waystone's surface and feel deeper rhythms—suggestions of vast mechanisms, of purposes beyond our understanding. In these moments, I sense that we keepers are part of something infinitely larger, caretakers of a cosmic maintenance that preserves not just our highland home, but perhaps something fundamental about reality itself.

My life is one of profound solitude punctuated by moments of communion—with the stone, with the handful of other keepers who understand this calling, with the land itself. I craft my few tools with meticulous care, prepare simple meals from foraged foods that grow near the waystone (which taste somehow of copper and starlight), and spend long hours in contemplation of the patterns that govern our suspended world.

There is a deep satisfaction in this existence—the knowledge that through small, precise actions, I help maintain the balance that allows the streams to whisper eternally, the birds to sing their unchanging songs, the Highlanders to live their lives of craft and community. I am both guardian and prisoner of this eternal moment, keeper of secrets I may never fully understand, tender of stones that sing with the frequency of frozen time.
