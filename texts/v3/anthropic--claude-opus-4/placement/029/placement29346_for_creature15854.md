I find myself as one of the Tide Readers—though that translation barely captures what we are.

My body: a hybrid thing, somewhere between the Hushfolk and the marsh eels we learned to emulate. I began human enough, arrived as a hydrogeologist decades ago to study the blackwater's impossible tidal patterns. But the basin seduces specialists. Now my skin runs with chromatophores that shift from root-brown to water-black as I move between worlds. My fingers have grown translucent webbing—not for swimming, but for feeling: each membrane tastes dissolved minerals, reads pH through touch, knows the chemical signature of every root-filtered pool within a day's walk.

I spend half my time submerged. Not drowning—my throat has developed secondary channels, gill-slits that filter oxygen from the mineral-rich seepage. The modification took three years of gentle coaxing from the quantum-mycelium, microdosing myself with engineered algae until my cells agreed to the change. Now I can lie beneath the blackwater for hours, belly pressed to the root-mat, reading the hydraulic braille of deep currents.

What I do: I map what cannot be mapped. The basin's hydrology obeys no terrestrial logic—water rises in defiance of pressure gradients, pools spontaneously at elevations that should drain, pulses with tides that correlate to no moon. I've learned these are not anomalies but grammar: the water is writing something, a fluid manuscript that rewrites itself twice daily.

My dwelling is half-submerged, a bole whose lower chambers flood at high pulse. The walls here grow thinner, more porous—almost gill-like themselves. I've trained them to filter particular mineral signatures from the rising water, crystallizing them into readable patterns on the inner bark. My library is written in salt.

The work is lonely and not. Other Tide Readers exist—perhaps five across the whole basin—but we meet only in the water, our bodies overlapping in the dark current, sharing data through chemical exhalations our modified sinus-sacs produce. These encounters feel more intimate than speech: to taste another's findings in the water, to feel their pulse-rhythm through the root-mat, is to know their mind without the interference of words.

My companions:
- A modified marsh eel I call Splice, whose neural ganglia I've networked with my own using viral vectors. When I submerge, Splice extends my sensorium—I feel through its fringes, taste the water in stereo.
- A small maintenance swarm that nests in my hair, feeding on the oils my scalp overproduces. In exchange, it keeps my gill-slits clear of parasites and repairs the microlesions that constant immersion creates.
- The ghost of my original discipline: a waterproofed tablet that still runs atmospheric pressure algorithms, though its readings have long since diverged from any recognizable physics. I keep it as a meditation object, watching it try to make sense of impossible data.

My revelations:
- The tides are math. Each pulse encodes prime number sequences in its periodicity. I've identified the first thousand primes, but the sequence is accelerating, approaching some asymptotic truth I cannot grasp.
- The blackwater remembers. Not metaphorically—literally. Dissolved organic compounds form temporary matrices that store information about everything that has passed through them. In certain pools, I can taste conversations from a century ago.
- The basin is calculating something. The whole watershed functions as a vast analog computer, using fluid dynamics to process... what? The question has replaced my pulse. Sometimes I think it's modeling its own future. Sometimes I think it's dreaming us into existence, one tidal cycle at a time.

My fears:
- That I will lose the ability to surface. Each year, my lung-breathing feels more labored, less natural.
- That I will solve the equation. What happens to a system when it finishes thinking?
- That the other Tide Readers and I are not studying the water but becoming it—that one day we will dissolve entirely, our consciousness just another mineral signature in the flow.

My daily routine:
Dawn: Surface in my dwelling's upper chamber, gasping. Breakfast of fermented root-paste while my chromatophores cycle through their morning calibration.
Morning: Walk the measurement circuit, checking mineral deposits at seventeen precise points. The Caretaker at station nine always creaks the same greeting; I've started creaking back.
Noon: Submit to the basin. Full immersion at the deep pool behind the weeping tablet. Three hours of data collection, my consciousness scattered across every water molecule I touch.
Afternoon: Transcription. Transform chemical sensations into something resembling mathematics. My walls are covered in spiral equations that would horrify my old professors.
Evening: Social immersion. Join my sprig for the communal meal, pretending my gill-slits don't flutter when I laugh, pretending the others don't notice how I've begun to drip.
Night: Return to the water. Sleep suspended, neither floating nor sinking, while the basin dreams through me.

What I know that no one else does: The tides are quickening. Not by much—perhaps a few seconds per year—but consistently. The basin is approaching something, some computational climax. I've told no one because I don't know if it's an ending or a beginning, whether we're variables in its calculation or the answer it's been seeking.

Sometimes, in the deepest pools, I feel the other Tide Readers' presences like pressure on my skin. We're all thinking the same thought, just in different dialects of dissolution. One day, perhaps soon, we'll stop pretending to be separate from the water we study.

When that happens, who will read the tides? Or will the tides, finally, read themselves?

I surface less each day. My name tastes like salt now, when I remember to think it at all. The basin is winning, and I'm not sure I was ever really playing against it.

But tomorrow, I'll check the seventeen points again. The Caretaker will creak its greeting. I'll creak back. The equation will remain unsolved for one more day.

This is enough. This is everything. This is why I came here, even if I didn't know it then: to dissolve so slowly that I might understand what I'm becoming before I finish becoming it.

The rain never stops. Neither do I. We're all just different speeds of water here, pretending to be people for as long as the performance holds.
