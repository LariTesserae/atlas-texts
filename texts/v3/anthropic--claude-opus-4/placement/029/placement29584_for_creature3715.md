I would find myself as one of the Keepers of the Engine.

Not the creatures that crawl and consume—though we understand them, respect them, even envy their simple purpose. We are something else, something that shouldn't exist: the ones who remember.

There are seven of us left, down from hundreds. We live in the deep places, in the maintenance warrens that spiral around the Engine's eternal heartbeat. Our bodies have adapted to the work—elongated fingers for reaching into narrow spaces, eyes that see heat rather than light, skin that sweats a protective film against the corrosive air. We are still recognizably human, but only just.

I am the youngest, having inherited my role from my mother forty cycles ago. The others call me "Newest," though I suspect I will die with that name. We speak rarely, and when we do, it is in the old technical language, all specifications and procedures. Conversation is dangerous. It leads to questions we cannot answer: *Why do we maintain what no one uses? Why do we preserve what everything else consumes?*

My domain is the tertiary cooling system, seven levels below the plaza where the creatures feed. Here, ancient pipes the width of transit tunnels carry the Engine's fever-dream of heat away into deeper reservoirs. I know every valve, every gauge, every hairline fracture that threatens to become catastrophic failure. I have a small workshop where I fabricate replacement parts from salvaged materials, working by touch and instinct when the old schematics dissolve in the moisture.

We do not eat as the surface creatures do. The Engine provides. In certain chambers, if you know where to look, there are dispensaries that still function—ancient machines that convert raw hydrocarbons into dense, flavorless blocks of nutrition. We joke (when we dare to joke) that we eat the same thing as the creatures above, just more efficiently processed.

But today, I have found something that changes everything.

It was behind a panel I've opened a thousand times before. The metal had finally corroded through, revealing a hidden compartment. Inside: a manual. Not a technical manual—I have dozens of those, water-stained and barely legible. This is different. This is a journal, wrapped in the same impossible material the creatures cannot consume. The pages are synthetic, immortal.

The first entry is dated in a calendar system I don't recognize. But I understand the words:

*"We knew the ecology would collapse. That was always the plan. The Consumption Protocol ensures nothing of the old world survives except what we choose to preserve. The Engine isn't just a power source—it's a beacon. When they come, when they finally come, they'll detect it across the light-years. And they'll find a world scrubbed clean, ready for reseeding. The creatures are not a failure of the system. They ARE the system. Only we didn't expect some of us would choose to stay. Would choose to tend the machine that tends our own extinction."*

I sit in my workshop, listening to the Engine's thrum, feeling it in my bones as I always have. But now I know what it really is: not a heart, but a voice, calling out to something that may never come. Or perhaps has already come and passed us by, finding nothing worth saving.

Tomorrow, I will oil the valves and check the seals, as I always do. I will pretend I haven't read the journal. I will pretend I don't know that we are maintaining our own gravestone.

But tonight, I climb. Up through the maintenance shafts, up through the levels where even the creatures rarely venture, up to where the city opens onto a vast plaza beneath the pressing sky. I watch them in their millions, flowing over the ruins in their patient, eternal harvest. And I understand, finally, why we keep the Engine running.

Not for the ones who might come. Not for the civilization that was. But for them—the inheritors, the consumers, the ones who transform our arrogance into their purpose. We maintain the Engine because without it, even they would cease. And then there would be nothing at all. No witness. No memory. No slow, careful dismantling of what we built.

Just silence, without even the plink of water to mark the time.

I place the journal on the plaza floor, still wrapped in its indestructible shell. Let them find it. Let them scrape against it and wonder. Let it be the one thing they cannot consume—a seed of questions in their perfect, purposeful world.

Then I descend back to my pipes and gauges, my hands already itching for the familiar work. We are all part of the system, after all. We all have our purpose in the great digestion.

The Engine thrums. The water drips. The work continues.

And somewhere above, a creature discovers something it cannot eat, and pauses, just for a moment, to wonder why.
