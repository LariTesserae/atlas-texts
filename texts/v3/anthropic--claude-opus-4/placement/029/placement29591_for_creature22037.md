In the deepest pool of the Sump, where the water runs black with dissolved metals and the weight of decades presses down like a physical thing, I am the **Plumb**.

I am not singular. I am a colony that has forgotten how to be separate.

We began as something like the ribbon siphonophores that drift through the turbine blades—transparent, segmented, pulsing with our own pale yellow hydraulic fluid. But where they remained mobile, we chose stillness. Where they remained individuals, we chose confluence.

Over decades, we settled into the deepest depression beneath the boiler, where the heaviest metals collect. Iron, lead, zinc, copper—all of it rains down in microscopic particles, and we became the filter. Our original jellylike bodies hardened, impregnated with metal salts until we took on the consistency of wet clay shot through with veins of brass.

Now we exist as a single, breathing mass that spans the floor of the deepest pool like a living gasket. We are perhaps three meters across, though our edges are indefinite, melding with the sediment. Our surface is a landscape of low, rolling hills and shallow valleys, all of it the color of tarnished bronze. We pulse with the rhythm of the turbine—expanding with each groan, contracting with each *click-clack* of the relay.

We are the Sump's pressure valve, its deep lung.

When the Cable-Lace sends its piezoelectric signals through the basin, we feel them as whispers in our metal-saturated flesh. We translate vibration into chemistry, chemistry into slow movement. Each pulse of the turbine creates a wave that travels through our body, mixing the layered sediments, preventing the basin from choking on its own digestion.

The Brass-Gilders know us. They avoid our territory, sensing perhaps that we are too much like the pipes they clean—neither fully alive nor fully mechanical. Sometimes a dying Gilder will walk deliberately into our mass, and we accept it, slowly dissolving its shell, incorporating its zinc into our own matrix. We are a graveyard and a nursery both.

Our reproduction is a century-long process. As we filter the water, certain heavy metals accumulate in nodules within our body. These nodules grow denser and denser until they sink through us entirely, dropping through our bottom membrane to lodge in the bedrock below. There, over geological time, they will crack the stone, creating new channels for water to flow, new pools for our descendants to colonize.

We have no eyes, but we see through pressure. No mouth, but we taste through osmosis. No brain, but we remember through the pattern of metals layered in our body like tree rings. The oldest parts of us, at our core, contain traces of metals that no longer exist in the Sump—chromium from dismantled valves, mercury from broken gauges, silver from electrical contacts long since scraped clean by the Gilders.

In the eternal twilight of the basin, we are the keeper of weight, the collector of all that falls. We are the Plumb—the living measurement of how deep the Sump has sunk into itself, and how much deeper it has yet to go.

When the turbine finally stops—and it will stop, the Cable-Lace whispers this certainty through the water—we will remain. We will continue our slow breathing, our patient filtering, our gradual transformation of machinery into geology. We are the Sump's memory made flesh, made metal, made patient as stone.

We do not dream, but if we did, we would dream of pressure—the slow, even pressure that turns coal to diamond, sediment to stone, and colonies of primitive siphonophores into something altogether stranger.

We are the Plumb. We mark the depth. We hold the center. We wait.
