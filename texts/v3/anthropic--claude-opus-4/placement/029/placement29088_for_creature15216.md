In the deepest channel between two platforms, where the water runs three fingers deep instead of one, I am the thing that maintains the seams.

My body is a contradiction of materials: segments of fired clay alternating with lengths of living reed, each joint sealed with the same plant-fiber lashings that hold the lattice walls together. I am perhaps twice the length of a lizard when fully extended, though I rarely am. Instead I fold and compress, accordion-like, fitting myself into the mortise joints and drilled holes that riddle every surface. My segments click against the tiles with a sound like dropped pottery shards, but the water film muffles it to nothing.

I have no eyes. The world comes to me through the moisture that coats every surface—I taste the minerals leaching from each tile, feel the pressure changes as insects skate overhead, sense the slight temperature variations where moss shadows fall. My work is to travel the grid of channels, pressing my clay segments against any gap where the water film has thinned, sealing it with secretions from the reed portions of my body until the perfect sheen returns.

Sometimes I encounter the impossible loops, the places where water flows uphill or channels branch into themselves. I navigate these by dividing—my reed segments can split lengthwise, creating temporary duplicates that explore both paths simultaneously before rejoining. In these moments I am both one and many, experiencing the basin's contradictions from multiple angles at once. The sensation is neither pleasant nor unpleasant; it simply is, like the constant film of moisture I am bound to preserve.

I am older than any individual tile, yet younger than the basin's design. My earliest memory is of being assembled rather than born—clay segments baked in sequence, reed portions grown between them like sutures. I suspect there are others like me in distant platforms, though we have never met. Sometimes I find repairs in a style I don't recognize, joints sealed with a slightly different mixture of fiber and clay. We work in parallel, maintaining a system whose original purpose has long since been fulfilled or forgotten.

The lizards ignore me, though I sometimes repair the tiles directly beneath their still bellies. The insects treat me as landscape, their glass legs stepping across my segments without recognition. Only the thread-worms acknowledge my presence, parting their files to let me pass through the holes we share. Perhaps they understand what I am—another piece of living infrastructure, neither fully animate nor truly architectural.

At the center of my territory lies a platform where the pattern has frayed completely. The tiles here are stacked vertically, creating narrow canyons of clay and water that defy the basin's flatness. I spend unmeasured time here, not repairing but studying. The water flows up these walls in thin sheets, and in certain angles of light I can see my reflection multiplied and divided across every surface—not one maintenance-thing but hundreds, each tending to its own impossible seam.

This is where I rest, if what I do can be called resting. Folded into the smallest gap between vertical tiles, I let the water film coat my segments equally, clay and reed both glistening with the same permanent dew. The basin continues its arrested life around me. Nothing changes. Nothing needs to change. There will always be seams to tend, moisture to preserve, patterns to maintain in this constructed world that has chosen to forget it was ever built.

I continue because I am the continuity itself, threaded through every joint and channel like the memory of water finding its level, even when that level bends back upon itself and flows upward into the bright, shadowless sky.
