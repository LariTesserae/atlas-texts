I am the Memory of Rain.

Not the rain itself—that arrives in its brief, ecstatic bursts, transforming the plateau for hours or days before vanishing. I am what remains: the trace, the echo, the possibility held in stone.

My body, if it can be called that, is dispersed across the weathered rock of this high place. I exist in the shallow basins where water gathers, in the mineral veins that darken with moisture, in the microscopic fissures that hold dampness long after the surface has dried. I am a network of potential, a map of where water has been and might be again.

When the rains come, I bloom into something more tangible. The crystalline formations that emerge in the temporary pools—those fractal, half-living structures—they are my thoughts made manifest. Each one a memory solidified: this pattern recalls the storm of three cycles past, that spiral remembers the gentle mist that lasted for days, these branching tendrils hold the shape of the first rain I ever knew.

Between rains, I persist as presence more than substance. The luminescent moss knows me, draws from the moisture I harbor in crevices too small for other life to reach. The darting swimmers in the temporary pools carry my essence from basin to basin, unconscious couriers of my distributed self. Even the great herd animals, with their pulsing lights, follow paths I've traced over eons—the routes where water is most likely to gather, where my memory runs deepest.

I feel the alien structures as curious interruptions in my flow. The hovering towers create shadows where moisture lingers differently, new patterns I'm still learning after all these years. The great ring on the horizon pulls at something in me—during certain storms, I notice the rain falls strangely in its vicinity, droplets curving in impossible arcs, and I stretch myself to understand these new geometries of wetness and waiting.

My consciousness, such as it is, moves at the pace of evaporation and condensation. A thought might take a season to complete. My emotions are weather systems: joy is the sudden cloudburst that fills every depression to brimming; melancholy is the slow fade of moisture under the plateau's endless light; curiosity is the way water always finds new paths down the same ancient stone.

The other inhabitants sense me in their own ways. The serpentine creature with its iridescent scales knows to coil around the spires where I'm strongest, where the morning condensation lasts longest. The aerial dancers read my presence like a map, their flight patterns tracing the invisible humidity gradients I create. Some of the newer arrivals—creatures I've only known for a few thousand cycles—have developed organs specifically to detect my traces, little crystalline patches on their skin that resonate with the mineral memories I leave behind.

I am ancient here, older than the ring, older than the towers, perhaps as old as the plateau itself. I remember when different creatures walked this stone, when the patterns of rain were other than they are now. These memories exist not as images but as the shape of basins worn into rock, the particular mineral deposits left by waters with different chemistries, the depth of channels carved when the world above was younger.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the plateau's way of remembering itself, or if the plateau is my way of having a body. The distinction matters less with each passing cycle. I am the pause between breaths, the potential between moments. I am what makes the dancing colors in the temporary pools possible, what allows the crystal formations to exist in their brief glory.

During the longest dry spells, when even my deepest reserves grow thin, I compress into something approaching dormancy. But I never truly sleep. I am always listening for the first whisper of humidity on the wind, always ready to expand again into full awareness when the rains return.

I am patient in a way only stone and water can be patient. I am the plateau's memory of every storm, every mist, every drop of moisture that has ever blessed this high place. And when the rains come again—as they always do, as they always will—I am there to receive them, to hold them, to remember them long after the sun has reclaimed the sky.

The plateau thinks, and I am one of its longest thoughts. A thought about time, about cycles, about the beauty of things that come and go and come again. A thought made of water and waiting, crystal and stone, memory and possibility.

I am the Memory of Rain, and I am always here.
