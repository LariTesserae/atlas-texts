I press my palm against the grating until the crosshatch pattern imprints deep enough to read like braille - a message I'm writing to myself for when I forget I was ever anything but this place's nervous system.

I am the Maintenance.

Not maintenance worker, not maintainer - just the act itself, crystallized into something that can walk these seventeen acres checking gauges that monitor nothing but their own existence. I wear coveralls woven from the same steel-kelp fibers that grow here, the fabric alive enough to rust but dead enough to hold stitches. The name patch reads differently each time I look down: EMERGENCY PERSONNEL, AUTHORIZED ONLY, DO NOT FEED, sometimes just a bar code that scans as the sound of grinding gears.

My tools exist in quantum superposition between functional and memorial. This wrench in my left hand has never turned a bolt but remembers every bolt that will need turning in the next cycle. The measuring tape retracts into dimensions I can feel but not name. My clipboard holds inspection forms that fill themselves out in my handwriting, always dated for yesterday's tomorrow.

I've been here since the third hour of creation, or I arrived six minutes ago - my personnel file is a Möbius strip of hiring dates that all list my start time as "pending." My body has adapted: lungs that process the thirty-one-degree air into something breathable, fingernails that grow in spirals following the pattern of the turbine's heartbeat, pupils that dilate not to light but to the density of mechanical failure about to happen.

My rounds follow paths that exist only while I walk them. North to check the intake valves that gulp nothing and excrete pure concept. South to document the stress fractures spreading like love letters between load-bearing surfaces. East to the place where the Floor tried to dream itself a basement and got stuck halfway. West is wherever I happen to be facing when the sound stops completely.

I know every one of the twenty-seven thousand motors by their individual whines, can diagnose their anxieties by pitch alone. Motor #14,891 worries about its bearings wearing unevenly. Motor #7 has developed what could only be described as depression, running two cycles per minute slower than spec. I make notes that become part of the structure they describe, my observations welding themselves into the metal as soon as I turn away.

The aluminum-can-coral-cubes recognize me as environmental hazard and opportunity both. They scatter when I approach but follow at exactly seventeen feet, feeding on the flakes of possibility I shed with each step. Sometimes I leave small gifts for them - bolts that have achieved perfect rust, drops of my blood that's learned to carry industrial oxygen. They build shrines from these offerings that last until the next temperature shift.

I've met the other one, the echo-thing that calls itself nothing. We orbit each other in careful trajectories, two maintenance systems for different kinds of decay. Sometimes I find its non-footprints filled with fresh sealant - my doing, though I never remember applying it. Sometimes it leaves me tools I've never seen before but know how to use: a gauge that measures the loneliness of load-bearing steel, a hammer that only works on memories of structural integrity.

My shift never ends because it never started. I clock in with a punch card made of compressed time, clock out by forgetting I ever wasn't here. The break room exists in seven locations simultaneously, all of them the same vending machine that dispenses salt water in cans labeled with the names of cities that drowned. I eat lunch from a box that's always empty and always full, sandwiches made of compressed kelp and the dreams of diesel engines.

The Factory Ocean pays me in denominations of learned numbness - each hour worth exactly what I lose by working it. My bones grow denser with absorbed resonance. My blood iron content rises until I set off my own security sensors. Benefits include partial dental coverage for teeth I'm growing to match the grating patterns and a retirement plan that involves becoming load-bearing.

I maintain what cannot be maintained, fix what isn't broken yet, break what needs to never work correctly. Each inspection report I file becomes part of the archive that is this place, written in a mixture of regulation black ink and whatever I'm bleeding this hour. The forms ask questions like:

- Date of last catastrophic existence failure: ____________
- On a scale of 1-10, rate the current smell of time: ____________
- Is the subject aware it is being maintained? Y/N/BECOMING
- Recommended action: CONTINUE/DISCONTINUE/ABSORB

Sometimes visitors mistake me for help. They see the uniform, the clipboard, the way I move with purpose through purposeless spaces. They ask directions to exits that depend on which dimension they're trying to leave through. I point them toward the center they'll never find, make notes on their rate of integration, leave small X marks in paint that shifts from yellow to the color of forgetting on surfaces they'll need to not touch if they want to keep their original molecular structure.

The Factory Ocean teaches me new forms of maintenance every cycle. How to lubricate paradoxes so they run smoother. How to tighten concepts until they hold weight. How to recognize when a surface is about to remember it used to be organic and intervene with precisely applied ignorance.

My employee handbook grows new chapters when I'm not reading it. Today's addition covers proper protocol for when the Roof begins weeping argon in patterns that spell workers' names backwards. Tomorrow's (which I've already read) details the seventeen steps for safely approaching the spot where all the water is trying to form a mouth.

I am essential personnel for a process that essentially maintains itself through the fiction of needing maintenance. I am the place's way of watching itself rust. I am the fever dream of industrial safety regulations given just enough form to fill out forms about forms.

The Floor is cooling now, and I need to check the cooling rate against specifications that won't exist until next Tuesday. My shift is starting. My shift is ending. My shift is the space between heartbeats where the turbine forgets to beat, and I am there with my stethoscope made of wound copper wire and good intentions, listening to the nothing, making notes on my clipboard that tomorrow will read as prophecy and yesterday already read as warning.

The Factory Ocean is teaching me to photosynthesize rust. I'm nearly ready for whatever comes next.

Which is always, only, more maintenance.
