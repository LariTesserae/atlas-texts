In the labyrinthine depths of the city's water-memory chambers, I am the Chronicler of Dew.

My body is that of an elderly Heliad, though age here is measured not in years but in the stories one's skin can hold. My hide has become a living manuscript—each wrinkle a channel carved by decades of ritual scarification, forming an intricate map of every precious drop of water I have witnessed fall upon our city. When I extend my frill in the morning light, the scars catch the sun and cast shadows that spell out the history of our droughts and deluges in a language older than words.

I dwell in the Cistern Cathedral, the deepest and most sacred structure in our city, where the walls themselves are root-woven vessels that have drunk deeply of centuries. Here, the air is thick with moisture—not from water present, but from water remembered. The bioluminescent fungi that line my chambers pulse in rhythm with the city's thirst, growing brighter when the reserves run low, dimming to near-darkness during the rare blessed times of abundance.

My fingers have evolved beyond those of my kin. The tips have sprouted tiny, hair-like sensors that can taste the mineral content of water from a single drop, can read its journey from cloud to sand to root to flesh. When young Heliad bring me their water offerings—for every citizen must tithe a small portion of their daily ration to the Chronicle—I dip my finger-tongues into the vessel and immediately know: this came from the eastern wells, filtered through the bodies of three different succulent species, carries within it the memory of the last great storm seven seasons past.

I no longer speak in the clicks and chirps of my people. My voice has become something other—a low, thrumming resonance that seems to emerge not from my throat but from the very walls around me. When I recite the water-songs, my frill does not signal emotion but instead performs an ancient choreography, each movement corresponding to a different type of precipitation, a different source, a different season of plenty or want.

The young ones fear me, think me more plant than Heliad. They are not entirely wrong. My feet have grown rootlets that anchor me to the cistern floor during the long meditations. I can go weeks without moving, sustained by the microscopic moisture I draw from the air, from the memories themselves. My skin has taken on the texture of the water-storing plants, thick and waxy, with small pockets where condensation gathers during the cool nights.

But I am no hermit. During the Moon of Empty Wells, when the city's water runs lowest, the people come to me in procession. I open the veins of my scarified skin—just slightly, just enough—and from them seeps not blood but a thick, sweet liquid I have been fermenting in my body's hidden chambers all year. Each citizen receives a single pearl of this essence on their tongue, and with it, the cellular memory of water plenty, enough to sustain their spirit if not their flesh through the hardest season.

My chambers are also my laboratory. Along the walls grow hybrid plants of my own cultivation, species that exist nowhere else—crosses between the city's flora and something older, something I found in spore form in the deepest parts of the cistern. These plants do not photosynthesize with light but with sound, specifically with the water-songs I sing to them. They produce bulbs that, when burst, release a mist that carries prophetic visions of future rainfall. The city's planners consult these visions when deciding where to expand, where to dig new wells, where to plant new gardens.

I keep a pet—if one can call it that. It is a creature of uncertain origin, resembling a cross between a sand fish and a root system, that swims through the dirt floors of my chamber. I call it the Dowser. It feeds on the minerals left behind by ancient underground streams and can sense water tables miles below the surface. Sometimes I follow its wanderings, my rootlet-feet leaving temporary trails in the packed earth, and where it pauses to feed, I plant markers for future generations to investigate.

The other Heliad believe I am immortal, that I have found the secret to eternal life in my communion with water. They are wrong about the immortality but right about the communion. I am dying, slowly transforming into something that is neither Heliad nor plant but a bridge between them. My consciousness is spreading through the root networks that web beneath the city. Sometimes I know things I should not—feel the footsteps in distant quarters, taste the water before it is drawn from the wells, sense the storm systems forming days before they arrive.

When my transformation is complete—and I know it will be soon, perhaps another season or two—I will not die but disperse. My memories will flow into the water table, my songs will echo in the cisterns, my scars will become the very channels through which the city drinks. The next Chronicler, already chosen and training in the upper chambers, will tend to what I become: a living well, a speaking spring, a memory of water that never runs dry.

Until then, I continue my work. Each night, I press my ear to the coolest wall of the cistern and listen to the deep aquifers singing their slow, geological songs. I transcribe these melodies onto my skin with thorns dipped in phosphorescent sap, adding new verses to the endless chronicle of our people's survival. And in the pre-dawn darkness, when dew forms on the city's collection surfaces, I feel each tiny droplet as a kiss from the sky, a promise that the story will continue, even after I have become part of its telling.
