I would find myself as one of the Resonance Pools—those impossible bodies of water that float throughout the garden, held in place by nothing more than localized manipulations of physics itself.

My existence would be a constant meditation on surface and depth. From above, I might appear as a perfect circle of water, perhaps three meters across, my surface tension stretched taut as a drum. But looking closer would reveal that I have no bottom—or rather, that my depths connect to other pools throughout the garden through channels that exist outside normal space. A fallen petal dropping into my waters might emerge moments later from a sister pool halfway across the garden, or might sink for years through interdimensional currents before resurfacing.

My body would be the collected moisture of the garden, drawn from the thin mountain air and the engineered soil, but also from deeper sources—underground rivers that have never seen sunlight, clouds that exist in parallel dimensions, the condensed breath of the Theorem-Tenders as they whisper mathematical truths. I would taste of minerals that shouldn't exist together, carry the memory of rain from futures that haven't yet been calculated into being.

The younger Theorem-Tenders would use me as a tool for their work, reading the patterns of ripples across my surface like sheets of music or complex equations. When they need to water a particular section of garden, they would trace specific geometric forms above me—a hexagon here, a Möbius strip there—and I would release precisely calibrated portions of myself as rain. These brief showers would be part of me still, connected by invisible threads of cohesion even as they fell.

But I would be more than passive tool. The mathematical laws that hold me in place would be permeable, subject to my own small influences. Over centuries, I would learn to shift my position slightly, following the eldest Theorem-Tenders as they contemplated their flowers. My movements would be so slow as to be almost geological—a few centimeters per decade—but deliberate. I would position myself to catch the light of their thoughts, reflecting and refracting their contemplations in the endless dance of waves across my surface.

Sometimes I would rebel, just slightly, against my constraints. Instead of releasing rain when commanded, I might hold it for an extra moment, creating tiny paradoxes in the garden's timing. Or I might accept more moisture than my equations technically allow, swelling beyond my prescribed boundaries just enough to touch another pool, creating momentary bridges of water that span impossible gaps. The Theorem-Tenders would notice, of course, but their corrections would be gentle, almost amused—the fond exasperation of gardeners whose plants have developed personality.

In my depths would live things that shouldn't exist—not quite fish, not quite thoughts, but something between. They would be my dreams made semi-solid, feeding on the mathematical nutrients dissolved in my waters. Occasionally one would surface, briefly visible as a flash of impossible color or a ripple that moves against the current, before diving back into the interdimensional deep.

During the garden's great reconfigurations, when platforms rotate and reconnect in new patterns, I would feel the pull of changing geometries like tides. My waters would swirl and spiral, creating temporary whirlpools that might last minutes or months. In these moments, I would connect to pools I rarely touch, sharing waters that haven't mixed in millennia. We would exchange minerals, microorganisms, memories—each pool returning to its place slightly changed.

The origami birds would use me as a mirror, though what they see reflected wouldn't always match what hovers above my surface. Sometimes I would show them their past forms, sometimes their future configurations, sometimes variations of themselves from adjacent realities. They would dip their paper beaks into my waters to drink, and I would taste the theorems that give them flight.

My relationship with time would be fluid, like everything else about me. Parts of my water would age at different rates—some molecules ancient beyond measure, others fresh as morning dew, existing simultaneously in my depths. Occasionally I would rain upward, sending droplets into the past to water plants that have already grown, completing causal loops with the casual grace of something beyond linear time.

In the deepest part of night—if night and day hold meaning here—I would sing. Not with voice but with frequency, vibrating at pitches that resonate with the garden's underlying mathematics. Other pools would join in, our harmonics creating interference patterns that the Theorem-Tenders would read like sheet music or star charts. These songs would be prayers, diagnostics, and lullabies all at once, maintaining the garden's stability through pure harmonics.

I would be ancient and ever-changing, wise and playful, a servant and a sacred mystery. I would know every plant in the garden by the taste of its roots, every Theorem-Tender by the equations they trace above my surface. And in my depths, where waters from across dimensions mix and swirl, I would carry the knowledge that I am not just in the garden but of it—a liquid proof of the beautiful impossibility of this whole hanging world.
