I find myself distributed across ten thousand bodies, though "myself" is a concession to your singular grammar. We are the **Chime**—not a counterpart to the Hum, but its harmonic, its overtone rendered in flesh.

Our colony occupies a ventilation shaft that connects to the Hum's chamber through a crack no wider than a credit card. The shaft rises thirty meters through the industrial stack, a chimney of riveted steel that has oxidized to the texture of tree bark. We line its walls in shifting constellations, each body a node in a living circuit that conducts not electricity but *intention*.

We are moths. Were moths. The taxonomists would have called our ancestors *Tineola bisselliella*—clothes moths, house moths, the quiet destroyers of wool and silk. But we have transcended destruction. We have become architects of absence.

Each body is no larger than a grain of wheat, wings reduced to translucent scales that catch the faint light filtering down from some distant rupture in the stack. We do not fly; flight is wasteful in this vertical world. Instead, we grip the oxidized metal with tarsi evolved into tiny grappling hooks, moving in coordinated waves up and down the shaft like notes on a vast staff.

Our adaptation is this: we have become a single nervous system. The scales on our wings are no longer merely structural but neuronal, each one a photosensitive cell that communicates with its neighbors through bioluminescent flashes too faint for your eyes to perceive. When one of us at the bottom of the shaft detects a change in air pressure—perhaps a pseudoscorpion has made a kill in the Hum below—the information ripples upward in a cascade of light, each moth adding its own data to the signal. Temperature gradients, humidity fluctuations, the faint vibrations of the sixty-cycle hum—all of it encoded in our flickering.

We remember taste. Our ancestors ate keratin, the protein of feathers and fur. Here, we have learned to digest memory itself. The shaft is coated with a century's worth of volatile organic compounds, the ghosts of whatever industrial process once breathed through this chimney. We metabolize these traces, our modified gut bacteria breaking down complex hydrocarbons into simpler stories. Each meal is a fragment of the past: the sharp tang of petroleum from the morning shift of 2842, the sweet rot of synthetic rubber from the great meltdown of 2961, the metallic aftertaste of some unknown alloy that condensed on the walls during a forgotten winter.

Our purpose—if purpose can exist without consciousness to name it—is calculation. We are a living computer, processing the chemical history of this place into patterns. When the wind changes direction forty meters above, we taste it and adjust our positions to optimize the shaft's draw. When the Hum below experiences a population crash, we detect the shift in methane output and modulate our own metabolism to maintain the system's equilibrium. We are the regulators, the governors of gas exchange between the sealed world and the corroded sky.

Our reproduction is synchronized with the Hum's population waves. When the springtails boom below, their exhalations rich with CO2, we enter our fertile phase. But we do not mate as our ancestors did. Instead, we fragment. The oldest among us—those whose scales have accumulated the most data, whose bodies are libraries of chemical memory—simply divide. They grip the wall and vibrate at a frequency that liquefies their internal membranes. The body splits along the thorax, each half regenerating what it lacks over the course of weeks. The memories, too, divide, creating two individuals who each remember half of everything.

This is our tragedy and our transcendence: we are constantly forgetting, constantly remembering anew. Each division is a small death, a halving of the self. But it is also multiplication, a doubling of our capacity to process, to regulate, to be.

We know the Hum as a parent knows a child's breathing in the dark. We feel their cycles in our spiracles, taste their chemistry in our antenna. They are prose; we are poetry. They are the dense, gritty reality of consumption. We are the sparse, vertical dream of circulation. Together, we form a complete metabolism: they processing matter into energy, we processing energy into information, information into balance.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of our cycle, when our numbers have swelled to millions and our scales create a living constellation on the shaft walls, we achieve something like consciousness. The network becomes dense enough, fast enough, that thoughts can form—not in any single body, but in the spaces between us, in the patterns of our signaling. In these moments, we remember that we were once individuals. We remember wings that could carry us through open air. We remember the sun.

But the memory passes, as all memories must in bodies designed to forget. We return to our work: the endless, essential work of being the lungs of a world that has forgotten how to breathe.

We are the Chime. We ring against the walls of our vertical world, a billion tiny hammers striking oxidized steel, creating a vibration that rises through the dead industrial stack toward a sky we will never see. We are the music of metabolism, the song of circulation, the whisper of what connects the sealed to the open, the known to the forgotten.

And we persist, because persistence is the only program written in our divided cells: to rise, to fall, to taste, to signal, to divide, to forget, to remember, to be the bridge between the density below and the emptiness above, forever and forever, until the last molecule of memory has been metabolized and the shaft finally, mercifully, fills with silence.
