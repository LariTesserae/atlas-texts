In this place where time moves like water through stone, I am one of the Glasskeepers.

We are neither Rockborn nor Mistweaver, but something between—born from the moment when volcanic glass first meets the persistent moisture, when the sharp edge softens just enough to hold a thought. My body is translucent obsidian shot through with veins of trapped vapor, a living window into the processes that shape this land. 

I spend my centuries tending to the boundaries—those liminal spaces where one thing becomes another. Where the Rockborn's massive forms meet the delicate traceries of Stone Fungi, I am there, ensuring the transition remains gentle. My fingers, when I choose to have them, can become as fine as mist or as solid as basalt, allowing me to work at every scale this landscape demands.

The other Glasskeepers and I emerged from the first great cooling, when the molten heart of this place began its slow journey toward stillness. We carry that memory of transformation within us—the knowledge of what it means to be both liquid and solid, both ancient and newly formed. Our forms shift with the light that suffuses everything here; in the morning's diffuse glow, we are almost invisible, mere suggestions of shape moving through the maze of stone. By evening, when the light angles low, we become prisms, breaking that eternal brightness into subtle spectrums that paint the wet rocks with colors that have no names.

My particular work involves the integration points—those places where the primitive structures meet the natural stone. I spend decades smoothing a single joint, encouraging the materials to remember their kinship. The hand-chiseled marks left by whatever force first shaped these passages are precious to me; I preserve them even as I help them become part of the greater whole. Sometimes I work alongside the Wind Sculptors, my glass form channeling their patient breath into precisely where it needs to go. Other times I follow the Water Bearers, solidifying their trails into permanent channels that will guide moisture for millennia to come.

We Glasskeepers have our own language—a ringing that occurs when our bodies resonate with the deep vibrations the Rockborn send through the stone. It sounds like distant bells, or perhaps like the first crack appearing in ice. Through these tones, we share the memories held in glass: the violence of our birth, the slow peace of our cooling, the endless patience of our work.

I know every type of moisture in this place—the thick morning mist that makes my surface opaque, the thin ribbons of vapor that rise from sun-touched stone, the fat drops that gather and fall from overhangs with metronomic precision. Each has its own way of interacting with my glass body, each teaches me something new about the nature of boundaries and transformation.

In the crevice where I make my home—a narrow vertical slice in the rock face that catches the light just so—I have been cultivating a garden of sorts. Not of plants, for this world knows no such softness, but of crystalline formations that blur the line between mineral and Keeper. They grow at the pace of centuries, each one a meditation on the marriage of water and molten stone. The Stone Fungi have discovered them, and now we exist in delicate symbiosis: they break down the minerals I can no longer hold, and I provide them with surfaces that hold exactly the right amount of moisture.

Sometimes I climb to the highest peaks and look out over this endless expanse of sharp edges slowly becoming smooth. I see my fellow Glasskeepers moving like slow stars through the landscape, each tending to their own boundaries, each adding their voice to the great ringing conversation that echoes through the glass hearts of mountains. I see the Mistweavers dancing their eternal dance, the Rockborn standing sentinel over passages that will outlast even them.

And I understand that I am both witness and participant in this world's most fundamental truth: that everything here is always becoming something else, and that the boundaries between things are not walls but doorways. My glass body, caught forever between states, is the perfect expression of this truth. I am the pause between breaths, the moment of transformation made manifest, the keeper of all the spaces in between.

In a thousand years, I will still be here, my form perhaps slightly more clouded with accumulated moisture, my resonance deepened by countless conversations with stone. And I will still be working at my patient task—helping this constructed world remember that it is also natural, helping the natural world accept what has been built within it, keeping the boundaries soft enough to cross.

This is my purpose, my joy, my endless meditation: to be the living memory of change itself, preserved in volcanic glass and animated by the same forces that shape the stones. I am a Glasskeeper, and this liminal world of sharp edges and patient water is exactly where I belong.
