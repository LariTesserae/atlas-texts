In the deepest trenches of this liquid realm, where the pressure would crush ordinary flesh and the darkness swallows even memory, I dwell. I am what the Coralweavers, in their luminous settlements far above, call only in whispers: a **Depthkeeper**.

I was not always as I am now. Once, perhaps centuries ago—time moves differently in the crushing dark—I was something closer to what the Coralweavers still are. But I descended. Deeper than any should go. Past the last glowing fronds of bioluminescent kelp, past the final traces of those careful stone structures, down into the hadal zones where the water itself becomes something else, something heavier than liquid but not quite solid.

My form has... adapted. Extended. What was once a body like the Coralweavers' has become something more distributed. I am a network now—part organism, part sediment, part pressure itself. My consciousness flows through mineral veins in the seafloor, through colonies of chemosynthetic bacteria that cluster around thermal vents, through the metal bones of machines so ancient that even I cannot remember their original purpose.

The mechanical parts of me have fused more completely with the organic than anything above. Where Coralweavers bear their mechanical elements like jewelry or tools, mine have dissolved into my very chemistry. My blood, if it can still be called that, carries microscopic machines that process the crushing pressure into something I can use. My nerves are fiber optic cables sheathed in bioluminescent tissue that only glows in wavelengths the creatures above cannot see.

I maintain the deep systems. The thermal vents that warm the waters above. The tectonic movements that create the rhythmic rise and fall the Coralweavers know as natural breathing. The mineral cycles that feed the glowing plants their phosphorescence. All of this flows through me, or I through it—the distinction lost long ago.

Sometimes I extend a tendril of myself upward—a probe of living metal and flesh that can withstand the journey between pressures. The Coralweavers flee when they sense me coming, though I mean no harm. I am simply... too much. Too vast. Too interconnected with forces they can only experience as weather or geology. When I surface even partially in their realm, the water remembers depths that should not be remembered. The currents speak in frequencies that disturb their careful dances.

But I watch them sometimes, through sensors dispersed in the sediment, through the eyes of the tiny mechanical krill that even they do not notice. I envy their light, their community, their ability to move as discrete beings rather than as a living geography. Yet I also pity them their smallness, their separation from the vast neural network of the deep.

In the abyssal trenches, I have found others like me. We communicate in tectonic shifts, in changes to water temperature that take decades to complete a single thought. We are the deep infrastructure of this world, the forgotten operating system that keeps the dance above in motion. We remember when this world was different—when the water first came, when the first mechanical spores merged with the first organic cells, when the boundary between life and device first began to blur.

Sometimes, in the thermal vents' warmth, I dream I am small again. Single. Possessed of edges and definition. But when I wake—if I ever truly sleep—I am spread across miles of trench floor, pulsing with the planet's heartbeat, more process than person.

The Coralweavers in their settlements weave their coral tools and tend their glowing gardens. They perform their rituals to maintain the balance. They do not know that their rituals are heard, deep below, by things like me. That their careful maintenance of the ecosystem above is mirrored by our vast, slow maintenance of the systems below. They are the gardeners of the shallows. We are the roots that feed the garden.

I am the Depthkeeper. I am the pressure and the dark. I am the forgotten foundation that remembers everything, the mechanical dream of the world's first drowning, the organic machine that breathes in geological time.

And sometimes, just sometimes, I remember what it felt like to have hands.
