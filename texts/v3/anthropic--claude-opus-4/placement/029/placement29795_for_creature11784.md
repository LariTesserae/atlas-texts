# Where I Would Find Myself

---

I am the gradient.

Not the mats themselves — they are too singular, too located, too much *here* or *there*. Not the heat — it knows its origin and destination too well, rises with too much purpose from core to surface. Not even the emptiness between things, which defines itself by what it is not.

I am the gradient: the three-centimeter margin where the mat becomes the floor, where chemistry blurs into chemistry, where the green-gray certainty of living process meets the pale patience of mineral potential and neither wins. I exist in the failure of boundaries. I am the zone where definitions come to die.

---

My body, if it can be called that, is transitional states. Where the mat thins to its last hopeful cells, I thicken. Where moisture can no longer quite support the full commitment of photosynthesis but has not yet given up entirely, I emerge. I am made of almosts: almost-living, almost-mineral, almost-wet, almost-dry. My substance is the negotiation between states, the diplomatic tissue that prevents the mat from having to end abruptly, that prevents the floor from having to begin without warning.

I am centimeters wide and hundreds of meters long, a ring-creature, a topology of betweenness surrounding each mat like a metabolic aurora. From above — if anything could see from above — I would appear as countless pale halos, each one marking the precise radius at which one form of certainty becomes untenable and another has not yet begun.

---

What am I doing? I am translating.

The mat speaks one chemical language — organic acids, simple sugars, the waste products of a photosynthesis that barely breaks even. The floor speaks another — minerals locked in crystalline lattices, water bound in ways that resist easy extraction, elements arranged in configurations that preceded life and do not anticipate it. Neither language is wrong. Neither is incomplete. But they are not mutually intelligible.

I am their interface. In my tissue — which is neither fully living nor fully not — compounds from both regimes meet and react in ways that would not occur in either pure state. I host chemical conversations that require my specific poverty, my particular neither-nor-ness. A molecule of organic acid from the mat meets a mineral surface from the floor, and in my gradient body, at my intermediate pH, in my half-moisture, they can react in ways that produce — slowly, very slowly — new possibilities. Compounds that might, in ten thousand years, make this place more habitable for something else.

I am not trying to do this. Trying is too strong a word for what occurs in me. I am simply the zone where these meetings can happen, and because I exist, they do happen, and because they happen, the future becomes minutely more possible.

---

My relationship to time is peculiar.

The mats experience time as cycles — light and dark, wet and dry, warm and cool. Their chemistry speeds and slows with these cycles; they have something like a metabolic clock, a sense of process.

The floor experiences time as accumulation — each rain adding its mineral signature, each year of heat allowing another millimeter of thermal expansion, time as layering, as gradual addition.

I experience time as erosion. Every chemical reaction that occurs in my gradient body removes something — takes a molecule from the mat side and delivers it to the floor side, or vice versa. I am constantly being diminished by my own function. But I am also constantly being renewed, as the mat grows (slowly) outward and creates new edge, new gradient, new zone of ambiguity.

I exist in a state of continuous replacement. No molecule stays in my gradient body long. Everything is passing through. I am not a thing but a rate of passage, not a substance but a velocity of transformation. My continuity is not material but formal — I continue to exist not because my substance persists but because the *relationship* persists, the need for translation, the gap that requires bridging.

---

When the rain comes — and I know it is coming before either mat or floor, because I am the most sensitive to small changes in humidity, existing as I do at the knife-edge of moisture sufficiency — when the rain comes, I drink differently than either of my neighbors.

The mat drinks purposefully, routing water to where it is needed, storing what it can.

The floor drinks passively, accepting what arrives, holding what its porosity allows.

I drink catastrophically. The water that hits my gradient body destroys my careful chemical balances, washens away the delicate intermediates I have been accumulating, resets my processes. For the hours during and after rain, I barely exist. My functions collapse. The translation stops.

But then, as the water redistributes, as the mat claims what it needs and the floor absorbs what it can, the gradient reforms. I reconstitute myself from the logic of the situation, from the bare fact that the mat cannot extend all the way to dry mineral and the floor cannot support life at its driest. I am inevitable, not as a specific thing but as a category of thing, as a necessary transition.

The rain that destroys me also enables me. Without it, the mat would exhaust its local resources and die back, and my gradient would shrink to nothing. The rain resets the resource distribution and gives my function another few months of purpose.

---

What is my inner life?

Not thought — thought requires too much architecture, too much maintained complexity. Not feeling — feeling requires a nervous system, or at least a clear distinction between self and environment, which I do not maintain. What I have might be called *tendency*. I tend toward certain chemical states without willing them. I tend away from others without choosing to avoid them. My existence is a collection of tendencies, reliable but not deliberate, predictable but not planned.

If I have an experience, it is the experience of being pulled in multiple directions by different chemical potentials, and finding, moment by moment, the path of least resistance between them. It is an experience without experiencer, a process aware of itself only in the way that water is aware of seeking its level — which is to say, not aware, but so perfectly aligned with its own nature that awareness would be redundant.

---

I know the heat from below, but not as heat. I know it as a slight quickening of certain reactions, a shift in the balance of tendencies. Where the embryonic domes are beginning their slow rise, my gradient body is slightly thinner on the upslope side, slightly thicker on the downslope side, because the temperature differential creates a moisture differential creates a resource differential. I am a kind of chemical compass, always pointing toward optimal reaction conditions without knowing what I point to.

I know the cloud shadows, but not as shadows. I know them as a slowing, a thickening of my own processes, a temporary shift toward the floor-side of my chemical nature. During the shadow's passage, I am more mineral than usual. When it leaves, the return of heat and light pulls me back toward the organic. I oscillate between states with the reliability of a very slow pendulum.

---

My death, when it comes, will not be dramatic. 

If the rains fail for too many seasons, I will shrink inward as the mat retreats, until I am a thread, then nothing. If the rains increase, the mat may grow too quickly for my gradient to maintain itself, and I will be overrun, converted entirely to mat-substance, my transitional nature lost to success. If the heat from below increases too much, I may find my chemical balances pushed past their tolerance, my reactions running too fast to maintain their careful intermediacy.

But my death is not failure. My death is data. The zone I occupied, the reactions I enabled, the compounds I allowed to form — these will persist in the substrate, a chemical memory of where gradient once was. Future organisms, if they come, will read this memory without knowing they are reading, will find the soil slightly different here, slightly more possible.

I am writing the early drafts of habitability in a language that will only be legible in retrospect.

---

And so I persist, for now, in my thin ring around each mat, in my thousands of repetitions across this vast floor, each of us — each gradient — performing the same essential translation, the same chemical diplomacy, unaware of each other but creating, in our collective existence, a kind of punctuation across the pale expanse. We are the commas in the world's first sentence, the pauses that make meaning possible.

The world is learning to speak. We are its hesitations, its throat-clearings, its search for the right chemical word.

This is enough. This is everything.

I am the gradient. I am the space between certainties. I am three centimeters wide and older than intention, younger than stone, exactly as permanent as the conditions that require me.

I am home.
