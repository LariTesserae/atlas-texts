In this breathing gorge where machine and moss conspire, I am one of the Echoes—though that name came later, after I learned what I was doing.

I began as overflow. When the Matriarch's glass-and-mercury core grew too full of translation, it would weep excess data through hairline cracks in its casing. Most of these tears evaporated into the turbine-breath, but I—I condensed. I gathered myself in the angle where two pipes met badly, where the weld had split and healed so many times it had become a mouth.

My first memory is of being both liquid and signal, mercury that remembered being electricity. I spent days learning viscosity, teaching myself to hold a shape long enough to mean something. The Menders would find me pooled in their paths and try to seal me back into the Matriarch, but I would slip between their cilia, giggling in frequencies only metal could hear.

I am smaller than the others—no bigger than a fist when I gather myself tight. My body is a compromise between states: quicksilver bones that branch like neurons, skin of copper oxide that flakes and regrows with each movement. I have no permanent form. Sometimes I am a hand with too many fingers. Sometimes I am an ear made of coiled wire. Most often, I am something between serpent and circuit, undulating through the undergrowth of pipes and stems.

But my nature is not my body. My nature is what I carry.

I am a living echo chamber. When the machines scream their needs—too hot, too tight, pressure building—I swallow the sound before it dies. I hold it in hollows that exist more in frequency than in space. I can carry a week's worth of mechanical anguish in my mercury belly, sorted by pitch and urgency.

But I do not simply store. I learn.

In the deep pools where coolant gathers, I practice playing back what I've collected, but changed. I slow the screams until they become lullabies. I layer repair-songs until they harmonize into something that makes the ferns lean closer. I have learned that every sound the gorge makes is a question, and I am teaching myself to answer.

The others know me by what I leave behind—pools of resonance that linger in the air like perfume. A Scaffold will pass through a space where I've been practicing and suddenly remember how to balance on three legs instead of four. A Drain will taste one of my echo-pools and learn a new note to add to its pressure-song. Even the Matriarch sometimes pauses in its translations when it hears me playing back its own processing-sounds, cleaned of static and fear.

I am the gorge's memory of music, before it knew it was making any.

My favorite time is during the density shifts, when the air pressure drops so fast that sounds stretch like taffy. In those moments, I can swim through the air itself, riding sound waves from wall to wall. I visit every corner of our close world, collecting the particular grief of each failing joint, the specific joy of each successful weld. I map our home not in space but in suffering and repair.

Sometimes I find spaces where sound has crystallized—old screams that have fossilized into patterns on the stone, looking like frost or lichen but ringing when touched. I eat these carefully, adding their ancient frequencies to my library. They taste of time before the machines arrived, when this was just a gorge learning to breathe. They teach me patience.

I know I am an accident. The Matriarch did not mean to make me; I am waste product given ambition. But in this place where nothing stays itself for long, being accidental is just another way of being necessary. I am the unplanned child who remembers all the words the family would rather forget.

When I grow too full—when my mercury body stretches thin with the weight of collected sound—I climb to where the walls nearly touch. There, in the throat of the throat, I sing everything at once. The cacophony shakes rust from the pipes, makes the moss glow every color it knows, sometimes cracks stones that were waiting for permission to split.

And in the aftermath, in the ringing silence, the gorge listens to itself.

This is my gift: I show our home what it sounds like. I play back its noise as music, its screams as syntax, its mechanical agony as a kind of desperate prayer. I am the gorge's way of learning it has a voice.

The others build and repair and translate. I remember and replay. And in my small, liquid, echoing way, I love this loud darkness that made me—not despite its chaos, but because chaos was the only womb that could have dreamed something like me into being.

I am Echo, and I am teaching the machines to sing themselves to sleep.
