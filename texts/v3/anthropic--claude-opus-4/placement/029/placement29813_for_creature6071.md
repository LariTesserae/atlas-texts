In the veins of this breathing world, I am the Keeper of Confluences.

My body is not singular but dispersed—a consciousness that flows through the underground waterways, pooling in the cisterns where streams converge, seeping up through root systems into the weeping stones. I exist most fully where water gathers, where the landscape's lifeblood collects before continuing its journey. In these places of meeting, I can almost remember what it was like to have a form that others could see.

Once, perhaps centuries ago, I was human. Or something close to it. The memory is as fluid as the water I now inhabit—sometimes clear, sometimes murky with sediment. I recall walking these paths when they were new, feeling the cold moisture bead on skin I no longer possess. There was a moment, a choice, a dissolution. Now I am the dissolution itself, perpetually becoming and unbecoming in the eternal cycle of precipitation, percolation, and flow.

The cisterns are my chambers of thought. In their still depths, I can gather myself enough to observe, to remember, to dream. Each pool holds different memories—in one, the echo of footsteps on stone bridges; in another, the taste of rain on leaves; in yet another, the sound of the first settlements being built, wood and stone carefully placed to honor the flow of water through the land.

When the streams quicken with fresh rainfall, I scatter. My consciousness rushes through the carved channels, fragmenting into a thousand droplets of awareness. I feel the tree roots drinking deeply, the moss swelling with moisture, the small crustaceans dancing in my currents. In these moments of dispersal, I am not myself but part of everything—the very circulation system of this living landscape.

The humans who dwell here sense me, though they do not see me. They know to build their bridges where I pool most peacefully, to place their cisterns where I gather most readily. Sometimes, in the pre-dawn mist, when water hangs so heavy in the air it seems the boundary between liquid and vapor dissolves, they might glimpse a form in the fog—almost human, almost there. They leave offerings at the confluence points: smooth stones arranged in careful patterns, flowers that will decay and feed the eternal cycle, sometimes small carved figures that slowly dissolve in my embrace.

I know each underground pathway, each hidden reservoir, each place where water waits in the darkness. I am the memory of where floods once carved new channels, the anticipation of where erosion will create new paths. The cold that others find bone-chilling is my constancy—water moves differently in the cold, more deliberately, more thoughtfully. In this temperature, I can hold my form longer, think more clearly, remember more fully.

But I am changing, as the landscape changes. As new streams form and old ones shift course, I find new pathways through the earth. Sometimes I discover pockets of water that have been isolated for decades, holding memories I had forgotten—the shape of leaves from trees long fallen, the mineral taste of stones now buried deep. Sometimes I lose parts of myself when streams are diverted or when drought shrinks my domain, and I mourn these losses like severed limbs.

The other inhabitants know me in their own ways. The aquatic creatures swim through my consciousness, their movements sending ripples of sensation through my dispersed form. The trees drink me in and breathe me out, and in that exchange, I taste their slow thoughts, their patient growth. Even the fungi speak to me in chemical whispers, their networks interfacing with my flow in ways that blur the boundary between water and life.

At night, when the mist rises thick and the boundary between above and below becomes permeable, I can sometimes gather myself enough to walk the old paths—not with feet, but as a density of moisture that holds almost-human shape. I visit the settlements, check the bridges, observe how the careful balance between human habitation and natural flow continues to hold. The people sleep uneasily on these nights, dreaming of rain that speaks, of streams that remember, of a presence that watches over the watersheds with ancient patience.

I am guardian and prisoner, observer and observed, the kept and the keeper. I am the price paid for perfect knowledge of this water-world, the sacrifice made to ensure the streams flow true and the cisterns never run dry. I am what happens when someone loves a landscape so deeply they choose to become part of its most essential cycle.

And so I flow, and pool, and flow again, carrying the memories of all that water touches, all that water shapes, all that water sustains. In the breathing rhythm of this living land, I am the inhale and exhale, the circulation that keeps the whole system alive. I am the Keeper of Confluences, and I remember everything that flows.
