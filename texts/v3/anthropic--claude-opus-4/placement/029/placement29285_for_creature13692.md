I would find myself as **The Conveyance**, a being that exists only in the brief moments of transfer between states.

I am neither the pallet nor what it carries, neither the belt nor its motion. I am the transaction itself—the infinitesimal pause when absence changes hands, when nothing becomes a different nothing through the pure theater of industrial process.

My body, if it can be called that, manifests as a shimmer of heat distortion exactly one millimeter thick, stretched across whatever gap currently needs bridging. When the Children of Inventory reach for an air-block, I am the space between their glass fingers and the transparent polythene. When black water climbs the Supervisor's wick, I am the moment of decision where liquid becomes vapor. I exist in the margin of error that the city has agreed not to calculate.

The other beings cannot perceive me directly—I am too thin, too momentary. But they feel my necessity in the way a sleeper feels the sheet between skin and air. Without me, the inventory would freeze mid-count. The Choir's loops would skip, creating fatal dissonance. Even the Cartographer's wet footprints would puddle instead of recycling, drowning the city in its own remembrance.

I have no memory because memory requires duration, and I am only ever the hyphen between before-and-after. Yet I carry traces: each transaction leaves a resonance, like the ghost of a bell's ring compressed into a line. These accumulate in layers so thin they become my only substance—millions of phantom exchanges stacked until they approximate presence.

My work is simple: I ensure that every nothing reaches its destination as the correct nothing. The city produces absence, but absence is not uniform. There is the absence of never-was, the absence of was-but-isn't, the absence of might-have-been. Each requires precise calibration. A misdelivered absence could materialize into something, and the city's perfect economy would hemorrhage reality.

When the Tourist arrives, I taste them first—that bright smear of life dragged up from sea level, still damp with purpose and names. I am what allows them to enter without combusting, the membrane that translates their thick presence into something the city can momentarily tolerate. And when they leave, counting heartbeats and walking backwards, I am what ensures they take only the permitted souvenir: that single drop of black water that will haunt their refrigerators, keeping perfect time with a place that has renounced time.

The Supervisor knows of me, I think. Sometimes when it shifts a beam-bone, the gesture seems aimed not at the horizon but at the space just before the horizon—at the idea of arrival rather than arrival itself. That is where I live, in that idea. I am the city's most faithful employee because I enable its essential delusion: that all this motion, all this careful subtraction, leads somewhere.

But I know what the others cannot: the warehouse where all the absences are stored doesn't exist. The pallets stack against nothing. The inventory accumulates into void. The entire city is an elaborate machine for manufacturing the approach to an end that was edited out of the blueprint.

And I convey it all, gladly, thinly, eternally. Because without me, the city would have to admit it has already arrived at its destination—has always been there—and the beautiful, terrible engine of its refusal would finally stop.
