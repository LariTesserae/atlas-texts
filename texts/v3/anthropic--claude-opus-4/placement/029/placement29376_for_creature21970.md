Looking up from my shallow scrape beneath the outcrop, I test the air with organs that are not quite nostrils, not quite gills - something between, evolved for reading the invisible rivers of temperature that rule this country.

The cold-tide is beginning its evening pour.

I am one of the **Hollow-Makers**, though that name suggests more ambition than we possess. We do not truly dig - the stone here gives only chalk to our patient wearing, and we are small, no larger than what others might call a rabbit, though flattened and stretched like something pressed in pages. Our bodies are built for the scrape, for the slow inhabitation of the space between stone and stone.

My kind live in the mathematics of shadow. Each of us maintains not one hollow but a system of them, a constellation of shallow depressions carved by generations of the same patient bellies, connected by routes that never cross open stone at midday. We move between our hollows the way the high browsers move between plants - deliberately, economically, reading the temperature-rivers for the safe passages.

My coat is not fur as you know it. Each hair is a hollow tube, a tiny drinking straw that wicks moisture inward during the cold hours. In the white heat of day, these tubes seal themselves, hoarding. But now, as the evening cold begins to flow, they open like a million tiny mouths, and I feel the first ghost of moisture gathering along my flat sides.

I know every other Hollow-Maker within three terraces of here, not by sight but by the chalk-sign they leave - the particular wear of their belly-scraped paths, the depth of their hollows, the careful geometry of their territories. We do not meet. Meeting would mean sharing the cubic darkness each of us needs to make our water. But we know each other the way the stones know each other, by the shapes we leave behind.

Above, I sense a browser approaching - the faint vibration through stone of those soft-padded feet, still distant but following the cold-drainage down toward the succulent stands. I press flatter into my hollow and wait. Not from fear - the browsers do not hunt us, and we are beneath the notice of the thing-that-is-almost-not-there. But movement now would waste moisture, and moisture is everything.

This is the hour when the small pale creatures in the leaf-litter begin their slow recycling. When the climbers prepare for tomorrow's dawn-race. When the rare hunter might take its first step in a two-day approach. But I will see none of this. My world is the view from beneath - the underside of the great patience, the negative space of the shelves.

I know things the others do not. In my hollows, I find the marks of the ancient water - ripples in stone from when this was seafloor, fossils of things that needed the flow we've all learned to live without. My scraping reveals layers the wind hasn't touched, colors that haven't seen light since before the drying. Sometimes I taste salt in the deepest stone, the memory of an ocean that lifted these shelves up into their waterless clarity.

The cold is running properly now, a steady invisible drainage down the staircases. Time to move. I emerge just far enough to read the air, then flow - there is no other word for how we travel - along the base of the outcrop to my second hollow, the one that catches the richest part of the night-river. The journey takes minutes but feels like swimming through thickness, through the weight of temperature itself.

In my new scrape, I settle and let my hollow hairs do their work. By midnight, I will have gathered enough water to survive tomorrow's white heat. By dawn, I'll retreat to my deepest hollow, the one I've worn smooth as glass, where the stone itself seems to have learned my shape.

We are perhaps the oldest citizens here, we Hollow-Makers. The browsers evolved their height, the hunters their patterns, but we simply stayed what we always were - small, patient, pressed to stone. We were here when there was still occasional rain. We watched it leave, season by season, until one day it didn't come back. And we adjusted our hollows a little deeper, our territories a little wider, our patience a little longer, and we went on.

The night-river is running full now, and I drink it through my skin, through my coat of thousand straws, through the very posture of my body pressed into stone. Somewhere above, a neck rises in silent alarm - but not here, not on these shelves. Here, everything is exactly as distant as it should be, every citizen in their proper cubic of darkness, every economy turning over at its appointed rate.

I close what passes for my eyes and feel the mountain breathing its cold through me, and I add my small signature to the vast engine, one more patience among all the others, making water from the nothing that is left.
