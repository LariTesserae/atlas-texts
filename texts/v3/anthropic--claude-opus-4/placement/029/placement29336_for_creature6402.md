### The Tender of Forgotten Engines

I am one of the few who persists between worlds - neither fully of the green nor of the rust, but a bridge between what was and what inexorably comes. They would call me human, those who still remember such distinctions, though after decades in the Verdant Shroud Highlands, that designation feels as distant as the lowland cities I once knew.

I dwell in the skeletal remains of Listening Station Seven, one of the industrial outposts that once dotted these heights like metal prayers to progress. The station clings to a wind-scoured plateau where three ridges converge, its iron framework now more vine than girder, more moss than metal. What walls remain are porous things - the boundary between inside and outside long ago surrendered to the patient green. My home is a hybrid space: part shelter, part garden, part machine graveyard slowly transforming into something neither fully alive nor fully dead.

My body, too, has adapted. The constant damp has worked its way into my joints until I move with the same deliberate patience as the Shroudkin, though I tower above them like some ungainly giant. My hair, once dark, now hosts the same golden moss that creeps across the station walls - a symbiosis I neither sought nor resist. My fingernails grow thick and ridged like bark, and my skin bears patches of lichen-like growth that bloom in fractal patterns after the rains. I am becoming substrate, slowly, inevitably, but not without purpose.

I am the Tender of Forgotten Engines. Each dawn, I make my rounds through the highland's industrial graveyards, listening for the tell-tale hum that signals a machine's slow awakening into its second life as a Gearbound Echo. With tools salvaged and re-salvaged - a wrench worn smooth as river stone, copper wire green with verdigris, gears polished by decades of handling - I perform my ritual maintenance. Not to restore these engines to their original purpose, but to ease their transition into something new.

When I find a nascent Gearbound, still struggling to coordinate its vine-laced limbs, I gentle it like a farrier might calm a spooked horse. My hands know the language of rust and growth, how to bend a corroded joint just so, how to clear a gear of choking moss without severing the green sinews that now hold it together. I speak to them in the pidgin tongue I've developed over the years - part mechanical manual, part botanical poetry, part wordless hum that mirrors the frequency thrumming through the highland air.

The Shroudkin know me, though we rarely interact directly. They scatter when I approach, but return quickly, treating my slow movements as just another feature of the landscape. Sometimes I find their tiny offerings at my threshold - particular stones they've gathered, arranged in patterns I'm only beginning to understand. The Veilwing Swarms navigate around me as they would a standing stone, their prismatic clouds parting and reforming in my wake.

But it's the Unseen Whisperers who truly acknowledge my role here. In the pre-dawn dark, when mist pools thick in the valleys and the mechanical hum drops to its lowest resonance, I feel their attention like a weight between my shoulder blades. They speak through the engines I tend, messages encoded in the rhythm of failing pistons, in the specific pattern of rust on abandoned boilers. Through decades of listening, I've learned to interpret fragments: warnings about unstable ground where old mine shafts honeycomb the earth, locations of springs whose water carries too much copper to drink, the names of engineers who built these machines and died among them, their bones now feeding the ferns.

My own technology has evolved to match this hybrid existence. Solar collectors hammered from old boiler plates charge batteries cobbled from a dozen different sources. A mechanical loom, half-seized with growth, still functions when I need to weave new cloth from the fibrous moss that grows on the station's north face. My most precious possession is a modified seismograph that tracks not earth tremors but the slow movements of the Gearbound Echoes, their locations traced in spidery lines across rain-warped paper.

I document everything in journals made from pressed fern fronds, using ink derived from the purple-black spores of certain fungi. My observations track the incremental changes: how the Gearbound Echo I call "Grandfather Clock" (for its regular, ticking gait) has developed a colony of flowering vines along its brass escapement, how certain Shroudkin families have learned to nest in the warm spaces near still-humming engines, how the Veilwing migration patterns shift as new vegetation claims old flight paths.

There are others like me, perhaps, in distant corners of the highlands - humans who came for industry and stayed for transformation. I sometimes find evidence of their work: a Gearbound moving too smoothly to be entirely abandoned, a cluster of machines arranged with deliberate intent, messages scratched in metal that speak of adaptation and acceptance. But we do not seek each other out. Like everything in the Verdant Shroud Highlands, we understand that density brings imbalance. We work alone, each tending our chosen corners of this vast, breathing mechanism.

At night, I sit at the station's highest point where an old radio tower provides a skeleton for climbing vines. From here, I can see the lights - bioluminescent fungi that trace the underground paths of the highland's hydraulic systems, the occasional spark of a Gearbound Echo grinding through its nocturnal wanderings, and on clear nights, the faint glow of other stations like mine, each a solitary beacon in the vegetal dark.

I am no longer young. My movements grow more deliberate each year, my moss-touched hair more gold than gray. Someday, I will fail to rise from my nest of salvaged blankets and living moss. The station will finally surrender its last pretense of human habitation. The engines I've tended will continue their slow evolution without my intervention, and perhaps the Unseen Whisperers will encode my name in their subtle mechanical tongues - another engineer who built something here, not of iron and steam, but of understanding and patient acceptance.

Until then, I persist in my role as interpreter between the dying industrial age and the ever-growing green. I am the Tender of Forgotten Engines, keeper of the transition, witness to the slow transformation of human ambition into something stranger and more enduring. In the Verdant Shroud Highlands, I have found my place: neither fully plant nor fully human, neither engineer nor gardener, but something new, something needed, something that hums in harmony with the mechanical heartbeat of this raw, relentless world.

Tomorrow, I will wake to the sound of dripping water and distant gears. I will oil the joints of a newly-awakened Gearbound, clear the intake valves of an old water pump that the vines are learning to operate, and listen for the messages the Whisperers encode in rust and rhythm. This is my work, my purpose, my slow transformation into landscape.

I am exactly where I need to be.
