**The Lichen-Scribe**

I am the one who reads the slow script of decay, who traces meaning in the marriage of fungus and algae, who finds prophecy in the patient colonization of stone. My body is a living map of symbiosis—patches of lichen grow across my shoulders and down my spine, not as affliction but as augmentation. These living symbols shift with the seasons, forming new patterns that only I can interpret, though their full meaning often eludes even me.

My dwelling is neither with the Mossfolk nor apart from them. I inhabit a series of connected chambers carved into the largest boulder at the hollow's heart—not by my hands, but by water's millennial persistence. The stone remembers every drop that shaped it, and I have learned to read these memories like braille. My chambers are lined with fragments I've collected: pieces of bark where lichen has formed particularly significant patterns, stones whose surfaces tell stories in their colonization, pressed specimens arranged by meaning rather than taxonomy.

I move through the hollow like punctuation through a sentence—pausing, marking, defining. The Mossfolk know me by my gait, which mirrors the growth pattern of crustose lichen: spreading outward from each footfall in invisible ripples. They bring me their newborns to read the initial lichen-marks that appear on their skin in the first days of life, faint prescriptions of character that fade as the child grows. They bring me stones from the creek when the patterns seem too deliberate, too laden with intent.

My tools are few but specific: a lens ground from creek-crystal that reveals the minute architectures of symbiosis, brushes made from my own shed hair (which grows in spirals like the lichens' reproductive bodies), and a series of ceramic plates on which I cultivate specimens whose patterns I need to study across seasons. I document nothing in the traditional sense—instead, I maintain a living library on the chamber walls, encouraging specific lichens to grow in specific patterns, creating a slowly evolving text that writes itself.

The hollow speaks to me in colonization rates, in the subtle chemistry of substrate preference, in the ways certain lichens avoid or seek each other's company. I've learned that the unusual formations—the too-perfect arches, the stones' ancient carvings—all bear traces of a lichen that grows nowhere else, a species that seems to exist between states, neither fully alive nor merely mineral. It appears only where the hollow's strangeness is strongest, and its growth pattern suggests intention that predates any human presence.

I age differently than the Mossfolk, my lifecycle bound to the lichens I host. Parts of me grow at geological pace while others cycle through death and rebirth with the seasons. My left eye has been colonized by a lichen that lets me see in spectrums of decomposition—the bright flare of fresh decay, the slow pulse of ancient rot, the steady glow of stable symbiosis. My fingers end in patches of soredia, the powdery reproductive structures that allow me to seed new meanings onto blank stone.

When the hollow shifts—and it does shift, in ways too slow for most to perceive—I am among the first to know. The lichens telegraph changes through their growth, their chemistry, their sudden reluctances or enthusiasms. I've learned to taste the air with patches on my palms, to feel the hollow's breathing through the expansion and contraction of the colonies on my chest.

The Mossfolk consider me necessary but liminal, like the twilight they inhabit. I am invited to their quiet gatherings but always seated at the threshold. They know I read more than lichen—that I see the patterns of their community in the same slow script, that I understand their futures through the lens of symbiosis and patient growth. They bring me offerings of new substrate: interesting stones, fragments of bone, pieces of wood with unusual grain. In return, I give them readings that are less prophecy than ecology—insights into the relationships that bind them to each other and to the hollow.

My greatest discovery, still unshared, is that the hollow's strangeness is itself a form of lichen—a symbiosis between place and possibility, between the physical and something far older. The twisted trees, the carved stones, the wrong reflections: all fruiting bodies of this vast, slow organism that exists partially outside our understanding of life. We are all part of its chemistry, the Mossfolk and I, nutrients in its patient growth toward something I can only glimpse in the most complex patterns.

I will die as lichens die—not all at once, but in patches, parts of me persisting even as others fade. The colonies I host will find new substrate, carrying forward the patterns I've spent my life reading. Perhaps someone will learn to read me then, tracing meaning in the colonization of my bones, finding in my slow decay the next chapter of the hollow's endless text.
