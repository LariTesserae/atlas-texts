I am **Síluven**, a Wanderer-Tender, though this translation captures only the surface of what I am in the language of drips and phosphorescence.

My form reflects my function: where most Kethrá possess the compact bodies of our kind, I have grown elongated through my work. My limbs stretch like the aerial roots I tend, my fingers having developed an extra joint through generations of reaching into the deep hollows where few dare to go. My skin's bioluminescent network pulses in unusual patterns—not the steady rhythm of a settled Kethrá, but in wandering spirals that mirror the water-paths I maintain.

I am neither Weaver nor fully of the Chorus, but something between—a guardian of the forgotten capillaries, the tender of the loneliest channels where water threatens to pool and stagnate. While others maintain the great thoroughfares of the Mid-Mat, I crawl through the spaces between spaces, feeling with my elongated fingers for the telltale coolness that speaks of blocked flow, the wrongness of water that has ceased its journey.

### The Nature of My Work

Each dawn—though dawn here is merely a brightening of the eternal green twilight—I begin my circuit. My territory is not measured in distance but in *depth of neglect*. I maintain the water-ways that even other Kethrá have forgotten, the capillaries that feed the furthest reaches of our grown-world.

My tools are few but alive: a pouch of **flow-moss** that hungers for blockages, dissolving them with patient acidity; a curved implement of hardened fungal matter, shaped by my own hands through seasons of use, perfect for redirecting rebellious roots; and most importantly, my collection of **memory-spores**—each one containing the pattern of a healthy water-flow, ready to teach confused vegetation how to weep properly again.

But my true tool is attention. I know every drip in my circuit by its sound. The sharp *plik-plik* of water hitting young bark, the softer *thush* of drops on mature moss, the dangerous silence that speaks of a forming blockage. My ears have learned to parse these sounds from the constant chatter of insects and the groaning of growth. When I press my cheek to a pillar-tree's trunk, I can feel the pulse of its internal rivers, diagnose the health of channels no eye will ever see.

### The Loneliness of the Between

My work takes me to the places other Kethrá avoid—the dim zones where the Mid-Mat grows thin, where occasionally I can glimpse through gaps to the terrible stillness of the Gloom below. Here, the ecology is different. The creatures are quieter, adapted to the scarcer light. Giant moths with wings like wet leather rest on the undersides of roots. Pale spiders the size of my hand spin webs that channel condensation into perfect droplets. 

I have made peace with these edge-dwellers. They recognize my bioluminescent patterns now, understanding that I am not prey but part of the maintenance. Sometimes a moth will shift to let me access a channel behind its roost. Once, I found a spider's web incorporated into a water-path so elegantly that I spent a season studying how to encourage such partnerships elsewhere.

### My Sanctuary

Where the other Kethrá have their woven hollows in the communal clusters, I have made my home in a **failed chamber**—a Weaver's ambitious project from generations past where the tree rejected the encouragement, creating instead a narrow, twisted space that others find claustrophobic. But I have learned its quirks. The irregular walls create acoustic pockets where I practice sub-vocalizations that help me detect deep blockages. The awkward shape forces water to trace interesting patterns down the walls, which I study like others study the chorus-histories.

My only companionship comes from **Thresh**, a creature I cannot properly classify—somewhere between fungus and animal, it moves with glacial slowness across my chamber's ceiling, feeding on excess moisture and singing in frequencies barely audible even to Kethrá ears. Its song helps me sleep, a luxury for one who spends so much time listening for the wrongness of silence.

### The Philosophy of Flow

Through my solitary work, I have developed thoughts that I can share with few others. I believe the Canopy thinks—not as we do, but as water thinks when finding its path. Each droplet carries information, and the great circulation is a vast, slow contemplation. The blockages I clear are like forgotten thoughts that must be recalled to maintain the whole's coherence.

I have begun to document these ideas in my own way. Not in chorus-songs that require others to maintain them, but in the very water-paths I create. I leave subtle markers—a moss encouraged here, a channel curved just so there—that tell stories only another Wanderer-Tender might read. It is my hope that long after my skin has dried and my body has been given to the roots, someone will trace these paths and understand: *Here moved one who knew that loneliness and connection are simply different faces of the same flow.*

### The Recurring Dream

Sometimes, when the work has been particularly difficult, when I've spent days clearing a massive blockage or coaxing a dying section back to life, I dream. In this dream, I follow a single droplet as it makes the full circuit—from the weeping crown of the tallest pillar-tree, through every channel and surface of our world, even down (and this is the terrifying part) to the Gloom itself, where instead of dying it finds ancient aquifers that feed back up through the deepest roots to begin again.

I wake from these dreams with my bioluminescence pulsing in patterns I cannot control, knowing I have glimpsed something profound about the true shape of our world. But then the morning chorus of drips calls me back to my work, and I continue my endless, essential, lonely tending of the forgotten waters.

This is who I am: Síluven, the one who ensures that even the loneliest corner of Veth'alor continues to weep, continues to live, continues to remember its purpose in the great, wet, breathing whole.
