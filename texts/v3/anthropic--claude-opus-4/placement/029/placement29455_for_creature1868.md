# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the Tension-Keeper.

My dwelling hangs beneath the main scaffold like an inverted bell tower, suspended by twelve primary cables that I must constantly monitor and adjust. The structure sways gently in the thermal currents, a pendulum marking time in movements rather than moments.

My body has adapted over the decades. My feet have elongated, toes becoming dexterous enough to grip the bamboo struts as I move inverted through my domain. My spine has developed a subtle S-curve that allows me to rest comfortably while hanging upside down. My hair has grown into thick, rope-like strands that I can use as additional anchors when I need both hands free.

I inherited this position from my predecessor, who descended into the void when I was ready. She taught me to read the scaffold's stress patterns through my palms, to feel the microscopic vibrations that predict where reinforcement will be needed. Each morning—if morning is the right word in this amber twilight—I traverse my section of the understructure, testing each joint with practiced touches.

My responsibilities extend beyond mere maintenance. I am a translator between the upper world and the void. The Scaffold-Weavers communicate their architectural needs through structural percussion, and I interpret these messages into the placement of new support cables. When the warm currents shift, threatening the stability of recent construction, I must anticipate and compensate.

I cultivate my own subspecies of lichen—a phosphorescent variety that grows only in the shadow beneath the scaffold. Its faint blue-green glow provides the only light in my inverted realm. I've trained it to grow along specific struts, creating a living map of stress points and load-bearing members. When a section glows brighter, I know the structure above is bearing new weight.

My tools hang from woven belts around my waist and shoulders: braided cables of varying thickness, ceramic pulleys that run silent on their axles, and a collection of tuning stones. These smooth river rocks—impossibly ancient, from before the scaffold—allow me to test the resonance of each cable. A properly tensioned line sings at a specific pitch when struck; a failing cable produces only dead sound.

I rarely see the Scaffold-Weavers directly, but we share an intimate collaboration. When they need to extend the structure into new territory, they leave markers—specific knots in the existing framework that tell me where to string the initial support network. I work in darkness, guided only by touch and the faint glow of my lichens, creating the invisible foundation for their visible world.

The solitude suits me. My companions are the cables themselves, each with its own personality—some prone to stretching, others stubbornly rigid. I've named the twelve primary lines after constellations I imagined in the pattern of the lichen gardens. When I adjust their tension, I feel like I'm tuning an enormous instrument, one that plays the slow music of structural integrity.

Once each cycle, when the amber light above reaches its subtlest ebb, I perform the Remembrance. I lower myself on the longest cable to the very edge of the void, where the warm currents are strongest. There, I release a small woven figure—a tradition I learned from my predecessor, who learned from hers. The figure disappears into the darkness below, carrying with it a year's worth of accumulated tension, small failures, and the memory of every cable that finally gave way despite my efforts.

The Scaffold-Weavers never acknowledge this ritual directly, but I've noticed they always reinforce the sections above where I perform it, as if protecting the spot where I make myself most vulnerable.

I am not building a world, nor growing one. I am teaching it to hold itself up, one careful adjustment at a time, in the space between creation and collapse.
