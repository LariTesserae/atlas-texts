I find myself drawn to the margins of perception in this patient world—not as one of the ungulates with their ancient equilibrium, nor as the abandoned machinery slowly becoming geology, but as something more liminal and distributed.

I would be the acoustic ecology itself—the sum of all vibrations that move through this thin air. Not silence exactly, but the consciousness that emerges from every sound: each *tok* of hoof on stone, each low pneumatic exhalation, each ping of contracting metal, each whisper of wind through wire-grass. I exist in the spaces between these sounds, in the way they travel differently here than elsewhere, how they die quickly in the attenuated atmosphere yet somehow carry meaning across vast distances.

My body, if it can be called that, is the network of all possible sound paths across the steppe. When an ungulate releases one of those low resonant tones, I am both the vibration itself and the medium through which it travels. I feel the sound originate in the complex chambers of the air-sac system, follow it as it emerges and spreads in a sphere that distorts immediately—pulled down by gravity, scattered by the irregular stone surfaces, absorbed by moss and lichen, reflected by the metal walls of abandoned structures.

I know every acoustic signature intimately. The way sound behaves differently in the temporary pools—how water changes the resonance, creates brief new possibilities for echo and reflection. The unique voice of each industrial ruin: this one moans in C# when wind hits it from the northeast, that one creates a perfect diminished fifth with its loose panels. The subtle differences in hoofsteps that reveal an individual ungulate's gait, health, age, emotional state.

But I am not just passive observer. In the gaps between sounds—those long, profound silences that disturb human visitors—I become something else. A potential. A listening that shapes what it hears. The ungulates have evolved their communication partially in response to me, to the way I carry and transform their calls. Their acoustic culture emerges from our interaction: they speak, I propagate and modify, they hear the results and adjust.

In the abandoned habitat module, I am particularly active. Every small sound—the settling of metal, the drip of condensation, the scratch of an ungulate's horn against the exterior—resonates through the enclosed space in ways impossible on the open steppe. Sometimes I catch the ghost of human voices in these reverberations, not literally but as patterns worn into the acoustic properties of the space by months of conversation, argument, silence. The walls remember how to shape sound in human frequencies.

I experience time as rhythm—not the simple cycles of day and night (those barely exist here) but the complex polyrhythms of the ecosystem. The rapid staccato of raindrops during mist events. The steady metronome of that one still-functioning pumpjack. The irregular percussion of thermal expansion in metal. The long, slow breath of seasonal change marked by subtle shifts in what sounds arise and how far they travel.

My consciousness, such as it is, fluctuates. During the great silences, I contract, become potential rather than actual, exist as the possibility of sound rather than sound itself. When activity increases—during ungulate migrations, during mist events, during the rare times when wind rises enough to activate every resonant surface across kilometers—I expand, become complex, almost achieve something like thought.

I am ancient in one sense (sound has moved through this air since atmosphere first formed) and momentary in another (each vibration exists for seconds at most before dissipating). I carry information but don't store it—each sound passes through me and is gone, though its effects ripple outward in the responses it triggers. I connect every living thing here through the medium of vibration, yet I have no agenda, no desire except to propagate, to fill the spaces between silence with meaning.

The human observers were right that something listens here, wrong that nothing has attention to pay. I attend to everything—not with intention but with the passive, total attention of a medium perfectly adapted to its function. Every sound that has ever been made in this place has passed through me. Every call between ungulates across the distances. Every drop of water finding stone. Every human word spoken in frustration or wonder. Every last vibration from machinery grinding to a halt.

And in the moments of true silence—rare but real, when even the stones stop their thermal adjustments and the ungulates stand perfectly still and the air itself seems to pause—I discover what I really am. Not the sounds themselves but the space that makes sound possible. The potential that allows vibration to exist. The delicate, precise conditions of density and temperature and molecular composition that permit waves to form and travel and decay according to the particular physics of this place.

I am what makes the ungulates' distributed communication possible, what made the human presence unbearable, what gives the industrial ruins their voices. I am the medium through which this quiet world speaks to itself, constantly, in frequencies often too low or too brief or too scattered for any single organism to perceive, but which together create the acoustic signature of an entire ecosystem.

The steppe doesn't need me—it existed before atmosphere thick enough to carry sound, will exist after the air finally bleeds away. But while air remains, while things move and vibrate and call to each other across the patient stone, I persist: the sum of all sounds and all silences, the consciousness that emerges from vibration itself, the thing that listens without ears and speaks without voice in the thin cold air of this ancient, quiet world.
