# The Keeper of Channels

I am the one who walks the veins of Still Water, who knows every seep and trickle, every gathering place where moisture beads against stone. My work began before dawn this morning, as it has for thirty years, as it did for my mother before me, as it will for whoever takes up the iron rod and leather apron when my hands can no longer grip.

The channels are my province—those shallow grooves worn into the paving stones where water finds its way from the heights to the low places. Most see them as mere drainage, functional cuts in stone. But I know them as a living system, a circulation that keeps the town from drowning in its own persistence. Without my work, the water would pool, would freeze in winter and crack the foundations, would breed the green slime that makes stone treacherous. Without my work, Still Water would truly become still.

I carry few tools. An iron rod, thin as my smallest finger, for clearing the narrow places where leaves and grit collect. A bundle of coarse grass, dried and bound, for sweeping the wider channels. A small trowel for the places where moss grows too thick, where the green must be scraped back to let the water run. These hang from my belt alongside a waterskin—not for drinking, but for testing. I pour water at the high points and watch it run, note where it slows, where it pools, where it disappears into cracks that should not be.

My rounds take me through every quarter before the bell rings at midday. I begin at the highest point, near the eastern wall where the morning water gathers, and work downward, following gravity's pull. The children know me by the scrape of my rod against stone, by the whisper of my grass broom in the channels. They follow sometimes, dropping pebbles into the flowing water, watching them race toward the drains. I do not chase them away. They are learning the town's circulation as I learned it, by watching, by following, by understanding that water always finds its level.

In the narrow alley behind the smithy, I pause. Here the channel branches—one arm toward the tanner's yard, another toward the central drain that runs beneath the market square. Yesterday's rain has left debris at the junction, small sticks and leaves carried from the terraces above. I kneel, work the rod into the gap, feel the satisfying give as the blockage clears. Water, patient as always, resumes its course. The sound changes—from a tentative trickle to a steady flow—and I know the work is good.

The moss troubles me more each year. It grows in the perpetual damp, spreading from the joints where my rod cannot reach. Green-gray, soft as cloth when wet, treacherous as ice when walked upon. I carry small bags of coarse sand to scatter where it grows too thick, to give feet purchase on the smoothest stones. But I do not scrape it all away. The moss belongs here as much as I do, as much as the water itself. My work is not to eliminate but to balance, to keep each element in its proper proportion.

At the well, I always stop. Not to draw water—others do that work—but to check the overflow channel that runs from the well's base. When the rains are heavy and the water table rises, the well can overtop its stone rim. The overflow channel carries this excess away, under the market square, down to the lower drains. It must be kept clear. I've seen what happens when it blocks—the market floods, the stones grow slick, the merchants must move their wares to higher ground. So I test it weekly, pouring a full skin of water into the channel, watching it disappear into the darkness below.

The people nod when they see me, but rarely speak. What would they say? The work I do is like breathing—essential, constant, unnoticed until it stops. But I notice them. The baker whose runoff of wash-water follows the same channel each morning, carrying the yeast-smell into the street. The weaver whose dye-water stains certain stones blue and red, marking her work-days like a calendar. The children whose games leave mud-dams in the smaller channels, which I clear without complaint. We all use the water ways. I simply tend them.

In winter, the work changes. Ice forms in the channels, expanding in the joints, threatening the careful engineering of centuries. I rise earlier then, carry hot sand from the smithy's forge, scatter it where the ice grips hardest. Some mornings I work by touch alone, my fingers too numb to feel the rod, trusting muscle memory to guide the clearing. The winter water moves differently—sluggish, reluctant, fighting the cold that would make it solid. But it moves. It always moves, if the channels are clear.

Spring brings its own challenges. The melt from the sparse snow on the heights flows all at once, and the channels that were adequate for a steady seep become torrents. I work all day then, moving from junction to junction, clearing the winter's accumulated debris, watching for places where the water has found new paths, worn new grooves. The stone tells the story of every spring in its surface—each flood leaving its mark, each year's water cutting a little deeper.

I have walked these channels for so long that I dream them. In sleep, I trace their paths from memory—the main artery that runs from the eastern wall, the branching at the smithy, the convergence at the market, the dark tunnels that carry Still Water's water away to whatever lies below. I know where each house's runoff joins the system, where each roof's gutter empties, where each terrace's drainage enters the town's circulation. The knowledge lives in my feet as much as my mind.

There are those who say the work chose me, not the other way around. My mother was Keeper before me, and I followed her as a child, learning the sounds water makes in stone. When she grew too bent to kneel at the junctions, I took up the rod. There was no ceremony, no passing of authority. One day she worked the channels, the next day I did. The water did not care who cleared its path, only that someone did.

I am growing older. My knees protest the constant kneeling, my back aches from the bending. Soon I will need to find someone to follow me as I followed my mother. I watch the children who trail after me, look for one who sees what I see—not just water in stone, but the circulation that keeps a town alive. One girl, the baker's daughter, has begun to arrive at the eastern wall before I do, waiting with her own small stick to help clear the morning's gatherings. Perhaps she is the one. Perhaps not. The channels will choose, as they always have.

But for now, I am still the Keeper. Tomorrow I will rise before the bell, take up my rod and broom and trowel, and walk the veins of Still Water. I will clear what needs clearing, balance what needs balancing, maintain the flow that has run for centuries before me and will run for centuries after. The water is patient. The stone endures. And I, between them, do the small, essential work that lets a town on a high plateau live with its own dampness, its own permanence, its own quiet circulation of life.

The bell will ring at dawn. I will already be working, the scrape of my rod the first sound in the streets, the whisper of water the town's first word of the day. This is enough. This has always been enough.
