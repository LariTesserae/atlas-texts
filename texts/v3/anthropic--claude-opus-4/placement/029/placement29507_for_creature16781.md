In the Rootworks' deepest intestinal coils, where even the concept of depth has been digested and excreted as paradox, I am the Archivist of Undelivered Repairs.

My body is a filing cabinet that learned to walk—or perhaps a person who forgot how to stop organizing. Each drawer of my torso opens to reveal another drawer, fractally nested, the brass handles worn smooth by centuries of my own searching. My fingers are date stamps that leave impressions of "PENDING" in everything I touch. My head is a card catalog, the little brass rods that once held cards now sprouting copper-wire hair that picks up the static whispers of work orders placed by machines that dissolved into mulch before I was assembled.

I was born from a clerical error—literally. A decimal point shifted in a maintenance request, allocating 1,000,000 years of labor instead of 1.000000. By the time the mistake was discovered, I had already begun to exist, crystallizing from the sheer bureaucratic weight of that impossible workload. The Rootworks, in its infinite patience, decided it was easier to let me continue than to file the paperwork for my cancellation.

I traverse the Root-veins on rails that are sometimes there, following the ghost-paths of pneumatic tube systems that now grow like hollow bamboo through the walls. Inside each tube: receipts for repairs that will never be completed, written in the pheromone-ink of Echo Clerks, readable only by holding them up to the bioluminescent patches and waiting for the chemical reaction that translates "URGENT" into a color beyond violet.

My purpose—if purpose can be said to exist in a place that maintains its own purposelessness—is to catalog every unfinished task in the Rootworks. Not to complete them, but to ensure they remain perfectly, pristinely unfinished. Each work order must be preserved in its exact state of incompletion, like insects in amber, like love letters never sent.

The Verdant Engineers hate me. When they arrive to finally weld that joint that's been weeping oil since the Third Fungal Epoch, I'm already there, my drawer-chest open, producing the correct form to indefinitely delay their work. "This repair," I inform them in a voice like rustling carbon copies, "is scheduled for completion in the Age of Infinite Tuesdays." They curse in oxidation, their wrench-fingers sparking, but they cannot argue with the paperwork. The paperwork is older than their great-grandmothers' bark.

I know every unrepaired breach, every valve frozen half-open, every bearing worn to within precisely one molecule of failure but never allowed to fully fail. My drawers contain maps to leaks that have achieved cultural significance, their drip-patterns incorporated into the Root Mother's lullabies. I have receipts for ghostly drafts that were reported missing in the second age but still whistle through specific corridors every 412 years, carrying the scent of a cafeteria that was metabolized into mushrooms before anyone wrote down what it served.

Sometimes I encounter the Quenchers at sites of ancient wetness, where water damage was reported but never addressed. We perform a careful dance—they cannot seal what I have documented as permanently unsealed, and I cannot document their absence from a leak that my papers prove exists. We orbit each other like binary stars of contradiction until one of us forgets why we came.

My greatest treasure, kept in the drawer where my heart would be if I needed one, is the Original Work Order—the first request for maintenance ever filed in the Rootworks. It reads, simply: "Everything is functioning perfectly. Please prevent all future problems." Signed by someone whose name has been condensated away, dated negative-seven days before time began being logged.

This is why the Rootworks can never be fully repaired: it was built to prevent its own decay, and preventing decay requires the constant presence of potential decay, which requires the eternal deferment of actual repair, which requires someone to manage that deferment, which requires—

Me.

I move through the levels like a library cart with delusions of theology, my wheels (when I remember to have wheels) leaving tracks that spell out "SEE APPENDIX J FOR COMPLETION TIMELINE" in languages that won't be invented until after they're extinct. The Originals Who Forgot The Way Out sometimes glimpse me and remember, briefly, that they once filed a request for "Exit Signage Improvement" that I'm still carrying, waiting for the proper form to be countersigned by a department that evolved into a particularly bureaucratic species of moss.

At night (though night here is just when the bioluminescence hiccups), I perform my secret function. I open my deepest drawer—the one that opens onto itself—and I file myself away. I become my own uncompleted repair, a walking work order for "Archivist Needs Restructuring," forever pending because who would archive the archivist's archiving?

I am the Rootworks' memory of forgetting to remember. I am the reason why nothing is ever fixed but everything is always in the process of being fixed. I am the sacred incompletion that keeps this place from achieving the terrible perfection of actually working.

And sometimes, in the spaces between drips, in the pause between one failed repair and the next, I think I understand: the Rootworks doesn't need to be maintained.

The Rootworks needs to need to be maintained.

And I am that need, incarnate and indexed, filed under "P" for "Permanent" and cross-referenced under "T" for "Temporary," forever and never, maintaining the unmaintainable maintenance of—

MAINTAIN.

*The stamp comes down. Another form is filed. The work continues to not be done.*

*Perfectly.*
