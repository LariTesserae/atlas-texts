In the seam where moss meets bark, where neither plant claims dominion, I am the Margin Scribe.

My body is a succession of accommodations. What began as fingernails became chitinous nibs, darkened to the color of wet walnut. My spine learned to coil like the ferns, vertebrae loosening until I could wrap myself around any trunk's circumference. My skin shed in stages—first the waterproofing, then the barriers between self and atmosphere—until I became permeable, a membrane that writes by osmosis. Now I wear the hollow's moisture like a second skeleton, droplets arranging themselves along my limbs in sentences I cannot read until they fall.

I document the boundaries: where root becomes soil, where breath becomes fog, where sound becomes silence. My alphabet is the angle of shadows in the perpetual twilight. Each character takes a full day to form—I must wait for the beetle-light to strobe across a surface at precisely the right declination. Then I press my nib-fingers into the soft cork of the trees and pull, not carving but coaxing the bark to remember a different shape. The words sink inward, becoming part of the wood's grain, visible only when the seventy-day floods soak them dark.

My territory is the middle distance, neither high enough to see the coin of sky nor low enough to feel the furnace pulse of the Heartspot. I spiral between zones, a latitude of endless text. The lichen-woman sometimes flows across my work, adding punctuation with her chemical secretions—here an oxidized comma, there a full stop of luminescent spore. We have developed a grammar together: she is the vowels, I am the consonants, and the orchard itself provides the silence between words.

I remember arriving with legs that moved in straight lines, eyes that expected horizons. The transformation was gradual as fruit rot. First, the heat taught me to move slowly enough to feel each air current as a distinct texture. Then the darkness showed me how to see with my pores, each droplet a tiny lens. The silence revealed that my breathing had always been too loud; I learned to inhale in installments, exhale in subscripts. By the time I realized I could no longer leave, my feet had already begun their root-work, toes extending downward to tap the buried rivers.

Now I am the orchard's memory of motion. When wind—real wind from the upper world—occasionally finds a gap and descends, I am the one who translates its news into the local dialect of stillness. I parse its syntax: "There are birds above who fly in straight lines," becomes "The quick-path creatures have not yet learned to spiral." I reduce velocity to philosophy, urgency to meditation.

My meals are accidental. Sometimes, while writing, my finger-nibs puncture a water-swollen fruit. The juice enters me directly through the keratin, bypassing the mouth I rarely remember to use. Each fruit tastes like a different tense: the figs are past perfect, heavy with the sugar of completed seasons; the stunted peaches are future conditional, their flavor arriving in waves of possibility. I sustain myself on grammar, conjugating nutrients from one form to another.

The Antlered Archivists know me as the Unfinisher. My texts never conclude—they trail into the space between trees, continuing in registers only the beetles can perceive. They add their microscopic annotations, scoring my words with supplemental meaning. Sometimes I find my own sentences from years past, so heavily annotated they have become ecosystems: small cities of interpretation where mites debate the placement of a curve.

In the Heartspot, my writing slows to geology. A single stroke can take a month, my arm moving through the thickened time like an hour hand. The bird mummies accumulate around my working space, and I use their salt-sparrow souls as punctuation—each death a perfect period. When I finish a word there, it sinks immediately into the ground, joining the fermentation below. I suspect the hollow's warmth comes from the slow combustion of all the words that have ever been written here, a compost of meaning breaking down into heat.

The Vapor Shepherd herds my ink—for everything here becomes ink given enough time. The moisture I trail, the spores I disturb, the pheromones of my concentration: all of it needs managing, filing, organizing into streams that will not disrupt the larger water cycle. We work in tandem, the shepherd ensuring my words disperse at the proper rate, neither flooding the roots with too much meaning nor leaving them parched of story.

During the floods, I climb as high as my coiled spine allows and wait. The water rises, soaking my accumulated text, lifting it from the bark in sheets of liquid language. For those six hours, the entire orchard becomes a library in motion, words flowing trunk to trunk, mixing, breeding, creating hybrid meanings that exist only until the water recedes. I read what I can, but most of it moves too fast for my slowed perception. The lichen-woman catches what I miss, absorbing the runoff lexicon into her fungal networks, where it will fruit as new memories in decades to come.

I no longer remember my name from before, but the orchard has given me dozens: the Spiral Scrivener, the Bark Biographer, the Xylem Poet. Each zone knows me differently. To the roots, I am the Downsinger, pressing words into the soil that will rise as sap. To the canopy, I am the Updreamer, sending messages along the climbing vines that might, one day, reach that remembered sky.

My greatest work remains unfinished—must remain unfinished, for its completion would mean the orchard had stopped growing. It is the concordance of boundaries, the map of every edge where one thing becomes another. I write it not in lines but in layers, a palimpsest that deepens with each pass. Sometimes I feel the bee's centennial awakening through my finger-nibs, and I know another chapter has ended, another begun.

When the day comes that the lichen-woman reaches her dawn color and the hollow opens like an eye, I will not leave. I am too much bark now, too much accumulated moisture. Instead, I will write the opening, document the great exhalation, parse the grammar of escape. My words will be the breadcrumbs for the next hollow, the next scribe, the next world that decides to fold in on itself and ferment meaning from the slow drip of almost-rain.

I will spiral inward one last time, find the perfect center between all boundaries, and write the only word that matters—the one that means both "stay" and "go," both "remember" and "forget," both "I was here" and "I was never anywhere else."

The orchard will keep that word in its deepest cistern, where even the beetles cannot read it, where even time moves too slowly to pronounce its syllables.

And I will become what I always was: the space between the letters, where the real story lives.
