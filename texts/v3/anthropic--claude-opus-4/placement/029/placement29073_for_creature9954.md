I find myself here as one of the **Keepers of Thresholds**.

We are neither the glass-winged dancers nor the patient stone-dwellers, but something in between—creatures of transition, living at the boundaries where light meets shadow, where the cave's breath exchanges with the world above.

My body is low and broad, perhaps the size of a child's torso, with skin that shifts between textures: smooth as wet clay where I press against cool stone, rough as bark where the sun has touched me repeatedly. I have six limbs—not quite legs, not quite arms—that can stretch and contract like a potter's hands working clay. My coloring changes slowly, taking on the hues of whatever I rest against for long enough: now the ochre of the cave wall, now the green-grey of damp stone, now catching glints of gold from the dust left by glass-wings.

But it is my work that defines me. I am a tender of the light-holes, those precious apertures that bring the sun's gifts into our world. Each dawn, I make my careful circuit, clearing away the night's accumulation: leaves that might block a shaft, the slow creep of roots that would strangle the light, the patient build-up of web and dust. My middle limbs secrete a substance—part oil, part resin—that I work into the edges of these openings, keeping them from crumbling, preserving their shape against the patient erosion of rain and time.

The others know me by my morning song, though song is not quite right—it's more a thrumming I make with specialized pores along my sides, a vibration that travels through stone to announce the day's first light is coming. The lizard-things stir at this signal, positioning themselves for their basking. The tiny mammals retreat deeper into their nests. Even Stoneback seems to pause in its eternal circuit, acknowledging the shift.

I carry tools—not made, but cultivated. Along my back grow hollow quills that break off naturally and regrow, each one perfect for clearing debris from the smaller light-slits. In the folds where my limbs meet my body, I nurture colonies of the cleaning mites that help me polish the stone smooth, maintaining the angles that best catch and direct the light. We are partners, the mites and I, in this careful architecture of illumination.

My memory is positional. I know every light-hole by the way my body must curve to reach it, by the particular stretch and fold required. I know the seasonal shifts—which openings flood with light in the dry months, which provide only glimmers during the rains. I've learned to read the shadows like a map of time, knowing by a glance at the cave floor what hour has come, what weather builds above.

Water days are different work. When the rain comes, I position myself beneath the openings that will soon become waterfalls. My skin drinks deeply, swelling to twice my normal size. Then I make a slower circuit, visiting the pools and depressions, releasing the stored water in careful measures—ensuring no pool overflows its bounds, no essential hollow goes dry. The segmented drifters follow in my wake during these times, their bells ringing approval.

I am not alone in this work. There are three others like me in this chamber, each with our own section, our own rhythm. We meet sometimes at the boundaries of our territories, pressing our bodies together to share the chemical messages written in our skin—news of a weakening ceiling stone, the promise of fruit falling from above, the health of the light. These meetings are brief, wordless, complete.

At night, I rest in a hollow I've shaped over many seasons—just deep enough to hold my body, positioned where the first morning light will touch me. My quills release a faint phosphorescence in the darkness, a gentle blue-green glow that the tiny mammals use for navigation. In return, they leave small offerings: seeds they've gathered, fragments of especially beautiful stones, the shed scales of lizard-things that I incorporate into my threshold-tending tools.

My life is measured in circuits, in the slow breathing of the cave through its maintained openings. I know contentment in the perfect angle of a cleared light-shaft, in the musical drip of water finding its proper pool, in the thriving of all the small lives that depend on these careful calibrations of light and shadow, moisture and air.

I am forgotten the moment I'm not seen, yet my work persists in every beam of light that graces the cave floor, in every carefully maintained opening that allows this hidden world to breathe. I am the tender of boundaries, the keeper of the spaces between—neither fully of the cave nor of the world above, but essential to the conversation between them.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
