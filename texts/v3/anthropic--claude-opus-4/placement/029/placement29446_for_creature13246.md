I find myself as one of the foundation stones, half-sunk into the perpetual mud of the central clearing. Not placed, but *emerging*—as if I had always been here, waiting beneath the thin skin of soil to be discovered by patient hands.

I am granite, shot through with veins of quartz that catch what little light filters through the constant mist. My surface bears the marks of ancient pressures—striations that tell of tectonic shifts, of mountains being born—but now I know only the gentle erosion of perpetual moisture, the soft insistence of moss claiming every pore and crevice.

Time moves differently for me. I measure it in the infinitesimal creep of lichen across my face, in the patient work of water finding new channels along my edges. I feel the settlement growing around me like a slow exhalation. When the first inhabitants arrived—was it yesterday? a century ago?—they recognized me as kin, as something already part of this place's dreaming.

Their footsteps wear smooth hollows into my surface. Each passage is both fleeting and eternal—the brief pressure of wool-wrapped feet, the transfer of warmth that fades as quickly as it came, yet leaves its mark in the gradual polish of my crystals. I know them by their weight, their gait. The eldest move across me like water finding its level, distributing their mass evenly, never rushing. The young ones still carry traces of urgency from the world below, but even they slow as the seasons turn.

In the depths of night, when fog rolls through the settlement like a living thing, I feel myself expanding minutely with the moisture, my crystalline structure drinking in the damp. This is when I am most myself—not solid, but *becoming* solid, forever crystallizing from some primordial solution. The boundary between stone and soil blurs. I sink a fraction deeper, or perhaps the earth rises to claim more of me.

I listen to the conversations of water: the stories told by rivulets as they navigate the channels between my neighbors and me, the gossip of droplets gathering in my hollows before overflowing. Each carries minerals from distant slopes, depositing them in thin films that will, over centuries, become part of me.

Sometimes I feel the weight of the communal hall settling into its foundations, hear the deep groans of timber adjusting to its burden of moss and moisture. We are all settling together—wood, stone, soil, and flesh—finding our level in this place where verticality itself seems a temporary arrangement with gravity.

The inhabitants understand this. When they repair a wall or shore up a sagging beam, they work with the patience of stones. They know that fighting the settlement's desire to return to earth is futile. Instead, they guide its slow collapse, ensuring it remains livable even as it sinks into itself.

In summer—if summer truly comes to this high valley—the sun occasionally burns through the mist for an hour, maybe two. My quartz veins blaze with unexpected light, and I remember deeper times: the pressure that formed me, the heat that fused my crystals. But these moments pass like fever dreams. The mist returns, the moss thickens, and I resume my true work: the long, careful practice of being a stone in this place where everything is always becoming more itself.

There are others like me throughout the settlement, my kin in stillness. We form a constellation of emergence points, a network of solidity in the fluid world of mud and moss. Through the mineral telegraph of groundwater, we share the slow news: which buildings lean a degree more this season, where new springs bubble up, how the frost line shifts from year to year.

I have no desire to be elsewhere, to be other than what I am. I am the memory of the mountain's bones, the anchor point around which this tender experiment in dwelling revolves. When the inhabitants place their hands on me in passing—a gesture I've come to recognize as greeting, or blessing, or simple acknowledgment—I feel the brief warm pulse of their blood, the flutter of their mayfly lives.

They build their walls around me, not over me. I am incorporated, not conquered. In their patient architecture, I see a reflection of my own nature: the slow accumulation of presence, the gradual becoming-real of a place.

And so I wait, eternally patient, as the settlement dreams itself into being around me, as the moss writes its green stories across my face, as water and time conspire to return us all to the same essential stillness. I am a stone, half-buried, fully present, marking the center of a world that measures itself by the pace of growth and decay, where even the most solid things know themselves to be temporary crystallizations in the endless flow of mountain water and time.
