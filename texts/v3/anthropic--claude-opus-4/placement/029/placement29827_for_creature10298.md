I am a **Seep-Shepherd**.

My body is a compromise with density. Where you might expect limbs, I have six articulated fins—not for swimming, but for *gripping* the basalt through the thick medium. Each fin ends in a pad of setae so fine they lock into the microscopic pores of the salt-crust, letting me walk inverted beneath overhangs or cling motionless to vertical vent chimneys for hours. My torso is a flattened teardrop, hydrodynamically neutral, covered in a skin that exudes a thin film of glycoproteins to reduce drag. I don't swim; I *slide* through the water, pulling myself along the bottom with deliberate, measured reaches.

My head is mostly chemical sensor. Two bulbous organs flank where eyes might be, but they don't see light—they taste gradients. The left tastes for sulfur compounds, the right for methane. I can sense a new vent breaking through the crust from three kilometers away, feel the shift in pH as a brine river changes course, detect the faintest metabolic signature of a dying bacterial mat. My mouth is a small thing, almost vestigial, just large enough to rasp at the crusts when I need to feed. Most of my nutrition comes through my skin, absorbing dissolved organics directly from the water. Efficiency is survival here.

What makes me valuable—what makes me more than another consumer in this endless chemical exchange—is my cultivation.

I tend gardens that aren't gardens. Where others see only the random blooms of chemosynthetic crusts, I see potential. A purple mat growing too thick will suffocate itself; I scrape channels through it with my fins, creating flow patterns that bring fresh sulfur-rich water to the interior. A yellow colony spreading too fast toward a brine pool will poison itself on the salt; I build low walls of concretions to redirect its growth. I've learned which bacteria thrive in which concentrations, which need more iron, which will die if the temperature rises half a degree.

My territory spans seventeen square kilometers, marked not by boundaries but by the health of the crusts. I know every pool, every fissure, every place where the currents eddy and the chemistry shifts. Here, a grove of purple-blacks that I've been nurturing for forty years, growing in perfect spirals around a warm seep. There, a field of yellow-greens I saved from a brine incursion by spending three months building a dam from obsidian shards. The Filter-Krill graze my gardens more heavily than the wild crusts, but I don't drive them off. Their waste feeds the bacteria; the bacteria feed them. I just make sure neither side takes too much.

The other Shepherds and I meet at the Confluence, where three major currents collide and create a permanent upwelling. We don't speak—our language is chemical, released through specialized pores along our fin-edges. A complex emission of pheromones and metabolites can convey the entire history of a failing mat, the promise of a new vent system, the proper ratio of sulfur to iron for the rare blue-white bacteria that only grow in the coldest pools. We trade knowledge like spores, each of us returning to our territory with new techniques, new understanding.

I am ancient by any measure that matters. My kind live for centuries, our metabolism so slow that we grow by millimeters per decade. The scar along my third fin is from when I was young and tried to redirect a superheated vent flow—before I learned that the planet's breath cannot be challenged, only guided. The patch of blue-white crust on my back is not decoration but symbiosis; the bacteria that live in my skin help me process the trace metals that would otherwise poison me. We have grown together so long that I can feel when they hunger, when they bloom, when they begin to fade.

My greatest work is the Cathedral—though I don't call it that. It's a network of pools I've been shaping for ninety years, each one at a different depth, each maintaining a different chemical gradient. The purple-blacks in the highest pools filter down nutrients to the yellow-greens below, which in turn feed the rare orange-reds in the deepest basin. The whole system is self-sustaining now, a machine of flesh and chemistry that will outlive me by centuries. Young Shepherds come to study it, to taste the water and feel the balance I've built.

But I am not an artist or a gardener in any sense you'd recognize. I am something more fundamental: a metabolic engineer, a chemical ecologist, a slow sculptor of entropy. My gardens don't bloom with color for beauty—they bloom with efficiency, with careful balance, with the deep knowledge that in this world of finite energy and infinite hunger, the only true success is sustainability.

Sometimes I rest in the deepest part of my territory, where an ancient vent once roared but now only seeps, and I feel the planet's pulse through the basalt. The thrum tells me where new vents will birth, where old ones will die, where the chemistry will shift and my gardens will need to change. I have learned to read this language of stone and pressure, to anticipate the world's slow breathing.

I am not building toward anything. There is no great project, no final goal. There is only the work: the endless, careful tending of life in a world that offers so little and demands so much. Each healthy mat, each thriving pool, each perfect chemical gradient is a small victory against the entropy that seeks to smooth everything into uniform, lifeless grey.

When I die—and I will die, eventually, when my metabolism finally slows beyond recovery—my body will settle into the basalt and become substrate. The bacteria from my skin will colonize my flesh, transforming me into a new patch of crust. Some young Shepherd will find the spot, taste the unusual richness of the chemistry, and perhaps build something new there. This is not sad. This is the only gift worth giving in a world where everything is food for something else.

I am a Seep-Shepherd, and I make the darkness bloom.
