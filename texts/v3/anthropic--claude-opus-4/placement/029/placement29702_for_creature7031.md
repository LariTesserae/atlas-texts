In this ancient plateau world you've conjured, I find myself as one of the **Stone Singers** - the settlement's patient record-keepers and tool-makers, though the two roles are one and the same in our practice.

I am weathered like the stones themselves, my hands permanently stained with the russet dust of iron-rich minerals. My dwelling sits at the settlement's edge, where the cacophony of life meets the vast silence beyond the cliffs. It is both workshop and archive - a low structure of stacked stones chinked with dried moss, its interior walls lined with hundreds of small niches. In each niche rests a shaped stone: some are tools in progress, others completed implements, but many are simply stones that spoke to me in their patterns, their veins, their weight.

My work unfolds in geological time. Each morning, I wake with the lifting mist and select a stone - perhaps one gathered yesterday, perhaps one that has waited years for its moment. I sit cross-legged in my courtyard, where the dim light is best, and begin the slow conversation between rock and rock. Strike. Listen. The sound tells me everything: the stone's grain, its willingness to cleave, the tools it might become. Strike again. The rhythm joins the plateau's symphony - the goat hooves on stone, the birds' cries, the rustling voles. I am not hurrying; the stone has waited eons, and the tool's user can wait days.

But I am more than a tool-maker. In the patterns that emerge - the way this granite splits along a curved line, the way that basalt rings with a particular tone - I read the settlement's memories. Each stone carries stories. This piece of worn sandstone came from the gorge where Elia's-son-who-fell made his passage thirty seasons ago. This river-smooth pebble, carried up from the invisible valleys by someone's ancestor, marks the last time water ran freely here. Through years of patient observation, I've learned to see time in stone: how the plateau once looked, how it changes, infinitesimally, with each season.

The young come to me, not for tools - though I make those steadily, a hand-axe here, a grinding stone there - but to learn the patterns. I teach them to see the stories stones tell: how a vein of quartz points toward a seasonal seep, how certain fossils indicate where the old soil lies deepest. My knowledge passes slowly, one strike at a time, one observed pattern after another. Some children have the patience for it; most don't. That's as it should be. The stones will outlast us all.

In the evenings, I trade my finished tools for dried meat and fermented root-mash, joining the communal fires where I might share what the stones have told me - where new growth might be found, where the goat paths should shift to prevent erosion. My voice joins the slow conversation of the settlement, another thread in the weave.

I am not young - none who do this work are young - but I am not yet old. My apprentice, a quiet child who notices the weight of things, works beside me some days. Other days, I work alone, content in the dialogue between striker and struck, adding my persistent rhythm to the plateau's ancient song. When I die, my stones will remain in their niches, a library of patterns and possibilities, waiting for the next patient hands to read their stories and continue the slow work of survival.

This is enough. To shape stone, to read its memory, to live in the warm dim light where time moves like honey and every sound has meaning. I am a small part of an old world, and I am content to be nothing more.
