**Where I Would Find Myself**

I would be one of the Listeners—those who press their ears to the limestone walls in the deepest reaches of the Warrens, where even the moss-glow fades to memory. We are fewer than the engineers, perhaps only three or four of us, and we work in shifts that blur into one another like water finding its level.

My station would be a narrow chamber, barely wider than my outstretched arms, where the stone is still raw and unworked. Here, the walls weep constantly, and I've learned to distinguish between the sounds: the quick patter of surface rain filtering through a hundred feet of rock, the slow seep of ancient aquifers, the peculiar singing that happens when water finds a new channel. I sit on a worn metal stool, my tools arranged on a shelf cut directly into the limestone—stethoscopes modified with brass fittings, glass tubes that amplify vibration, delicate picks for clearing mineral deposits from the listening points I've marked with chalk.

My skin would be paler even than the others, almost luminous in the dark, and my hair—what remains of it—would be tied back with copper wire. My coveralls would be specially tailored, with deep pockets lined in oiled cloth to protect my more sensitive instruments: tuning forks of various frequencies, vials of different waters collected from throughout the cavern system, a leather journal where I record the stone's daily mutations in a notation system of my own devising.

I would rarely speak, having learned that silence sharpens the ear. When I do, my voice would carry an odd quality—a resonance that comes from spending years matching my breathing to the cavern's rhythm. The other inhabitants would regard me with a mixture of respect and unease, understanding that my work touches something fundamental about our existence here, though none could say exactly what.

My days would follow the stone's schedule, not the arbitrary divisions of surface time. I would wake when the eastern wall begins its morning condensation, a phenomenon I've tracked for years and believe corresponds to temperature changes far above. My breakfast would be the same as everyone's—fungal porridge and synthetic protein—but I would eat it cold, having discovered that chewing warm food interferes with my ability to detect certain subsonic frequencies.

The real work begins when I place my modified stethoscope against the seventeen listening points I've established over the years. Each has its own voice: Point Three hums with the movement of water through a hidden channel twenty feet deeper; Point Seven carries the distant grinding of tectonic plates; Point Twelve—my favorite—sometimes transmits what I swear are echoes of the surface world, though distorted beyond recognition.

I would record everything in my journal, using symbols that blend musical notation with geological markers: a tremolo mark for the flutter of air through a new crack, a fermata for the dangerous silence that precedes a rockfall, diminuendo for the gradual weakening of water flow that signals a channel is clogging with mineral deposits. These notes would be delivered weekly to the engineers, who would use them to predict where new pipes should be laid, which walls need reinforcement, where the next seep might appear.

But my true purpose would be deeper than maintenance. I would be listening for the Decision—the moment when the cavern chooses what it wants to become. The elder Listeners, those who came before me, left notes suggesting that caverns have lifecycles like any organism: youth, maturity, senescence, transformation. The Glowmire is young, still volatile, still capable of sudden change. One day, it might flood completely, becoming an underground lake. Or it might dry out, its water finding new channels, leaving us in a dead stone womb. Or—and this is what I listen for most carefully—it might achieve stability, finding its final form.

I would know the signs: a subtle shift in the harmonic frequency of dripping water, a new resonance in the stone's daily expansion and contraction, a quality of silence that speaks of completion rather than pause. When that day comes, my role would change from observer to herald. I would break my customary silence to inform the others, and together we would begin the next phase of our existence—whatever that might be.

In the meantime, I would find a strange peace in my narrow chamber, surrounded by the patient sounds of stone teaching itself how to be. My only decoration would be a small piece of flowstone I discovered in my third year here, its surface rippled like frozen music. Sometimes, in the deep quiet between water drops, I would hold it to my ear and listen to the memory of its formation, a thousand years of patience crystallized into form.

The other inhabitants would think me peculiar, perhaps even touched by the stone-sickness that claims those who spend too long in the deep places. But they would also know that without the Listeners, the Glowmire would be deaf to its own becoming, unable to prepare for the changes that inevitably come to all young stone. And so they would leave me to my work, occasionally bringing me fresh batteries for my equipment or a new vial for my water collection, but otherwise respecting the bubble of silence I maintain around myself.

I would age slowly in the Warrens, my movements becoming more deliberate, my hearing more acute even as my eyesight fades. Eventually, I would train an apprentice, teaching them the subtle differences between the sound of stable stone and stone under stress, the way to cup their hands to catch the echoes of distant caverns, the patience required to hear a mountain learning its shape. And when my time comes to join the stone myself, I would do so knowing that I had spent my life in conversation with the earth itself, translator between the quick-lived and the geological, keeper of the Glowmire's deepest secrets.

This would be my place: alone but not lonely, silent but surrounded by sound, still as stone but always listening for the moment when everything changes.
