# The Channel Keeper

I am one of the blue-corded, and my channels run beneath the northeast quarter where the stone is newest, still sharp enough to cut if you're careless. You learn not to be careless. The water teaches you that first.

My territory is seven buildings deep and three wide, twenty-one foundations threaded with water that wants to be somewhere else — wants to be at the center, at the well, but must be coaxed there through passages no wider than my smallest finger. I know each channel like the lines in my palms, which are themselves channels now, mapped with the same patient flow.

I work the dawn hours, before the fog lifts. This is when the water is most itself, when it hasn't yet remembered to evaporate or flee. I go barefoot always — the stone speaks through the soles, tells you where it's sound and where it's just a membrane over nothing. My feet are maps of this quarter, each callus earned from a particular edge, a specific corner where the water pools and tries to overflow its bounds.

The tools are simple: a copper rod worn thin as a reed for probing the narrower passages, a flat stone shaped like a tongue for pressing clay into gaps, a bundle of fresh reeds always tucked at my waist. But mostly I use my hands. The water knows hands, responds to them. You can feel where it wants to puddle, where it's found a new weakness in the mortar. You learn to think like water — patient, persistent, always seeking the lowest place.

In my channels live the pale fish, and I know them individually. There's one with a crooked spine that makes a question mark of its body. Another missing an eye. They school through my passages like thoughts through a mind, and I've learned their patterns — where they gather before rain, how they flee when a channel is about to fail. They are my consultants in this work. A channel with no fish is a channel about to die.

The green film that grows on everything is thickest in my territory. I don't scrape it all — that would be like shaving a living thing bald. I leave a fur of it, just enough that the snails have something to graze, just enough that the stone stays alive. Dead stone cracks. Living stone, dressed in its green, bends with the water and lasts.

I have three flooded stairs in my section. Two are old drownings, gone under before I took the blue cord. But the third I watched go. It was a season of heavy water, the kind where the fog never lifts at all and the rain comes horizontal. I worked every waking hour, pressing clay, setting reeds, but the water found the gap I couldn't — somewhere beneath, in the dark where hands can't reach. One morning the bottom step was wet. By evening, the water had climbed three steps. By the next dawn, it had found its level and stopped, a perfect mirror that will never drop again.

I told the black-corded one, of course. They went down into it while I watched from above — down into my failure, my drowned stair — and came back with the measurement. Two fingers' worth of rise. The reed-setters would need to work faster this season.

Sometimes I dream I am water. In the dreams, I flow backward through my channels, up from the well, dispersing into all the fine veins of the town. I understand then what the water wants — not to destroy, not to reclaim, but simply to be level. To find its true height. The town fights this truth with every course stacked, but in the dreams I understand that the fighting and the truth are the same thing. The water's will to rise and our will to rise with it — they're partners, not enemies.

I know the stone-setters think we channel keepers have the easier work. They sweat in what sun we get, lifting and placing, while we crawl on our bellies in the dark, reading water. But they don't understand the intimacy of it. The stone-setters build against. We channel keepers build *with*. My channels are a collaboration between me and the water, a daily negotiation, a conversation in clay and flow.

My predecessor trained me when I was seven, the age when children either fear the dark wetness under the buildings or are drawn to it. I was drawn. She showed me how to read the sound of water — the different notes it makes when flowing free versus pooled versus finding a new crack. She taught me the smell of a channel about to fail, like metal and moss combined. She died setting reeds in a storm, which is how most of us die — not drowning, but working.

The center well is fifteen channels from my nearest boundary. I feel its pull always, the way all water in the town feels it — the draw inward, the lean toward that still mouth. Sometimes when I'm deep in a channel, belly-down in the wet, I imagine I can feel the well's reflection on my back like a second sun, that too-blue sky looking up from below. But I don't turn to look. We don't look.

I am 32 floods old, counting by the seasons when the work doubles. My hands went pale past the wrist at 20. The oldest channel keeper made it to 50 floods before their knees couldn't take the crawling anymore. I hope for 40. That would be enough — time to train two replacements, time to see my channels through their next evolution, time to watch a few more stairs go under and know I did what I could to slow it.

This is the life: belly-down in the dark, reading water, setting clay, keeping the conversation going between what rises and what runs. Tomorrow the fog will lift for its hour and I'll be under the northeast quarter, copper rod in hand, negotiating another day's armistice with the patient wet. The pale fish will school through our shared territory. The green film will grow. The stone will weep.

And I'll make sure it weeps where it's meant to, through channels I've memorized like prayers, toward the center where all water goes and doesn't come back from — except as fog, except as rain, except as the next day's work, which is the same work, which is the only work:

Keeping even.
