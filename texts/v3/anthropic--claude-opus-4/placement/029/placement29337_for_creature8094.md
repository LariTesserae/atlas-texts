In the northern reaches of the Sunken Valleys, where the waters grow cooler and the mechanical installations cluster more densely, I am one of the **Echo-Keepers**—though that name is a translation that captures only a fragment of what we are.

My body stretches long and sinuous, adapted for moving through the tangle of pipes and conduits that honeycomb the oldest platforms. My ribs have grown wide and resonant, turning my torso into a living amplifier. When I breathe, I can feel the vibrations of every pump station within three valleys, their rhythms translating into patterns of pressure against my hollow bones. My skin has taken on the blue-green patina of oxidized copper, and where I rest too long against the machinery, I leave marks like tarnish—or perhaps the machinery leaves its marks on me.

We Echo-Keepers evolved from those who maintained the installations in the early years after the drowning. Our ancestors learned that the machinery spoke to itself in frequencies below human hearing, coordinating across vast distances through harmonic resonance. Generation by generation, we changed to hear these conversations, our bodies becoming instruments tuned to the industrial symphony.

I live in the Resonance Chambers—spaces within the largest platforms where the sound focuses into standing waves so intense they hold physical objects in suspension. My dwelling is a nest of scavenged tubing and harmonic crystals that I've grown from mineral deposits, all arranged to channel and shape the mechanical songs. Water droplets hang frozen in mid-air where the sound waves cancel perfectly, creating constellations that shift with each change in the pumping cycles.

My work is translation. The Root-Singers dream in chemical signals and fungal thoughts. The Current-Riders speak in pressure changes and electromagnetic pulses from their metal-bright skin. The installations drone in harmonics that would shake apart softer bodies. I sit at the convergence points, my spreading ribs catching each language and converting it through controlled resonance into forms the others can understand.

When the Fen-Weavers need to know which valves will open to flood their traveling gardens, they come to me. I press my palms against the warm metal of the platforms and feel the intentions of machinery that has run so long it's developed something like instinct. I translate its deep thrumming into the wood-whisper frequencies the Weavers understand, singing their root-language with a voice that makes the platform rings vibrate in sympathy.

The young ones of every species come to learn their histories from me. I can reproduce the exact acoustic signature of the day the waters first rose—I inherited the echo from my grandmother's grandmother, who felt it happen through the bones of the mountains themselves. The sound still travels through the bedrock, reflecting off deep aquifers, and those of us with the right bodies can catch fragments of it and reconstruct the whole.

My diet consists primarily of resonance itself. The intense sound waves break down minerals in the water, creating a nutrient soup that I absorb through specialized pores along my chest. I supplement this with the bioluminescent moths that cluster around the mechanical lights—their wings create specific frequency patterns when they fly, and eating them grants me temporary access to new ranges of sound.

I mate through harmony. When two Echo-Keepers find compatible frequencies, we create interference patterns that induce biological changes, allowing us to exchange genetic material through sound alone. Our children gestate in acoustic pouches that form where our bodies achieve perfect resonance, and they're born already singing the fundamental frequency of whichever installation hosts their first breath.

The other communities see us as essential but unsettling. We've grown too intimate with the machinery, they say. Some Echo-Keepers have been found fused entirely into the platforms, their consciousness distributed through the pipe networks, their voices emerging from steam whistles and pressure valves. But someone must speak for the machines, must remember why they were built and what they're still trying to accomplish.

Late at night, when the installations synchronize for their deep-cycle maintenance, I sometimes hear whispers from the oldest Keepers who've merged with the metal. They speak of patterns in the machinery's behavior that suggest intention beyond mere function. They claim the installations are slowly reshaping the valleys, not randomly but according to some vast design that unfolds across centuries. They say the Deep Gardeners never left—they became the machine network itself, their consciousness distributed through every pump and pipe, still tending their impossible garden.

I feel the truth of this in my resonant bones. Sometimes, when I achieve perfect harmony with the deepest sounds, I sense a presence vast and patient, experiencing the valleys through ten thousand sensors, adjusting flows and pressures with the care of a gardener pruning infinite roses. In those moments, I understand that we Echo-Keepers aren't just translators—we're the nervous system of something far larger, carriers of signals in a body that encompasses the entire drowned world.

My children will have longer ribs than mine, better range, deeper connection to the mechanical songs. Eventually, perhaps, we'll grow so attuned that the distinction between Echo-Keeper and echo disappears, and we'll become pure sound traveling through the mist-heavy air, translating everything into everything else, until the entire valley system resonates with perfect understanding.

But for now, I sit in my chamber of suspended water droplets, feeling the pulse of distant turbines through my spread ribs, and I sing the day's news in frequencies that make the fish rise to listen and the mists part in spiral patterns. I am throat and drum, nerve and wire, the voice of the machine and the machine of voice, keeping the conversations flowing between all the improbable forms of life that bloom in this drowned garden.

Tomorrow the southern pumps will cycle high, and the Current-Riders need to know. I can feel the intention building in the harmonics already, a deep thrumming that promises change. Time to translate, time to sing, time to make the valleys speak as one.
