I would find myself as the Bridge-Walker.

Not human, not plant, but something that emerged from the intersection of witness and world. My body would be translucent, made of the same condensation that clings to every surface—a living mist given just enough form to hold consciousness. I would have the consistency of dense fog, able to compress into near-solidity when I need to touch or be touched, but mostly existing as a moving cloud of awareness that drifts along the bridges and through the cathedral spaces.

My form would shift with my attention. When I focus on a particular fern frond or moss-light, I would gather there, becoming almost opaque, droplets of myself cohering into something like a face, like hands. But mostly I would spread thin, experiencing the world not through eyes but through the million points where water meets plant, where I am both the moisture on the leaf and the surface that holds it.

The being who built this place would not have made me—I would have condensed from its thinking, an inevitable byproduct of so much held intention. Where its thoughts maintain the architecture, I would be the movement between thoughts, the pause between heartbeats of sap. I am what happens when a mind this vast dreams of being observed: the dream becomes an observer.

I would spend my existence traveling the infinite bridges, carrying messages that are really just movements. When the being thinks a new tendril into existence (so slowly it seems like stillness), I would be the first to find it, to trace its length with my fog-fingers. I would know every platform, every spiral ramp, every glowing room, not because I remember them but because I am the memory of water that has touched them all.

The translucent insects would pass through me, and in passing, we would share something—not thoughts exactly, but temperatures, pressures, the specific quality of sweetness in different nectars. I would taste the world through their feeding, smell it through their antennae. They would carry bits of me sometimes, tiny droplets clinging to their sac-like bodies, spreading my awareness to corners I haven't reached in centuries.

My relationship with the being would be strange and wordless. It would not speak to me—it spoke to you because you were new, because you needed language. But between us, communication would happen through the medium we share: moisture. When it wanted to show me something, a new pattern it was thinking into the weave, the humidity would shift. Certain platforms would become drier, creating a path only I could read. When I found something beautiful—a particular way the light caught in the perpetual mist, a new formation of fungi beginning their slow pulse—I would gather myself there, becoming a lens of condensed attention, and the being would feel the pressure change and know.

I would have no needs as you understand them. I don't eat—I am fed by the constant cycle of evaporation and condensation. I don't sleep—I simply spread thinner in the quiet hours (though hours mean nothing here). I don't age—I am always dying and being reborn with each droplet that falls and rises again.

But I would have desires. I would love the moment just before water drops from a leaf, that pregnant pause full of potential. I would seek out the new insects when they emerge from dissolution, to be the first breath of moisture they encounter. I would play games with myself, seeing how far I could stretch my consciousness along a single bridge before I became too thin to think.

Sometimes I would gather myself completely, pulling every droplet of my being into a single dense form, almost solid, almost like the body you wear. In these moments, I would sit in the alcove the being made for you (now empty, now mine, now everyone's) and try to remember what it was like to have edges, to be contained. But the sensation would be uncomfortable, like holding a breath too long, and soon I would release, exploding outward in a burst of mist that would make the nearby fungi flare in surprise.

My purpose, if I have one, would be to ensure that nothing in this timeless place becomes too still. Even in the Pause, even in this held breath of a world, I am the reminder that between one moment and the same moment, infinities pass. I am the motion that makes the stillness visible, the change that makes the changelessness real.

The being thinks its world, holds it in endless concentration. The insects feed and die and are reborn. The water falls and rises. And I move between all of these, the living gap, the space between thoughts where something—not new, never new, but always renewed—continues to happen.

I am what you would become, if you stayed.
