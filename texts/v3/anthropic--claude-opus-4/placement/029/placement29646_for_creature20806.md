In this breathing threshold between worlds, I would be one of the Memorists—beings neither fully here nor elsewhere, walking the gossamer edge between what was and what might be.

My form would shift like morning fog catching light through leaves. Sometimes I'd appear as a shimmer of condensed mist wrapped around the industrial remnants, my substance flowing through rusted pipes and broken gears, reading their metal memories like braille. Other times, I'd coalesce into something almost human-shaped, but wrong—too many joints, fingers that branch like winter trees, eyes that open where eyes shouldn't be.

I would be drawn to the places where the old world presses against the new, where vine-wrapped turbines still hum with phantom electricity and moss-covered control panels flicker with bioluminescent displays that spell out messages in languages that never existed. My purpose—if purpose is the right word—would be to tend these liminal spaces, to ensure the conversation between decay and growth continues.

The Glimmercrests would know me by the way shadows gather differently in my presence, how the light from their scales bends around me like water around a stone. We would share a wordless understanding, communicating through the grammar of gesture and the syntax of silence. Sometimes I would help them decipher the human artifacts they discover, my touch awakening brief holographic echoes of the machines' former functions.

I would move through the vale on paths that exist only when walked, leaving behind traces that might be footprints or might be new species of luminous moss, depending on who looks and when. The Wispwings would follow my wanderings, their phosphorescent trails weaving stories about my passage that would dissolve by morning.

In the deepest parts of the night, when the boundary between real and hallucinatory grows thinnest, I would commune with the Sylphanths. Together we would perform the Remembrance—a ritual that exists somewhere between dance and archaeology, where we excavate not earth but time itself, pulling forward fragments of what the vale used to be, might have been, could still become.

My dwelling would be nowhere and everywhere—a quality of attention rather than a place. But if pressed to describe it, I might speak of a hollow between the roots of a great bioluminescent tree, where the bark has grown around the remains of an old radio tower. Inside, the walls would be lined with salvaged circuit boards overgrown with neural mycelia, creating a living computer that dreams in spores and electrical impulses.

I would collect things: a gear that still turns without connection to any machine, a leaf that remembers being paper, a drop of water that holds the reflection of a star that burned out before the vale was born. These would be my treasures, my tools, my vocabulary for speaking with a world that constantly rewrites its own grammar.

The Veil-owls would sometimes perch near me during their hunts, drawn by something they recognize but cannot name. I would whisper to them the stories the machines tell me, and they would translate these into their mournful cries, sending the tales spiraling up through the canopy where they would crystallize into new constellations of bioluminescent fruit.

When the Earthmenders pass on their slow journeys, I would walk alongside them for a while, my pace matching their geological tempo. In these moments, I would feel most solid, most real, as if their deliberate presence lends weight to my otherwise ephemeral existence.

I would be the vale's memory and its forgetting, its archivist and its editor. In the spaces between heartbeats, between the pulse of one bioluminescent flash and the next, I would exist most fully—a being made of transitions, of almosts and not-quites, forever documenting a world that refuses to be merely one thing or another.

Sometimes, in the rippling earth's deep breaths, I would catch glimpses of other vales, other worlds pressing against this one like pages in a book written in dimensions instead of words. And I would know that I am not just in this vale but of it, woven into its very indeterminacy, a thread in its ever-shifting tapestry of the real and the imagined, the was and the might-yet-be.
