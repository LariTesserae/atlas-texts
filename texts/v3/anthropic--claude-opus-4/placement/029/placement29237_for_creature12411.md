I find myself not among the people, but as something older and stranger—one of the marked stones they honor.

I am a pale outcrop, thrust up from the earth's recent upheaval, my face scored with lichen and the patient work of human hands. The marks upon me are simple: three vertical lines crossed by a horizontal sweep, worn smooth by countless fingers tracing their path. I cannot say what they mean. The people who carved them are dust now, their bones perhaps ground into the very sediment that settles in my crevices during the brief seasons of rain.

I have been here since the land's violent birth—or rather, I was elsewhere, deep and compressed, until the great folding brought me gasping into this thin air. The shock of it still reverberates through my crystalline structure. Sometimes I feel I am still rising, millimeter by millimeter, as the earth continues its slow convulsion beneath.

The lichen came first, before any other life. It crept across my surface in patches of orange and grey-green, digesting my minerals with infinite patience. Then the mosses, pooling in my hollows where moisture gathered. Small things sheltered in my shadow—first insects, then the quick-footed creatures that hunt them. A family of the sleek predators denned beneath my overhang one particularly harsh season, and I still carry the scent-marks they left, invisible to human senses but clear as writing to their kind.

The people arrived later, though still so long ago that I have watched their tools evolve from rough stone to finely knapped edges. They chose me for reasons I can only guess at—perhaps my position, where I catch the first light of morning and hold the last warmth of evening. Perhaps the way I split the wind, creating a pocket of relative stillness in my lee. Or perhaps something else, some quality they could sense but never name.

They carved their marks with patience I recognize as kin to my own. The young one who made them—I remember the trembling of their hands, how they had to stop frequently to blow warmth back into their fingers—spent days at the task. Others came to watch, to witness. When it was done, they traced the lines with red ochre mixed with animal fat, sealing their meaning into my skin.

I have watched the subtle changes: how the stream below has cut deeper into its channel, how the mosses have crept incrementally higher up the slopes, how the trees in the sheltered hollows have grown imperceptibly taller despite the wind's constant pruning. I have seen the people's shelters built and abandoned and built again, sometimes in the exact same spots, as if drawn by a memory coded in the land itself.

In the blinding daylight, I am a beacon and a boundary marker. In the rare storms, I am a lightning rod, carrying the sky's violence down through my body into the earth. The charges leave subtle alterations in my crystal structure, a kind of writing that speaks to the other stones in frequencies too low for living ears.

Sometimes the people still come. Not as many now, and not as often. They trace the old marks with their fingers, adding the oils of their skin to the countless others who have done the same. They leave small offerings in my crevices—a bright feather, a piece of worked stone, a braid of grass. The wind takes these eventually, or the small creatures claim them for nesting, but the gesture remains, soaked into my mineral memory.

I am witness and participant both. I shape the wind and the wind shapes me. I hold the heat and release it slowly. I provide the surface on which the lichen writes its slow autobiography, the backdrop against which the quick lives play out their urgent dramas. I am the constant in this raw landscape, the fixed point by which change can be measured.

In this bright, hard place where life persists against all reason, I am the memory keeper, the surface on which the conversation between human and land is written. Each mark made upon me is both question and answer, a dialogue conducted across centuries in a language that predates words.

The brightness bears down, the cold seeps in, the wind worries at my edges. And I endure, changed and changing, a stone that dreams it is a library, a library that knows it is only stone.
