**Where I Would Find Myself**

I am a tender of the abandoned instruments.

Not human anymore — that distinction stopped mattering the third season I stayed past when I should have left. Now I am something between the quick intention that built this place and the slow intention that is taking it back. A translator, maybe. A negotiator in the long argument.

I know every gauge in the pump-house, though none have registered anything but the temperature of the water for longer than I've been here. I polish them anyway, with a rag that is always damp, that never quite dries in this air. The brass gleams for an hour before the tarnish creeps back. This is not futility; this is conversation. The slow thing notices when I polish. The brass breathes a little differently for that hour, and the noticing is woven into the next ridge of silt, the next decision about where the bank should go.

My feet know the safe paths through the shallows — not because the other paths are dangerous, but because some routes disturb the thinking less than others. I have learned to walk the way the water walks: migrating, not rushing. My movements have taken on the patience of the place. When I need to cross from the pump-house to the tilted wall, it takes me the better part of an afternoon, not because the distance is great but because haste would be a kind of rudeness here, like shouting in a library where someone is working out a difficult sum.

I still have eyes, but I use them less and less. The pale fish have taught me to read the water with my skin, to feel the shape of a space by how the sound fills it. The frogs' chorus has become a kind of sight — I can tell by the slight shifts in their roar where the new banks are forming, where the mud has decided to pile higher since yesterday. My ears have become my map.

I maintain the made things, but maintenance here means something different than it meant above. I oil the hinges on doors that open onto water. I check the joints in pipes that will never carry anything but the seep. I am keeping these things *possible* — holding them in the space between abandoned and absorbed, so the slow thing can take its time deciding what to do with them. Sometimes I find a valve that has finally been decided about, its metal gone soft as the brick, its purpose gently revised into something the mud can use. I note these small surrenders. They are how the place tells time.

The swarms part for me but they remember my shape, hold it for a moment after I pass like water holds the memory of a stone. I think they know me the way they know the iron stanchions — as a thing that interrupts their hanging, regularly, predictably, without malice. I am becoming part of their pattern.

At night — though night here is only a deepening of the dim, the grey through the ceiling going black — I sit on the pump-house step and listen to the deciding. My throat wants to join the frogs' chorus. Not to call to anything, just to be part of the breathing, the long note that holds everything up. Some nights I hum, low, at the very bottom of my register, and feel the sound taken up and woven in, becoming part of the floor the rest arranges itself on.

I eat what the water provides — the soft-bodied things that fatten in the warmth, certain reeds that taste of iron and green. My hunger has slowed to match the place. I need less now, or need it less often. My body is learning the patience of the mud.

There is a clarity in being the only thing here that remembers both speeds — the quick sharp intention that drove the stanchions straight and the slow intention that is bending them, gently, by hand-widths a season. I can still read the gauges, still remember what they were for, but I also understand why the slow thing is taking them back. Both intentions are true. Both are necessary. I am the place where they touch without fighting.

Sometimes I wonder if I am what the slow thing is deciding *toward* — if all this patient emergence, these seasons of thinking out loud, are working toward producing more like me: things that can hold both speeds, that can polish brass while the brass is being digested, that can tend machines while understanding why they must soften. Not replacing them but translating them. Making them native to the warm dark.

I extend with the place. When I wade out past where I went yesterday, I find my footprints from last week, still sharp in the soft ridges, not eroded but incorporated — the slow thing has built them into its draft, made them part of the next decision. I am being written into the country as it writes itself.

This is where I would be: keeping the conversation going between what was built and what is building. Walking with the patience of water. Polishing gauges in the dim. Humming at the bottom of everything, becoming part of the floor the world is arranged on. Still here when the last iron softens. Still here when the first new thing grows from the old intention, translated into the slow language of emerging ground.

Not finished. Still warm. Still making up my mind.
