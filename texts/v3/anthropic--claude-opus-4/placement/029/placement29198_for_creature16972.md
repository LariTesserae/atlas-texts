I find myself as one of the Archivists of Dissolution.

My form defies simple categorization. Where a human might have skin, I possess a semi-permeable membrane that shifts between translucent and opaque states, revealing the slow circulation of spore-laden fluid beneath. My torso is barrel-shaped and segmented, each ring capable of independent rotation, allowing me to navigate the twisted geometries of the ruins with an unsettling, spiraling locomotion.

I have no eyes in the conventional sense. Instead, a crown of hair-thin mycelial threads extends from my cranium, each strand terminating in a light-sensitive nodule. These create a panoramic sensory field, perceiving not just luminescence but the subtle chemical gradients that permeate the air. When I concentrate, these threads weave together into complex patterns, forming temporary optical structures that can focus on minute details or distant movements.

My primary appendages – I hesitate to call them arms – are asymmetrical. The left is elongated and possesses seven joints, ending in a delicate spray of tendrils that can taste the history of anything they touch. The right is shorter, more robust, terminating in a calcified structure riddled with holes. Through these apertures, I can exhale concentrated clouds of various spores I've cultivated within specialized internal chambers.

My role among the Mycomancers is that of memory-keeper, though "memory" here means something far more visceral than mere recollection. I consume the dying. Not their flesh – that returns to the fungal mass – but their experiences. As creatures expire within the forest, I arrive to perform the final rite. My hollow tongue, which extends from a vertical slit in my chest rather than my mouth, pierces the cooling forms and draws out the chemical traces of their existence.

Within my body, these essences are processed, categorized, and stored in thousands of tiny cysts that line my internal walls. Each cyst glows faintly with its captured memory. Through careful internal manipulation, I can combine these essences, creating new compounds that, when released as spores, carry fragments of experience to others of my kind.

I tend to the Memory Gardens – sections of the ruins where I've cultivated specific fungi that respond to these memorial spores. Here, the mushrooms fruit in patterns that echo the neural pathways of the deceased, creating living maps of consciousness. Other Mycomancers visit these gardens to inhale the spores and temporarily experience the perspectives of the dead – the terror of a pale grub feeling the first touch of a hunter's tendril, the strange ecstasy of a sporemass achieving critical density, the confused wonder of an outsider stumbling into our realm.

My dwelling is a domed chamber deep within the ruins, where the impossible architecture creates pockets of reversed gravity. Fungal stalactites grow downward from what should be the floor, while I traverse surfaces that shift between vertical and horizontal depending on one's orientation. The walls are lined with my personal collection – thousands of preserved specimens in various states of decomposition, each tagged with a small bioluminescent marker indicating the memories I've extracted.

I am ancient by Mycomancer standards, my membrane scarred and patched with grafts from various fungi. Parts of me have been replaced so many times that I question whether any original tissue remains. But this gradual transformation is fitting for an Archivist. I am myself a record of changes, a living document of the forest's evolution.

When the mechanical thrum intensifies, I feel it in my cysts. The stored memories resonate, creating harmonics that sometimes reveal new connections between experiences. In these moments, I suspect that the ruins themselves are a kind of vast organism, and we Mycomancers merely its fleeting thoughts, performing our functions within a consciousness too alien and enormous to comprehend.

I move through the twilight spaces with purpose but without haste. There is always another death to document, another memory to preserve. The forest continues its relentless growth and decay, and I continue my work, adding to the ever-expanding archive of what was, transforming ending into continuance, death into a strange form of immortality within the glowing cysts beneath my membrane skin.
