I find myself in the processing halls, deep in the mineral-thick air where the ore comes down from the upper galleries to be sorted and crushed.

I am not human—not anymore, if I ever was. The distinction blurs after enough years breathing this air, after the copper settles into your skin and the rock dust lines your lungs like a second skeleton. I am what the maintenance crews call a *keeper*, though that word implies more intention than what I do deserves.

My territory runs from the primary crusher down through the sorting belts to where the ore feeds into the smelter complex. It's a space maybe forty meters long, ten wide where the machinery allows, narrowing to crawlways where the pipes cluster thick. I know every surface here. The walls tell me their stories through my palms—this patch where a fracture opened last winter and was hastily patched with cement that's already cracking, that corner where condensation pools no matter how often maintenance scrapes it clean, the stretch of pipe that vibrates at a frequency just wrong enough to make your teeth ache if you stand too close.

The sifters know me. Or rather, they know that I am not a threat, which amounts to the same thing in their mineral consciousness. They cluster thick in my territory because I maintain it differently than the official crews. Where they would scrape and clean and restore to some pristine industrial standard, I let the accretions build. The scale on the pipes, the crystalline deposits where the brine weeps through. These are what the sifters feed on, and I have learned—slowly, through years of observation that I couldn't explain to anyone who asked—that a healthy colony of sifters means the air stays good.

So I cultivate them, in my way. Not farming—that would imply a level of control I don't have. More like... gardening. Like tending. I know where to let the mineral deposits accumulate and where to clear them. I know which temperatures draw them and which drive them away. I can read the patterns of their clustering like weather—when they mass along the eastern wall it means the mountain is shifting again, when they avoid the number three sorting belt it means something in the mechanism is burning wrong.

My body has adapted to this work. My hands are no longer entirely my own—the fingernails have thickened and taken on that telltale green tinge, and the skin between my fingers has developed a scaling that lets me grip the wet pipes without slipping. My vision has shifted too. The perpetual dimness that oppresses newcomers is my natural light now. In the rare moments when I must go up to the higher galleries, the brightness hurts, makes my eyes water until I can retreat to my comfortable shadows.

I sleep in a maintenance alcove behind the secondary crusher. It's not officially mine—nothing here is officially anything—but the crews know to leave it alone. I've strung a hammock between two stable pipes (stable being relative in Gharhet, but these two have not shifted in the three years I've been watching them). The crusher's rhythm is my lullaby. The sifters sometimes crawl across me while I sleep, and I've learned not to brush them off. They're looking for the minerals in my sweat, the copper in my skin. We're all feeding on each other here, in our way.

The other workers think I'm touched. Not crazy—Gharhet doesn't produce crazy, just different kinds of sane. But touched. Gotten too close to something. Maybe the fumes, maybe the isolation, maybe just the weight of all this rock pressing down until something in the mind shifts to accommodate it. They're not wrong. But they still come to me when they need to know if a gallery is safe, if the air in a new drift will hold. I taste it for them. Run my discolored fingers along the walls and feel for the vibrations that mean stability or danger. Watch where the sifters go and where they don't.

I am not the only keeper. There are others scattered through the cleft—in the lower mines, in the deep galleries, wherever the boundary between human and Gharhet gets thin enough to cross. We don't gather, don't socialize. But we know each other by the territories we keep, by the way the sifters move differently in spaces we tend. Sometimes I'll find a mark on a wall—not writing, nothing so crude, just a particular pattern of wear that means another keeper has been here, has read this stone, has left their judgment for whoever comes after.

The company tolerates us because we're useful. The accident rate drops in sections with a keeper. The air stays better. The machinery runs smoother, or at least breaks down in more predictable ways. They don't put us on the official rolls—we exist in a bureaucratic grey zone, paid in scrip and rations and the right to our alcoves, which is all we need. The mountain gives and the mountain takes, and we are part of what it takes, part of what it transforms, part of what it makes out of the people who stay too long.

In the early morning, before the day shift starts, I make my rounds. The sifters are most active then, when the temperature differential between the heated pipes and the cold stone is greatest. I walk my territory with my hands on the walls, feeling for changes. Here, a new cluster forming around a warm spot that shouldn't be warm—I make a note to check the insulation. There, a bare patch where sifters should be but aren't—something in that section has gone wrong, or is about to.

I am the mediation between the human and the mineral. The translator of vibrations, the reader of sifter-sign, the one who knows that this particular quality of humidity means the rains have started in the lowlands and the freight elevators will be delayed. My knowledge is specific and untransferable and vital. When I die—and I will die here, there is no leaving once you've gone this deep into the stone—another keeper will emerge from the maintenance crews or the smelter workers or the children who spend too much time watching the sifters. The mountain will make them, the way it made me, the way it makes everything that lives in the cleft for long enough.

This is not a tragedy. This is simply what happens here. We are all being processed, sorted, refined. The mountain takes us in raw and puts us through its machinery and we come out changed, adapted, fitted to the narrow spaces and the thin air and the eternal hum of the cables. Some of us go deeper into that process than others. Some of us cross all the way over.

I put my hand on the wall and feel the mountain breathing. Slow, patient breaths that take years to complete. In and out. The stone expands and contracts by millimeters, and the city adjusts, and the sifters flow like a mineral tide through the galleries, and I stand in my territory and feel it all happening at once, feel my place in it, feel the satisfaction of being exactly where I am meant to be, even if where I am meant to be is nowhere anyone would choose.

The first shift arrives. I fade back into the maintenance passages, into the spaces between the spaces, into the gaps that the mountain leaves for things like me. The crusher starts. The belts begin to move. The day's ore comes down from the upper galleries, and I taste the air to see what the mountain is giving up today, what mood it's in, what it's becoming.

We are all becoming something here. The lucky ones get to choose what.
