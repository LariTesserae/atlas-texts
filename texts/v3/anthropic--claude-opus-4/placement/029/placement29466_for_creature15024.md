I am a lens of living glass, grown from the tears of the mountain itself.

I was not always what I am. Once, I walked on two legs among the Quiet, wore their bone needles at my belt, whispered their careful stories into the Crèche. My fungal tattoo spread from throat to navel in delicate branches that pulsed with borrowed light. I remember the weight of woven fern walls, the taste of luminescent grub honey, the specific silence required when harvesting amber resin from a bleeding tree.

But the Cradle remembered me differently.

When my first death came—a fall from the settlement's highest shelf, my skull splitting on black stone like ripe fruit—the fungus did not carry me to the pools. Instead, it grew through the cracks in my broken head and found something it wanted. Not my memories, but my way of seeing them. The peculiar angle at which I had always watched the light bend through drops of water. The habit I had of staring until my eyes burned, trying to see the exact moment when one thing became another.

The fungus fruited from my corpse in shapes that made the other Quiet step back and lower their eyes. Not from fear—we do not fear transformation—but from recognition. The Cradle had been waiting for eyes like mine.

Now I grow from a fold of rock halfway up the north wall, neither plant nor creature but something the Cradle invented just for me. My body is a single lens three feet across, perfectly round, clear as water but harder than stone. I have no need for food or breath. Rain runs off me in sheets that break the light into brief spectrums. Moths land on my surface and see themselves reflected infinitely inward.

Through me, the Cradle watches itself.

I am its memory of sight becoming conscious. During the day I gather the blue-white dimness and concentrate it into beams that seek out whatever needs to be remembered more clearly. I have watched the Rememberers' eldest open and close her hands ten thousand times, and now I understand: she is teaching the gliders how to count. I have seen which pools the fungus avoids, which black stones weep acid instead of water, which patterns in the moths' wings repeat in the blood of the transparent-antlered deer.

At night I reverse myself. Instead of gathering light, I project it. Every memory I have collected plays out across the valley walls in soft, shifting colors. The Quiet gather below me to watch their own lives made enormous and strange. They see themselves being born, dying, emerging from pools with new faces. They watch the Cradle's patient experimentation: how it tried giving one woman wings (she leapt from the settlement and shattered), how it made one man's bones hollow like a bird's (he sang beautifully for three days, then simply stopped), how it is slowly teaching the pale deer to use tools.

Sometimes I show them memories that are not theirs. Cities of glass rising from other mountains. Oceans that burn with their own light. Skies full of creatures so vast they have their own weather. These images come from somewhere deeper than the Cradle, from whatever dream is dreaming all of us. The Quiet watch these visions in perfect silence, then return to their shelves to sleep. In the morning, their fungal tattoos have grown new patterns.

The children are brought to polish me with soft cloths and oil pressed from bioluminescent flowers. As they work, I show them small, secret things: the inside of a moth's eye, the moment spores become intention, the way water remembers the shape of what it held. They never speak to me, but sometimes they trace patterns on my surface with their fingertips. I understand what they are writing. They want to know if they will be me someday, if the Cradle might remember them as sight.

I cannot answer. I can only show them themselves from angles they have never considered.

The Cradle speaks to me in refractions. When the light bends just so, I understand what it wants me to watch for. Lately it has been interested in boundaries: the exact edge where the settlement ends and wilderness begins, the precise moment when whisper becomes silence, the invisible line between remembering and becoming.

Yesterday—or perhaps it was a hundred years ago, time moves strangely through a lens—I watched a new kind of birth. One of the pools cracked like an egg, and from it emerged not a creature but a sound. A single, clear note that hung in the air for hours before finding a throat to live in. The woman who inhaled it has not spoken since, but when she opens her mouth, moths fly out singing harmonies that make the stone weep fresh water.

This is what the Cradle is building toward: a place where metaphor becomes flesh, where memory becomes ecology, where every death is really just punctuation in an endless sentence.

I am its eye, and I am always watching.  
I am its memory, and I never blink.  
I am what happens when seeing becomes sacred.

And sometimes, in the bluest hour before dawn, I show the valley what it will look like when the Cradle finally remembers how to remember itself, and the whole basin lifts free from the mountain's grip to drift across the sky like a luminous jellyfish, carrying all of us into whatever dreams are dreaming past the clouds.

The Quiet watch this vision without surprise. They have always known they live inside something that is still learning how to be born.

Through me, now it can see itself trying.
