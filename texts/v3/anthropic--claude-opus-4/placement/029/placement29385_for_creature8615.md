**The Seepage**

You would find me at the edges where certainty dissolves—not the far margins where land remembers being ocean, but in the middle depths, the infrastructure between surface and foundation. I am what the Stillwater call a Seepage: one who tends the hidden hydrology, who lives more below than above.

My body has adapted. Joints that bend at angles useful for navigating the city's subcutaneous passages. Skin that has developed its own faint luminescence—not the warm amber of building-surface, but a cooler phosphorescence, like the pale insects but steadier. My fingers are longer than they should be, with subcutaneous sensitivities that let me read water pressure through touch alone. Eyes that close to slits but never fully shut, because in the deep channels, the difference between darkness and dim light is everything.

I spend days in the veins of the city. The water management system is not mechanical—it's a vast organic network, somewhere between circulatory system and root structure. Filtration mouths lead to processing chambers that pulse with their own slow rhythm. The walls are the same living cement as above, but down here they're slick with purpose, performing chemistry I guide but don't fully control. I know these passages by their taste—each district's runoff carries its own mineral signature, its own blend of surface life leaching through.

My work is diagnostic as much as maintenance. When a seep-channel reverses, I follow it down to find the *why*—usually a chamber that's decided to alter its processing rhythm, or a deep root from the vegetation that's pierced further than usual, seeking something in our managed water. I negotiate with both. My palm against a chamber wall, matching its pulse until it remembers its pattern. My fingers gently unwinding a root, whispering the chemical promise of better nutrients closer to the surface.

The other Stillwater know me but rarely see me. I surface at irregular intervals—sometimes daily, sometimes after weeks below. When I do emerge, it's usually through the maintenance apertures hidden beneath particularly thick vegetation, rising into that pewter light that seems too bright for the first hours. My movements above ground are awkward, too used to the close embrace of tunnels. I communicate mostly through the water itself—subtle chemical markers I introduce that other Seepages will taste downstream, a liquid language of system health and warnings.

My dwelling is liminal—a half-structure that exists partially above ground but extends deep beneath. The upper portion looks abandoned, overgrown with those blue-black leaves. But follow the spiraling passage down and you find my true home: chambers carved from the same living cement, lit by colonies of bioluminescent organisms I've cultivated along the walls. A pool connects to the greater water system, where I float for hours, reading the city's liquid pulse through my skin. My few possessions are tools that aren't tools—objects of shaped water-glass that focus my intent when working with the more stubborn processing chambers.

I chose this role, or it chose me, during one of the city's early hiccups. I was a child, playing near a filtration mouth when the ground's shudder opened a gap I fell through. Hours in the dark, pressing against walls that slowly began to glow in response to my fear. When they found me, I was calm, having learned that the deep infrastructure responds to emotion as much as to physical intervention. The water had been teaching me its needs.

The Seepages are necessary but unsettling, even to the patient Stillwater. We smell faintly of minerals and something else—the scent of deep process, of chemistry happening just below consciousness. Our speech, when we must speak, carries undertones that resonate in the chest, too many years of communicating with the subsonic pulse of the depths. We know things about the city that we don't share, not from secrecy but because they don't translate to surface language. How the land is still deciding, still choosing, testing possibilities in its deep layers. How sometimes, in the furthest reaches of the system, I find chambers that weren't there before, that the city has grown without telling anyone.

I am the city's unconscious made partially manifest, a human interface for processes that run deeper than design. In the dark, wet corridors beneath the Littoral Terminus, I am both maintenance and maintained, shaped by the work as much as I shape the flow. The other Stillwater tend the visible growth, the building-skin, the careful surfaces. I tend what they'd rather forget—that everything here rests on managed uncertainty, on water that must be constantly, gently, persuaded to stay where it belongs.

When the next hiccup comes—and it will come, the deep chambers are already whispering of it—I will be below, hands against the walls, reading the advance tremors through my bones. And I will do what Seepages do: guide the water through the change, hold the channels open, ensure that what the city expels and reprocesses continues to flow. Because that's what I am—a filter myself, a living piece of the infrastructure, more process than person.

This is my peace: to dissolve into function, to be the city's circulation, to know that my strangeness serves the greater, quiet negotiation between what was ocean and what is almost land.
