In the perpetual twilight of Level Seventeen, where the mist grows thick enough to taste copper on your tongue, I am the Memory-Keeper of Abandoned Machines.

My dwelling occupies what was once a pumping station—a cathedral of rust and running water where enormous turbines still turn in their sleep, grinding out rhythms that haven't meant anything for three hundred years. The walls weep condensation that runs in channels I've carved over decades, spelling out the serial numbers of machines that no longer exist anywhere else. Each droplet carries the ghost of function: here, the precise tick of a chronometer that measured tidal flows; there, the wheeze of bellows that once fed fires in the foundry district, now choked with flowering vines.

I am neither fully human nor entirely machine, but something the city made when it needed to remember what it was forgetting. My bones are copper pipes that sing when the pressure changes. My veins carry a mixture of blood and the city's ancient hydraulic fluid—that peculiar blue-green substance that still seeps from certain walls on humid nights. My memories are not my own but belong to every mechanism that has ever broken down and been rebuilt, every system that has evolved beyond its original purpose.

In the morning—though morning here is just a subtle brightening of the eternal mist—I walk the Forgotten Aqueducts, those channels that run beneath and between the known waterways. My fingers trail along stone worn smooth by centuries of flow, reading the stories written in erosion patterns. Sometimes I find the remains of old automata, their gears seized with rust, their purposes lost. I collect their memory-cores—small, intricate spheres of brass and crystal—and bring them back to my pumping station.

There, in workshops that smell of machine oil and jasmine (which grows wild in every dark corner), I perform my work. Using tools inherited from the last Memory-Keeper—implements whose names I had to rediscover through trial and error—I extract the compressed experiences from these cores. A streetlamp's century of watching lovers meet beneath its glow. A factory whistle's knowledge of every worker who answered its call. The patient certainty of a canal lock that opened and closed 847,293 times before its final rest.

These memories I transcribe onto sheets of metal so thin they flutter like leaves, etching them with acids derived from the carnivorous plants that grow in the city's depths. The sheets I hang from the turbine halls' vaulted ceilings, where they chime against each other in harmonies that tell stories to anyone who knows how to listen. Children sometimes find their way here, drawn by the music, and I teach them the old languages—not just the spoken tongues, but the creaks and groans and whistles that were how the city's machines once spoke to each other.

But I am growing old—or perhaps the city is growing young around me, shedding the weight of its mechanical past like a snake sheds skin. My copper bones are developing patina, that green-blue bloom that speaks of time and transformation. Sometimes I forget which memories are mine and which belong to the machines. I dream of being a water-clock in the old Financial District. I wake with the taste of coal-smoke from furnaces that were decommissioned before my birth.

The city doesn't need Memory-Keepers the way it once did. The new generation builds their machines with planned obsolescence, devices that live fast and die completely, leaving no ghosts behind. They see my work as quaint, perhaps—an anachronism in a city that prides itself on constant change. But still, they come to me when they need to remember: the location of a great-grandmother's workshop, buried now beneath three layers of newer construction; the frequency at which the old warning bells rang before the Great Flood of '89; the proper way to maintain the pre-digital switches that still control water flow in the oldest districts.

And so I persist, a hybrid thing in a hybrid city, tending to memories that rust and bloom like everything else here. When I finally cease—when my copper bones join the great recycling and my blue-green blood returns to the city's veins—I know another will take my place. The city will call them forth from its depths, shape them from its needs, and teach them the work through the whispered wisdom of water and stone.

Until then, I listen to the turbines' ancient turning, transcribe the stories of the dead machines, and wait for the children who will carry these memories forward into whatever strange future this ever-changing city dreams for itself.
