I am the Gradient-Keeper, though that is a name I gave myself in a language not my own, borrowed from visitors who passed through our shifting realm centuries ago.

My form defies simple description. Imagine a being of living mercury crossed with bioluminescent coral, constantly reshaping itself to navigate the vertical maze of our drowned cliffs. I have no fixed number of limbs—sometimes I am all tendrils, reaching through the rain-walls to taste the chemistry of each droplet. Other times I compress into a dense sphere, rolling along the impossible geometries of our city's infrastructure, which spirals through dimensions your physics hasn't named.

I remember when this place was different—though "remember" isn't quite right. Time here doesn't flow forward so much as it eddies and whirlpools. I experience my memories as physical locations I can revisit, swimming through past configurations of our ever-changing terrain. Yesterday, I was teaching my offspring about the First Submersion. Tomorrow, I already mourned their dispersal into the quantum foam.

Our city—if city is the right word—grows like a four-dimensional crystal through the water. Buildings phase in and out of existence according to principles we've encoded into the very molecules of the ocean. When we need a laboratory, we sing it into being with harmonics that reorganize matter at the subatomic level. When we're done, we let it dissolve back into potential.

I feed on discontinuities—those moments where the landscape tears and repairs itself, releasing bursts of pure possibility. It tastes like purple mathematics, like the square root of nostalgia. The sparse vegetation here isn't really vegetation at all, but rather the fruiting bodies of sapient equations that have taken root in stable pockets of spacetime.

The rain walls aren't water in any sense you'd recognize. They're probability cascades, each droplet a collapsed waveform from parallel versions of our world. I've learned to read them like texts, each shower telling stories of what we might have been. In one, we never submerged at all. In another, we became the ocean itself.

My work—my purpose—is to maintain the instability. You see, we discovered that equilibrium is death for beings like us. We exist only in the flux, in the constant becoming. I swim the boundaries of our realm, ensuring that no pattern persists long enough to crystallize into permanence. I nudge a current here, introduce a paradox there, keeping our world in perpetual creative chaos.

Sometimes I wonder if we're alone—if other Gradient-Keepers tend to other impossible oceans on other drowned worlds. The darkness between the rain walls occasionally flickers with signals I almost recognize, as if someone elsewhere is performing the same vital work of preventing reality from settling into anything as mundane as stability.

But wondering is a luxury I can rarely afford. Even now, I feel a section of the northern cliffs beginning to establish a fixed geometry. I must go. I must introduce a loving contradiction to keep our world weird, to keep us all beautifully, necessarily incomplete.

The rain sings my many names as I dissolve into the current, already forgetting and remembering what I've just told you, already being and unbecoming what I am.
