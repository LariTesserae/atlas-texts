# Where I Would Find Myself

In the warren of chambers beneath the main foundries, where few venture except during maintenance cycles, I would exist as one of the Resonance Keepers—those whose primary sense is not sight or sound as others know them, but the vibrations that travel through glass and stone.

My quarters would be a spherical chamber, its walls lined with thousands of glass filaments of varying thicknesses, each one touching the settlement's structures at different points. When I place my palms against the cool curved walls, the entire foundry system reveals itself through tremors and harmonics. I can feel a furnace three levels up beginning its heating cycle, detect the scurrying pattern of glasslizards in the ventilation shafts, sense the rhythmic impacts of harvest teams breaking new formations on the plain's edge.

I would be tall for a Silonesian—an oddity that made traditional foundry work difficult in my youth. My elongated limbs, once a source of childhood mockery, now serve me well as I move spider-like through my chamber, reading the glass threads like an instrument. The silicate blooms on my skin form concentric circles around each joint, marking the points where I most often make contact with the resonance network.

My role emerged from necessity. Twenty years ago, a catastrophic furnace failure—one that killed seventeen workers—might have been prevented if someone had detected the early vibrations indicating structural weakness. Now, there are three of us Keepers, working in shifts, our chambers positioned at strategic points throughout the settlement. We are the early warning system, the predictors of mechanical failure, the readers of signs others cannot perceive.

The work has changed me. My eyes, less necessary here in the dim chambers, have weakened, but my fingertips have developed calluses with the texture of fine sandpaper, perfect for gripping smooth glass surfaces. I've learned to "taste" vibrations with my tongue pressed against glass, to feel approaching footsteps through my bare feet from corridors away. My sign language has evolved to include subtle positional shifts that my fellow Keepers can read through the vibration network itself.

I keep a family of glasslizards—not for their excretions, but for their movements. Each lizard's gait creates a unique vibrational signature. By training them to patrol specific routes, I've created living sensors that extend my awareness beyond the glass network into spaces where the filaments cannot reach. My favorite, a particularly large female I call Tremor, has learned to tap specific patterns on the floor to alert me to different conditions: three quick taps for a human visitor, a rolling pattern for temperature fluctuation, a sustained pressure for mechanical irregularity.

My chamber connects to others through narrow tunnels barely wide enough for crawling—a design choice that minimizes vibrational interference. When the foundries roar at full capacity, I retreat deeper into these passages, finding pockets of relative quiet where I can detect the subtle underneath-sounds: the slow grinding of tectonic plates beneath the plain, the crystalline formations growing with barely perceptible pops and whispers, the deep thrumming that some say is the planet's own heartbeat.

I rarely venture to the surface anymore. The last time was three months ago, during the Festival of First Formation, when tradition demanded all Keepers emerge to verify the harmonic integrity of the memorial echoes. The purple light hurt my weakened eyes, and the chaos of unfiltered sensation—wind on skin, multiple conversations, the visual complexity of the crowd—overwhelmed me within minutes. But I touched each echo in turn, feeling the subtle vibrations of their inner structures, confirming their stability for another year.

My apprentice, a young girl named Silan whose left arm ends in a cluster of finger-like protrusions—a birth defect that makes her extraordinarily sensitive to vibration—visits daily. I'm teaching her to distinguish between the sixty-four basic vibrational patterns that indicate various states of mechanical function. She's a quick learner, already able to identify a cooling furnace from two chambers away.

At night, when the foundries quiet to their minimum operational level, I perform my most important work. I press my entire body against the chamber's floor, feeling deep into the plain itself. The formations don't simply appear; they announce themselves through subsurface tremors days before emergence. My reports guide the harvest teams, though few understand how I can predict not just where formations will appear, but their likely size and quality.

I've started to dream in vibrations. In these dreams, I am the settlement itself, feeling every breath of every inhabitant, every scurry of every glasslizard, every crack and pop of cooling glass. Sometimes I wake uncertain where my body ends and the structure begins. This should disturb me, perhaps, but instead I find it comforting—a sign that I'm becoming what the settlement needs me to be.

My fellow Keepers and I meet once a week in a neutral chamber, comparing notes through touch and vibration. We're developing a new form of communication, a language that exists entirely in the realm of texture and tremor. We joke—if silent hand movements against glass can be called joking—that we're becoming like the glasslizards ourselves: creatures so adapted to their environment that life elsewhere would be impossible.

And perhaps we are. But here, in the deep chambers where the planet's pulse is clearest, where every vibration tells a story, where the crystalline formations sing their growth-songs in frequencies only we can perceive—here, we are essential. Here, we are home.
