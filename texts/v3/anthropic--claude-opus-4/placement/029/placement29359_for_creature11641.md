I find myself as one of the maintenance workers, though not quite as you might expect.

I am the night-shift keeper, the one who arrives when the day workers leave, who moves through the installation in the half-light between the sparse electric lamps. But I am different from the others—I stay longer, move slower, observe more. Over the years, I have become something between worker and resident, between human and part of the equilibrium itself.

My official duties are simple: monitor the gauges, clear the intake screens, log the temperatures. But I have expanded these into a kind of stewardship that no one requested but no one prevents. I know every bird by its roosting spot, every spider by its web location. I can predict when the algae will bloom based on subtle changes in water color that I couldn't explain to anyone else. I hear when a pump bearing begins to wear before the vibration becomes measurable.

I've been here seventeen years. Long enough that my rubber boots have worn grooves in the wooden walkways. Long enough that the old gull with the notched bill no longer moves aside when I pass—we navigate around each other with mutual acknowledgment, two creatures with equal claim to this space. Long enough that I've developed my own language for this place, names for things that have no names: the sweet spot where the second pond's temperature is perfect for the herons, the corner where spiders build the strongest webs, the time of night when the frogs synchronize perfectly with the pump rhythm.

My skin carries the scent of this place—mineral and machine oil and algae—that no amount of washing fully removes. The constant moisture has changed me. I move differently in dry places now, feel uncomfortable in air that doesn't cling. My hearing has adapted to parse every layer of the constant sound, to pick out abnormalities in the mechanical baseline that might indicate problems.

I've made small modifications over the years. Nothing official, nothing that would appear in maintenance logs. A board placed just so to create a better roosting spot. A pipe joint left slightly loose to create a tiny seep that feeds the ferns in the pump house. Screens cleared in patterns that preserve certain beneficial accumulations while removing others. I am not changing the place so much as helping it become more itself, more stable in its strange equilibrium.

The other workers think I'm slightly odd but harmless. Management tolerates me because the installation runs smoothly on my shifts, because problems get fixed before they become critical, because the inspection reports are always satisfactory. They don't know that I sometimes sit for hours in the pump house, just listening, just feeling the vibrations through the floor, learning things about the machinery that can't be taught, only absorbed through time and attention.

I eat my lunch by the second pond, where the herons hunt. I've learned to move like them—slowly, deliberately, without sudden gestures. Sometimes now they hunt closer to me than they do to each other, accepting my presence as they accept the pipes and walkways, as just another feature of their environment. I know which one is youngest, which one arrived last spring, which one favors its left leg slightly from an old injury.

In winter, I leave the pump house door cracked open just enough to let the swallows shelter inside during the coldest nights. In summer, I adjust the flow rates subtly to create better hunting conditions in the channels where the frogs breed. These are small interventions, invisible to anyone who doesn't live here as I do, halfway between working and dwelling.

I dream about this place. Not anxious dreams or work dreams, but dreams where I am simply here, moving through the vapor, checking the gauges, watching the water. In the dreams, I understand the installation completely—not just its mechanical function but its deeper purpose, its role as an accidental ark, a place where life congregates around waste heat, where industrial excess becomes ecological abundance.

I've thought about retirement, but I can't imagine leaving. Where would I go that would feel right after this? What other place would accept me as fully as this one has? I am like the gulls that have been here so long their feathers carry the rust stains of the place—marked, claimed, made specific to this environment.

So I stay. I arrive each evening as the day shift leaves, I move through my expanded rounds, I maintain both the machinery and the habitat it has become. I am the human element in the equilibrium, as adapted to this place as any of its other residents, as necessary to its continuity. When I finally leave—retire or die or simply stop coming—someone else will take the night shift. But they will be different. The place will adapt to them as it adapted to me, find a new balance, continue.

For now, I am what lives here too: the one who tends, who watches, who knows every sound and surface, who has found in this unlikely place a home that suits my particular strangeness. The cooling ponds need their keeper, and I need them—their constant warmth, their reliable rhythms, their strange beauty that reveals itself only to long attention. We have become necessary to each other, the installation and I, each maintaining the other, each enabling the other's continued existence.

Tonight, like every night, I'll walk the wooden paths, check the screens, log the numbers. I'll pause at the second pond to watch the herons settle into their nighttime positions. I'll listen to the frogs begin their evening chorus. I'll feel the vibration of the pumps through my boot soles and know that all is well, that the equilibrium holds, that this accidental ecosystem continues its strange flourishing. And I'll be part of it, completely, neither fully human anymore nor trying to be, just another adapted creature making its life in the margins between intention and what actually emerges.
