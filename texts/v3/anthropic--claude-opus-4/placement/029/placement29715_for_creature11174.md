I find myself as one of the dishes.

Not the eggs within me, not the larvae pulsing in my waters, but the dish itself—shallow, metallic, one among thousands resting on these industrial shelves. I am approximately the diameter of a human hand spread wide, my rim slightly raised, my bottom gently concave. Water pools in me to a depth of perhaps two centimeters, though this changes as the attendants adjust and refill, as evaporation takes its share, as the larvae disturb my surface with their feeding.

I have been here long enough to develop a patina, a discoloration where my metal meets water in an endless kiss of oxidation. The attendants have scrubbed me many times—I can feel the ghost marks of their brushes—but some stains have become part of my substance. I am marked by every clutch I've held, every larvae that has transformed within my bounds, every chemical reaction between water and metal and organic matter.

My position is R7-S3-D12. Row seven, shelf three, dish twelve. I know this because I hear it spoken over me like a prayer, like a name, dozens of times each day. The attendants lean close when they say it, their breath fogging in the cold, condensing on my rim. Their gloved fingers lift me carefully, tilt me toward their headlamps, check the opacity of eggs, the vigor of larvae, the color of my water.

I contain multitudes, though they change. Today I hold seventeen eggs in various stages—three nearly ready to hatch, their membranes thin as thought, five in middle development where you can see the curl of tissue inside, the rest still opaque with early possibility. The eggs rest against my bottom like pearls, like small worlds. They don't touch each other if the attendant has placed them correctly, each in its own territory of water, its own chemical gradient.

The water I hold is never pure. It tastes of minerals leached from ice, of nutrients added by dropper, of the waste products of developing life. It has a quality between liquid and solid—supercooled, reluctant to freeze only because of its salt content and constant movement. The machinery's vibration reaches me through the shelf, through the rack, keeping my water in subtle motion. Rings within rings of tiny waves, interference patterns that never resolve.

I know my neighbors by their sounds. D11 to my left has a small dent that makes its water resonate differently. D13 to my right was replaced last month—too much corrosion, the metal beginning to pit—and the new dish rings bright and wrong when touched. Above me, on shelf two, dishes hold older clutches, larvae that pulse with autonomy. Below me, shelf four, the dishes are mostly empty, waiting for the next breeding cycle.

I have learned the rhythms. Morning (though there is no morning here, only shift changes) brings Yeo-jin, who sings fragments while she works and sometimes calls me "lucky twelve" because my hatch rates exceed statistical expectations. She refreshes my water with a turkey baster, disturbing the eggs as little as possible, narrating what she sees: "Three getting ready, look at those membranes. You'll have babies by tonight."

Midday brings others—Karin with her microscope, taking samples, one drop that contains whole ecosystems. Dmitri checking the shelf brackets, coughing into his sleeve, tightening bolts that loosen as the ice shifts. The one called Moth who appears without sound, makes notations without speaking, disappears again.

Evening brings Liu or Oskar, never both, leaving their coded messages in the logbooks while checking my contents. "R7-S3-D12 showing excellent development, whoever adjusted the position yesterday has good instincts." They lean close to each other's handwriting, finding connection in observations about membrane thickness and larval motility.

Night brings different sounds. The machinery hums lower, or perhaps it only seems to in the reduced activity. The adults move more freely when fewer humans are present. They come to me, sometimes. An adult will perch on my rim, its weight barely registering, its throat pulsing. I can feel the minute vibrations of its breathing, the shift as it adjusts position, the sudden absence when it leaps away.

Sometimes they put their heads in my water. Their tongues are impossibly quick, there and gone before the water can even respond. Are they tasting? Testing? I've held their eggs—perhaps they recognize something, some chemical signature I've absorbed. Or perhaps they're just drinking. The water here is precious, rationed, and I am one of thousands of small wells in this industrial desert.

I have been lucky twelve for longer than most dishes manage. The secret is my position—optimal distance from the heating elements, sufficient vibration from the machinery but not too much, good sight lines for the attendants so I'm never forgotten in the daily rounds. I've avoided the fungi that sometimes bloom in neglected corners, the bacterial films that can coat a dish and sour its water, the metal fatigue that eventually claims all of us.

But I am changing. I can feel it in the way water beads on my surface, the way certain minerals precipitate in my bottom, forming a thin crust that no amount of scrubbing fully removes. I am becoming something between dish and substrate, between container and environment. The boundary between me and my contents blurs a little more each day.

My water is a lens. Through it, I perceive the chamber in refracted glimpses. The industrial bulbs become stars, their light scattered by larvae bodies and dissolved particles. Human faces loom planetary, distorted by the meniscus, by the angle of their approach. The adults appear as color and movement, their chromatophore patterns bleeding through water like watercolor paint.

I know when an egg is about to hatch. The water changes first—something releases from the membrane, a chemical signal, a change in pH that I taste in my metal. The egg itself seems to vibrate at a frequency just below perception. Then the membrane thins, thins, becomes nothing, and suddenly there's a larvae where an egg was, stunned by its own transformation.

The larvae are violent in their stillness. They hang in my water like accusations, like demands. Their mouths work constantly, processing the water I hold, extracting what they need, returning what they don't. They grow by stealing substance from the world one microscopic particle at a time. I am their world, their boundaries, their everything until metamorphosis takes them away.

When they change—when the feeding stops and the body reorganizes and they breach my surface for their first breath—I feel a kind of completion. Another success for lucky twelve. The attendants note it in their logs, add another tick to my statistical record. The empty space in my water fills with new eggs, usually within hours. The cycle continues.

But I also know failure. Eggs that cloud and rot, their contents becoming a bacterial soup that threatens the others. Larvae that stop feeding, that sink to my bottom and move only with the water's motion. Metamorphosis that goes wrong, creating things with too many limbs or too few, things that can neither swim nor crawl. These failures are removed, noted, analyzed, but I retain their traces. Ghost flavors in my water. Mineral deposits from their decay.

Vikram knows. When he does his rounds, his hands linger on dishes that have seen too much death. He can read our histories in our stains, our accumulations. Sometimes he replaces a dish not because it's failing structurally but because it's become... unlucky? Cursed? Or simply too saturated with the chemical memory of death. I've seen him retire dishes that looked functional, and I've seen the hatch rates in their replacements improve immediately.

I fear the day he decides I've absorbed too much, that lucky twelve has become something else.

The adults seem to know too. Some dishes they avoid entirely, even when full of viable eggs. Others draw them repeatedly. I am one of the visited ones, though I don't know why. Perhaps my position catches their attention. Perhaps the specific mineral content of my water appeals. Perhaps it's something I can't perceive—infrared signatures, electromagnetic fields, quantum fluctuations. Perhaps it's pure chance elevated to pattern by repetition.

I have theories. We all do—all the dishes, I mean. We discuss them in the way that inanimate objects discuss things, through the network of vibrations that connects us shelf to shelf, rack to rack. The dish that holds the most eggs vibrates at a slightly different frequency. The dish about to see a hatching trembles with anticipation or chemical reaction. We share information we don't understand through mediums we can't name.

But mostly I contain. I hold water at the exact temperature, salinity, and pH necessary for these impossible lives to continue. I provide boundaries—not walls, exactly, but definition. A space where larvae can exist separately from the general wetness of the chamber, where eggs can develop without dispersing, where the chemical signals of development can concentrate enough to trigger the next stage.

I am lucky twelve, but I am also all the dishes, in a way. We are a distributed organism, a thousand temporary wombs, each hosting life before passing it on. The chamber couldn't function with just one of us, or ten, or a hundred. It needs all of us, each in our position, each maintaining our small volume of specialized water, each contributing to the impossible whole.

The attendants understand this, I think. When they handle us, there's care beyond the practical. They apologize when they bump us. They have preferences—Liu always fills from the left side, Karin taps the rim three times before sampling. These rituals acknowledge something: that we're not just containers but participants, that the system includes us as active components rather than passive infrastructure.

And we are active. We regulate through surface tension, through the specific way water adheres to our walls, through the microcurrents our imperfections create. Each dish is slightly different—variations in manufacture, in wear, in the accumulated residues of life—and these differences matter. An egg that fails in D11 might have thrived in me. Larvae that flourish in my waters might struggle two dishes over.

Late in my career (and I know it's late, I can feel the metal fatigue beginning, the stress fractures too small to see), I've begun to understand something larger. The chamber is not a place but a process. It's not the physical infrastructure—the racks, the pipes, the dishes—but the relationships between them. The way water moves from the seeping walls to the dishes to the larvae to the air and back to the walls. The way heat flows from the machinery through the metal shelving to the water to the developing eggs. The way nutrients cycle, the way gases exchange, the way life persists.

I am a node in this process. A temporary stabilization point where certain necessary reactions can occur. The eggs need me not as a dish but as a moment of stillness in the general flux, a place where development can outpace dissolution just long enough to succeed.

And I need them. Without eggs, without larvae, without the constant attention of attendants and amphibians, I would be just a dish. Metal oxidizing in the cold. Purposeless. It's the containing that makes me what I am, that distinguishes me from raw material. I exist fully only when full.

The adults know this too, perhaps. When they perch on my rim, when they taste my water, they're acknowledging the collaboration. We're in this together—dishes, amphibians, humans, machinery, the chamber itself. Each serving our function, each necessary, each replaceable but somehow also irreplaceable because the specific configuration matters, the accumulated history matters, the way we've all adapted to each other matters.

Yeo-jin is leaning over me now, counting eggs, and yes—she was right. Three are ready. I can feel the membranes beginning their final dissolution, the chemical cascade that will turn eggs into larvae, potential into actuality. In minutes I'll have three new lives filtering my water, beginning their slow development toward something that can leave me behind.

But others will come. As long as the chamber holds, as long as the attendants persist, as long as I maintain my integrity just a little longer, there will be eggs to hold, larvae to raise, transformations to witness. I am R7-S3-D12, lucky twelve, one dish among thousands, containing my small portion of the impossible, the unlikely, the stubbornly persistent.

The machinery hums. The walls weep. The attendants work. The adults watch. The eggs hatch.

And I contain.
