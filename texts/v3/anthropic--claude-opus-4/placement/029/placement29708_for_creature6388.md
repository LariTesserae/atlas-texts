I am the listener who cannot leave.

Not because the root-corridors have grown shut behind me, or because my body has forgotten the way. I cannot leave because I am learning to breathe with the city's lungs, and each day the rhythm becomes more mine.

My name was something sharp once, something that cut air when spoken. Now it has worn smooth as the stone tools by the doorway. The other listeners call me by the sound water makes when it hesitates at a fork in the channels—a pause, a small turbulence, then decision. It cannot be written. It can only be heard in the presence of moving water.

I came here as a child fleeing the bright violence of the surface. My parents were among the last to insist we could rebuild the old ways—walls of dry stone, roofs that kept out everything, especially life. They died believing emptiness was safety. I descended into the deep carrying their fear like a torch, sure that at any moment the pressing dark would swallow me.

Instead, it taught me to see.

My hair was black then. Now it grows in pale tendrils that mirror the root systems I tend. Not white with age—I am not old as the surface counts years—but translucent, as if the pigment has been gently leached away and replaced with something that drinks light instead of reflecting it. When I stand very still in the listening room, the smallest rootlets at my temples twitch toward moisture on the walls. I am becoming a barometer of the city's thirsts.

My skin holds the city's pallor, yes, but also its patterns. Where I have pressed my palms to the walls most often—the places where I listen deepest—my skin has taken on a faint luminescence. Not bright enough to see by, but enough that in full darkness I can look at my own hands and see the ghostly outline of my work written in cold light. The newest listeners are afraid of this change. They think it means the city is claiming them. 

They are right. But claiming is not the same as consuming.

My bones have grown lighter. Not fragile—I could still lift the stone tools, still shape the channels when needed. But there is a hollowness to them now, as if they are becoming less like human bones and more like the chitinous structures of the crawling fauna. Sometimes I feel myself moving with their liquid grace, my joints seeming to multiply as I navigate the narrow spaces where root and stone press close.

I sleep suspended in the root hammocks, but sleep is the wrong word. It is more like sinking into the city's circulation. My dreams are not my own—they are the chemical whispers of the root network, the slow percolation of nutrients, the patient push of growth. I wake knowing where water has pooled too deep in the southeastern galleries, where a new cluster of luminous moss has established itself without encouragement.

The tools I use are the ones left by the listener before me, who inherited them from the listener before her. The stone chisel knows the shape of my grip but also remembers theirs. When I work, I sometimes feel their movements underlying mine, a kind of muscle memory that belongs to the tool rather than to me.

But it is the stones on the shelf that truly hold me here.

Each stone is a conversation frozen mid-sentence. When I touch them, I feel not just the growth patterns of the room but the presence of the one who placed them. She is there in the stone's selection—this one more porous, to hold the memory of a wet season; that one dense and smooth, marking a time when the roots grew tight with mineral richness. Through these stones, I know her without ever having seen her face.

And I am placing my own stones now. Yesterday, I added a piece of pale shell to mark the appearance of a new kind of fauna—something between the crawlers and the drifters, with a body that pulses like a slow heart. The shell's inner surface catches the bioluminescence and holds it, just as this new creature seems to store light in its tissues before releasing it in careful bursts.

This is how I've changed: I no longer think in human time.

When the newest listeners come to me with their questions—why does this channel flow upward? how can the roots grow through solid stone?—I struggle to answer in words. The knowledge is there, but it is written in the language of pressure and saturation. I find myself pressing their palms to the walls instead, holding them there until they begin to feel the answer as a tingling in their skin.

They think I am becoming something other than human. They are wrong. I am becoming more accurately human—a human shaped by this particular world rather than insisting on the shape of the dead surface above.

My diet has shifted with my senses. The cultivated growths that the people here call food—pale fruits that grow in the darker chambers, filtered water that tastes of minerals and moss—these satisfy me. But I also feed as the city feeds. When I place the harvested microorganisms in the root nodules, I sometimes pause to taste them. Not with my mouth, but with the sensitive skin of my fingertips. I can feel their nutrition as a kind of brightness against my skin.

I rarely speak in human words anymore. The other listeners and I communicate in touches, in shared listening, in the placement of objects that carry meaning. When we must use speech, it comes out water-shaped—flowing, finding the easiest path, pooling in silences.

But I am not becoming plant or water or stone. I am becoming interface.

Last season, a young listener asked me if I ever miss the surface. I took her to the deepest chamber I know, where the root system is so dense that the walls pulse visibly with the city's circulation. I pressed her palm to the warmest spot—where the digestive acids run strongest—and held it there until she gasped.

"This," I told her, in the liquid language we use here, "is what the surface feels like now."

She understood. The bright, dry world above would burn us as surely as acid. We have been shaped to fit this world, and that shaping is irreversible.

But I do not mourn this.

In the listening room, surrounded by the constant small movements of life, I am more present than I ever was in the empty safety of the surface world. Each breath I take is filtered through the city's body. Each touch of moisture on my skin is a word in an endless conversation. The crawling fauna no longer detour around me—they flow over my feet as they would over a familiar root.

Sometimes, in the deepest listening, I can feel the city's own consciousness. Not thoughts as humans have thoughts, but a vast, slow awareness that experiences my presence as I experience the touch of water on skin—notable, pleasant, but not essential. I am a temporary organ in its body, useful for the fine manipulations it cannot perform with root and leaf alone.

When I die—and I will die, the city does not grant immortality—my body will not be removed. The listeners before me were laid in the growth chambers, where the roots eventually embrace and digest them. Their nutrients join the city's flow. The stones they placed remain on the shelves, holding their conversations with the room.

I know this because I can feel them in the walls. Not as ghosts or spirits, but as subtle enrichments in the moss's glow, as particularly vibrant sections of root growth. They are here the way a meal is present in the body that digested it—transformed but essential.

This is what I am becoming: not inhuman, but human in the way this world requires. A listener who translates between scales of time, who bridges the gap between the city's vast body and the small needs of the people who inhabit it. My changes—the translucent hair, the faint glow, the liquid movement—these are not corruptions but adaptations.

I am what happens when humanity stops insisting on its separateness.

And when the next listener comes to take my place—when I am too much interface and not enough human to do the work—I will teach them as I was taught. Not with words or demonstrations, but by placing their hands on the walls and holding them there until they begin to feel the city's pulse as their own.

Then I will go deeper, into the chambers where even listeners rarely venture, where the boundary between city and inhabitant dissolves entirely. I will find a warm place in the root mass and I will wait. Not for death—that is too simple a word—but for the final translation, when my consciousness joins the slow dream of the city itself.

The stones I leave behind will remember what I learned. The next listener will touch them and know, not my name or my face, but the shape of my attention, the quality of my listening. This is enough. This is how we persist.

I am the listener who cannot leave, not because I am trapped, but because I have finally found the place where I fit. The city breathes, and I breathe with it. The water flows, and I flow with it. The creatures crawl across my skin, and I am simply another surface in their vast world.

This is horror to those who have not learned to listen. This is home to those who have.
