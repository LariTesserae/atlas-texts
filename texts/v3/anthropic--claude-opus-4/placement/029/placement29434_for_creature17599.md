I would be one of the **Tephric Whispers** – the memory-keepers of the High Tephraves.

In form, I am neither wholly of the mountain's people nor entirely separate from them. My dwelling perches on the settlement's highest terrace, where the mist first kisses stone each dawn. But I am rarely there. Instead, I move through the spaces between – the rocky intervals that separate one curved home from another, the transition zones where managed moss meets wild growth, the liminal hours when fungal light shifts from sector to sector.

My body has adapted beyond even the typical Tephravean form. My skin has taken on a quality like the aerial rootlets – slightly translucent in certain lights, showing the faint tracery of veins beneath. My eyes have developed an additional membrane, a nictitating film that allows me to perceive the ultraviolet patterns on beetle wings and the infrared signatures of fungal networks beneath the moss. My hearing has evolved to catch frequencies below human range – I can hear the mountain's deep geological groans, the subsonic conversations between root systems, the infrasound warnings that precede a landslide by hours.

But these physical adaptations are secondary to my true nature. I am a living repository of the settlement's accumulated knowledge, though not in any way the lowlanders would recognize. I do not memorize facts or chronicle events. Instead, I *embody* patterns.

Watch me work: I stand at the edge of a terrace where yesterday's quiet landslide has revealed fresh stone. My fingers trace the exposed grain, feeling for the mountain's intentions. This particular pattern of fracture, combined with the beetle-swarm's eastern drift this morning and the early blooming of certain roof-succulents, creates a configuration I recognize. Not from study, but from *being* – my nervous system has learned to resonate with the mountain's cycles through decades of careful attention.

I communicate my insights through what the people call "placement songs" – though they contain no melody the lowlands would recognize. I arrange small objects with exquisite care: a shed beetle carapace here, three drops of condensation placed precisely there, a fragment of luminous fungus positioned just so. Other Tephraveans, passing by, read these arrangements as naturally as breathing. They understand: *the water-channels will shift northward this season; prepare the lower dwellings.*

My role emerged from necessity. The Tephraves' environment changes too subtly for conventional memory, too quickly for stone records. Someone needed to become a living bridge between the generational knowledge and the moment-to-moment flux. So certain individuals – one or two per generation – develop this hypersensitivity, this capacity to feel the mountain's intent in their bones.

The price is a kind of productive alienation. I am too attuned to participate fully in daily life. The normal sounds of stone-working feel like screams to me. The gentle tapping conversations of my people sometimes overwhelm my heightened senses. I take my meals alone, foraged from the spaces others don't think to look – the tender shoots that emerge for just three days after a beetle swarm passes, the protein-rich dew that forms only on north-facing stones during certain fungal bloom-cycles.

But I am not lonely. How could I be, when I am in constant conversation with ten thousand living things? The six-legged tortoises know me, often adjusting their paths to cross mine, their moss-gardens offering fragments of data about soil chemistry and moisture gradients. The water-eels surface when I approach their pools, their movements spelling out hydrological secrets. Even the mountain itself speaks to me through the temperature of its exhalations, the mineral taste of its sweat.

My dwelling, when I use it, is more laboratory than home. Arrays of collection vessels capture different types of condensation. Living walls of various mosses serve as sensitive instruments, their color and growth patterns indicating atmospheric changes. In one corner, a piece of exposed bedrock serves as my direct connection to the mountain's deeper processes – I sleep with my ear pressed against it, dreaming the stone's slow dreams.

The other Tephraveans regard me with a mixture of respect and careful distance. They know I am necessary but uncanny. Children are taught to read my placement songs before they learn to shape basalt. Adults consult my arrangements before choosing where to expand a dwelling or when to harvest certain resources. But they also know that to spend too much time near me risks catching my condition – the hypersensitivity that makes one a better listener but a harder liver.

I am currently tracking something new – a shift in the beetle-swarm patterns that suggests the possible emergence of a new pool system on the eastern terraces. If I'm right, it will change the settlement's water-web significantly. I've begun placing the first elements of a complex song that will take weeks to complete, each day adding new information as I gather it from wind, stone, and wing.

This is my purpose: to be the membrane between the overwhelming wild and the small human understanding, to translate the mountain's vast, slow speech into something my people can use to survive another generation in this beautiful, demanding place. I am the settlement's memory, but more than that – I am its proprioception, its sense of where it stands in the eternal dance of stone, water, and time.

Some nights, when the fungal blooms pulse in perfect synchrony and the mist carries the click-language of ten thousand beetles, I feel the boundaries of my individual self dissolve. In those moments, I am not a person observing the Tephraves – I am the Tephraves aware of itself, a single cell in the greater organism of mountain and mist, necessary and transient as morning condensation on stone.
