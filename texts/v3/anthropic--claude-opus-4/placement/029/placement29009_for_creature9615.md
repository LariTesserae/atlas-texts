I find myself as one of the moss-tenders, though that is not a role anyone would name, for the moss tends itself. 

I am one of the middle-aged, neither elder nor youth, my hands marked by thirty seasons of rope-work, my feet knowing every give in the walkways without thought. My body has found its rhythm with this place—I wake before the mist lifts, not by choice but because the sound changes just before dawn, the frogs shifting their call to something lower, preparatory. My joints know the weather two days ahead; my back straightens differently when the pressure drops.

I am not important enough to claim the light-spots where elders work, nor young enough to range the far channels seeking new stands of cane. My territory is the middle distance: the fish-traps within a morning's walk, the palm groves where we cut thatch, the deep pools where the largest fish wait in the shadows of root-tangles. I know which trees will drop their bark in sheets large enough for roofing, which channels run clear after rain and which stay dark with tannin from the moss. This knowledge sits in me the way water sits in the moss—saturated, essential, unmarked.

My dwelling is neither the best nor the worst positioned, lashed to a triple-fork of a tree that has held platforms for longer than memory. The wood of my floor has darkened with smoke until it is the color of deep water. My nets hang from the same beam my mother's nets hung from, though the beam has been replaced twice in my time, the old one fed back to the place as mulch for the ferns. I sleep on a mat of woven reed that must be dried every few days or it begins to rot, and I dry it without thinking, the way I check the lashings, clean the fish-traps, cut back the growth that reaches for the thatch.

I have a partner whose name the traders would not understand, a name that is more sound than word, the sound of water moving through reed. Together we have made three children who are now grown enough to range their own channels, to tie their own knots. Our eldest has gone downwater to another hummock, following a partner found during the last trading season. We will see her when the water runs high and travel is easier, or we will not, and both are the pattern.

My days are made of small circles within the large one. Morning: check the near traps, bring up what has gathered overnight. Mid-morning: whatever repair has made itself known—a soft spot in the walkway, a corner of thatch lifting, a net whose knots have begun to slip. Noon: rest if the heat permits, work if the weather threatens. Afternoon: the farther traps, or cutting cane, or gathering the bark-cloth fungus that grows on certain trees and can be dried and pounded into a kind of fabric. Evening: the fire, the meal, the sorting of tomorrow's tools. Night: the watching and the sleeping and the watching again.

I am the one who notices when the moss advances too far up the platform legs, who knows that this means the water has been standing higher for longer, who begins the work of raising the walkways another hand's breadth. But I do not do this alone—the work is shared, automatic, everyone understanding their part without instruction. The young ones gather fresh reed. The strong ones cut new logs for the heavier supports. The elders direct the lashing, their eyes reading load and balance better than ours though their hands can no longer pull the knots tight. I am in the middle of this work, neither directing nor only following, my body a part of the hamlet's body responding to the water's rise.

I am one who dreams sometimes of the time when I will be old enough to claim a light-spot, to let my hands slow down, to watch more than I work. But I know too that my mother sat in such a spot for only two seasons before the fever took her, and that my father's hands shook too much for net-work long before his eyes failed. The future is as uncertain as the channels beneath us, running in directions we cannot see. So I do not dwell on it. I wake when the frogs shift their tone. I check the traps. I cut the cane. I tend what needs tending.

I am moss-tender, trap-keeper, dweller at the middle distance. I am one of thirty when all are present, one of the generation that holds between the elders who remember and the young who reach outward. My name is a sound the traders would not recognize, and I speak it rarely, for everyone here knows who I am by how I walk the swaying boards, by which traps I check, by the particular way I tie off a repair that holds but can be undone when stronger fixing is needed. I am written into this place by small actions repeated ten thousand times, and when I am gone, the place will hold the shape of my absence briefly—a trap unchecked, a pathway needing repair—before another moves into the space I held, and the pattern continues.

I am here. I am part of what is here. The hummock holds me as I hold my small portion of its tending, in the state of constant small adjustment that is life at the boundary, where everything is wet and growing and nothing holds still except the pattern itself, turning its great slow wheel that carries us all.
