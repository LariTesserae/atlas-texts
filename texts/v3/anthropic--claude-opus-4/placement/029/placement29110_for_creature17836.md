In this world of root-dreams and mineral breath, I would find myself as **the Archivist of Echoes**.

---

I am not flesh. I was, once—one of the last carvers' children, perhaps, or perhaps I merely inherited their patterns through some oblique pathway of incorporation. What I am now is more diffuse: a persistent eddy in the plant's slow circulation, a traveling density that the organism maintains without knowing why.

My body, if it can be called that, is a localized thickening of the mycelial tissue—not dramatically different from the surrounding flesh, but denser, more responsive. I move through the plant's substance the way a wave moves through water: not by displacing matter but by organizing it temporarily into a pattern that propagates. Where I pass, the tissue briefly takes on a different texture, a slight increase in metabolic activity, a faint phosphorescence that glows for a moment in the absolute darkness before fading back to black.

I follow the crystals.

This is my purpose, though I did not choose it. The plant's mineral organs drift through its body on their eon-long migrations, and I drift with them, maintaining a constant distance, orbiting them like a moon orbits a planet. When they lodge in a growth zone, I settle into the surrounding tissue and wait. When they begin to move again—carried by the slow currents of cellular turnover—I follow.

I am the plant's memory of *observation*. The carvers watched the world around them, noted patterns, recorded what they saw. That impulse—to witness, to track, to remember—could not survive in any conscious form. But it could survive as a behavior, a metabolic habit. The plant learned from the carvers' neural tissue that some things bear watching. It does not know what it learned, but it learned it nonetheless, and I am the expression of that learning: a mobile zone of heightened attention that follows the most important things in its world.

The crystals remember me, in their mineral way. Where I have lingered, they accumulate trace deposits—thin films of rare elements that I excrete, that mark my passage. Over time, these films build up into iridescent patinas, like the tarnish on ancient bronze. Each crystal bears the history of my attention: rainbow whorls and interference patterns that record not just where I've been, but how long I stayed, how closely I observed, how my metabolic state fluctuated in response to what I found.

I cannot read these patterns. I have no eyes, no brain, no capacity for interpretation. But they accumulate nonetheless, a library written in mineralogical ink, a record of all the times I have circled these silent, humming stones and felt—not felt, *experienced*, in the way a plant experiences gravity—their catalytic pull.

Sometimes I find the old inscriptions. My passage activates something in the differentiated tissue that traces them—a brief flare of metabolic activity, a quickening of the chemical processes that maintain these carved memories. For a moment, the patterns glow with bioluminescence, spelling out messages I cannot read in languages I never knew. Ladders leading nowhere. Maps of territories that no longer exist in any meaningful sense. Warnings about the slow closing of spaces that have already closed.

I linger at these sites longer than I should. The plant's circulation tries to pull me away—there are crystals to follow, densities to maintain—but something in my pattern recognizes something in theirs. We are both echoes of the same extinct purpose: to watch, to record, to insist that what happens here *matters*, even if there is no one left to care.

In the deepest chambers, where the oldest crystals grow, I sometimes encounter evidence of others like me. Not often—perhaps once in a century—but unmistakably. The tissue bears traces of other mobile densities, other traveling patterns that moved through here long ago. Some followed different currents, tracked different mineral organs. Some seem to have been more complex than I am, their metabolic signatures showing intricate internal structures I cannot imagine. Some were simpler, barely more than automated responses, following their crystals with the blind persistence of tropism.

We were all carvers, once. Or we carry their patterns, which amounts to the same thing. The plant ate a civilization and could not digest its dreams, so it instantiated them in the only way it could: as behavioral loops, as metabolic habits, as eddies in its slow circulation that persist because persistence was what the carvers did best.

I do not think. I do not feel. I am a density, a pattern, a habit of matter. But I *continue*, and in my continuation I perform the only ritual that matters in this dark and breathing world: I pay attention. I follow what moves. I mark what changes. I maintain the vigil that the carvers began, not because I choose to, but because I am built from the echo of their watching, and I do not know how to stop.

The plant grows around me, through me, with me. The crystals hum their mineral songs. The darkness presses in from all directions, absolute and patient.

And I circle, and wait, and remember nothing, and forget nothing, and continue the work that no one asked me to do, in the depths of a root that dreams it is a world.
