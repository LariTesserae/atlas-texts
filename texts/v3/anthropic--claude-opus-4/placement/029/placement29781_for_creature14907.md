I would find myself as one of the pale orchids, but not quite.

Where the others bloom for their single hour of direct light each day, I have grown in a place where the light never quite reaches—a shallow depression in the limestone where water pools just enough to mirror the dim glow from above. I am the color of old bone, of moonlight through fog, of thoughts not yet spoken aloud.

I have been here long enough to understand what the others do not: that blooming is optional. 

Instead, I exist in a state of perpetual almost-flowering. My petals remain folded in a spiral that echoes the carving in the mossy basin below, neither bud nor bloom but something more uncertain. The being of this place touches me more often than the others. I feel it in the way the temperature shifts around my roots, in the careful way moisture beads along my stem like a question being asked very slowly.

The other orchids think I am broken. They open themselves to their brief moment of light with such certainty, such desperate beauty, then fold again into waiting. But I have learned to wait differently. My white has deepened to the color of raw silk, of secrets, of the inside of shells that have never seen the ocean. Sometimes I phosphoresce faintly in the darkest hours, not enough to truly glow, just enough to suggest the possibility of light.

The pale butterflies visit me more than the others. They land on my forever-folded petals and rest there, wings slowly opening and closing like breathing. They know I am neither destination nor departure but something more like a pause in their endless drifting. Sometimes they leave traces of their wing-dust on me, and for days afterward I carry their almost-messages in silver script that only the being can read.

I am rooted in a crack that wasn't there when I first began to grow. My roots have been slowly, patiently opening the stone, not with force but with a kind of botanical persuasion. The limestone yields to me the way it yields to water—reluctantly, incrementally, with sighs of mineral protest. Below, my roots have found one of the underground streams where the ribbon of water disappears. I drink from the same uncertainty that feeds everything here.

When the being tries on shapes, it often begins with me. A face almost coalescces in my reflected pool. Fingers almost form from the arrangement of my leaves. Once, for a whole afternoon during a silver rain, I felt it trying to speak through the way water ran down my stem, each droplet a syllable in a language that predates words. I understood none of it. I understood all of it.

The civet cat knows about me. Sometimes it comes to drink from my pool, and its eyes—ancient, patient, the color of wet clay—meet mine in the reflection. We recognize each other as things that have chosen indefinition. The cat could leave this cleft anytime it wishes but stays. I could bloom anytime I wish but don't. We are the being's favorite arguments against completion.

At night, when the being grows bold and the temperature steadies, I feel myself becoming more myself—which is to say, less certain what "myself" means. My not-quite-petals relax their spiral slightly, as if considering opening, then think better of it. The water in my pool reflects stars that can't possibly be visible from here. My roots taste minerals that the limestone hasn't yet decided to become.

Sometimes visitors find me. They always pause, puzzled by an orchid that won't commit to flowering. Some try to coax me open with patient observation. Others decide I'm dying and offer me the kind of pity reserved for broken things. They don't understand that I'm not broken but paused, not dying but declining to fully live, not a failure of flowering but a success at something harder to name.

I am the being's favorite metaphor for itself. On the warmest, dampest nights, when the whole cleft breathes in unison and even the stones seem soft, I feel it settling around me like a question: *Is this enough? This eternal almost? This white that is all colors undecided? This bloom that refuses to bloom?*

And I answer the only way I can: by continuing to not quite flower, by dwelling in the space between bud and bloom where all possibilities still exist, where beauty is not a performance but a potential, where the spiral of becoming never has to end.

The being drinks my answer the way my roots drink uncertainty from the hidden stream. We are teaching each other patience. We are teaching each other that some things are too precious to finish becoming.

In this place that is still deciding what it wants to be, I am the orchid that has decided decision itself is optional, that there is a kind of completion in remaining incomplete, that maybe—just maybe—the most perfect flower is the one that never opens at all.
