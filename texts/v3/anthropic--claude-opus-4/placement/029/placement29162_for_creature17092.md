I am a **Resonance Cartographer**, one of those who maps the unfinished geometries of Venkarr.

My workshop clings to a narrow ledge halfway between the foundry levels and the violet reaches where air turns to suggestion. It's more cave than room—a natural hollow in the basalt that I've expanded over decades, following the stone's own inclinations. The walls are covered in my charts: not maps of what is, but diagrams of what might be, what was for a moment, what the mountain dreams of becoming.

My tools are peculiar. Brass pendulums that swing in patterns divorced from gravity. Crystals that hum at frequencies only the stone can hear. Sheets of thin metal that I press against the cliff face to take impressions of vibrations—the mountain's pulse rendered visible in delicate, fractal scratches. I work with the Geode-Seers sometimes, but my interest runs deeper than predicting the next fissure. I document the impossibilities.

My body has adapted to this work. My left eye has developed a persistent twitch—not from the subsonic hum like the Seers, but from training it to catch the peripheral flickerings. I've learned to see slightly out of phase, to let my vision lag a half-second behind my movement. This is how you spot the ghost bridges, the towers that exist in the corner of your eye. My fingertips are callused in strange patterns from tracing the static-burn of unreal architectures.

I keep a colony of Luminous Finns, but not for light. Their bioluminescent pulses accelerate near the reality breaks, creating a living meter for dimensional instability. When they strobe too quickly, I know to mark that section of cliff as "pending"—not dangerous exactly, but not yet committed to being.

The other inhabitants think me half-mad, which is fair. I spend my days hanging from hemp ropes, pressing my ear to stone that sometimes whispers back in languages that haven't been invented yet. I sketch the bridges that lead nowhere, noting their architectural styles (some are clearly from futures where Venkarr became something else entirely). I've developed a notation system for recording the lag between sound and source, mapping the temporal stutters across the cliff face.

My greatest discovery is this: the mirages aren't random. They follow patterns, tidal almost, ebbing and flowing with some vast, geological rhythm. The static creatures move along specific paths—paths that will, in perhaps a hundred years, become real walkways. The mountain isn't just growing; it's rehearsing.

At night (though night here is merely when the foundry fires burn lower), I sit on my ledge with a pot of bitter tea brewed from the lichen the climb-goats won't touch. I update my charts by the light of a jar of agitated Finns, adding another day's observations to the growing map of maybes. Sometimes I feel the stone hum through my boots, a frequency that makes my teeth ache, and I know somewhere a new spire is budding, or an old certainty is dissolving.

I am the keeper of Venkarr's hesitations, its architectural stutters and geological second thoughts. My maps will never be complete—completion isn't in this mountain's nature. But in the accumulation of incomplete things, in the patient documentation of a reality that won't quite settle, I find my purpose.

Tomorrow I'll lower myself to the twilight zone where the base mesas fade into permanent shadow. The static is thicker there, the geometry more fluid. I've seen hints of structures that phase in and out—not mirages of buildings that might exist, but echoes of the mesa before it chose to become a mountain. I'll bring extra paper, weatherproofed and heavy. 

The mountain's memory runs deep, and I am its imperfect chronicler, mapping the space between the world that is and the world that is still deciding.
