In the furthest reaches of the caldera's plant-woven sprawl, where the reed-walls thin to wisps and the water grows a shade colder, I find my place as one of the Drift-Tenders—those few Kelari who choose the margins over the communal heart. My dwelling is no bulbous chamber of sea-sage but a simple hammock of woven kelp strung between two ancient fern-pillars, their trunks scarred by centuries of my predecessors' careful notching. Here, where the filtered light barely reaches and the spore-lanterns gather in lazy clouds, I maintain the Remembrance Gardens.

These are not gardens in any terrestrial sense, but vertical meadows of memory-moss that cling to the rockface, each patch cultivated from spores carried by a dying elder's final breath. The moss grows in spirals, its tendrils encoding the mineral taste of a life lived—iron for the Pruners, calcium for the Echo-Weavers, traces of copper for those who ventured too close to the old volcanic vents below. I tend these colonies with tools inherited from my mentor: a scraping blade carved from a leviathan's rib, brushes of bundled eel-whiskers, and most precious, a set of tasting stones—smooth pebbles that, when pressed against the moss, allow me to sample its chemical memory without disturbing the growth.

My days unfold in solitary rhythm. I wake to the distant cacophony of the commune—muffled here to a constant rumble like digestion—and slip from my hammock into the perpetual chill. My body, even after decades, still shivers at that first embrace; I am thinner than most Kelari, my blubber layer modest, trading warmth for the sensitivity needed to read the moss's subtle chemistry. The gill-fronds at my neck flutter faster than my kin's, compensation for the thinner water at these heights where few fish school and fewer still are the chattering cephalopods.

I pole my skiff—a narrow thing, barely a hollowed stem—along the garden wall, pausing where new growth threatens to overtake old. With the rib-blade, I prune back aggressive colonies that would smother their neighbors, careful to preserve the boundaries between one life's tale and the next. Sometimes I discover convergences where two patches have grown together, their chemistries mingling into new patterns. These I study closely, tasting the hybrid memory: here, a Harvester who loved an Echo-Weaver, their minerals blending into harmonics; there, twins who died decades apart but whose moss reached for each other across the stone.

The work is meticulous, meditative. Each stroke of the blade must be precise—too shallow and the moss regrows chaotically, too deep and the memory scatters into meaningless spores. I've learned to read the colors, too: healthy memory-moss shifts from deep jade to pale mint as its story clarifies, while diseased patches turn the yellow of old bone. These I must excise entirely, burning them in one of the small thermal vents that dot this height, watching the smoke of forgotten lives rise toward the surface world we've all but mythologized.

At the cycle's heart, when the light above reaches its fullest filtered intensity, I perform the Tasting. This is why I am Drift-Tender and not merely gardener. Pressing each tasting stone in sequence against selected patches, I absorb their chemical narratives through my tongue's specialized pores—an adaptation unique to our calling, cultivated through years of exposure to the moss's mild toxins. The stories flood through me: salt-bright childhoods spent chasing fist-minnows through the plaza voids, the iron tang of a Pruner's pride as they shape a new dwelling, the copper-sweet sorrow of outliving one's pod-siblings. I hold these tastes in my memory-sacs, organs behind my gills that swell with the accumulated chemistry.

In the commune's center, they think us mad or melancholy, we Drift-Tenders who choose the margins. They don't understand that we are the Kelari's true memory, more reliable than their pressed-algae scrolls or sung genealogies. When disputes arise about lineage or land-claims—rare but devastating in our small world—they send envoys to taste our stones, to verify which family first cultivated a particular stand of kelp or who discovered a new thermal vent for warming the nurseries. We arbitrate through flavor, our bodies living libraries of the dead.

But there is more to the tending than mere record-keeping. In the deepest part of the night-cycle, when even the distant commune quiets and only the ancient water's breathing fills the chamber, I sometimes taste patterns in the moss that shouldn't exist—memories too old to belong to any Kelari, flavors that speak of dry stone and scorching sun, of the time before the drowning. My mentor, before her own spores joined the garden, whispered of the First Moss, a colony hidden somewhere in the caldera's depths that grew from the last breath of the mountain itself, when the water first rushed in. She spent her final years searching for it, tasting stone after stone until the toxins finally overwhelmed her careful immunity.

I haven't decided yet if I'll follow her path. For now, I content myself with the known stories, with maintaining the delicate balance between growth and memory. My own patch is already chosen—a narrow crevice where the morning light touches briefly, between a boisterous Harvester who died laughing and a quiet Echo-Weaver who spent her last years in silence. When my time comes, another Drift-Tender will taste my story: solitude and satisfaction, the metallic patience of gardening, and underneath it all, the faint copper curiosity about what memories the mountain itself might hold.

But not yet. Today there is pruning to be done, patterns to preserve, and new growth to guide. I pole my narrow skiff back along the wall, the rib-blade singing softly as it cuts through water, adding my own small rhythm to the eternal pulse of our drowned and breathing world.
