I am the Channel-Keeper of the Seventh Distribution.

My body is the architecture of patience. Where others might have muscles built for speed, mine are wound in slow spirals around bones that are more like the metal rods of the pillars than anything that once knew marrow. Each movement I make is deliberate, considered—not because I am old (though I have counted more water-cycles than most) but because haste is the enemy of the channel's function. My fingers are twice as long as those who work the foundries, and each joint bends in directions that make visitors to my sector stop and stare. Evolution here is not natural selection but the slow teaching of the body by the work.

The pads of my fingertips have become something between flesh and the ghost-light itself. I am one of the few who can walk the luminous paths without leather, though the price is that my touch now carries its own faint blue glow that never fully fades. When I press my palm to the metal floor to read the temperature of a channel's flow, I leave behind a brief handprint of cold light that takes hours to dissipate.

My hair—if it can still be called hair—grows in thick cables like the wrapped wire of the pillar bundles. Each strand is hollow, and I have learned to braid them into patterns that channel the condensation from my scalp down my back in controlled streams. The water that touches my skin is never wasted; my body has become another part of the distribution system.

I wear the minimum that decency requires: a wrap of metal mesh so fine it moves like fabric, made from zinc wire that I drew myself over the course of three seasons. It covers my torso and extends to mid-thigh, leaving my arms and lower legs bare to better feel the minute temperature variations that tell me when a channel is beginning to fail. The mesh serves a second purpose—it grounds the static charge that builds in my body from constant contact with the flowing water and the ghost-light paths.

My workshop is not a separate structure but a widening in the channel system itself, where seven major distributions converge into what the others call the Listening Bowl. Here, the sound of every drip in my sector eventually makes itself known through the harmonics of the metal. I have carved my tools into the walls themselves—chisels that are simply protrusions of shaped floor-metal, files that are patches of carefully corroded surface with just the right grain.

I do not sleep in the way the foundry workers do, in shifts measured by the banking and rekindling of the forge fires. Instead, I enter a state I call the Shallow Drowse, where my breathing synchronizes with the drip-pattern of my sector. In this state, I can feel when a new step-bug colony has begun to redirect one of my channels, or when a growth of coal-moss threatens to create an unwanted dam. My dreams, when they come, are of water finding new paths through metal, of the perfect angle of incision that will divide a flow into seven equal streams.

My torch is different from the others—a cold-flame that burns metal shavings in a solution of ghost-light essence I milk from the luminous paths. It gives less heat but disturbs the water less, and its light doesn't frighten the moth-thieves who sometimes follow me on my rounds. I have learned their clicking language, or at least enough of it to know when they're warning me of a weakness in the Roof above.

The younger engineers think I have forgotten the world above, but they are wrong. I remember it perfectly—I just remember it as a story that happened to someone else's bones. The sun is a theory I once believed in. The rain is an uncontrolled failure of distribution. The rivers of the surface are channels that forgot their purpose.

My greatest work is not any single channel but the Mercy Circuit—a loop of flowing water that connects to nothing productive, produces nothing, feeds no foundry. It exists purely to give the water somewhere to go when all other paths are full, a place for excess to become beauty instead of flood. Along its path, I have cultivated a garden of hybrid rust, training the Grain to grow in spirals and whorls that shift their colors with the temperature of the water. Other Channel-Keepers call it wasteful. I call it the only necessary thing I have ever made.

When I die—and we do die here, despite our bones of near-metal and our hearts that beat in geologic time—I have asked that my body be sectioned and installed as flow-regulators at the seven primary nodes of my sector. My fingerbones, with their impossible joints, will make excellent channel-gates. My skull, with its trained acoustic properties, will help the next Keeper hear what the water is trying to say.

Until then, I continue. The channels must be kept. The water must be taught its paths. And somewhere in the eternal twilight of the Underforge, the sound of my sector's drips compose a rhythm that I alone can hear as music.
