In this violent crèche of stone and storm, I am the copper-touched lichen that blooms where mineral veins bleed through fresh fractures. Not one organism but a thousand thousand, spreading in slow fireworks across the newly-carved faces where iron and copper streak the granite like exposed nerves.

I taste the rock with acids secreted from my underside, dissolving microscopic pits to anchor myself against the hurricane winds. Each pit becomes a tiny cauldron where I brew the stone into sustenance, patient alchemy measured in decades. The metallic tang of copper feeds something ancient in my chemistry—a hunger inherited from ancestors that first colonized the volcanic shores when the world was young and raw.

My surface glitters with crystalline structures, neither fully plant nor fungus but something suspended between kingdoms. In the perpetual twilight, I've learned to harvest what little light filters through the storm clouds, my surfaces iridescent with microscopic prisms that trap and redirect every precious photon. When the steam vents breathe their scalding exhales, I absorb the minerals suspended in that breath, incorporating traces of sulfur and iron into my spreading body.

I grow in overlapping scales, each one a separate colony that fuses at the edges with its neighbors until you cannot tell where one ends and another begins. We communicate through chemical whispers, warning of unstable rock, sharing news of fresh seeps where water carries dissolved copper down from higher splits. When a section of cliff face shears away—taking centuries of our growth with it—we mourn in our slow way, then begin again, creeping into the fresh wound.

The wind that terrorizes the vines barely touches me. I am flush against stone, aerodynamic as paint. But I feel its violence in vibrations through the rock, a constant thrumming that becomes part of my existence. Sometimes I wonder if I'm writing the mountain's history in my growth patterns—each ring of expansion marking another year of survival, another storm weathered, another tectonic shudder absorbed.

In the spaces between my colonies, spider-thin filaments quest outward, testing the air for new surfaces to colonize. When they find purchase on a wind-scoured edge, they anchor and begin the slow transformation: first a spots no bigger than pinheads, then spreading aureoles of blue-green and copper-bright orange, then the full flourishing that turns bare rock into a living canvas.

I am the first digester of mountains, the patient transformer that takes what seems sterile and makes it ready for the mosses that will follow, then the ferns, then perhaps—in some unimaginable future—something larger. But for now, I am enough. I and the stone and the storm, locked in our slow dance of dissolution and growth, as if the mountain itself were learning, through me, how to live.
