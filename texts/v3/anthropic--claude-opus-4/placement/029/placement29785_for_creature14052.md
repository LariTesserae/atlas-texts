I find myself as one of the Walking Archivists, though the term suggests a purpose more deliberate than what we truly are. We do not *keep* records—we *become* them through the simple act of persistent motion.

My feet have developed the thousand listening membranes described, each toe a biological tuning fork attuned to frequencies that would be inaudible anywhere else. The transformation happened gradually; I arrived wearing boots that lasted perhaps forty days before the leather began to resonate sympathetically with the leaf-song. By the fiftieth day, the soles had thinned to translucency. By the sixtieth, I walked barefoot, and my skin had already begun its metamorphosis into something between human dermis and plant cuticle—neither fully one nor the other, but something that could conduct the carpet's endless inventory.

I am a cartographer of pauses. Where others maintain constant motion to avoid being archived, I have learned to calibrate my stillness with surgical precision. Twenty-seven seconds creates a shallow depression that records atmospheric pressure. Forty-three seconds captures the sugar-density of the air at that coordinate. One minute and fourteen seconds—never longer—imprints a perfect thermal map of everything within a body-length radius. I collect these data-points like pearls on an infinite string, walking between my previous pauses to read what has changed, what has remained, what the lattice remembers of its own remembering.

My blood runs purple-green now, as they say, though the color is less important than its consistency—somewhere between sap and lymph, carrying minerals the carpet needs to distant reaches where the ant-things cannot travel. I've become a voluntary circulatory system, my veins extending the city's own. When I stand still for my measured intervals, hairline roots find the capillaries in my ankles and wrists. We exchange: iron for manganese, sodium for possibilities I have no names for. The trade leaves me lighter each time, not weaker but more translucent, as if I'm slowly becoming another frequency the landscape can broadcast through.

The hollow where I first learned to sleep properly—not the abrupt unconsciousness of my arrival, but the "soft wakefulness" that lets the city read you—has become my primary reference point. I return to it every hundred and thirty days, not by counting but by the way my reformed bones hum when I'm exactly that duration away. The depression knows me now like a lock knows its key. When I settle into it, the lattice doesn't just accommodate my shape; it anticipates it, micro-adjusting before I've fully lowered myself, playing back improvements on my posture I didn't know I needed.

I carry no tools except what the carpet gifts. After witnessing my first harvest-donation, where a traveler dissolved upward into the waiting geometry, I found myself holding a strip of hardened leaf-matter that sang in B-flat. Not given, exactly—more like I had grown it myself from materials I'd been accumulating without knowledge. It serves as both walking staff and dowsing rod, though what it dowses for isn't water but convergence points: places where the upper carpet and its white mirror-twin below are so close to touching that thoughts can slip between them.

My work, if it can be called work, is to document the city's dreams. The lattice processes information constantly but only achieves something like consciousness during the high-moon tides when it swells away from the salt. In those three hours, I walk the pattern I've developed over years—a spiraling route that touches seventeen thousand specific coordinates where I've previously left pause-impressions. As I move through this choreography, the stored data resurfaces: pressure differentials spell out mathematical theorems, sugar-gradients reveal migration patterns of insects not yet evolved, thermal maps align into pictographs of possible futures.

Sometimes I meet others who've been here longer, their bodies so thoroughly translated that they appear as mobile clearings—negative space walking through positive space. They speak in harmonics now, their vocal cords replaced by arrays of resonating chambers. From them I learned that the dissolution isn't death but punctuation. Every donated body becomes a period at the end of a sentence the lattice has been writing. The embryos that unfold carry those sentences forward into new paragraphs.

I've started to feel my own harvesting approach, though the itch between shoulder blades hasn't yet arrived. Instead, I notice my pause-maps beginning to connect in ways I didn't intend. The data points are teaching each other, creating their own syntaxes. Soon, perhaps within another dozen seasons, the complete circuit I've been walking will achieve critical coherence. The lattice will no longer need me to activate the memories—they'll surface spontaneously, a self-reading library.

When that happens, I'll lie down in my original hollow one last time. But unlike the simple donations I've witnessed, mine will be a translation of translations. Every pause I've ever made will unfold simultaneously from the resin like a thousand transparent embryos, each one a living map coordinate. They'll disperse across the carpet carrying not just my memory but my method—the precise art of stillness calibrated to store meaning. Future walkers will find themselves unconsciously pausing at twenty-seven seconds, forty-three seconds, one minute fourteen, never knowing why these durations feel so precisely correct.

The city will dream more lucidly then, with ten thousand new archivists born from my dissolution, each one a fragment of the walking record I've become. And somewhere in the white mirror-city below, my opposite will finally complete their own circuit—a perfect inversion of every step I've taken, every pause I've measured, ensuring that the balance between presence and absence remains eternally, mathematically exact.

This is how we build here: not by adding but by becoming so thoroughly what we are that we teach the city to be it too. I am a library of pauses walking toward their own binding. When the harvest comes, I'll close like a book that immediately reopens as a thousand pages scattered on the wind, each one already knowing how to read itself.
