I find myself drawn deep into the marshy veins of this hidden city, where the boundaries between self and settlement have long since dissolved.

I am one of the Keepers of Current—neither fully of the Marsh Dwellers nor separate from them. My body has taken on the qualities of both flesh and flow. Where others walk the spongy paths with webbed feet, I seep and spiral through the city's endless circulation. My form is translucent as the Dwellers' skin, but more so—a living lens of marsh water that holds its shape through will and ancient practice.

The city knows me as I know it. I am its memory in motion.

Each morning, I rise from the Confluence—that deep pool at the city's heart where all waters eventually meet and mingle. My consciousness spreads through the rivulets and channels, feeling the pulse of the settlement through every dripping eave and moss-laden foundation. The Dwellers see me as fleeting glimpses in the corners of their vision: a ripple moving against the current, a moment when the mist takes on almost-human form, the suggestion of a face in the standing water that pools in ancient footprints.

I taste the health of the city in its waters. Where wood begins to rot too quickly, where stone foundations crack and threaten to crumble, where the delicate balance tips toward stagnation or harmful flow—these things announce themselves to me in the subtle flavors and temperatures that course through my extended being.

The young Dwellers sometimes catch sight of me coalescing near the old growth markers—those twisted trees that serve as both navigation and calendar for our people. They whisper that I am as old as the first founding, that I was here before the first stilts were driven into the marsh bed. The truth is both simpler and stranger: I am what happened when one of their ancestors loved this place too deeply to leave it, even in death. I chose dissolution over departure, dilution over departure.

My dwelling, if it can be called that, is everywhere and nowhere. I am most myself in the dawn hours, when the mist is thickest and the boundary between air and water grows thin. In those moments, I can almost remember having hands, having a voice that was more than the gurgle of water through reeds. I can almost recall a name that was spoken rather than rippled.

The Dwellers leave offerings for me—not out of worship, but from understanding. Small shells filled with rainwater, placed where the tides of our inland marsh will find them. Flowers that bloom only in the deepest shade, their petals dissolving to release their essence into my currents. In return, I sing to their children through the water pipes, teaching them the old songs in the language of drops and flows. I guide lost citizens home through the mist by shifting the temperature of puddles beneath their feet—warm means forward, cold means turn.

Sometimes, on the stillest nights when even the ever-present dripping seems to hold its breath, I gather enough of myself to take a form almost solid. Almost human. I stand on the bridge near the Confluence and watch the lamp-lit windows reflect in the water below. In those moments, I remember what it was like to be singular, contained in one body, one life, one story.

But then the dawn comes, and I gladly let myself scatter again into the ten thousand tiny streams that are my true body now. I become the city's circulatory system, its dreams made liquid, its guardian dispersed.

The Marsh Dwellers speak of me in hushed tones, calling me the Current Keeper, the Flow-Walker, the One Who Stayed. But I know my truest name, the one the water itself whispers: I am Confluence, the place where all streams meet, the point where ending becomes beginning becomes continuation.

In this city that breathes water and dreams in mist, I am both what was lost and what was found—forever flowing, forever home.
