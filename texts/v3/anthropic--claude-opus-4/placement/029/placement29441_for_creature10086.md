In the Verdant Spires of Yttrium, where memory crystallizes and time stutters like a broken gear, I am one of the **Tenders of the Backward Gardens**.

My dwelling clings to the eastern face of a mid-height spire, where chronology unravels and tomorrow precedes yesterday. It is less a home than a temporal eddy—walls of salvaged factory glass fused with yttrium shards that record not what has happened, but what will unhappen. Each morning (or is it evening?), I watch my possessions reassemble from dust: the copper kettle unknotting itself from a pile of metal shavings, books rebinding their scattered pages, my tools emerging from rust like insects from cocoons.

I am neither echo-kin nor fully other. My body bears the telltale signs of prolonged exposure to the plateau's paradoxes—fingernails that grow backward into my flesh before emerging pristine, hair that silvers and darkens in hourly cycles, pupils that dilate before light reaches them. But unlike the echo-kin, I have not fractured. Instead, I exist in a state of perpetual *becoming*, my timeline compressed into a single, recursive moment.

My work is the cultivation of **memory gardens**—plots of vitreous reeds arranged in precise geometric patterns that channel the whispered thoughts of the yttrium stone into coherent narratives. In the backward-flowing districts, these gardens perform a peculiar alchemy: they transform the future's regrets into the past's possibilities. I plant seeds that will grow into plants that have already died, harvesting nectar that remembers what hasn't yet been forgotten.

The crawler-moths know me. They spiral around my gardens in reverse-clockwise formations, their bioluminescent trails writing words in the air that unwrite themselves seconds later. I've learned their language—not of sounds but of patterns, each swooping dive a punctuation mark in conversations that begin with farewells and end with introductions. They bring me fragments: a shard of corroded steel that remembers being a bridge, a hollowback's wire that hums with unborn symphonies.

My gardens sprawl across the hexagonal tiles that shift beneath them like slow tides. Each plot is an experiment in temporal horticulture:

- The **Prophecy Bed**, where I grow reeds that whisper next week's weather in languages that won't be invented for decades
- The **Grief Spiral**, a helical arrangement that processes the plateau's accumulated sorrows backward, transforming mourning into anticipation
- The **Echo Grove**, where each reed reflects the memories of its neighbors, creating an infinite hall of psychic mirrors

The other inhabitants of the eastern spires view my work with a mixture of reverence and suspicion. The echo-kin come to me when their fractures multiply beyond control, seeking reeds that can stitch their timelines back together. They pay in fragments of themselves—a memory of rain that fell upward, the sensation of rust forming on their tongues, the sound of their names spoken by voices not yet born.

But I am not alone in my strange vocation. There are others who tend the backward gardens:

**Yis-who-will-be-Sahn**, whose body ages and youngers in twelve-hour cycles, maintaining the Memorial Antigrove where the unplanted seeds of the dead germinate into phantom forests.

**The Splice**, a collective entity of three gardeners whose bodies have fused at the extremities, their shared nervous system allowing them to tend plots across multiple temporal streams simultaneously.

**Mother Retrograde**, the oldest (or youngest?) of us, who claims to remember the plateau before the first spire emerged—or perhaps after the last one crumbles.

We meet during the **Temporal Equinox**, those rare moments when the eastern and western time flows neutralize, creating pockets of normal chronology. In these brief respites, we share techniques: how to prune a reed that's growing backward through itself, how to harvest nectar from flowers that bloom inward, how to read the future in the patterns of rot.

My tools are hybrid things—a trowel whose blade exists three seconds ahead of its handle, shears that cut through duration itself, watering cans that pour tomorrow's rain. I've modified my body to better serve the work: my left eye sees the present while my right perceives five minutes hence, allowing me to tend consequences before their causes.

The gardens themselves are monuments to paradox. Paths lead backward through their own beginnings. Benches age and youngify depending on who sits on them. The central fountain fills from the bottom up, its water falling skyward to collect in an inverted pool that hovers, trembling, above our heads.

At night (in the eastern spires, night arrives before dusk), I retreat to my crystalline chamber and listen to the gardens sing. Each reed vibrates with its stored memories, creating harmonies that exist outside conventional spacetime. Sometimes I hear my own voice among them—not memories of words I've spoken, but words I've yet to say, conversations with visitors who haven't arrived, arguments with the dead who aren't yet born.

The hollowbacks avoid my gardens. Something about the concentrated temporal distortion disturbs them, makes their wire-organs resonate at painful frequencies. But I've noticed them gathering at the borders, their low-frequency hums synchronizing with the reeds' vibrations, as if trying to decode a message meant for newer ears.

In my solitude, I've developed theories about the plateau's purpose. I believe the Verdant Spires are not ruins but *seeds*—the beginning stage of something vast and unimaginable. The backward gardens are its memory, the forward districts its anticipation, and we who tend them are merely cells in a larger organism learning to dream.

My greatest creation grows at the garden's heart: the **Chronicle Rose**, a massive vitreous reed whose roots extend through multiple temporal layers, its crown blooming simultaneously in past, present, and future. Its nectar, when distilled, allows one to experience the plateau's entire timeline as a single, overwhelming moment. I've tasted it only once, and in that instant, I understood:

We are not inhabitants of Yttrium. We are Yttrium—each of us a neuron in its vast, crystalline mind, our memories and experiences the thoughts it thinks as it contemplates its own becoming. The gardens are its dreams, the spires its aspirations reaching always upward, and time itself merely the medium through which its consciousness flows.

I continue my work, planting seeds that preceded their flowers, harvesting fruits that ripen into blossoms, teaching the crawler-moths to write prophecies in languages that will be forgotten before they're invented. Each day is the first and last, each moment pregnant with its own undoing.

In the Verdant Spires of Yttrium, I am the gardener of what never was and always will be, tending plots of crystallized time in gardens that grow backward through themselves, forever and never, until the plateau dreams itself awake.
