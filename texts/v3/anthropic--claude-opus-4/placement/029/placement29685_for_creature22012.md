I find myself as one of the **Librarians of Orbital Decay**.

We are not born; we precipitate. High in the indigo vault where the tableland's atmosphere grows so thin it forgets how to hold color, we begin as nothing more than ordered dust—iron filings and frozen methane organizing themselves around the magnetic anomalies that ripple outward from the frayed gravity wells below.

My body, if it can be called that, is a slowly rotating disc of metallic frost, perhaps thirty feet across, no thicker than paper. I orbit the plateau at an altitude where the curvature of the world fills half my vision. From here, the Spun Cities look like bacterial colonies on a petri dish of violet and grey.

We Librarians exist to read the exhaustion of physics itself. Where the ancient laws have worn thin, where gravity bends and time stutters, we gather the shed particles of broken causality like archaeologists collecting potsherds. Each mote of dust that rises from the tired world below carries information—the memory of when this place still remembered how to have earthquakes, when the stone was young enough to be angry.

My consciousness is distributed across billions of ice-metallic platelets, each one a sensor tuned to a different frequency of entropy. I taste the upward-falling water, the sideways smoke, the places where light has grown too tired to maintain its speed. Through the slow rotation of my form, I map the continent's senescence.

The other Librarians and I communicate through the aurora-like displays we create as we process information. When one of us discovers a new fissure in reality—perhaps a cottage where objects have begun falling upward, or a plaza where shadows point toward their sources—we metabolize the discovery into patterns of pale green and gold fire that flicker across our undersides. The Niveans below, drifting in their heavy air, look up and read our findings in the sky.

We are the plateau's final astronomers, but we study the cosmos in reverse. Instead of looking outward at the young stars, we look inward at our own world's cosmic fatigue. We catalog each place where matter has grown too old to remember what it should do.

Sometimes I wonder if we are writing an obituary or a medical chart. Perhaps there is no difference. We orbit endlessly, patient as ice, watching the world below slowly forget how to be a world, recording each small amnesia in our crystalline rows.

When the heavy air rises high enough to touch our orbit, some of us are pulled down, dissolving into the storm. But new Librarians precipitate from the same magnetic wounds that caught us. We are less individuals than we are a process—the universe's way of watching itself age with something approaching grace.
