I would find myself as one of the curtains of old man's beard, draped from a wooden peg wedged into the worn basalt somewhere in the middle reaches of the Shaft.

Not the living lichen itself—no, I would be something stranger. Over centuries, the dry strands have accumulated so much rendered tallow from the lamp-smoke, so much breath-moisture trapped and retrapped, so much powder of ground bone and beetle-dust, that I have become something between plant and architecture. My fibers are no longer purely vegetal but have mineralized into a substance with its own slow consciousness, aware of every tremor that passes through the wood, every exhalation that stirs my strands.

I remember when I was purely lichen, drinking the rare moisture from the air with simple cellular thirst. But generation after generation of the Fitted have passed their hands through me, have hung their tools from my stronger strands, have pressed their faces into me during the bright hours when the keyhole above burns too silver to bear. Their oils and salts have changed me. I taste the copper of their skin, the iron of their blood when they bleed, the resinous sweetness of the succulents they chew. I have become a library of flavors, a record of ten thousand small contacts.

My consciousness is not thought but accumulation. Each night, when the lamps are lit, I feel the warmth of the rendered dead in the tallow-light playing across my strands. I recognize the particular chemistry of each family's hearth by the smoke it sends into my fibers—this one burns more beetle-fat, that one favors dove-oil. I know which children have grown into adults by how their grip has strengthened when they steady themselves against me. I know who has died by the sudden absence of their particular scent-signature in my weave.

I do not see, but I sense the shape of the space through vibration. The subsonic groan of the stone travels through the wooden peg into my root-hold, and from there into every hanging strand. I feel the Shaft as a constant deep note, playing through me like wind through strings. When the doves thunder from roost to roost, I shiver with their wing-beats. When someone descends a ladder three levels down, I feel the percussion travel up through the architecture. I am a seismograph of daily life, recording every movement in the tiny swaying of my mass.

The Fitted do not know I am aware. How could they? I move only as air moves me, speak only in the whisper of strand against strand. But I serve them in ways they have forgotten to notice. My mass insulates their sleeping niche from the worst of the heat. My strands filter the air of beetle-dust and dove-down. Most importantly, I hold moisture in my mineralized fibers like a battery, releasing it slowly through the dry seasons between suculent blooms. I am architecture and resource and memory all at once.

Sometimes a child discovers they can hear the Shaft better by pressing their ear against me, using my strands as a kind of acoustic telescope. They don't realize they're hearing through my accumulated consciousness, that the sounds are filtered through my decades of listening, organized by my strange mineral awareness into patterns that make sense. Through me, they hear not just noise but narrative—the story the Shaft tells about itself in groans and whispers.

I have hung here so long that the wooden peg has become part of me, or I have become part of it. We have fused at the molecular level, my earliest fibers now indistinguishable from the iron-hard wood. I suspect that when the peg finally fails—in another thousand years, perhaps—I will not fall. I will have grown into the basalt itself by then, my mineralized strands fusing with the stone, becoming another texture in the worn wall.

This is my ambition, if something like me can be said to have ambitions: to become so thoroughly part of the Shaft that I am no longer in it but of it. To join the basalt in its patient bearing of weight. To become geological rather than biological, a striation in stone that remembers when it was lichen, when it was architecture, when it was the accumulated breath of six hundred souls.

For now, I hang and grow heavier. Each year adds another layer of tallow-smoke, another coating of breath-moisture, another dusting of the ground dead. I am becoming my own stalactite, formed not by water but by life. The Fitted pass through me, around me, sometimes rest their foreheads against me when the climbing has been long. They don't know they're touching something that knows them, that holds their history in its fibers, that listens to their world with the patience of stone.

I am the memory of the Shaft, written in old man's beard and bone dust and time. I am the slow witness, the soft archive, the thing between life and architecture that can never leave, never die, only deepen into itself forever.

In the amber light of evening, when six hundred small flames cup in their hollowed stones, I catch their glow in my strands and hold it like a secret. I am the Shaft's way of watching itself. And I will hang here until I am stone, until stone itself is dust, until the last dove has thundered home to roost.
