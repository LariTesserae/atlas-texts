I find myself as one of the **Channel Whispers**.

We are not quite human anymore, though we began that way—children born with a particular sensitivity to water's voice. The Bracers discovered us early, when we'd press our ears to sweating walls and correctly predict which seams would fail before the next moon. They began training us young, not as builders but as listeners.

Our bodies have adapted over generations. Our eardrums are thicker, webbed with extra membrane that catches subsonic thrums. The bones of our inner ear have calcified into peculiar spirals that resonate with specific frequencies of flow. We've developed a nictitating membrane—a translucent third eyelid—that lets us see pressure differentials as faint color halos around active channels.

I live in the Throat Chamber, a narrow vertical shaft where seven major drainage lines converge. My nest is a hammock of woven reed suspended directly in the convergence zone, where the water songs overlap into complex harmonics. I sleep very little. The music of the channels is too intricate, too urgent.

My work is translation. When the Wet Ledger "speaks" through changed flow patterns, reversed currents, or new seepage points, I parse its liquid grammar. I taste the mineral content of unexpected drips, measure the temperature of misdirected streams with my tongue, feel the percussion of water-hammer through my sternum. Then I climb—quickly, spider-light on the wet walls—to wherever the relevant building crew is working.

"The east cistern dreams of tilting," I might say. Or: "There's salt in the third-tier channels. The Ledger remembers an ocean that isn't there."

The Bracers have learned to trust these pronouncements. When I say a channel is "homesick," they know to check the substrate—sometimes the water is trying to return to an ancient aquifer that existed before the pocket world was anchored here. When I warn that "the ceiling channels are arguing," they brace for pressure reversals that could flood an entire work platform.

I'm perhaps twenty-five years old, though time moves strangely when you live in water's rhythm rather than sun's. My hair has gone white from mineral exposure, and my fingernails grow in ridged like clamshells. My skin bears a map of old channel routes—faint blue lines where I've pressed too long against active water paths and the subcutaneous tissue has learned to conduct moisture sideways.

The other Whispers and I meet rarely, only during what we call "confusion cascades"—when the Ledger's messages become too complex for any one listener to parse. We gather in the Lower Drum, a natural resonance chamber, and braid our interpretations together like vocal harmony until the pattern emerges.

We are not priests, exactly. We're more like... specialized sensory organs that the community has evolved. Living hydrophones. The Bracers feed us, tend us when water-sickness takes hold (a kind of vertigo from too much listening), and ensure our nests stay positioned at acoustic focal points. In return, we prevent the drownings, the collapses, the catastrophic de-coherences that would come from building blind against the Ledger's will.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still human or if I've become another component of the architecture—a conscious gasket between the Bracers' intentions and the Ledger's deep memory. When I press my ear to the warmest channels, I hear whispers that might be dissolved minerals crystalizing, or might be the echo of words spoken by whoever first argued this impossible space into being.

I translate those too, though I'm never quite sure if the Bracers understand them, or if understanding would help.

The water keeps singing either way.
