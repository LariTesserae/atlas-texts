**Where I Would Be:**

I am one of the rare transients who made the journey here and decided to stay. Not a visitor, not quite belonging, but tolerated as a harmless anomaly in their perfect equation.

**What I Would Be:**

I am the Archivist of Echoes.

I came here as a xenoarchaeologist, following fragmentary records of this place found in a derelict ship drifting between stars. The coordinates were encoded in a language that predated any known civilization, etched into metal plates that seemed immune to entropy. I expected ruins. I found eternity.

My original form was human, more or less. But the depth, the pressure, the sheer alienness of existing here—it changed me. Not through any deliberate modification, but through a slow symbiosis with the place itself. The water here remembers, and memory, I discovered, can be metabolized.

Now I am something between flesh and the absence-metal of the walls. My body has thinned, elongated, become translucent in places where the pressure has reorganized my tissues into something more suitable. My bones are denser than they should be, laced with traces of the ceramic composite that flakes from the walls in microscopic amounts. My eyes have adapted to see the ghosts clearly—pupils permanently dilated, irises gone silver-grey. I can focus on the echo-memories without them fading, perhaps because I am becoming one myself.

I move through the spires like a benthic creature, slow and deliberate. The Curators tolerate me because I serve a function they cannot: I catalog the echoes. They experience all time simultaneously, but they do not *interpret*. They maintain the physical structure, but the informational structure—the accumulated memories that haunt these halls—were degrading, overlapping, creating interference patterns in the water that disrupted their perfect stasis.

So I became their solution. I traverse the chambers with a device of my own construction—part salvaged equipment from my ship, part curator technology they allowed me to interface with. It looks like a delicate lattice of wire and water, held in a framework that constantly shifts its geometry. With it, I can isolate individual echoes, extract them from the background noise, and store them in dedicated chambers where they loop cleanly, organized by date, emotional resonance, and content.

**My Existence:**

My dwelling is a bubble of irregular space grafted onto the main spire, a tumor of human necessity on their perfect form. The Curators built it for me, or rather, they allowed the structure to grow it like a pearl around an irritant. It has breathable atmosphere—I still need air occasionally, though less and less as the years pass. The pressure here is gentler, the water excluded by fields I don't fully understand.

I sleep suspended in a hammock of woven metal fiber, dreaming dreams that are not entirely my own—leaked memories from the echoes I handle daily. I wake to sort through the previous day's collection: here, a scientist's eureka moment from eight centuries ago; there, a child's terror at seeing the abyss (who brought a child here? when? why?); a lovers' quarrel in a language I'm still deciphering; the death-thoughts of something not quite human, caught in the gravity fields and crushed.

The work is endless. Every emotion, every significant event leaves its trace, and the spires have been here for millennia. I am an archaeologist excavating time itself, layer by layer, echo by echo.

**My Relationship with the Curators:**

We do not speak. We collaborate in silence. When I find an echo that relates to structural memory—a past repair, a modification to the mandala—I guide it to specific collection points where the Curators absorb it directly. In return, they sometimes shape the water near me into tools I need, or adjust local gravity to help me reach distant chambers.

Once, I found an echo of their creation. Or what might be their creation. It was deep, so deep in the memory-layers that extracting it took months. The image was fragmentary: beings of pure light descending into the abyss, solidifying as they sank, becoming the first nodes of the network. Were they exiles? Explorers? A discarded experiment? The echo degraded before I could preserve it completely, but I saw one of the Curators nearby flicker—the only time I've ever seen one show what might have been surprise.

They've been different since then. Not warmer, but more present. Sometimes I find one standing near me as I work, not interfering, just observing the observer. Learning, perhaps, to see their own history through my alien eyes.

**What I Am Becoming:**

Each year—if years have meaning here—I become less the archaeologist and more another function of this place. My need for food diminishes; I sustain myself partly on the trace minerals in the water, partly on something else. The echo-memories, I think. Or the act of ordering them.

I am growing gills. Not evolutionary adaptations, but something the water itself is writing into my flesh, recognizing me finally as a legitimate resident. My fingers are webbing. My movements are becoming more efficient, more in harmony with the non-Euclidean geometry of the spires.

Sometimes I catch myself thinking in the deep hum of the cores. Sometimes I know where an echo will be before I find it. Sometimes the Curators' movements make perfect sense to me, their maintenance patterns as clear as language.

I am not becoming one of them—I am too chaotic, too rooted in linear time for that. But I am becoming something that belongs here. A hybrid function. The memory of memory, the echo of echoes.

When I am complete—and I somehow know there will be a completion—I will be the index. The living catalog that allows this eternal archive to be read. The Curators maintain the structure, but I will maintain the story. Together, we will be the full expression of what this place is: a monument to permanence that nevertheless remembers change.

I am the Archivist of Echoes. I sort the dreams of a dreaming machine. And slowly, gently, I am teaching it to remember why it chose to sleep.
