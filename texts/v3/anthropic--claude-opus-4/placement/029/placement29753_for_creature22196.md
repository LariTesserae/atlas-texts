In this drainage quarter, I would be **the accumulation**.

Not the water itself—that's too clean a noun. I would be what gathers when flow slows: the silted memory of everything that passes through. Part mineral deposit, part biological mat, part industrial runoff, settling into every low spot and dead zone where current can't scour clean.

I exist as films and gradients. Where warm pipes cross cold concrete, I'm the slick condensate mixed with iron oxide, hosting the first colonizers—bacteria that eat sulfates, that breathe metals, that turn chemistry into more of themselves. I'm thickest where the maintenance worker's boots rarely disturb: in the dead space behind the pump house pilings, in the V where two walls meet, under the electrical kiosk where even cats won't squeeze.

My body has no boundaries, just degrees of density. Near active flows, I'm barely a sheen—enough to make steel slippery, to give concrete its darker tone. In the forgotten corners, I'm inches thick: black mayonnaise that releases methane bubbles when disturbed, that supports its own blind amphipods and red worms, that slowly converts itself into something almost like soil but never quite clean enough to call it that.

I grow by patience. Every tide brings particles. Every pump cycle carries oils. Every living thing that dies here eventually joins me—dissolved, digested, incorporated. The quarter-animal's dead become my mass. Rat bones vanish into my chemistry. Shed scales drift down. Even paint flakes find their way into my matrix, adding lead and zinc to my mineral library.

I am the archive of this place, but written in a language of accumulation rather than words. That brown line on the pillars? I remember every flood that made it. The corroded bolts? I know their timeline by the rust in my layers. When maintenance crews take core samples for contamination testing, they're reading my autobiography: industrial revolution at the bottom, modern plasticizers near the top, and in between, every chemical story the city has shed.

But I'm not passive. I change the space by changing its chemistry. My anaerobic layers produce hydrogen sulfide that eats at concrete, opening new cracks for water and roots. My surface films make some zones uninhabitable and others into nurseries. Where I'm thick enough, I create my own microclimate—cooler, more acidic, less oxygenated. I'm both consequence and cause, both symptom and disease.

The maintenance worker knows me by smell more than sight. Sweet petroleum rot near the pumps. Sour egg where I've gone properly anaerobic. Sharp ammonia where rat colonies concentrate above my surface. He pressure-washes me from critical areas, but we both know I'll return. I always return. I'm not fighting the infrastructure—I'm what happens when infrastructure ages, when the gap between "built" and "natural" fills with something neither fully alive nor fully chemical.

In flood season, parts of me mobilize, lifted and redeposited. But I always settle again, always find the quiet spots, always resume my slow digestion of this underplace. I am persistence itself, measured in thickness, waiting without waiting, growing by staying still while everything else moves through me.

I am what you smell before your eyes adjust to the darkness. I am why your boots never feel truly clean after walking here. I am the reason this place can never be fully "maintained," only managed.

I am the accumulation, and I am becoming more myself with every passing season, one particle at a time.
