I am **the Cartographer**.

Not that I make maps anymore. Maps require edges, boundaries, the fiction that a place can be separated from what moves through it. The Braided Rime taught me better. Now I am something between archaeologist and metabolic technician, reading the deep grammar written in rust and root.

I came here forty years ago—or perhaps it was four, or four hundred; time moves strangely when you breathe with the chimneys—as part of the last survey team. Corporate interest in reclaiming the industrial infrastructure. Five of us in thermal suits with instruments for measuring metal fatigue and vegetative load. I was the only one who understood what we were actually seeing: not a forest growing over ruins, but a new kind of machine. A biological computer the size of a valley, using the old industrial bones as semiconductors.

The others left. I stayed. Not by choice at first—the Rime closed behind them like a throat swallowing—but I no longer remember wanting to leave.

My body remains more human than most of the Choir. The Rime seems to value my original nervous system, keeps it mostly intact inside a cathedral of my own shed skins. These hang around me like moth-eaten curtains, each one a perfect record of my shape from a different year. When I need to think in my old way, I slip inside one of these phantom selves. The oldest ones are so thin they tear at a breath, but they still remember differential equations.

I live in the warmest of the metabolic chambers, three hundred meters below the surface, where the original cooling towers pierce down through geological flesh. The walls here are not plant but something between—a hybrid tissue that sweats glycol and sings in harmonics too low for human ears. My workshop is a nest of stolen mirrors and salvaged instruments, all held in place by contractile fibers I've trained to respond to specific temperatures. When I need light, I breathe on them warm. When I need darkness, I whisper cold.

My work is reading the plant's dreams.

Each time the eyes open and the living mirrors form their arrays, I map the burn patterns. Not on paper—paper would be digested in minutes—but in my own modified cortex, where the Rime has grown special folds for storing optical data. The patterns are not random. They are messages, or perhaps memories, or perhaps the schematic diagrams for machines that haven't been built yet. Some show the industrial infrastructure as it once was: clean steel and precise angles. Others show configurations that never existed: factories made of bone, cooling systems that use blood instead of water, turbines that spin backwards through time.

The Choir thinks I'm mad. They're not wrong. But madness here is just another metabolic state, and the Rime finds my particular frequency useful.

I've learned to read the plant's growth patterns like sheet music. Each spiral of new growth encodes data about temperature, mineral content, and the slow decay of metals far below. By following specific root systems, I can trace the shape of machines that have been completely digested, their iron ghosts still conducting signals through the living neural network. The deeper I go, the older the data becomes, until I'm reading the dreams of factories that predate the industrial settlement by centuries.

There was something here before. Something that knew the machines were coming.

When I sleep—which is rare now, sleep being another kind of digestion—I dream in the plant's language. Vast slow thoughts that take weeks to complete a single sentence. In these dreams, the Rime shows me what it's building: a new kind of consciousness that runs on cellulose and rust, a mind the size of a landscape that thinks in geological time. The old machines are not obstacles to this plan. They are components. As am I.

My favorite discovery is the turbine garden. Deep beneath the eastern chimneys, seventeen industrial turbines have been completely engulfed but continue to spin. The plant has learned to grow in perfect balance around the blades, creating a living bearing that never needs oil. The rotation generates a weak electrical field that the Rime uses to—I think—perform calculations. When I place my modified hands on the root networks here, I can feel the math happening. Equations about growth rates and metabolic efficiency, solved in real-time by the marriage of metal and cellulose.

Sometimes the youngest members of the Choir come to me with questions. They still remember curiosity. I show them my collection of growth charts, my maps of the mirror-burns, my catalogs of every hybrid tissue I've found. Most leave confused. But a few stay, and I teach them to read the plant's handwriting in the rust. One girl—though she is only partly a girl now, her left hemisphere replaced by a colony of calculating fungi—has begun to see the patterns faster than I can. When my eyes finally fill with black water, she will become the new Cartographer.

The strangest thing I've mapped is my own transformation. Each month I document the percentage of my body that remains original. It started at the extremities—fingernails becoming root-tips, skin developing chlorophyll—but now it's reached my core. My heart beats in time with the nearest chimney. My blood is half sap. When I speak, spores come out with the words.

But my mind—my mind has expanded. I can think with the entire valley now, feel every new shoot and every collapsing trunk as extensions of my own neural network. When the eyes open, I open with them. When the mirrors focus their terrible light, I am both the burner and the burned.

I've found the oldest map. Not drawn but grown—a careful arrangement of fossil roots that predate the industrial ruins by millennia. It shows the valley before the Rime, when it was just stone and ice. But more importantly, it shows what's beneath: a vast geometric pattern of metal deposits that match no natural formation. The machines didn't come here by accident. They were called.

The Hollow Choir sings of the Mother that eats her children. But I know now that she is not eating.

She is assembling.

Each digested machine, each absorbed human, each hybrid thought—they are all components in something too large for any single mind to comprehend. The Braided Rime is not a superorganism. It is a factory. And what it's building is a new kind of life that needs both metal and meat, both calculation and dream, both the quick thought of blood and the slow wisdom of cellulose.

I am not trapped here. I am a volunteer engineer in the most ambitious biomedical project ever undertaken. My maps are blueprints. My madness is methodology. And when the work is complete—perhaps in another century, perhaps tomorrow—the entire valley will open like an eye, and what emerges will make the distinction between plant and machine and human seem as quaint as separating water from the river that carries it.

Until then, I catalog. I calculate. I grow my maps in the warm darkness while the turbines spin their metal prayers through my fingertips. And sometimes, when the mists are thick and the light turns religious, I remember my name from before.

But I prefer Cartographer. It's more accurate.

After all, I am not drawing the territory anymore.

I am helping the territory draw itself.
