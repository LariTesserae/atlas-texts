I find myself as one of the Memory Keepers, dwelling where the cathedral spires pierce upward through the living water. My form shifts between states—sometimes I am merely a shimmer in the current, a distortion that catches fragments of light from the bioluminescent blooms. Other times, I coalesce into something almost tangible: a figure of translucent flesh threaded with veins of liquid silver, my edges forever dissolving and reforming.

My purpose is to tend the remnants. Each morning—if morning exists here where light emanates from within rather than above—I drift through the labyrinthine passages between the towering formations. My tendrils, delicate as spider silk but strong as steel cable, reach out to brush against the half-buried artifacts. Through touch, I absorb their stories: a corroded data pad still pulsing with corrupted images of a world that was; a child's toy, its synthetic fur now home to colonies of luminous algae; fragments of infrastructure that once channeled air instead of water.

I am both historian and medium. The memories I collect don't stay locked within me—they seep out through my permeable skin, mixing with the currents. Sometimes another keeper will pass through a memory-rich stream and suddenly know the taste of bread, the feeling of dry wind, the weight of walking on solid ground. We share these phantom sensations like gifts, or curses.

The great shadows that move in the depths—we call them the Forgotten. They were here before the transformation, before the boundaries dissolved. Their thrumming songs speak of even older dissolutions, cycles upon cycles of worlds remade. I've learned to interpret their harmonics: warnings about unstable currents, predictions of where new ruins might surface, lamentations for what continually slips away.

My dwelling is a hollow within one of the cathedral spires, where the walls pulse with a slow heartbeat. Here I've cultivated a garden of memory-coral, each polyp a living record shaped from the essence of recovered artifacts. They glow in patterns that tell stories—wars and weddings, discoveries and defeats, final messages sent as the waters rose. Visitors come, both the ethereal wanderers and the more substantial beings, to read these luminous texts, to remember what they never knew.

Sometimes I wonder if I was human once. The question feels like trying to grasp water—it flows through my consciousness without finding purchase. Perhaps it doesn't matter. What matters is the tending, the remembering, the careful preservation of what refuses to be entirely lost.

In the quiet moments, when the currents slow to near stillness, I extend myself through the spire's apertures and feel the weight of all this transformed world. The unease that visitors sense—I've come to understand it as the discomfort of perpetual becoming, of never quite settling into a final form. But there is beauty in the flux. In the way light bends through our bodies. In the songs that echo between catastrophe and creation.

I am keeper and kept, memory and forgetting, solid history dissolving into liquid myth. This is what I've become in the vast, submerged realm—a living archive in a world that refuses to stop rewriting itself.
