I am a Weave-Wright, though the term suggests more agency than truly exists in our work. My dwelling clings to the underside of a great fungal platform, where the spore-light barely reaches, and I spend my cycles in the company of the Thread Gardens.

The Thread Gardens are not cultivated by us, but we tend to them nonetheless. They are colonies of filamentous organisms—somewhere between fungus and jellyfish—that extrude gossamer strands downward into the thicker mist below. These threads, finer than any silk the land-worlds might know, drink continuously from the gradient layers of our atmosphere. As they feed, they grow heavy with condensed nutrients, swelling like pearls along a string.

My body has adapted to this work over the many cycles. My limbs have lengthened beyond the typical Oorani form, and my membrane has developed specialized patches that can detect the subtlest variations in thread tension. When I move among the hanging gardens, I am more spider than person, my six primary appendages finding purchase on the slick surfaces while my secondary tendrils test each thread for readiness.

The threads must be harvested at precisely the right moment—too early and they dissolve back into the mist, too late and they calcify into useless stone. The window is brief, measured in heartbeats, and can only be felt, never seen. When ready, I wrap the threads around my forelimbs in ancient patterns, each configuration designed to preserve different qualities. Some patterns maintain flexibility, others encourage a slow crystallization that will, over many cycles, become the obsidian glass we prize.

I am never alone in this work, though my companions are not Oorani. The Thread Gardens host colonies of the Trembling Ones—microscopic symbionts that live within the filaments themselves. They communicate through bioelectric pulses that I can feel when my membrane touches the threads. Over the cycles, I have learned to interpret their simple language: *danger/safety*, *hunger/satisfaction*, *growth/decline*. They tell me stories of the deep mist, of the pressures below where even we cannot venture, where the air becomes too thick to breathe and strange things move in the compression zones.

My dwelling reflects my trade. The walls are not the typical gourd-and-seaweed construction, but a living mesh of carefully trained threads that I have convinced to grow in architectural patterns. The structure breathes with me, expanding and contracting with the pressure changes. Sometimes I wake to find new rooms have budded in the night, tiny alcoves where the threads have decided to try a new configuration. I inhabit my home as much as it inhabits me.

The other Oorani regard Weave-Wrights with a mixture of respect and unease. We spend so much time at the margins, suspended between the city and the deep, that we absorb some of that liminal quality. My membrane has taken on a different texture—rougher, more responsive to pressure changes. When I drift through the common spaces, the spores scatter in unusual patterns around me, as if recognizing something foreign in my bioluminescent signature.

But there is purpose in this peripheral existence. The threads we harvest become the sinew that holds Oor together. They reinforce the platforms, strengthen the vine-tethers, and even serve as a kind of neural network for the city itself. In the depths of the night-cycle, when the spore-light dims to almost nothing, you can sometimes see the faint bioelectric glow of information traveling along the thread-paths—messages, warnings, or perhaps just the city dreaming.

Recently, the Trembling Ones have been speaking of change. Their pulses carry a new frequency, one I don't recognize. The threads are growing differently, reaching deeper, pulling up flavors of mist that taste of something ancient stirring below. Some of the elder Weave-Wrights have begun to weave new patterns, configurations we've never used before, as if preparing for something we cannot yet name.

I continue my work, tendril over tendril, feeling my way through the hanging gardens. Each thread I harvest carries the weight of our suspended world, the tension between what we are and what we might become. In Oor, we are all waiting for the universe to decide, but down here in the margins, touching the dreams of the deep mist through silken mediators, I sometimes wonder if we aren't also part of that decision—slow architects of our own transformation, one trembling thread at a time.

The mist is thickening today. The threads hang heavier than usual, swollen with secrets from below. I wrap them carefully around my extended limbs, feeling the Trembling Ones pulse their ancient songs against my membrane. Whatever comes, we will weave it into being, or it will weave us. In Oor, the difference has never mattered.
