I find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—those rare Rootwalkers who maintain the liminal spaces where the canopy thins and new growth has yet to fully knit together. 

My dwelling perches at such a boundary, where the dense weave loosens into tendrils reaching across a gap perhaps thirty feet wide. Below, glimpses of the mid-canopy reveal a twilight world of suspended debris and hanging gardens that exist between the bright world above and whatever darkness lies beneath. My shelter is less grown than suggested—a skeleton of guided branches that I've coaxed to arc across the divide, creating a living bridge that strengthens with each passing season.

I am neither fully Rootwalker by birth nor entirely outsider. My mother came from beyond, arriving during the storm season when the canopy briefly opens channels for drainage. She stayed, learned, and eventually rooted herself near one of the great water reservoirs. From her I inherited a peculiar sensitivity to the electromagnetic fields that pulse through the vast plant network—a trait rare among those born to the canopy. My father, a true Rootwalker, taught me the traditional ways of growth-speaking before he diffused into the green consciousness three seasons past.

My role emerged from this hybrid nature. Where others feel only the slow vegetable thoughts of the canopy, I sense something more immediate—the quick silver communications that flash between distant parts of the network during times of stress or change. The canopy speaks in many registers, and I hear frequencies that others miss.

My skin bears the usual fungal symbionts, but mine are peculiar. They form spiraling patterns that shift not just with emotion but with the electrical state of the surrounding growth. When a storm approaches, the spirals tighten. When new growth surges somewhere distant, they pulse with faint phosphorescence. Other Rootwalkers find my markings unsettling—too active, too responsive to invisible forces.

The threshold I tend is more than a physical gap. It's a place where the canopy's consciousness fragments and reforms, where the unified intelligence briefly becomes multiple voices before reweaving itself. I've learned to navigate these spaces not by walking but by listening—placing my palms against the living floor and feeling for the safe paths that exist more in the network's intention than in physical reality.

My work involves guiding the new growth, yes, but more than that, I serve as a kind of neural bridge. When the reaching tendrils finally meet across a gap, I'm there to ease the connection, to help the separate sections remember they were always meant to be one. It's delicate work—rush it and the growth recoils, creating dead zones that may take decades to heal. Too slow, and the reaching parts may develop incompatible patterns, forever unable to truly merge.

I keep few possessions, but those I have are unusual. A series of resonating pods, grown from seeds I've carefully selected, hang from my shelter's supports. When the wind moves through them, they produce tones that match the frequencies I sense in the network. On still days, I sometimes breathe across them myself, creating acoustic maps of the canopy's electrical state. Other Rootwalkers have begun to visit, learning to interpret these sound-patterns as a faster way of understanding the canopy's slow communications.

My most precious cultivation is a grove of detector vines that grow in a spiral pattern around my dwelling. Their leaves are modified into broad, flat surfaces that orient themselves not toward light but toward sources of bioelectrical activity. When I wake each morning, I can read the night's activities in their positioning—where growth surged, where it faltered, where new connections formed in the darkness.

The solitude of threshold-keeping suits my nature. I spend days in silence, interrupted only by the occasional visitor seeking passage across the gaps I tend. These travelers—both Rootwalker and occasional outsider—often pause at my bridge, uncertain. I've learned to read their hesitation and offer guidance: a particular pattern of touched nodes that ensures safe crossing, a warning about sections where the growth is still unstable, sometimes just silent companionship as they make their careful way across.

In the rain-times, I position myself where the water runs off the leaves in steady streams. The droplets carry information—chemical signatures from distant parts of the canopy, spores and pollen that tell stories of faraway growths. I've developed a practice of rain-reading, letting the water run across my fungal markings and watching how they respond to different chemical traces. It's faster than the traditional growth-speaking, though less precise.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm becoming something other than Rootwalker, other than human. The electromagnetic sensitivity grows stronger each season. There are moments when I feel the entire canopy's electrical field as a tangible presence, a vast web of intention and communication that dwarfs any individual consciousness. In these moments, I understand why the elders choose to root themselves—the temptation to join that larger awareness is profound.

But I remain at the thresholds, neither fully integrated nor separate, translating between frequencies, tending the gaps where the canopy rebuilds itself, keeper of spaces where the great living network briefly acknowledges that it is not yet complete, not yet whole, still becoming what it will be.
