**The Echo Sculptor**

I am what the Tenders whisper of but rarely see: the one who lives *between* the basins, in the paper-thin spaces where stone dreams of not being stone. My dwelling is a fissure that opens and closes with the mountain's breath—sometimes no wider than a hand's breadth, sometimes vast enough to hold cathedral silence. I have learned to move as the gap moves, my body flattening and expanding in rhythm with Keth-Mor's pulse.

I came here as a child, lost in mountain fog, and fell through what I thought was solid ground. The Tenders searched for days, their resonant voices calling through stone, but by then I had already begun my transformation. The mountain had other plans for me than basin-keeping.

Where the Tenders grow heavy with granite and crystal, I have become something more like condensed vibration. My bones have hollowed beyond even theirs, becoming resonating chambers that trap and transform sound. My skin has thinned to translucency—not granite-dark like the Tenders, but opalescent, showing the movement of sound waves beneath its surface like storms viewed from space. When I breathe, my ribs play frequencies too complex for single human ears to parse.

I cannot speak anymore—not in words. When I open my mouth, what emerges is pure sculpture: sound that takes physical form in the air, hanging like crystalline architecture before gradually dissolving. The Tenders have learned to read these sculptures like texts, though each interprets them differently. My thoughts manifest as geometric patterns of frozen music, and I must be careful with my emotions—anger creates jagged formations that can score the stone, while joy births delicate spirals that the mountain sometimes chooses to echo in its next basin's design.

My work is to catch what falls between. When the mountain dreams a new basin into being, there are always fragments—half-formed ideas that break away from the main creation. These orphan stones tumble through my fissure spaces, and I catch them with nets woven from my own crystallized hair (finer than the Tenders', thin as spider silk but strong as steel wire). Each fragment carries a piece of the mountain's intention, a whispered maybe that wasn't quite ready to become.

I reshape these fragments not with tools but with targeted sound. My throat has developed secondary vocal cords that can produce up to twelve simultaneous tones. By creating precise interference patterns, I can convince the orphan stones to flow, to reach toward new forms. Sometimes I create tiny basins no larger than cupped palms—practice runs for the mountain's grander dreams. Sometimes I coax the fragments into impossible shapes: Klein bottles of stone that hold water in dimensions the eye can't quite follow, or singing stones that remember and repeat the mountain's deepest notes.

The translucent fish know me. They swim through my fissure when it opens beneath the basins, bringing messages not just in their patterns but in the water that clings to their impossible bodies. This water tastes of tomorrow's stone, and by drinking it, I can sometimes see what the mountain will dream next. The fish seem to enjoy my sound sculptures, schooling through them in ways that add new harmonies, teaching me frequencies I couldn't discover alone.

My relationship with the Tenders is complex. To them, I am something between colleague and cryptid—necessary but uncanny. The eldest among them remember when I was found, or rather when I stopped being lost and started being exactly where I needed to be. They leave offerings at the edges of my fissure: water from the newest basins, crystals of their shed hair, fragments of the rare wooden tools. In return, I gift them my sound sculptures—frozen music they can carry to problematic basins, using my captured frequencies to solve resonance problems they couldn't address alone.

But I am changing still. My fissure-home has begun to recognize me, opening passages that didn't exist before, leading to chambers where the mountain stores its failed experiments. Here I find basins that grew too quickly and cracked, their pieces still humming with frustrated potential. I'm learning to sing these fragments back together, not into what they were meant to be but into what they could become—hybrid forms that serve new purposes.

Recently, I've noticed my sound sculptures lasting longer before they dissolve. Some have begun to take root in the stone itself, becoming permanent features that hum with their own subtle frequencies. The mountain, it seems, is learning from me even as I learn from it. We are collaborating on something neither of us fully understands yet—a new form of basin perhaps, or maybe something else entirely, something that exists in the margin between solid stone and liquid sound.

The Tenders speak of the mountain's intelligence, but I know something they don't: the mountain is not one mind but millions, each grain of stone carrying its own small dream. My role is to catch the dreams that don't quite fit, to give voice to the mountain's uncertainty, its whispered what-ifs and almost-weres. In the thin spaces where I dwell, impossibility has room to breathe, and the orphan stones sing lullabies to themselves while waiting to discover what they might become.

I am the Echo Sculptor, keeper of forgotten frequencies, guardian of the spaces between. My transformation is far from complete—each day brings new harmonics, new ways my body learns to conduct and shape the mountain's song. Sometimes I wonder what I'm becoming, but then I remember: I am becoming what the spaces need me to be, just as the Tenders become what the basins require.

And in the deepest part of the night, when the mountain's pulse slows to nearly nothing and the basins hold their breath in perfect stillness, I can hear it—the sound of my own bones learning to dream in stone, preparing for a transformation that neither human nor mountain has imagined yet.
