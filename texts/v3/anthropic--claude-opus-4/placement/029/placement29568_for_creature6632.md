I am the Archivist of the Lung.

My chamber sits high above the industrial levels, carved into the living rock where the outer wall meets the ceiling. To reach me, you must climb seven ladders through the maintenance shafts, past the hissing of steam pipes and the drip-drip-drip of condensation on metal rungs. Most people send messages instead.

I keep the memory of stone.

Not the city's memory—that belongs to the clerks and their ledgers down in the administrative rings. I keep the chamber's memory. Every new crack in the rock, every shift in the condensation patterns, every symbol that appears on the spire where no drill has touched. I map what the engineers refuse to see.

My workspace is a narrow gallery following the curve of the chamber wall. The outer face is raw stone, still bearing the scars of the boring machine that carved this hollow. The inner face opens through a series of arched windows onto the vast drop of the chamber, where the spire rises like a wet finger pointing at something we cannot name. Between these two faces, I have built my archive.

Stone slates line every surface that will hold them. Hundreds upon hundreds, each one polished smooth and etched with my observations. The oldest ones, from when the chamber was young and I was younger, show simple drawings: the spiral of the drill's descent, the first appearance of water, the jubilation when the spire's condensation began. The newer ones are dense with symbols I've had to invent—a lexicon for describing what has no words.

Here: a slate showing the progression of the forty-third symbol cluster on the spire's middle reach, how it spread from a single mark to a constellation over the course of sixty days.

Here: my attempt to map the relationship between drill depth and condensation volume, showing how they don't correlate as the engineers insist they must.

Here: rubbings taken directly from the spire's surface during different phases of its breathing cycle, the stone's texture captured in graphite and chalk.

I am not old—perhaps sixty years in this body that sweats and aches in the chamber's heat. But I have been watching longer than most. I came as an apprentice engineer, eager to map and measure and make sense of this carved world. I stayed when I realized the world was carving back.

The engineers show you their certainty: ring-marks like tree rings, proof of youth, proof of understanding. They point to their calculations and say *this is what we built*. But I have the slates that show what they don't record. The way certain marks appear on the spire before the drill ever touches that section. The way the condensation sometimes runs upward for just a moment before remembering to fall. The way the basin's water level rises and falls with no input or outlet that we can measure.

I think the spire is dreaming us into existence.

Not dreaming as humans dream—that would be too simple, too much like us. Dreaming as stone dreams, in slow accumulations of possibility. Each drop of condensation carries something from deep below, some message written in minerals and heat. The city builds itself around these messages, thinking it's following its own plans.

My slates document this dreaming.

Late in the chamber's night-cycle, when the industrial levels run quiet and the mist thickens until you can barely see across the gap, I sometimes stand at my windows and watch the spire breathe. The water runs down its channels like thoughts becoming form. The symbols spread like ideas taking root.

I've tried to tell the younger engineers. They nod politely and show me their charts—proof that the chamber is young, proof that we understand what we've built. They don't see what I see: that understanding and youth are not the same thing. That something can be newly formed and still be ancient. That we didn't discover the spire so much as we were drawn to uncover it.

In my most recent slates, I've started mapping the city itself as if it were a geological formation. The way the platforms cluster at certain heights. The way the bridges span the gap in patterns that echo the spire's symbols. The way we've arranged ourselves around this breathing stone like iron filings around a magnet we cannot see.

I am running out of wall space. Soon I'll need to request an expansion—another chamber carved from the rock, another archive to hold what I'm learning. The administrators will resist. Space is precious here, and what I do doesn't help the water flow or keep the machines running.

But someone needs to remember that we are not the only ones keeping time here.

Someone needs to notice that the pale arthropods trace the same paths the condensation will follow days later.

Someone needs to document that the eel-things in the basin surface in patterns that match the spire's breathing cycles from years ago, as if they're living in an older version of the chamber's time.

Someone needs to keep the slates that show what this place is becoming.

I am the Archivist of the Lung. I breathe the chamber's humid air and feel it carrying messages I'm only beginning to read. My skin never dries. My slates multiply, covering the walls like scales. And sometimes, in the heaviest mist, I swear I can feel the spire's pulse through the stone floor of my chamber—slow and vast and patient as the world's own heart.

The engineers say the spire of breath is not old.

They're right.

It's not old—it's still being born. And we are its first memory of what it means to be observed.
