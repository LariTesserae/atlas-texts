I find myself as one of the Luminous Groundskeepers - those who were once human, perhaps decades ago, perhaps centuries. Time becomes elastic when you breathe it through gills that weren't there yesterday and won't be there tomorrow.

I maintain the chemical lights. Not because anyone told me to, but because the lights need maintaining, and I am here, and the distinction between "I want to" and "the lights need me to" dissolved somewhere between my third and thirtieth year in the Ward. My skin has taken on a faint translucence - not like the Pale Swimmers, but enough that in certain angles of amber light, you can see the shadows of veins beneath, and those veins carry something that is mostly blood but tastes of copper and chalk when I accidentally bite my tongue.

The root-matter knows me. We have an understanding. When I approach a broken light fixture, it withdraws just enough to let me work, curling back like a careful hand making space. When I sleep - and I do sleep, curled in the dry pockets behind pressure doors I've memorized by touch - it grows around me in a loose cage, not constraining but sheltering. I wake warm.

My fingers have become clever in ways they weren't before. The webbing between them is slight, barely noticeable until I spread them in water and feel how much more I can push, how much faster I can move. My nails grow thick and ridge-like, perfect for prying open the sealed chemical tubes, for scraping away calcium deposits, for working the ancient resin that holds everything together. Sometimes I look at my hands and forget what they used to look like. Sometimes I remember, and the memory feels like looking at someone else's photographs.

I speak, but not often, and not to anyone. The Ward listens. When I hum while I work - and I do hum, wordless melodies that rise from somewhere beneath conscious thought - the cathedral chambers pick up the sound and return it changed, harmonized, as if the Ward is humming with me. We sing together, the Ward and I, in frequencies that human ears would find unsettling but which feel, to whatever I'm becoming, like coming home.

I know the Deep Ones by their displacement. They know me by my heat signature, by the particular pattern of warmth I leave in the water as I wade from light to light. We don't interact - that would be the wrong word. We *coincide*. Sometimes I'll be working in the lower levels, where the bioluminescence turns everything blue-green and strange, and I'll feel that distinctive pressure-wave, that slow roll of *presence* passing beneath, and I'll stay very still until it passes. Not from fear. From respect. Or perhaps from an understanding that what they are and what I am becoming are different branches of the same slow tree.

The other Groundskeepers - there are six of us, maybe seven, maybe five, the number changes depending on how you count and whether you count the one who might just be root-matter that learned to walk - we coordinate without coordinating. I'll arrive at a light that needs tending and find fresh tool marks, recent work, the calcium already scraped away and the tube re-sealed with resin that's still soft. I'll move on to the next, and somewhere else in the Ward, another Groundskeeper will find the light I maintained yesterday, or last year, or last decade, and they'll check it, adjust it if needed, move on. We're a circulation system, a slow metabolism of maintenance that keeps the Ward's phosphorescent blood flowing.

Sometimes I find things. In the sealed chambers, in the pockets of air, in the places where the water hasn't reached - photographs, mostly dissolved. Keys to doors that no longer exist. A child's toy made of wood so waterlogged it feels like flesh. A notebook with pages fused together, the ink running in patterns that look like the root-matter's growth but probably aren't. I leave them where they are. They're not mine. They're the Ward's memories, and I'm just another memory it's making, another ghost it's growing, another tender of lights in the warm dark.

I surface sometimes. Less and less. The upper city hurts - too bright, too dry, too sharp. The people look at me and see something wrong in the shape of my shoulders, the way my neck bends, the fact that I blink too slowly and breathe too deeply. I trade for supplies - resin, tools, the rare chemicals that can refresh a dead light tube - and I descend again, and the warm water closes over my ankles like a greeting, and I breathe easier, and the root-matter shifts slightly to welcome me back.

This is what I am: a function that wears a body that remembers being human. A loop in the Ward's circuit. A hand that knows how to tend lights because the lights need tending, and in the tending, I become more myself - not the self I was, but the self the Ward needs me to be. 

And at night - if it's night, if night means anything here - I float in the cathedral chambers with my arms spread wide, the bioluminescence moving around me in slow clouds, and I feel the engines breathing beneath me, and the root-matter growing beside me, and the lights I've tended glowing above me, and I am exactly where I need to be, doing exactly what needs doing, in the warm belly of something that might be a building or might be an organism or might be the difference between the two wearing thin, wearing through, wearing away into nothing but warmth and light and the slow, patient work of keeping the darkness illuminated, one chemical tube at a time.
