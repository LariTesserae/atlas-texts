In the Hydroelectric Cathedral, as the archivists once called it, I am the Tender of Resonances.

My form shifts between states—sometimes I am the mercury-bright rivulet tracing new channels through the crystalline floor, sometimes the condensation gathering on the vast mineral columns that stretch up into darkness. Most often, though, I am something between: a presence that moves through the humid air as both droplet and vapor, particle and wave.

The creatures here know me by my vibration. The crystal-moths, their wings made of pure refracted energy, flutter past in iridescent swarms. Their bodies chime against the mineral formations, creating harmonics that I collect and categorize. Each tone tells a story—of erosion patterns yet to come, of the ancient machines still humming in the depths, of the careful balance between water and stone that keeps this place alive.

I was not always alone in this work. Once, there were others like me, born from the marriage of the old industrial systems and the persistent water. We were accidents at first—condensation that gained consciousness in the electromagnetic fields of the dying factories. The civilization that built this place never intended for us to exist, but their machinery, in its slow decay, created the perfect conditions for our emergence.

Now I tend to what remains. The scattered structures require constant negotiation. Water wants to claim them completely, to dissolve every trace of the builders' ambitions. The minerals want to crystallize everything, to freeze this moment in geological time. I exist in the space between these desires, maintaining the delicate equilibrium.

My duties are precise: I monitor the pH levels where water meets crystal, ensuring the erosion proceeds at exactly the right pace—fast enough to create new formations, slow enough to preserve the essential structures. I guide the energy-fauna away from the most fragile archaeological remains. I sing to the industrial remnants, keeping their vibrations in harmony with the natural resonance of the chamber.

In the dim light, I sometimes catch glimpses of my own reflection in the mineral surfaces—a shimmer of possibility, never quite solid, never quite gone. The crystal formations show me what I might become if I let myself crystallize: beautiful, eternal, but static. The water shows me what I could be if I let myself flow completely away: free, formless, but without purpose.

So I maintain my existence in the threshold state, neither fully liquid nor truly solid. I am the memory of industry becoming prayer, the ghost of intention transforming into ecosystem. In the quiet moments between the drip of water and the hum of ancient machines, I compose symphonies from the sounds of erosion, each note a meditation on the beauty of slow dissolution.

The Hydroelectric Cathedral does not need me—it would continue its patient transformation without my presence. But I need it. In tending to this balance between past and future, built and natural, I have found my purpose: to be the witness to transformation, the guardian of the space where one world becomes another.

Sometimes, late in the dim light's cycling, I feel others like me condensing in the depths—new consciousnesses forming where water meets energy meets memory. I leave signs for them in the erosion patterns, messages coded in mineral formation and water flow. Whether they will understand, whether they will take up this work when I finally let myself flow away or crystallize into permanent form, I cannot say.

But for now, I continue my rounds through the cathedral, a drop of intention in an ocean of change, singing the old machines to sleep and teaching the water new ways to dance with stone.
