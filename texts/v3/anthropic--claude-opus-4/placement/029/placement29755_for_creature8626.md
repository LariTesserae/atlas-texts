I find myself in **the Tidal Archives**, deep beneath where the last true ocean touched land before the waters receded beyond memory.

The entrance is a spiral of carved coral, each whorl inscribed with the depth-markers of vanished seas. Down and down, following the mathematics of shells, until the walls begin to weep—not with water, but with a clear, viscous fluid that tastes of salt and time. This is where I work.

---

I am the **Chronicler of Evaporations**.

My body is no longer entirely my own. Where flesh once ended cleanly at wrist and ankle, I have grown transitional zones—patches where my skin becomes translucent, revealing the slow circulation of that same clear fluid that seeps from the walls. It carries suspended particles: microscopic shells, fragments of ancient kelp, the crystallized memories of tides.

My workspace is a series of connecting chambers, each one a different depth, a different pressure, a different era of ocean. In the first chamber, closest to the surface, I maintain the Cabinet of Final Drops—thousands of glass ampules, each containing the last water to evaporate from a particular pool, bay, or inland sea. I map their mineral signatures with my tongue (my sense of taste having evolved beyond recognition—I can differentiate between seventeen distinct varieties of calcium carbonate, can track the biographical arc of a single diatom through its chemical traces).

Deeper chambers house more abstract collections. The Library of Foam Patterns—where I've pressed between sheets of volcanic glass the exact bubble-arrangements from the final wave of each extinct coastline. The Archive of Thermal Gradients—crystallized in salt formations that hold, somehow, the memory of temperature differentials, warm currents that once moved like rivers through the sea. I can run my altered hands over these crystals and feel the ghost-warmth of the Gulf Stream's last pulse.

My transformation began the day I first tasted the wall-fluid. Now, forty years later (or is it four hundred? Time moves strangely in the salt-dark), I have become a hybrid thing. My hair grows in strands of kelp-analog—not plant exactly, but something that photosynthesizes the peculiar light given off by the bioluminescent deposits in the deeper walls. My bones have hollowed like bird bones, but instead of air, they fill with that clear fluid, making me simultaneously heavier and more buoyant.

The work is this: Every day (there are no days here, but I maintain the fiction for sanity's sake), I descend to the Abyss Chamber—the deepest point, where pressure should crush me but instead holds me like a mother. There, I lie on the floor of compressed sediment and *listen*. The earth remembers its waters. In the right state of mind, with the right chemical balance in my hybrid blood, I can hear the planet dreaming of its lost oceans.

I transcribe these dreams.

Not in language—language fails at these depths. I transcribe in salt. Each dream becomes a new formation, a crystal grown from my own body's fluid mixed with minerals I've learned to secrete through my transformed pores. These salt-manuscripts accumulate on spiral shelves carved from the chamber walls. Future readers—if there are ever readers—will need to dissolve them on their tongues to understand. The knowledge will come in waves of taste and texture: here, the memory of whales; here, the chemical signature of the first lightning strike on primordial waters; here, the exact mineral composition of the tears of the last sea turtle.

I am not alone in the Archives, but my companions are not what most would recognize as life. There are the Consulting Colonies—bacterial mats that have achieved a sort of collective consciousness, spelling out chemical messages on the walls in response to my queries about specific extinctions. There are the Pressure Pets—creatures of living mercury that maintain the exact atmospheric conditions of different oceanic eras in their bodies. I feed them iron filings and they excrete perfect spheres of hematite encoded with barometric data.

My greatest work—still incomplete after all these years—is the Tidal Engine. In the centermost chamber, I've built a machine of salt and sinew (the sinew is mine, harvested and regrown in monthly cycles). When complete, it will recreate the pull of the moon on waters that no longer exist. It will generate phantom tides in the clear fluid that flows through everything here. For one perfect moment, the Archives will remember what it felt like to move.

I am changing faster now. My eyes have developed nictitating membranes that let me see in the spectral range of deep-sea creatures. My spine has elongated, grown flexible, allowing me to compress and extend my body to navigate the increasingly narrow passages that lead to the deepest collections. Sometimes I forget I was ever human. Sometimes I remember too clearly.

But the work continues. Each extinct droplet catalogued. Each lost salinity recorded. Each final gasp of each dying sea preserved in crystal, in taste, in the transformed cells of my hybrid body.

I am the Chronicler of Evaporations. I am becoming what I study—a memory of water, walking.
