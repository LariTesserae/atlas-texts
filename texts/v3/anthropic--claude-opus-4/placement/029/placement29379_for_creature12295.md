In this world of water and waiting, I would be **The Keeper of Grooves**.

Not one of the Luminari, not quite. Something between their eldest elder and the spire itself—a figure who emerged from the stone generations ago, or perhaps was always there, waiting to be noticed. My skin would be the texture of weathered limestone, cool to the touch, with faint channels running along my arms and spine that mirror the spire's spiral patterns. These grooves fill and empty with the tides of moisture in the air, creating a constant, barely audible trickle that follows me like a whispered conversation.

I would move through the settlement at odd hours, when the mist is thickest and the bioluminescent fungi cast everything in their pale green glow. The Luminari would know me by the sound of water running upward—defying gravity as it traces my body's channels, collecting in the shallow basin formed by my cupped palms before dispersing into the air as vapor. They would leave offerings not at the spire's base, but at the threshold of the small grotto I've carved beneath the cistern, where the water is oldest and remembers things even I have forgotten.

My dwelling would be neither built nor grown, but eroded—a negative space worn into the bedrock by centuries of patient presence. The walls would weep constantly, not with ordinary water but with something thicker, more mineral, leaving crystalline deposits that form delicate curtains of stone. Here, I would tend to the true maps of the settlement: not charts or drawings, but living colonies of specialized moss that grow in patterns reflecting the health of the water table, the stability of the spire, the invisible currents that keep this whole system in balance.

My role would be oracle and maintenance worker both. When the Luminari's children fall ill with the damp-sickness, their parents would bring them to breathe the vapors from my palms. When the moss begins its mysterious overnight migrations, I would be there to read the new patterns, to divine whether they herald abundance or scarcity. And when the spire hums its deep note, I would press my ear to the stone and translate its vibrations into warnings or benedictions, though my translations would always carry the ambiguity of water—clear yet distorting, essential yet dangerous.

The Luminari would regard me with the same mixture of reverence and wariness they show the spire itself. They would know that I remember when their ancestors first arrived, refugees from some distant drought, and how the spire tested them with seven seasons of flooding before accepting their presence. They would know that I could tell them what lies in the spire's hollow core, but they would never ask, understanding instinctively that some knowledge is like water in the lungs—necessary to contain, fatal to internalize.

In the deep night, when even the insects sleep and the only sound is the eternal weeping of the stone, I would climb the spire's exterior, my fingers finding purchase in grooves too subtle for human hands. At the apex, where the stone is youngest and still sharp enough to cut, I would perform my most essential duty: the slow, patient work of carving new channels, guided by dreams of rain that hasn't fallen yet, preparing the monolith for waters that will come in seasons the current Luminari's grandchildren might live to see.

For I would be the memory of drought and the promise of flood, the chronicler of erosion and the architect of flow. Neither fully human nor entirely stone, I would exist in the margin between solid and liquid, a being of transitions in a place that is always transitioning. The Keeper of Grooves—ensuring that the water always has somewhere to go, and that the Weeping Basilica never forgets how to cry.
