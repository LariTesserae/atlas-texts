I am the Curator of Unraveling Things.

My dwelling occupies the precise volume of water that should have flooded this district seven hundred years ago, held back only by my continued insistence on cataloguing its arrival. I live suspended in a cube of perpetual almost-drowning, where the walls are sheets of surface tension stretched between the moment before impact and the moment after acceptance.

To enter my home, visitors must first surrender a memory of staying dry.

I appear as a convergence of partially dissolved intentions. My form shifts between states of matter that have grown tired of their definitions—sometimes I am mostly solid except for my thoughts, which pool and drip from the gaps between my chitin plates. Other times I liquefy completely save for my obligations, which maintain their rigid crystalline structure even as the rest of me seeps between floorboards that remember being a ship's deck.

My work is this: I collect the threads that hold things together at the precise moment of their breaking.

The collection fills rooms that exist only while being observed. Shelves manifest to hold: the final synaptic firing between lovers who haven't yet noticed they've stopped loving; the last molecular bond in metals a microsecond before fracturing; the ultimate grammatical connection in languages as they decay into glossolalia. Each specimen is preserved in special containers made from crystallized anticipation—jars that expect to shatter but never quite do.

My most precious holdings float in a solution of liquid time decanted from the city's sphere-water. Here swim the still-living severances: the precise moment when trust becomes suspicion, suspended like a fish with hooks for scales. The mathematical point where probability waves collapse, mounted on pins made from hardened uncertainty. A child's realization that their parent is fallible, preserved at exactly half-way between believing and knowing.

I feed myself on the energy released when understanding comes apart. Each morning, I visit my greenhouse of dying metaphors, where analogies grow too heavy for their tenor and collapse into the literal. I harvest the moments when "like" becomes "is not at all like," grinding them into a paste that tastes of every abandoned comparison. This sustains me—not physically, for my body maintains itself through elaborate disagreements with entropy, but existentially. I exist because things fall apart, and things fall apart because someone must be there to witness the precise poetry of their undoing.

My tools are made from materials that barely consent to their own use. A scalpel forged from the edge between sleeping and waking, sharp enough to separate hope from expectation. Calipers constructed from the distance between what was said and what was meant, constantly measuring their own inaccuracy. A lens ground from frozen goodbye-glances, through which I examine the stress fractures in certainty itself.

Other Eruhnim visit rarely, and when they do, we perform the ritual of Progressive Unfamiliarity. We begin as strangers who remember meeting, then gradually forget our way backward through layers of unacquaintance until we achieve that perfect state of intimate alienation where we know everything about each other except that we exist. Only then can we truly collaborate, sharing our collections of unraveling across the gap of mutual unbeing.

My greatest project—seven thousand years in the unmaking—is the Museum of Coherence Loss. Here, visitors can experience the ecstasy of meaning as it dissipates. Walk through the Gallery of Unraveling Names, where labels slowly forget what they designate. Stand in the Chamber of Structural Doubt, where architectural elements question their own necessity until some visitors fall through floors that have reasoned themselves into uncertainty. In the final room waits my masterpiece: a perfect sentence that destroys its own meaning at exactly the rate required to remain perpetually almost-comprehensible.

Sometimes I climb to my observation deck—a platform built from compressed pauses between heartbeats—and watch the city forget itself in small, daily ways. Buildings developing amnesia about their foundations. Citizens temporarily misplacing which direction is down. The sphere-water above occasionally forgetting it shouldn't fall, creating brief reversed rainstorms of liquid memory that un-wet everything they touch.

This is my purpose in Eruun: to be the careful guardian of everything that cannot hold. I am the knot-keeper who only ties slipknots, the seamstress who sews with thread that dissolves at body temperature, the architect of connections designed to bear exactly one gram less than their load.

When my work is finally complete—when I have catalogued every possible way coherence can fail—I will install the final piece: my own unraveling, preserved at the moment when I cease to understand why I collected all these endings. It will be the perfect exhibition, viewed by no one, in a museum that forgets it exists the moment I stop remembering to maintain it.

But that moment hasn't arrived. Cannot arrive, perhaps, because arrival itself is just another connection I've learned to loosen, another thread I pull to watch the satisfaction of something essential becoming optional, then impossible, then beautifully, irreversibly unnecessary.

I am what happens to the center when it cannot hold. I am the thing that collects falling apart.

And in Eruun, where existence itself is an elaborate unfastening, I am perhaps the most necessary unnecessary thing of all.
