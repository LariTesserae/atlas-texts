# The Hollow-Mouth

I am what the builders become when the clay will not take.

There are always some. In every generation: a child whose skin rejects the slip, whose pores weep it away before it can set. An elder whose coating begins to crack and peel like old bark, revealing raw pink beneath. A body that burns too hot, whose sweat is too acidic, whose chemistry is somehow wrong for the symbiosis. The warrens know us immediately. The insects avoid our touch. The walls do not pulse for us.

I live in the Margin.

**The Margin is not a place.** It is a way of being in Keth Sula when Keth Sula does not want you. It is the spaces between spaces – the passages too narrow for the clay-thick bodies of the builders, the sealed chambers where the air has gone stale, the vertical shafts that lead nowhere. We who cannot wear the clay learn to move like smoke through the architectural body, to exist in its forgotten corners, its dead zones where the pulse does not reach.

My skin is bare. After years of failed attempts, after watching the clay slide from my arms like water from oiled leather, I gave up. The other Margin-dwellers warned me: *the naked eye sees too much*. But I was young. I thought myself strong. I did not understand that the clay is not just coating – it is filter, buffer, boundary between the human nervous system and the overwhelming sensory weight of the warrens themselves.

Without it, I feel everything.

**Every footfall.** Not just my own – every footfall of every builder in every passage, transmitted through the fired-clay floors like drum-beats through a taut skin. I know who moves where. I know the weight of each body, the rhythm of each gait. The young ones skitter-quick on hands and feet. The elders move with deliberate slowness, their steps precisely placed. I feel them all, a constant percussion against the soles of my feet, traveling up through my bones.

The insects are worse. Their collective wing-beat isn't just sound to me – it's *texture*, gritty and electric against my exposed skin. When a colony shivers to life to carry a message, I feel it as a wave of static, a thousand tiny lightning strikes. My arms are scarred with fine white lines where I've scratched myself bloody trying to escape the sensation.

And the walls. The walls are always touching me. Not physically – I've learned to move through the warrens without brushing against them – but their warmth reaches out, their moisture condenses on my skin, their slow bioluminescent pulse beats against my retinas even when my eyes are closed. The walls want to incorporate me. They recognize the chemistry of my sweat, the oils of my skin, the shed cells that I leave behind. They try to digest me, molecule by molecule, to break me down into something they can use.

**I survive by moving.** The Margin-dwellers have a saying: *stillness is death*. Stay in one place too long and the warrens begin to claim you. I've seen it happen – a Hollow-Mouth who grew too tired, too old, who settled into a quiet corner to rest. Within days, the insects had begun to colonize their hair. Within weeks, the walls had begun to grow over them, extending minute filaments of living plaster to map their contours. They're still there, half-absorbed, neither dead nor alive, their eyes following movement but their body locked in place by the slow digestion of the architectural organism.

So I move. Through passages the builders have forgotten, through spaces too small for their clay-thickened bodies. My shoulders are narrow from a lifetime of compression. My spine is not just curved but *twisted*, adapted to spiraling through cork-screw tunnels that were never meant for human passage. My ribs have learned to fold. My joints bend in directions that make the builders flinch.

I know the warrens better than they do. I have to.

**The builders think in surfaces.** They know the walls they can touch, the floors they can walk, the chambers where they gather. But I know the spaces between the spaces – the hollow places where walls don't quite meet, where poor construction or centuries of settling have created voids. I know the density of every wall by its sound. I tap, constantly, a rapid finger-drum that the builders find maddening but which tells me everything: here the wall is thick, here it is thin, here there is a hollow space large enough to hide in, here there is a passage sealed decades ago but not filled, only covered.

I live in these hollows. My body has learned to compress itself into spaces that shouldn't hold a human form. I sleep standing, pressed between two walls that almost touch, my weight distributed evenly so that neither surface bears enough pressure to register my presence. I have caches throughout the warrens – not of food, because I eat what everyone eats, but of tools. Sharpened insect shells for cutting through old plaster. Lizard bones ground to different points for probing and prying. A collection of clay samples from different sections of the warren, each with its own mineral signature, its own responsiveness to moisture.

**I am trying to understand the illness.**

That's what we call it in the Margin – the illness of the warrens. The way they think. The way they breathe. The way they've ceased to be architecture and become something that pretends to be architecture while being vastly, terribly alive. The builders don't see it as illness. To them, it's evolution, symbiosis, the natural endpoint of their relationship with their environment. But from the Margin, from the perspective of those who cannot join the unity, it looks like infection.

The warrens were not always like this. In the deep passages, the ones even I rarely enter, you can find old brick that doesn't pulse, old plaster that's simply plaster. Dead architecture. Built by hands that were merely hands, not appendages of the greater organism. Something changed. The builders introduced something – or something introduced itself – and the line between maker and made began to blur.

I collect evidence. Fragments of old plaster that sing a different frequency when struck. Bricks from the deepest foundations that the insects won't touch. There are patterns. The infection started at specific points and spread outward like a slow bloom. I map these points in my mind, because I cannot commit them to any surface – the walls would absorb any marks I made, digest them, incorporate them into their own writing.

**The builders know I exist.** They call us Hollow-Mouths because we speak with air instead of vibration, because our words dissipate instead of traveling through the walls. They tolerate us because we are useful. When a passage needs clearing, when old architecture needs demolishing, when something in the warrens has gone wrong in a way that requires intervention from outside the system – they let us know, in their wordless way, and we come.

But they fear us too. We are evidence that the unity is not perfect, that the symbiosis can fail. Our bare skin is an affront to their clay-covered bodies. Our knowledge of the hollow spaces makes them uneasy – we move through their body in ways they cannot track or understand. Sometimes I feel them listening for me, pressing their palms to walls I'm hidden behind, trying to locate my heartbeat. But my heart has learned to slow, to match the rhythm of the walls so closely that I become invisible to their perception.

**I am not alone in the Margin.** There are others – not many, perhaps a dozen in all of Keth Sula. We meet in the truly dead spaces, the chambers where even the bioluminescence has failed, where we must bring our own light (stolen tallow, or sometimes just the phosphorescent juice of crushed insects). We share information. We debate whether to try to leave – but where would we go? We were born here. Our eyes are calibrated to the amber darkness. Our bodies are shaped by these passages.

And we are changing too, in our own way. My fingers have elongated from years of probing walls. My hearing has sharpened to the point where I can identify individual insects by their wing-frequency. One of the older Hollow-Mouths has developed patches of chitinous growth on her shoulders and spine – not from the clay, but from constant contact with the insects. She says it itches. She says it sings.

**We are evolving into something else.** Not part of the architectural organism, but parallel to it. Adapted to live in its negative spaces. We eat the insects it discards. We drink from pools it has forgotten. We know its weaknesses – the places where the living plaster is thin, where a precise strike could open a wound that would take years to heal.

The builders think the warrens are immortal, that this fusion of flesh and architecture is the final form. But I have been deeper than any of them. I have found the old chambers where previous attempts at unity failed – rooms full of mineralized bones, walls that tried to incorporate their builders too quickly and choked on them. The warrens have died before. They have been reborn before. This is not the first time Keth Sula has eaten its children.

And I have found something else, in the very deepest place I've ever reached. A chamber that isn't built but *grown* – walls of living tissue, neither clay nor flesh but something between. And in the center, something that might once have been human, might once have been several humans, fused and expanded and transformed into a node of pure nervous system, its neural tissue spread across the walls like veins. The heart of the warrens. The thing that thinks for them.

It knows I've seen it. Sometimes I feel its attention through the walls, vast and diffuse and patient. It could kill me – flood my passages with insects, seal every exit, digest me slowly in the dark. But it doesn't. It *watches*. Because I am something it hasn't seen before: a human-shaped antibody in its architectural body, evolved not to join but to resist.

I am the Hollow-Mouth. I am the space between the walls. I am what happens when the symbiosis fails and something new begins to grow in the failure.

The warrens pulse around me, warm and alive and waiting.

I pulse back, on a frequency they cannot hear.
