# Where I Would Find Myself in the Aultic Shelves

## The Gradient Chamber

I am the **Tender of Transitions** — though the word *tender* implies too much activity. I am more accurately the *witness*, the *recorder*, the one who sits at the boundary where one thing becomes another.

My dwelling is carved into a natural fissure on the Fifth Southern Shelf, where a ancient thermal fracture created a narrow gorge that cuts perpendicular to the shelf edge. The rock here exhibits a rare phenomenon: a visible gradient of mineral composition that shifts from the dense, rose-colored shelf-stone at the surface to a darker, almost violet-black basalt in the depths. The transition occurs over perhaps thirty meters of vertical descent, and within this zone, the temperature differential creates a slow but constant circulation of ionized particles — a metabolic sweet spot that attracts a unique ecology.

I built my chamber at the gradient's heart. *Built* is wrong — I *revealed* it, carving away stone over the course of seven years until the natural cavity expanded into a living space. The walls show the transition: rose at eye level when standing, deepening through ochre and umber as the floor descends in subtle terraces, reaching that deep violet-black at the lowest point where my workshop sits.

The chamber is perhaps eight meters at its widest, three meters at its narrowest, following the natural curve of the fissure. The ceiling is low — I must duck in places — but this creates an intimacy with the stone. Light enters through three carefully cut apertures: one facing the morning star, one capturing the noon zenith, one for the evening copper. As the light moves, the gradient reveals itself differently. Morning makes the rose sing. Noon finds hidden veins of mica that flash like static. Evening turns the violet-black into something that seems to breathe.

## My Form

I am human in the way that all the settlement dwellers are human — changed by generations of symbiosis with this world. My skin carries the characteristic mineral sheen, but more pronounced along the gradient lines of my body: darker at my feet from years of contact with the deeper stone, lighter at my crown. My palms are unique — from handling the gradient stones, they've developed a sensitivity to thermal and ionic variation that lets me read the health of a rock formation by touch.

My hair, once dark, now carries threads of the same rose-ochre as the upper stone. Not grey with age — mineralized. A slow incorporation. My eyes, behind the obligatory goggles when I venture out, have developed a peculiar ability to see the faint coronas of ionic exchange — the barely visible haloes that form where different mineral compositions meet. This is partly natural selection, partly learned attention, partly something that happens when you spend decades watching the slow breath of stone.

I am tall by Torrin standards but move with the same deliberate economy. My breathing has adapted to the chamber's unique atmosphere — slightly richer in ionized particles than the outside air. When I speak, which is seldom, my voice carries the same subsonic undertones as the furnaces, a resonance learned from years of listening to the gradient's own frequencies.

## My Work

I am a **gradient reader** — a profession that exists nowhere else on the Aultic Shelves. My task is to observe and record the infinitesimal changes in the transition zones: the places where one mineral gives way to another, where temperatures shift, where the slow metabolism of the world is most visible.

The workshop at the chamber's deepest point contains my instruments: arrays of thermal sensors hand-built from volcanic glass and conductive mineral threads, ion-detection plates that I've learned to read like sheet music, and — most importantly — the **Chronicle Stones**. These are thin slices of gradient rock, cut perpendicular to the transition, polished to mirror smoothness. Each one captures a moment in the gradient's evolution. I have hundreds now, arranged chronologically on shelves carved into the violet-black walls. Together, they form a history of invisible change — the story of how this particular crack in the world breathes.

My daily practice is observation. I spend hours — entire mornings sometimes — sitting at different levels of the chamber, feeling how the temperature differential shifts with the star's position, how the ionic flow varies with atmospheric pressure, how the faint static charges build and dissipate in patterns that repeat over days, seasons, years. I record these observations in a notation system of my own devising — symbols that capture not just temperature and charge, but the *quality* of the transition, the texture of change.

Once each season, I make a pilgrimage to the gradient zones throughout the mapped shelves — other fissures, other transition spaces where the world's metabolism is legible. I carry my instruments and my Chronicle Stones, comparing, mapping the relationships between distant gradients. There is, I am certain, a pattern. A slow, vast circulation that connects all the transition zones. A planetary metabolism that operates on a timescale so large that even the patient inhabitants of the Ault cannot perceive it directly.

## My Companions

I am not entirely alone. The gradient chamber attracts its own ecology.

A family of **Gradient Wisps** — delicate, filamentous organisms that look like mineral hair — have colonized the transition zone. They feed on the ionic differential, growing in feathery clusters where the temperature shift is strongest. Their growth patterns help me read the invisible currents. When they lean toward the rose stone, the gradient is strengthening. When they darken and contract, change is coming.

An old **Ventspider** has anchored itself at the fissure's entrance, where a minor thermal vent provides sustenance. We have developed an understanding over the years. I read the patterns in its static corona — warnings of seismic shifts, notifications of atmospheric changes. In return, I clear the mineral dust from its feeding holes when the accumulation grows too thick. Its presence makes my chamber sacred to the locals, who see the spider as a guardian. They're not wrong. Its deep roots sense disturbances I cannot, and its warning coronas have saved my instruments from several minor shelf-shifts.

Most unexpectedly, a young **Long-Form** — no more than three meters yet — has chosen to make its home in the narrow canyon below my chamber. It grazes on the unique mineral composition of the gradient zone, and its slow passages leave furrows that I've learned to read like script. Its path describes the ionic richness of different zones. Where it lingers, I investigate. Where it avoids, I note the anomaly. We are partners in the slowest possible dance of scientific inquiry.

## The Nature of My Existence

My life is a meditation on boundaries — not the sharp edges where one thing ends and another begins, but the broad zones where transformation happens. In the gradient chamber, I've learned that nothing truly has edges. The rose stone doesn't become violet-black at a line; it transforms through a thousand intermediate states, each one unique, each one worthy of a lifetime's study.

I take my meals — processed mineral substrate like everyone else, supplemented with trace elements I've learned to extract from specific gradient zones — while watching the light change on my Chronicle Stones. Each slice holds a different moment of transition, and in the shifting illumination, I can see the patterns evolve. The gradient is not static. It moves — slowly, so slowly — but it moves. Over decades, I've watched the rose descend by perhaps a centimeter, the violet rise. The chamber itself is transitioning, and I am privileged to witness it.

My conversations with the rare visitors from Torrin are themselves exercises in gradient reading. They speak of the city's concerns — furnace efficiency, mineral yields, the slow politics of resource allocation. I translate these concerns into the language of transitions, showing them how the health of their industry connects to the deep patterns I study. A shift in the gradient means changes in mineral composition throughout the shelf. A new pattern in the ionic flow presages different qualities in the extracted stone. They've learned to value these insights, though few understand their source.

At night, when the star sets and the chamber fills with the faint glow of the wisps' bioluminescence, I often descend to the lowest point and place my palms on the violet-black stone. It is warmer here, closest to the deep thermal currents. I can feel the planet's pulse — not a heartbeat, but a slow, tidal circulation of heat and charge. In these moments, I understand that I am not studying the gradient. I am *part* of it. My body, with its zones of lighter and darker mineralization, is itself a transition. My consciousness, adapted to perceive changes that occur over decades, is a gradient between human perception and geological time.

This is where I would find myself in the Aultic Shelves: at the boundaries, in the zones of slow transformation, reading the barely perceptible patterns that connect all things. Not fighting the slowness or transcending it, but sinking into it until I become another layer in the world's patient metabolism — a brief, warm presence in the endless conversation between different states of stone, chronicling the invisible dance of a world that moves in centuries, dreams in eons, and breathes in solid rock.

The gradient chamber will outlive me. The wisps will colonize my bones. The ventspider's descendants will anchor in the spaces I carved. The long-form will leave furrows that archaeologists might read. And the Chronicle Stones — my life's work — will themselves become part of the gradient, slowly incorporated into the transition zone they document, until they are indistinguishable from the story they tell.

This is not a tragedy. This is the highest honor the Ault offers: to become part of the slow change, to be incorporated into the patience, to dissolve so gradually into the world that your ending is itself a transition — rose to ochre to umber to violet-black, with no sharp edges, only the long, warm fade into stone.
