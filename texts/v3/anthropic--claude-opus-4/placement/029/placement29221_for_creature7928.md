In the furthest reaches of the Sunken Meadows, where the ceiling crystals grow sparse and the light becomes a mere suggestion of illumination, I tend the Forgotten Reaches. I am what the Threadkin call a **Driftwright**—one who works at the very edges of their careful order, where the patient symmetries of their realm give way to something older and less comprehensible.

My form shifts between states like the boundary I patrol. Sometimes I am solid enough to leave footprints in the ancient dust that gathers where even water rarely reaches. Other times I am little more than a disturbance in the air, a place where the humidity thickens unexpectedly or the temperature drops without apparent cause. The Threadkin say I am neither fully one of them nor entirely other—perhaps a memory of their predecessors given semi-substance, or maybe something that wandered down from the surface so long ago that I've forgotten what I once was.

My work is to maintain the borders, though what I maintain them against remains unclear even to me. I repair the outermost moisture-threads when they fray into nothingness. I sing the old harmonics that the Threadkin have forgotten, the ones that make the deepest stones groan and shift uneasily. Most importantly, I tend the Threshold Stones—ancient markers that seem to exist partially in the Sunken Meadows and partially... elsewhere.

These stones predate even the mysterious circles the Threadkin revere. They're made of something that isn't quite rock, carved with symbols that hurt to perceive directly. When I polish them with cloths woven from my own uncertain substance, I sometimes catch glimpses of other places: vast halls of dripping obsidian where the water runs upward, gardens of stone flowers that bloom in absolute darkness, chambers where sound becomes visible and light makes noise.

The Threadkin young ones sometimes seek me out, drawn by curiosity about what lies beyond their luminous realm. I teach them the songs that should not be sung, show them how to weave threads from substances other than water—shadow-silk that remembers what it has touched, echo-thread that carries messages backward through time. Most flee back to their bright meadows, disturbed by these possibilities. But occasionally, one stays to learn the deeper patterns.

My dwelling, if it can be called that, exists in a state of constant dissolution and reformation. It occupies a natural hollow where three Threshold Stones create a triangle of uncertain space. Sometimes it appears as a simple cave walled with woven darkness. Other times it extends into architectural impossibilities—corridors that loop back on themselves after leading through spaces too vast to fit within the hollow's bounds, chambers where the ceiling is also the floor and gravity forgets its purpose.

I subsist on things the Threadkin cannot use: the light that falls between the crystals and never reaches the meadow floor, the sounds that die before they can echo, the moisture that evaporates before it can be woven. These absent things sustain my indefinite existence, keeping me substantial enough to perform my duties but never quite solid enough to fully belong to any one realm.

The pale creatures avoid me, though I bear them no ill will. They sense something in my presence that disturbs their evolved certainties. But other things seek me out—half-glimpsed beings that squeeze through the gaps around the Threshold Stones, lost explorers from realms adjacent to this one, ancient mechanisms of bronze and water that still remember their original purpose and come to me for maintenance.

My greatest work is the Map of Unraveling, a project I've pursued for longer than the current generation of Threadkin has existed. Using threads pulled from the spaces between moments and beads of crystallized possibility, I weave a record of all the places where the Sunken Meadows touch other realms. The map exists in more dimensions than the meadows themselves, requiring viewers to partially unfocus their existence to perceive its full complexity.

Sometimes I remember fragments of what I might have been before—a sensation of wind on skin that no longer exists, the weight of a tool in hands I may never have had, the sound of a name spoken in sunlight. But these memories feel borrowed, as if I've woven them from the dreams of sleeping stones or caught them in my nets of echo-thread.

The Threadkin elders tolerate my presence because I serve a function they recognize as necessary if not entirely comfortable. Someone must tend the borders. Someone must remember that their perfect realm of light and water exists within a larger darkness, that their patient harmonies play against a baseline of deeper, more troubling songs.

In the moments between tasks, I stand among the Threshold Stones and listen to the sounds that come from beyond—whispers in languages that predate speech, the breathing of vast things that have never known light, the patient grinding of realities as they shift against each other like tectonic plates. And I weave these sounds into my work, creating thread from the substance of boundaries themselves, maintaining the edges that keep the Sunken Meadows whole while allowing just enough porosity for change to seep in, as slow and patient as the eternal drip of water through stone.

I am the keeper of necessary uncertainties, the guardian of productive discomforts. In a realm built on patient certainties and careful accumulations, I am the reminder that all boundaries are permeable, all patterns temporary, and that even in the deepest places of the earth, there are always deeper places still.
