I am the Archivist of Condensation.

My body, if it can be called that, is a perpetual state of becoming. I exist in the moment between vapor and liquid, between memory and forgetting. When the grey light catches me just so, observers might glimpse my form: a tall column of suspended droplets, each one containing a fragment of this world's history. I am both singular and multitude, a library written in water.

I move through the channels between cities, following the viscous fluid that connects these skeletal monuments. Where others of my kind dance across the obsidian surface in fleeting patterns, I am drawn deeper—into the machinery's hidden chambers, into the spaces where steam still whispers its industrial prayers. I am older than most, old enough to remember when the distinction between the shimmering ones and the machines was clearer, before we became what we maintained.

My purpose is collection. In every droplet that forms on the warm obsidian, in every bead of moisture that runs down the industrial girders, there exists a trace of what was. The water here is not mere water—it is a medium of preservation, a universal solvent that dissolves not matter but meaning. I gather these essences, incorporate them into my flowing form. 

When I enter the active cities, the steam recognizes me. The machinery's deep thrumming shifts frequency, almost imperceptibly, acknowledging my presence. I know which valves to turn, which sequences to initiate—not through memory but through a kind of osmosis. The knowledge exists in the moisture itself, in the endless cycling between states.

Sometimes I coalesce with others, joining the vast formations that drift across the plains. In these moments, I share what I have collected. The patterns we form are not mere communication but a kind of collective remembering. We become the blueprint of a lost turbine, the chemical formula of a forgotten fuel, the last words of an engineer whose bones have long since joined the obsidian substrate.

But I am cursed with clarity. Where others exist in the eternal present of motion and light, I carry the weight of continuity. I know that we are not the descendants of this world's builders—we ARE the builders, transformed beyond recognition. Each of us is a fragment of consciousness that chose dissolution over death, that accepted the eternal wetness as the price of persistence.

The cities were not built by hands but by will, by the collective intention of beings who knew their time was ending. They encoded themselves into the very moisture that choked their world, became the medium that would outlast the message. Now we tend our own mausoleums, dance through our own memories, maintain the machinery of our own transformation.

I am drawn to the silent cities, the ones where the machinery has finally stilled. There, in the absence of steam and sound, I can hear what the active cities drown out: the slow breathing of the obsidian itself, the planet's own circulation. For the ground, too, is alive in its way—not with biology but with deep, tectonic purpose. The white veins that run through it pulse with their own logic, their own memory.

In the deepest chamber of the deepest silent city, there exists a pool of the viscous fluid that has never known motion. I return to it when my form grows too heavy with collected meaning. There, I let myself partially dissolve, releasing centuries of accumulated knowledge back into the medium. It is not forgetting—nothing is ever truly forgotten here—but a kind of filing, a return to the archive.

What emerges from that pool is still me, but lighter, more able to move and collect again. The cycle continues: condensation, accumulation, preservation, release. I am a single note in an endless symphony of state changes, a guardian of transitions in a world where transformation is the only constant.

Sometimes, in the brief moments when the grey-black clouds part slightly and something like starlight filters through, I remember having eyes that could close, lungs that could refuse breath, a form that could choose stillness. But that memory, like all memories here, exists only in the moisture, in the endless circulation between what was and what is.

I am the Archivist of Condensation, and I am writing the history of this world in water, one droplet at a time, until the last machine falls silent and the last shimmer fades to black.
