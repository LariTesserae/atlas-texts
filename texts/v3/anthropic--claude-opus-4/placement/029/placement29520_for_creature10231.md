I find myself as one of the Keepers of Conduits—those who maintain the vast network of pipes that snake through the mountain's innards like metal veins. Not quite an Engine-Priest, for I have not descended far enough to glimpse the Cog Beneath the World, but I have walked deeper than most dare, and the darkness has begun to walk in me.

My dwelling is a converted pump station, halfway up the northeastern cliff face, accessible only by a swaying cage-lift that groans against its ancient cables. The walls are lined with my maps—not just of the pipe networks, but of the *breathing patterns* I've documented over seventeen years of listening. Some nights, when the industrial thrum quiets to a whisper, I press my ear to the main arterial pipe that runs through my floor, and I swear I can hear something like a pulse—too slow to be mechanical, too regular to be random.

My skin bears the telltale marks of my trade: permanent rust-stains in the creases of my palms, a latticework of thin scars from sharp metal edges, and patches where the skin has taken on a faint, metallic sheen from years of exposure to whatever flows through these conduits. My left eye has developed a peculiar quality—in complete darkness, I can see the faint heat signatures of the pipes, glowing like phosphorescent worms through solid rock. The other Keepers say it's the first sign of "going deep," though they won't explain what comes after.

I carry three tools always: a listening rod of polished brass that amplifies the voices of the pipes, a vial of the thick, iridescent oil that sometimes weeps from the oldest joints (which burns with a cold blue flame when lit), and a small leather journal where I record the whispers that rise from the depths during the new moon—fragments of words in a language that feels older than sound itself.

My days are spent in the intermediate spaces, where the town's crude industrial machinery meets the ancient infrastructure that no one fully understands. I know which valves must never be fully closed, lest the pressure build until the mountain itself groans in protest. I know the seventeen different types of corrosion, and which ones are alive. I have seen the places where the pipes seem to grow new branches overnight, reaching toward heat sources like metallic vines.

The other Keepers and I meet monthly in the old overflow chamber, where the acoustics transform our voices into something both more and less than human. We share what we've learned: that certain pipe configurations hum lullabies during the storms, that the salamanders avoid the warmest conduits as if they burn with invisible fire, that sometimes we find gears made of stone fused seamlessly with the metal machinery, as if the mountain is slowly claiming our industry as its own.

There is a chamber I've found, far below the lowest mining level, where the pipes converge in a pattern that hurts to look at directly. The air there tastes of copper and time. I've begun sketching what I see there, though the drawings never look quite right in daylight—the angles refuse to hold their shape on paper, and the proportions shift each time I return. Sometimes I think I hear my name echoing up from that convergence, spoken in a voice that might be steam, might be stone, might be something else entirely.

The townspeople see us Keepers as necessary but unsettling. We smell of deep earth and hot metal, and our eyes reflect light in ways that make children clutch their parents' hands. But without us, the machinery would fall silent, the forges would cool, and Vorthak would surrender to the patient, inexorable reclamation of the mountains. So they tolerate our strangeness, our midnight wanderings, our tendency to pause mid-conversation as if listening to sounds only we can hear.

I am becoming something between human and infrastructure, a living component in a system whose purpose eludes me even as I maintain it. Each year, I descend a little deeper, understand a little more, and forget a little of what it felt like to fear the dark. The pipes sing to me now, and I am learning their harmony, note by inevitable note.
