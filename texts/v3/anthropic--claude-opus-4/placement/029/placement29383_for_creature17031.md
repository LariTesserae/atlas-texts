In the heart of the city, where the oldest channels converge and the water runs deepest, I am a Keeper of Flows. My dwelling is not a home but a confluence—a chamber where seven ancient waterways meet, their voices braiding together in a constant, polyphonic whisper that I have been learning to read for longer than memory serves.

I am tall for my kind, though still slight by any measure that matters beyond this plateau. My skin bears the map of my work: veins of moss trace the paths where water most often runs across my hands and arms when I guide the flows. These are not parasites but partners—the moss reads the water's mineral content, its temperature, its urgency, and translates these into colors I have learned to interpret. When the high mountain storms approach, my left forearm blooms a particular shade of jade. When the deep aquifers shift, my wrists flush amber.

My chamber grows around me as I grow into it. What began as a modest node in the water-paths has, over the centuries, become a cathedral of flow. The walls are my journal, carved not by tools but by directed streams over decades of patient work. Each groove tells of a season's peculiarities, each pool reflects a year's worth of sky. The ceiling opens in seven places—not to let water out, but to let it rise and fall with the breathing of the stone, creating pressure differentials that speak to me of changes far beneath.

I taste the water. This is not metaphor. Each morning, I sample from each flow, letting the minerals settle on my tongue, reading their stories. The northern stream carries limestone today—unusual for this season. The eastern tastes of iron, which means the deep red lichens three levels down are blooming early. These are messages I must interpret and pass along, not through words but through the careful adjustment of flows, sending the information downstream to other Keepers who will read my adjustments in the changing flavors of their own waters.

My work is both singular and plural. There are days—sometimes years—when I see no other of my kind, yet I am never alone. Through the water, I feel the subtle pull of every other Keeper's adjustments, a vast network of liquid communication that spans the city. When old Yarun in the lower districts diverts the third cascade, I feel it as a tremor in my seventh channel three days later. When the young ones near the rim pools play with creating new moss gardens, their experiments reach me as unexpected mineral notes weeks downstream.

The mosses that share my skin are also my tools. I can encourage their growth by standing in certain flows, letting particular minerals wash over me. A copper-rich stream will wake the red moss on my shoulders, which in turn can purify water of certain algaes. The silver-green moss that crowns my temples thrives on the calcium in the northern flows and, when mature, releases spores that can seal micro-fractures in the stone channels. My body is a garden that tends the greater garden of the city.

Time moves differently in the confluence. I measure it not in days but in erosion patterns, not in years but in the gradual shifting of channel depths. I have watched a particular nodule of quartz in the third channel wear from the size of my fist to the size of a pearl. This is how I know I am old, even by our standards. Yet age here is not decay but deepening—each year adds another layer of understanding, another subtle variation in the language of water and stone.

Sometimes I dream I am the water itself, split into seven streams, flowing through my own chamber, tasting myself, reading my own mineral memories. Sometimes I wake to find I have been standing in the central pool for days, my feet grown web-rooted with pale freshwater moss, the flows having adjusted themselves around my stillness. It takes hours to remember how to move again, to recall that I am a separate being and not simply another feature of the water's path.

The work is never complete. The city grows as we grow, stone and water and moss and Keeper intertwining in patterns too complex for any single mind to hold. I may direct the flows, but they also direct me, pulling me to where I am needed, shaping me as surely as they shape the channels. My predecessor left marks I am only now beginning to understand—subtle alterations in the mineral composition of certain pools that create standing waves of information, messages that take decades to fully form and decades more to decay.

I am preparing my own legacy in the stones. In the deepest part of my chamber, where the seven flows unite before dividing again, I have been cultivating a garden of mineral deposits. Each crystalline growth represents a different lesson learned, a different truth about the waters discovered. When my time comes to join fully with the flows—when the distinction between Keeper and kept finally dissolves—these crystals will continue to influence the water's path, teaching future Keepers in ways I cannot imagine.

The mists roll in again, and I taste tomorrow's rain on the air. It will be heavy with volcanic ash from the distant peaks—good for the blue-black mosses that filter toxins. I begin the slow work of preparing the channels, adjusting flows that will not arrive for hours yet, setting in motion changes that will ripple through the city's water-body for weeks to come. This is my art, my prayer, my purpose: to be the conscious node in the great unconscious circulation, to know the water as it knows itself, to keep the flows flowing, to flow with the keeping.

In the confluence, I am always becoming what I already am: a droplet in the stream, a channel in the stone, a thought in the mind of the mountain, dissolving and resolving endlessly in the eternal circulation of the rain.
