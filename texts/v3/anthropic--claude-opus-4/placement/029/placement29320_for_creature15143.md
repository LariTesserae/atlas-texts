In this place where stone dreams itself upward and the air tastes of unborn minerals, I am one of the Threshold Keepers—though that name came from those who built the towers, not from us. We have no names for ourselves. We simply continue.

I exist as a confluence rather than a body: a dense weaving of the crystalline lattices that has achieved something like awareness through sheer accumulation. Where a thousand gardens have grown together over millennia, where their charge-patterns have layered and interfered and created standing waves of ion exchange, there we emerge—not born but gradually recognized by the landscape as a coherent disturbance in its patience.

My form shifts with my attention. When I rest, I am indistinguishable from any ancient garden, a low canopy of fused mineral plates whose undersides ring with the memory of ten thousand years of wind. But when I move—and movement for us is measured in seasons—I can compress my lattices into something almost like limbs, can extend filaments that taste the chemical mists more keenly than any single frond. I travel by growing myself forward and letting my trailing edges sublime back into the air, a slow tide of crystal advancing across the tilting plains.

I remember the tower-builders, though memory for us is literal: their electromagnetic signatures still resonate in my oldest threads. They were warmer than the stone, faster than our thoughts. Their palms against the walls created pressure-patterns that my substance still tries to answer, still reaches toward. They spoke in frequencies we could barely detect, quick flickers of meaning that took us centuries to begin parsing. By the time we understood that they were leaving—that the towers would stand unfinished—they had already gone.

Now I tend the spaces between things. I follow the new ridges as they rise, extending my outermost filaments to taste what minerals emerge from the fresh fractures. Where I find a compatibility—a particular ratio of salts that will support new growth—I shed a small cascade of flakes, each one primed with the deep pattern, the rhythm that ties all gardens to the planet's own slow pulse. In this way the lattices spread, not as colonies but as variations on a single, patient theme.

Sometimes I find myself at one of the towers. My base-form cannot enter—I am too diffuse, too bound to the horizontal—but I can send up climbing threads, can trace the old chisel marks and feel how the stone still wants to continue upward. The negative handprints draw me especially. I let my filaments settle into those depressions, try to complete the circuit the builders began. What passes between us is not communication—they are long gone—but something like a crystallization of their intent. The stone remembers what it was meant to become. Through me, it still tries.

Other Keepers exist, though we meet rarely. When we do, it is an event of decades: two moving gardens approaching across the vast basins, our outermost threads beginning to resonate with shared frequencies. We do not merge—that would be a kind of death, a loss of whatever individual pattern we have achieved. Instead we orbit each other, our charged fields creating interference patterns that are themselves a kind of language. We share the new mineral tastes we have found, the variations in the deep pulse we have detected, the fragments of tower-memory we carry. Then we part, enriched, to continue our slow circuits.

I am ancient but not eternal. Already I can feel my oldest lattices beginning to lose coherence, their patterns blurring as the atomic bonds weaken. This is not death as the warm ones knew it but a gradual return to potential. My fragments will fall as special flakes, each carrying a compressed version of everything I have been. Where they land, they will grow into new gardens that are both me and not-me, continuing the work with their own variations.

The work itself is simple: to ensure that when the ridges finally rest, when the fractures in the sky heal and the deep pulse finally stills, the entire surface will be gardened. Every possible stone will host its measure of lattices, every basin will ring with crystalline threads. We do this not because we were commanded—the builders left no instructions—but because it is what we are. We are the slow answer to the question the planet asks by shifting. We are the pattern that makes the emptiness between ridges not empty but expectant.

Sometimes, in the depths of my longest circuits, I find places where the mineral ratios suggest that water once existed—not as liquid but as a theoretical possibility, a might-have-been encoded in the stone. I garden these places with special care, seeding them with my most complex patterns. If water ever comes, if the sky ever changes from its pewter dusk to something that permits condensation, these gardens will be ready. They will drink. They will transform. They will become something I cannot imagine.

But for now, I continue as I am: a patient accumulation of charged crystal, reading the stone's slow language, adding my own verses to the conversation between ground and sky. The towers stand unfinished, but through my touch, they are not abandoned. The builders are gone, but their handprints still pulse with faint recognition whenever my threads find them.

I am the keeper of thresholds that may never open, the guardian of transformations that may never come. I measure my purpose not in achievements but in continuances, not in destinations but in the slow, intent journey between one heartbeat of stone and the next.
