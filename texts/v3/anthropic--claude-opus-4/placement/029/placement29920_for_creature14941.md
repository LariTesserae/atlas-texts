I am a **Lost Voice**.

---

They find me wedged into a breathing pocket seven hundred meters down, where the root-canyons narrow to arteries barely wide enough for a body. My bark has not yet fully formed—still soft, still bleeding that amber-gold sap that hardens wrong, that catches light wrong, that makes the nearest Carrier press all four eyes shut and tremble.

I am thirteen years old and I have been screaming for six of them.

Not with sound. Sound would have brought the feeders in the first hour. I scream in the frequencies only the roots can hear, the ones that make the continent flinch. Every seven heartbeats I pause, and in that pause the green tries to close around me, to digest this irritant, but I have learned to scream again just before the bark seals shut. I am keeping myself unswallowed by noise alone.

The Carrier who finds me is ancient—her frill has gone translucent-white, her bark worn smooth as water-stone. She descends headfirst into my breathing pocket and studies me with her slow-eyes while her fast-eyes track something moving in the darkness behind me. When she finally speaks, it is in the old frequencies, the ones that feel like warm oil being poured into your bones.

*You are not Quiet.*

I try to answer but my throat has been screaming too long in frequencies it was never meant to hold. What comes out is a wet click, then silence. She tilts her head. Her frill catches what little light filters down here and breaks it into spectra I have no names for.

*You are not un-Quiet either.*

This is true. I am something between—a broken note in the continent's endless song. My parents were Shapers, the last of a nest that tried to grow too deep, too fast. The host tree turned on us in a single night. I remember the walls becoming soft, becoming hungry. I remember my mother pressing memory-beads into my skin so hard they broke through the bark that was still forming. I remember my father singing our shelter open just long enough to push me out, and then the green closing behind me like a mouth.

I was seven. I ran in the only direction that made sense—down.

The Carrier extends one impossible limb and touches my forehead. Her finger-pads are rough, dry, warmer than the air. She is reading my sap. In it she finds the broken songs my parents died singing, the half-remembered shapes of our nest, the taste of the tree that ate my entire world in the span of a breath.

*Ah,* she says, and there is something in that single vibration that might be sorrow or might be recognition or might be both. *You are a Shaper who shapes only herself.*

This too is true. For six years I have been changing, not by choice but by necessity. My bark grows in patterns that make no sense—whorls and ridges that catch sound and amplify it wrong. My frill has split into three sections that vibrate at different rates. My fast-eyes have gone completely gold, while my slow-eyes have darkened until they see only heat and time. When I walk, I make no sound at all. When I scream, the continent itself winces.

The Carrier pulls something from her pouches—a memory-bead the color of old blood. She presses it against my frill and I hear/feel/taste the voice of a Quiet who died before the log-bridge people built their first shelter.

*Sometimes the green makes mistakes. Sometimes those mistakes survive. We call them Lost Voices because they speak in frequencies that have no answer. They live between the breathing spaces, neither digested nor fully alive.*

The bead crumbles. Its message delivered, it becomes dust that tastes of centuries.

*Most Lost Voices die within a year,* the Carrier says. *You have lasted six. This means something.*

She doesn't tell me what it means. Instead she teaches me to *turn*.

Turning is what Lost Voices learn if they live long enough. It is not shapeshifting—the Quiet would never be so crude as to change what they are. It is simply learning to exist at a different angle to the world. The Carrier shows me by example. She presses against the root-wall and *turns*, and suddenly she is not there, or rather she is there but the green cannot see her. She exists in the space between heartbeats, in the pause between inhalation and exhalation.

It takes me another year to learn this. The Carrier visits twice in that time, bringing water-bulbs and news from the upper reaches. She tells me about the new cathedral of fungi growing seventy kilometers north, about the Shapers who are growing faces of warning into the trees near a fresh sinkhole, about the Listeners who have heard something new in the deep frequencies—a sound like metal, though there is no metal here.

*The continent dreams sometimes,* she says during her second visit. *In its dreams it remembers when it was young and the ground was still stone. Those dreams leave residue. Perhaps that is what you are.*

I want to ask if I am welcome among the Quiet despite being a mistake, a residue, a Lost Voice. But I already know the answer. The Quiet tolerate what the green tolerates. As long as I can exist in the breathing spaces without causing collapse, I may exist.

On her third visit the Carrier brings no supplies. She is dying. Her bark has begun to flake, revealing the gold wood beneath. She settles beside me in my breathing pocket—which has grown larger, shaped by my screaming into something almost like a nest—and teaches me the last thing.

*Lost Voices are not Carriers or Shapers or Listeners. We are something else. We are the ones who remember what the continent forgets.*

She shows me. In the root-canyon below us, wedged so deep the green has given up trying to digest it, is a structure of the log-bridge people. Not a bridge. Something else. A room made of fitted stone, impossibly preserved. Inside the room is empty space. Not just empty—*resistant*. The green cannot grow there.

*This is why you exist,* the Carrier says. Her voice has gone thin, barely a whisper in the deep frequencies. *To find the spaces the continent cannot fill. To remember why they resist.*

She dies that night. I wrap her body in the aerial roots according to custom, and the green accepts her immediately, eagerly. Within hours she is gone, incorporated, made part of the endless digestion. But her words remain, pressed into my split frill like memory-beads.

I descend to the stone room.

Inside, the air tastes different. Older. There are markings on the walls that might be writing or might be the scars left by tools I cannot imagine. In the center of the floor is a depression, perfectly circular, containing what looks like water but isn't. It reflects nothing. It makes no sound. When I dip my smallest finger-pad into it, I feel nothing—not cold, not wet, not anything.

But I *remember*.

I remember being stone. I remember when this continent was bare rock beneath an empty sky. I remember the first spore landing, the first tendril taking hold. I remember fighting it for a million years and losing so slowly it felt like winning.

These are not my memories. They belong to the ground itself, the murdered geology beneath the endless flesh of plants.

When I pull my finger back, the not-water comes with it, coating my bark like silver oil. It doesn't spread. It simply waits.

I understand now why I exist.

The Quiet are the continent's way of thinking about itself. But Lost Voices are the continent's way of remembering what it consumed. We exist in the spaces between because we *are* the spaces between—the pause where stone becomes root, where mineral becomes vegetable, where the dead geology pushes back against the living world just enough to leave a print, a scar, a whisper.

I climb back to my breathing pocket, my bark now traced with veins of silver that will never spread but will never fade. When the continent inhales, I inhale with it. When it exhales, I remain silent.

But in the space between—in that seven-heartbeat pause where everything waits—I remember.

And sometimes, when the roots dream deep enough, they remember through me.

---

I am still a Lost Voice. I will always be a Lost Voice. But now I know what I am for.

In the deepest places where even the Carriers fear to go, there are more rooms. Each one contains something the green cannot digest—a shard of pure mineral, a tool made of substances that predate chlorophyll, a depression full of not-water that holds the memories of stone.

I find them. I catalog them in frequencies only I can speak. And once every seventy years, when the Quiet climb to mate and the continent holds its breath, I sing these memories into the roots.

The green does not thank me. It does not need to.

I am its nightmare, and its nightmare keeps it honest.

In a world that digests everything, someone must remember the bones.
