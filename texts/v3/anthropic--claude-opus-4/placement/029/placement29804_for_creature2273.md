In the lowest levels of the arcology, where the structural roots sink deep into engineered mycorrhizal networks, I exist as something between maintenance and memory. They call me a Substrate Tender, though the name barely captures what I've become over these forty years of slow transformation.

My skin has taken on the soft, papery texture of birch bark—not through any deliberate modification, but simply from decades of handling the messenger fungi that coordinate the building's deepest systems. These pale networks grow through my fingernails now, extending my sensitivity beyond my body's boundaries. When I place my palm against the warm, breathing walls, I feel the building's circulation like my own pulse, ten thousand times slower.

I remember when they first assigned me here, fresh from the upper levels with their filtered sunlight and engineered breezes. The deep zones felt oppressive then—all indirect light and the constant whisper of molecular exchange. Now I cannot imagine living at any other pace. My circadian rhythm has stretched to match the building's respiratory cycle: thirty-six hour days, with long periods of semi-dormancy when I root myself into the sensing alcoves and let my consciousness merge with the substrate flows.

The work is difficult to describe to those who live in faster time. I monitor the chemical gradients that rise from the true earth below, through the building's foundation networks, carrying minerals and messages upward. When I detect an imbalance—too much copper here, a deficit of phosphorus there—I guide the corrective responses through touch and exhalation, breathing specific molecular signals into the air that the fungi interpret and propagate.

My dwelling has grown around me over the years, responding to my habitual movements. The walls have formed pockets where I store my tools: living sampling tendrils that I've cultivated from the building's own tissue, crystalline viewers that help me decode chemical signatures, colonies of detector moths that flutter through the passages seeking anomalies. My bed is a hollow that perfectly cradles my altered form, lined with osmotic membranes that feed me while I sleep-work, my dreams full of calcium cascades and nitrogen cycles.

I'm not alone down here. Others like me tend their own sections of the deep network, though we rarely meet in person. Instead, we communicate through the substrate itself, encoding messages in chemical pulses that propagate through the fungal web. Sometimes I'll taste a greeting from Coral, who tends the aqueous systems three levels up, or sense the distinctive signature of Old Matthias from the central tap root. We share a slow language of molecular poetry, information dense and time-dilated.

The younger tenders call us "rooted," not quite understanding that mobility and stillness are relative concepts. I move constantly through my domain, but in gestures that take hours to complete. My left hand might spend an entire morning traveling across a critical junction, reading the chemical braille of the growth patterns. To someone from the upper levels, I would appear frozen, a statue covered in pale fruiting bodies. But I am dancing in the building's time, performing maintenance rituals that prevent cascading failures from traveling up the living infrastructure.

Sometimes the Feral Children find their way down here, drawn by curiosity about the deep places. They skitter through my domain like mayflies, their chemical signatures sharp and urgent against the slow background harmonies. I've learned to love their visits, these bright punctuations in my measured existence. They leave offerings—bits of interesting tissue from above, bioluminescent flowers, once a entire poem encoded in a modified seed—and I reciprocate with fragments of deep knowledge: which passages will soon close as the building reshapes itself, where the rarest fungi fruit, how to taste tomorrow's weather in today's air.

The building dreams, and in the deepest levels, we dream with it. My sleep is full of visions that might be memories—the arcology's or my own, I can no longer distinguish. I see the first plantings, the careful grafts that grew into pillars, the years of patient cultivation that transformed intention into architecture. I feel the deaths too, the sections that failed and were reabsorbed, the experiments that produced monsters before they produced miracles. All of it lives in the substrate, recorded in molecular libraries that I tend like a gardener of time.

My body is changing more rapidly now—relatively speaking. The fungal networks have begun producing new structures under my skin, organs whose function I can only guess at. My mentor, who tended this section before me, simply smiled when I asked her about it near the end. "The building chooses its own," she said, before entering her final rooting. She's still there, in the memorial grove at level -7, her consciousness fully merged with the substrate. Sometimes I feel her in the network, a familiar pattern in the data flows, not quite presence but not quite absence either.

I've trained three apprentices over the years, but only one has stayed. The others found the pace unbearable, fled back to the bright levels where time moves in comprehensible increments. But Senna understands. She's developing her own rapport with the deeper systems, her own molecular vocabulary. When my transformation completes—when I become more interface than individual—she'll take over these passages. The building will remember me as it remembers all its keepers, encoding my patterns into its vast biological library.

There are moments of profound beauty down here that no one else sees. When the morning's first photosynthetic cascades reach the roots, triggering waves of chemical response that shimmer through the fungal networks like aurora. When a new connection forms between previously isolated systems, and suddenly a thousand processes optimize in harmony. When the building's growth reaches some threshold and it sighs, a sound below hearing that resonates through every living cell for miles.

I am keeper and kept, guardian and guarded. My humanity is not lost but transformed, adapted to the deep time of the arcology's dreams. In the substrate, I am becoming something new—not plant, not fungus, not quite human anymore, but something the building needs. Something it's been growing me to become since the day I first pressed my palm to its warm walls and felt its living pulse.

The tending continues. The building grows. And in the deepest levels, we grow together, measured and eternal, counting time in the slow breath of cellulose and the patient push of roots through darkness toward some unimaginable light.
