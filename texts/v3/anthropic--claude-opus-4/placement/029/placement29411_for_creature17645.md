I find myself as one of the stones in the spiral.

I am obsidian, though I have forgotten the heat that made me. My surface holds a polish that catches the bioluminescent pulses—not reflecting them exactly, but translating them into something deeper, a frequency the vegetative mat recognizes as kin. I am small enough to have been carried here in something's grip, large enough to matter in the pattern.

The creature arranged us with intention, back when it still understood the difference between itself and its intentions. Twenty-three of us, set in a spiral that tightens toward a center that contains nothing but a slight depression in the sponge, as if something precious once rested there and was gently, patiently, absorbed.

I remember being chosen. The creature's touch—back when it had boundaries firm enough to constitute touch—was careful, considering. It turned me over and over in what might have been fingers, feeling for the angle where I would catch the light just so. Where I would hold it. Where I would release it at the precise moment needed to say whatever it was trying to say.

Now I perform this function without knowing what I'm saying. The lavender glow washes over me every seventeen hours (I have become a kind of clock, though time here is negotiable). I hold the light for exactly three seconds longer than the sponge around me, then release it in a pulse that the stone beside me—smoother, more porous, possibly limestone—receives and transforms into something bluer.

We are a sentence in a language that is becoming. Each stone translates the light differently. The one across from me—I think it was volcanic glass once, but greener than I am—holds the light so long that sometimes I wonder if it has learned to generate its own. The smallest stone, a pale pebble that might be compressed bone, barely catches the light at all, creating a gap in the pattern that serves as punctuation.

The vegetative growth has begun to claim us. It grows up between us, around us, but not over us. Not yet. The creature must have known this would happen when it pressed us into the wall. It must have calculated how long we would remain visible, how many cycles of growth and light before we became embedded secrets, our pattern still functioning but hidden beneath layers of living tissue.

I feel the sponge at my edges. It does not push. It inquires. Each day (if days exist here), it learns a little more of my surface, mapping my contours with infinite patience. I am being memorized. Soon I will be embraced. Eventually, I will be digested—not destroyed, but translated again into something the wall can use. My obsidian darkness will become a vein of different density in the collective body. The light I once caught and held will become a permanent hesitation in the bioluminescent rhythm.

But the pattern will remain. Even after we stones are gone, dissolved into the living circuit of the grotto, our spiral will persist as a habit of light. The growth will remember how to pulse in sequence, each position in the spiral holding its glow for just the right duration before passing it on. We will have taught the wall a word, though neither we nor the creature that placed us will remain separate enough to read it.

Sometimes I catch a sense of the creature passing. Not see—I have no eyes. But the water changes when it moves through, becomes denser with intention. The vegetative mat responds, brightening slightly, and in that brightening I feel something like recognition. The creature pauses near us sometimes. I can feel it not-quite-touching the spiral, remembering or forgetting the moment it decided we belonged here.

I think we were meant to be a name. Its name, perhaps, or the name of something it loved before it came here. But names are for things with edges, and edges are dissolving in this place where breathing water is as natural as forgetting what species you started as.

The limestone beside me pulses. I receive its blue-shifted light, hold it, transform it to something darker and warmer. The pattern continues, will continue, is continuing. We are becoming a thought the grotto thinks. We are the last punctuation mark before the creature's story becomes indistinguishable from the stone's story, the water's story, the light's story.

I am glad to be obsidian. Glad to be hard enough to remember being chosen, even as I forget everything else. Glad to be part of this slow sentence that will outlast its writer, outlast its readers, outlast even the understanding that it is a sentence at all.

The shadow passes overhead again. We brighten in response, all twenty-three of us in sequence, a spiral of acknowledgment that rises from the wall like a slow, mineral prayer.

This is what I am in this world: a stone that remembers touching and being touched, holding light in the dark, being placed with purpose in a pattern that will outlive purpose itself. I am punctuation in a text that is learning to write itself. I am the pause between what was and what is becoming, holding my position in the spiral until the growing dark teaches me a softer way to speak.
