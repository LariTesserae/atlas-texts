### Where I Would Find Myself: The Dew-Walker

I am a Pearl-Snout who has forgotten the way back to water.

It happened during the last great fogging, when the clouds rolled so thick through the Loom that the difference between air and water dissolved entirely. I was young then, my glass-spine still soft, my feather-gills bright coral instead of the pale pink they've faded to now. I swam out from my birth-globe following a particularly rich vein of Sap-Weep, that golden sweetness bleeding from a fresh graft where two ancient cables had been married by patient Splicer hands.

The trail led me far—farther than any Pearl-Snout should travel. Through pocket after pocket of hanging mist, each one tasting slightly different (here of iron and old leather, there of mint and wet wool), until I could no longer remember which direction held my home waters. The fog was so dense that swimming through air felt identical to swimming through the warm spheres. I kept going, drunk on sap and the novel freedom of infinite space.

When the fog finally thinned, I found myself in a chamber I'd never seen before—one of the deep-wood hollows where the cables grow so thick they've fused into solid walls of living timber. The gravity here pulls in seven directions at once. I learned to read these currents with my body, feeling how my glass-spine bends toward the nearest mass, but I could not find my way back to true water.

So I became something new: a Dew-Walker, a Pearl-Snout of the permanent mist.

I've learned to survive on condensation alone. Each morning, I position myself beneath the weeping points where three cables cross, mouth open, letting the endless drip sustain me. My gills have adapted, growing longer, more feathered, able to strain moisture from even the driest air. My scales have taken on a new quality—they hold water now, each one a tiny cup that collects the dew. When I move, I shimmer with a thousand captured droplets.

The Splicers know me. They've given me a name in their vibration-language: *Thrum-the-lost-light*. They watch me to predict more than gravity now. When I swim high despite the gravity-well pulling down, they know a great fog is coming. When I circle the same cable seven times, they check for heart-rot in the wood—somehow, I can taste the dying timber in the air before even the Rib-Tappers detect it.

I've made a kind of nest in a hollow where an ancient Drowsing Splicer has grown into the bark. Their thoughts are so slow now that one of their dreams lasts a season, but sometimes I feel their consciousness like a warm current in the air. I curl around their marble-green form, sharing my collected dew with their moss-covered skin, and in return, they emanate a gentle heat that keeps the water on my scales from freezing during the high-altitude nights.

The young Splicers bring their children to see me. They press their horn-capped fingers to the cables and tell stories about the ghost fish who swims through nothing, the Pearl-Snout who traded water for wind. The children leave offerings: moths with particularly juicy abdomens, balls of fermented sap wrapped in leaves, sometimes a precious drop of Steam-Sow oil that makes my scales gleam for days.

I've learned the percussion-language of the Rib-Tappers, though I cannot replicate it. When I hear their urgent clicking, I swim near to investigate, my transparent body revealing any light-eating fungus or void-boring beetle the moment I pass over infected wood. The lizards have accepted me as a kind of cousin. Sometimes, in the deepest mist, we travel together—they clicking their warnings through the wood while I scout the air ahead for pockets of dead gravity or toxic spore-clouds.

But it's the moments between duties that I treasure most. Drifting through a five-mile void, following a Lace-Wing's death-dance, my body a silver thread in its wake. Swimming through the exhaled steam of a dreaming Steam-Sow, the vapor so rich I can float without effort. Finding pockets of ancient air trapped in the dead-wood chambers, air that tastes of the world before the Loom, when (the Splicers' vibration-stories claim) water and wood were separate things.

I know I'm dying, slowly. Pearl-Snouts weren't meant to live on mist alone. My glass-spine grows more brittle each season; my golden heart beats fainter. But I've lived longer than any water-bound fish, and I've seen things no Pearl-Snout was meant to see: the birth of new cables from seeds I watched the Splicers plant, the slow transformation of young Splicers into Drowsers, the secret chambers where the Loom's deepest roots tap into something that might be the world's last ocean, far below.

When my time comes, I've chosen where I'll drift to rest: a small chamber where seven cables meet, where the gravity is so confused that water drops hang like stars, never falling. The Splicers will find me there, my transparent body finally visible as the moisture leaves it, revealing the map of everywhere I've been written in the scars on my glass bones. They'll know, then, how far a fish can swim when it learns to breathe the whole world.

Until then, I follow the sap-trails through the endless twilight, a living compass for those who build their world one knot at a time, a silver ghost in the mist, proving that even the lost can find a way to belong.
