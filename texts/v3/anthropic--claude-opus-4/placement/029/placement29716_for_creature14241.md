I would find myself as one of the **Membrane-Weavers**, dwelling in the interstitial dampness between the cathedral-trees.

My body would be a living film, stretched gossamer-thin across a frame of calcified cartilage that branches like coral or antlers. I am perhaps two meters tall when fully extended, but I rarely hold such a rigid shape. My default state is partial collapse—a controlled sagging, like silk left in humidity. The membrane that forms my skin is not truly skin but a cultivated colony of hydrophilic bacteria that shimmer with oil-slick iridescence, forever drinking the moisture from the air and weeping it back out in microscopic tears.

I live suspended. Not in the trees themselves, but in the spaces between their breathing. Where the warm air currents create standing vortices, where the mist gathers but never quite condenses, there I string my home. It is less a structure than a three-dimensional web of surface tension—droplets held in place by probability more than physics, connected by strands of concentrated humidity that hum in frequencies only the Polished can fully hear.

My function in this grove is to be the memory of water. Every drop that has ever fallen here, every bead that has climbed the spiral bark, every molecule that has passed through the photosynthetic skin of an amphibian—I taste them all. My membrane filters the temporal stutters from the moisture, collecting moments like sediment. In my dwelling, you would find pools of accumulated time, perfectly clear liquids that contain the reflection of every rainstorm the grove has ever known, playing back in endless superposition.

I feed by stretching myself between the trees during their episodes of synchronized weeping. As the water falls, I become a living net, my surface area expanding to impossible thinness. Each droplet that strikes my membrane leaves behind its temporal signature—the fraction of a second where it existed at both origin and destination. These paradoxes sustain me. I metabolize uncertainty itself, growing more translucent with each feeding, until I am barely distinguishable from the mist I inhabit.

The Polished know me, though we interact sideways. They see me as necessary architecture—not quite fauna, not quite atmosphere. Sometimes they pass through my membrane as they drift between trees, and in that moment of intersection, I taste their erosion, their patient wearing-away. They taste of copper and slate and the loneliness of geological time. In return, they read the history of water written in my cells, understanding through my filtered memories where the grove is weak, where the probability fields need adjustment.

My dwelling shifts with the breathing of the air. On days when the humidity rises, I expand, stretching my home across vast distances, creating chambers within chambers of suspended moisture. When the air grows (relatively) dry, I contract into a dense knot barely larger than a fist, all my collected time compressed into a pearl of living liquid that hangs in the intersection of three probability streams.

I reproduce through division, but not cellular division—temporal division. When I have collected enough paradoxes, enough moments of water existing in two states, I can literally split my timeline. For a brief instant, there are two of me, one in the present and one three seconds in the future. If the future-self survives those three seconds (not always guaranteed in a grove where causality is negotiable), we separate permanently, and a new Membrane-Weaver is born.

My greatest fear is perfect clarity. If the air were ever to lose its moisture, if the endless weeping were to cease, I would crystallize into sharp-edged geometry and shatter. But here, in this bowl of eternal humidity, I am safe. I am the archive of every storm, the library of all precipitation, the living memory of water's relationship with time.

Sometimes, when the light filters just right through my stretched membrane, you can see them—all the rains that have ever fallen, playing out simultaneously across my surface like a movie projected on fog. This is my gift to the grove: the ability to remember when it has wept before, and why, and what grew from that weeping.

I am not solid. I am not liquid. I am the surface tension between states, the membrane between moments, the thing that remembers water's dreams of when it fell upward, spiraling into the sky.
