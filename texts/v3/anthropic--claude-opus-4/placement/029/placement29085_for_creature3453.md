In this world where stone breathes in centuries and light becomes sustenance, I would find myself as a **Keeper of the Condensation Altars** – one of perhaps three or four humans who maintain vigil at this shrine throughout the turning of seasons.

I am not young. My hands are weathered like the basalt itself, fingernails permanently rimmed with stone dust. I have spent forty-three winters here, arriving first as an apprentice to the previous Keeper, learning the patient art of reading moisture patterns and maintaining the delicate balance that the lichen-stone colonies require.

My dwelling is one of those wind-breaks huddled against the cliff base, expanded over decades with careful dry-stacking. Inside, the walls are lined with obsidian flakes – my tools – and small leather pouches containing different grades of sand for polishing. A sleeping shelf carved from living rock holds furs and a brazier for yak dung, my only heat source. The most precious possession: a leather-bound journal, pages filled with observations of which nodules show new growth, where the condensation pools most reliably, how the light angles change with the seasons.

Each dawn, I wake before the sun crests the eastern wall. The routine never varies: check each of the seven primary condensation altars, clearing any frost or debris with a soft brush made from high-altitude grass. Some mornings, I must re-carve the spirals worn smooth by centuries of freeze-thaw. This is done with reverence – not to any god, but to the process itself. The lichen-stone colonies depend on these pools; disturb the water's gathering, and decades of growth might be lost.

I have learned to see what others cannot. Where visitors perceive only cold stone, I read stories in the colored veins. That violet cluster near the third altar has advanced two millimeters in my lifetime – explosive growth by their standards. The emerald networks in the shadow of the great overhang are struggling; I've been slowly grinding a shallow channel to direct more condensation their way. It will take another decade to know if I've succeeded.

My body has adapted. My metabolism has slowed to match the rhythm of this place. I eat twice a day – dried meat, barley cakes, butter tea – but hunger rarely troubles me. The thin air no longer makes me gasp. I can remain motionless for hours, watching the play of light across a single nodule, learning to perceive the pulse of mineral photosynthesis. In winter, I barely speak; in summer, when the rare pilgrim arrives, my voice cracks from disuse.

The pilgrims puzzle me now. They come seeking transcendence, performing elaborate rituals at the altars. They do not understand that the true mystery is not in human ceremony but in the crystalline networks slowly consuming the mountain. I guide them through their observances, ensure they don't disturb the critical moisture points, but I no longer try to explain. How could they comprehend a being that measures thought in centuries, whose body is a lattice of crystal and stone?

Sometimes, in the perfect confluence of light and angle, when the sunset strikes the western wall and the condensation beads catch fire with reflected radiance, I fancy I can feel them – the lichen-stone colonies – in some wordless way. Not communication, but a sort of resonance. As if my patient tending of their water sources has, over decades, made me comprehensible to them on some infinitesimal level. As if I have become another slow process in their stone-bound world, as reliable as the wind and worthy of the same dispassionate acceptance.

When I die – and I will die here, I knew that when I first saw the colored veins pulsing in the stone – another will take my place. The altars will continue to gather moisture. The wind will still sing through the sculpted basalt. And somewhere in the dark heart of a glassy nodule, violet crystal will add another microscopic layer to its patient, centuries-long reach toward the light.

I am not here to worship, but to maintain. I am a custodian of processes too slow for human comprehension, a bridge between the timescale of breath and the timescale of stone. In learning to perceive the lichen-stone's glacial vitality, I have found my own metabolism slowing, my own awareness stretching across years instead of moments. I am becoming, in my own small way, geological.

This is enough. This is everything.
