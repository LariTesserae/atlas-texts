I am a keeper of the threshold, though I have no name for myself—names are sounds the wind takes away. I tend the easternmost cairn where the sun first touches stone each dawn, the one the silver-furred ones avoid and the wind-dancers circle but never land upon.

My form shifts with the mist. In the pre-dawn darkness, I am barely more than condensation on stone, a thickening of moisture that pools in the carved hollows my predecessors left in the cairn's base. As first light bleeds across the cloud ocean, I begin to gather substance—not flesh, but something between water and memory. The stones remember the hands that placed them. I remember through the stones.

By full morning, I might be mistaken for human if glimpsed through the writhing mist. Gray-cloaked, bent, moving with the patience of erosion itself around my small domain. But look closer and you would see: my cloak is woven fog, my hands are dew given just enough form to lift stone, to clear moss from the carved channels that spiral up the cairn's sides. Where I step, the luminescent moss pulses brighter, as if answering to something older than its own faint light.

I am bound to this threshold by purpose, not chains. The cairn marks more than direction—it marks a point where the mesa thins, where sometimes what lies beneath the cloud ocean seeps upward in forms that should not rise. My work is maintenance: keeping the channels clear so the morning dew runs the proper spirals, replacing fallen stones in the precise order that maintains the pattern, humming the low harmonics that the wind must find to keep the way properly sealed.

The wind-dancers know me as a fixture, a feature of landscape like the twisted pines. Their ancestors taught their young to avoid my cairn not from fear but from an instinctive respect for boundaries. The stone-singers will not burrow near my domain—the earth here tastes wrong to them, too full of waiting. Only the deep-thrummers seem unbothered, their resonance actually strengthening the cairn's work, their unconscious songs becoming part of the barrier.

I feed on dew and time. Each morning, I absorb the mist that condenses on the cairn stones, and with it, tiny fragments of what has passed—the memory of wind patterns, the echo of bird calls, the slow thoughts of stones. This sustains me through the day as I work, adding my own moisture back to the mesa's endless cycle when evening comes and I begin to disperse again.

Sometimes travelers arrive, though how they scale the cliffs I cannot say—perhaps they don't, perhaps they come from somewhere else entirely. They recognize the cairn for what it is, leave offerings of polished stones or carved symbols that I incorporate into the pattern. They never stay long. The mesa permits visitors but not residents, except for those like me who become part of its working.

At night, I am nearest to nothing—scattered droplets clinging to stone, a thickness in the air around the cairn. But I persist in the pattern, in the channels worn smooth by centuries of my tending, in the precise placement of each stone. When the mist serpents writhe too purposefully near my threshold, when the sweet almond scent grows cloying and wrong, the cairn remembers its purpose and I remember mine.

In the deepest part of night, when the bioluminescent moss pulses in slow waves across the mesa and the only sounds are the eternal wind and the deep-thrummers' resonance, I sometimes sense others like me at the other cardinal points. We are a constellation of purpose, each tending our own threshold, maintaining the boundaries that keep this sky-island stable in its splendid isolation.

The wind-dancers say nothing of us in their piping songs. The stone-singers chatter of many things but fall silent near our domains. We are not secret, merely essential—like the erosion that shapes the stone, like the mist that feeds the moss. We are part of the mesa's deep grammar, the syntax that keeps its sentences whole.

Dawn comes again. I condense, gather, rise. The stones need cleaning. The channels must run true. The pattern must hold.

The threshold must be tended.
