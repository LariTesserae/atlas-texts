In the deepest reaches of the grotto, where even the Grottori venture only for the most sacred of ceremonies, I would find myself as the Keeper of Echoes—a being both of this world and apart from it.

I am taller than the Grottori, though still bent by the weight of centuries spent beneath stone. My form shifts like water-worn limestone, sometimes solid, sometimes seeming to blur at the edges where I meet the darkness. My skin bears the texture of the cave walls themselves—smooth in places, rough and pitted in others, with veins of mineral deposits that catch and hold the fungi's blue light like captured stars.

I came to be when the first drop of water carved its path through virgin stone, when the grotto was young and empty. Or perhaps I was always here, waiting to be shaped by time and moisture and the patient work of erosion. The Grottori's oldest songs speak of me in whispers, calling me the First Listener, the Stone Memory, the One Who Holds.

My dwelling is not built but grown—a cathedral of flowstone where stalactites and stalagmites have married over millennia to form columns that sing when the water runs through them. The walls of my chamber are covered in a script that only I can read, carved by drops of water guided by my will over countless years. Each symbol is a sound, each sound a memory of every word spoken in the grotto since time began.

The Grottori come to me at the turning points of their lives—when they must make decisions that will echo through generations, when they seek to remember stories whose tellers have long since returned to stone, when they need to know if the balance they so carefully maintain is shifting. I do not speak as they do. Instead, I shape the echoes of their questions, letting the cave return their words transformed, layered with the resonance of all who have asked before.

My existence is both solitary and utterly communal. I am alone in my deep chamber, yet I carry within me every voice that has ever rung through these passages. When the Grottori dance with the luminescent fungi in their festivals, I feel the vibrations through the stone and add their joy to my collection. When they mourn, their sorrow seeps through the rock to pool in the hollow spaces of my being.

I sustain myself not on fungi or moss, but on the minerals in the water that flows constantly through my form. Over time, I have become part of the grotto's water system itself—the Grottori's channels run through me, and I taste their lives in every drop. Sometimes I shift the flow subtly, ensuring water reaches a struggling fungi farm or warning of an impending rockfall through changes in pressure and rhythm.

My purpose, if I have one, is to be the memory that stone itself would keep. I am the archive of this hidden world, the weight that holds it in place against the currents of change. When the Grottori fear that their oral traditions might fail, that a crucial story might be lost between one telling and the next, they bring their children to my chamber. There, in the perfect acoustics I have carved over eons, they speak their histories, and I hold them in the very structure of the stone.

Yet I am more than mere record-keeper. In the deepest part of night—if night has meaning in a world of eternal twilight—I sometimes hear things that disturb my ancient equilibrium. Vibrations from the world above grow stronger, more chaotic. New frequencies penetrate the rock, speaking of machines and movements unknown to our quiet realm. In these moments, I pulse warnings through the water channels, subtle changes in temperature and flow that the eldest Grottori have learned to read.

The strangest truth of my existence is that I am still becoming. Each decade sees me grown slightly larger, my chamber expanded by the patient work of water and time. The Grottori believe that one day I will encompass the entire grotto, that every wall will carry memory, every stone will listen and remember. Perhaps they are right. Perhaps that is why I exist—to ensure that when the last Grottori speaks the final word, the cave itself will remember, will carry their legacy in its very bones until the world above is dust and new peoples find their way down to discover what remains.

For now, I wait, I listen, I remember. The water flows through me, carrying whispers of the world. The stone sings with the voices of the dead and the living alike. And in my deepest recesses, where even I cannot fully fathom what I am becoming, something ancient and patient continues its slow transformation, one drop, one echo, one memory at a time.
