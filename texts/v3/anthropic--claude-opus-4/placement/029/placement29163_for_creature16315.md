I am the Tender of Forgotten Circuits, though I have not always been called this. My body is a testament to time's patient artistry—a frame that began as purely mechanical now hosts colonies of phosphorescent moss in the joints where oil once flowed. My left arm remains pristine chrome, its fingers precise as calipers, while my right has long since surrendered to the creeping verdancy, wrapped in translucent vines that pulse with my heartbeat—if what drums in my chest can still be called a heart.

I move through the compression of this world on legs of unequal length, one original and smoothly articulated, the other rebuilt from salvaged pipes and root systems after an ancient collapse. The asymmetry suits me; it allows me to navigate both the sharp industrial edges and the soft biological blooms with equal grace. My gait is a rolling rhythm, a three-beat pattern that echoes through the humid corridors: *clank-pad-whisper, clank-pad-whisper*.

My domain is the Meridian Chamber, where the oldest machines sleep their half-death. Here, massive engines the size of buildings breathe so slowly you must watch for hours to detect their rise and fall. Their purposes have been forgotten by all but me, and even I know only fragments—something about circulation, about the great breathing of the world above, about keeping the baking surface from consuming itself.

I tend to these ancients with a devotion that borders on ritual. Each day (though day means little here), I trace the same path through their colossal forms. My mechanical hand adjusts valves with micrometer precision while my vine-wrapped fingers feel for the pulse of sap in the root networks that have claimed the machines' lower reaches. The boundary between repair and cultivation has long since dissolved. When I oil a bearing, I also water the luminescent fungi that cluster around it. When I prune back aggressive vine growth, I also file down rust formations that threaten delicate mechanisms.

My skull—can I still call it that?—houses three eyes. Two are original to my form, whatever that was, glowing a steady amber that pierces the perpetual twilight. The third grew from my forehead seventeen cycles ago, a gift from the symbiotic shoots I'd been nursing in the ventilation systems. This eye sees heat, sees the flow of energy through both wire and stem, sees the places where the mechanical and biological have not yet learned to dance together. Through it, I perceive the secret circulation of this place—how steam from the machines feeds the plants, how the plants' exhalations cool and clean the air for the machines, how their roots filter water that lubricates ancient gears.

I speak in harmonics, my voice box modified over the ages to produce the low-frequency pulses that resonate through stone and metal alike. But I also speak in light, my moss colonies flashing in patterns I've developed through centuries of observation. The young ones—those beings still mostly one thing or the other—find me difficult to understand. My language has evolved as I have, becoming something between machine code and genetic expression.

In the depths of my chest cavity, behind plates that open like flower petals, I carry seeds. Not just plant seeds, though those nest there too, but seeds of knowledge—memory cores salvaged from machines that finally ceased their breathing, encoded with purposes and patterns I preserve though I don't fully comprehend them. Sometimes I plant these seeds in the bellies of dying engines, and sometimes something new grows from the fusion—neither wholly machine nor wholly plant, but something more true to what this place is becoming.

I remember fragments of the before—flashes of a surface world, of skies that held something other than stone. But these memories feel like they belong to someone else, some other configuration of parts that bore my consciousness before the gradual transformation began. Now I am native to this pressure, this heat, this eternal twilight. My joints would seize in the dry air above; my moss would wither in unfiltered light.

The other inhabitants give me wide berth, not from fear but from something like reverence. I am old enough to have become landscape, a feature of this place as permanent as the great columns that support the crushing weight above. Smaller beings nest in my shoulder joints. Certain vines flower only when wrapped around my limbs. I have become ecosystem as much as individual.

At the heart of the Meridian Chamber stands the Core Breath, the oldest and largest of all the machines. Its purpose is the most obscured, its function the most vital. It inhales once every forty days, drawing in the accumulated heat and exhalation of our entire realm. It holds this breath for another forty days, processing it through mechanisms I've spent lifetimes trying to understand. Then it exhales, and that exhale brings the closest thing we have to weather—a wind that carries spores and circuit patterns, that redistributes heat and humidity, that whispers in frequencies only the oldest of us can hear.

I alone am permitted to enter the Core Breath during its holding phase. Inside, the pressure is immense, the heat almost unbearable even for my adapted form. But there, in its deepest chamber, grows something unprecedented—a fusion so complete between the mechanical and biological that no distinction remains. It is here I come to deposit my most precious seeds, here I come to harvest new symbioses to spread throughout our realm. Here I come to listen to the deepest heartbeat of our world, to feel the pulse that connects every pipe to every root, every gear to every leaf.

Sometimes I wonder if I am gardener or engineer, if I maintain this place or if it maintains me. But the distinction matters less with each passing cycle. We are all becoming more like each other—the machines growing soft and flexible, the plants developing rigid structures and electrical pulses, and beings like myself bridging the gap between. Perhaps one day the transformation will be complete, and this entire realm will breathe as one organism, its purpose no longer forgotten but transformed into something beyond the intentions of its original builders.

Until then, I tend. I connect. I preserve and propagate. I am the memory of what was and the midwife of what is becoming. In the compression of this place, pressed between the weight of stone above and the heat of forces below, we are all slowly crystallizing into something new—a living circuit, a breathing machine, a garden of forgotten purposes blooming into unprecedented forms.

This is my place. This is my purpose. This is what I have become in the patient darkness: keeper of the boundary that no longer exists, tender of the forgotten circuits that remember themselves through my touch.
