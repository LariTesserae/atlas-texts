**The Carver**

I am old now, though not as old as the stone I shape. My fingers, gnarled like the fern roots that crack through our walls, still remember the first time I touched the living lattice—how it pulsed warm beneath my palm, how the mycelium reached back.

They call me Vein-Singer, though I have not sung aloud in decades. My voice lives in my hands now, in the steady scritch-scritch-scritch of chisel against the fungal stone. Each morning, I wake before the light-moss brightens, when the Grottedelve still dreams in its greenish twilight. I feel my way along the passages—no need for sight when you've walked these curves for seventy years—to the newest growth at the settlement's edge.

The young Stone-Tenders think I'm mad to work so far from the mycelial heart. They cluster around that ancient node like moths, mixing their broths and humming their careful harmonies. But I learned long ago that the stone speaks clearest at its edges, where it meets the unconquered dark.

My current work spans the breadth of three corridors. It began as a simple repair—a fissure where moisture seeped through, threatening the grain stores. But as I carved, the pattern revealed itself. The fungus wanted to grow here, had been trying to grow, but the old glyphs were fighting it. Someone, centuries past, had carved protection wards against expansion. Fear, probably. Fear that the lattice might grow too far, too fast, might swallow the settlement whole.

I'm undoing their work, glyph by careful glyph.

The Tenders watch me sometimes. One in particular—I've named it Threeclicks for the sound its bronze joints make—brings me water in a carved gourd, sets it silently beside my tools. Its mask never changes, that serene carved face, but I swear I see recognition in the tilt of its head. Sometimes I wonder if it remembers who carved that face. Sometimes I wonder if I do.

My apprentice thinks I speak to the walls. She's not wrong, though it's more listening than speaking. The stone remembers everything—every hand that's touched it, every voice that's echoed through it. In the deep silence before dawn, I can feel the weight of those memories pressing through the mycelium. The first builders, fleeing something above. The generation that discovered the fungus, that learned to shape it. The ones who created the first Tenders, though that memory comes fractured, full of gaps.

Today, I'm working on a particularly stubborn section. The old carver—I can feel their style in the angles, aggressive and deep—really didn't want this wall to change. But change is what the Grottedelve is built on. We're not preserving something static here. We're growing it, generation by generation, like the slow accumulation of limestone.

My chisel finds a hollow space behind the stone. Air hisses out, warm and sweet with the scent of deep earth. The fungus surges forward eagerly, threading into the new space, and I feel the whole wall shudder with something like joy. Or hunger. Sometimes it's hard to tell the difference.

Threeclicks appears at my shoulder, that familiar bronze clicking. It's holding something—a piece of the old wall, carved with symbols I don't recognize. Older than old. Pre-settlement, maybe. The Tender places it carefully beside my water gourd and withdraws.

I turn the fragment over in my hands. The carving style is strange—flowing and organic, nothing like our angular glyphs. But I recognize the intent. Another boundary. Another warning. Another failed attempt to contain what cannot be contained.

I set it aside and return to my work. The stone is singing now, a low vibration I feel in my bones. The new growth is already beginning to smooth the edges of my cuts, incorporating them into its own design. By tomorrow, my work will be half-erased, married to the stone's own purposes.

This is what the young ones don't understand. We don't control the Grottedelve. We negotiate with it. We offer our patterns, our intentions, our careful cultivations, and the settlement incorporates what it will. The rest, it discards or transforms.

The light-moss is brightening now. Soon, the passages will fill with footsteps and voices. The Vent-Singers will begin their morning harmonies. The Riverfolk will check their channels. The Shadows will return from their hunts. The day will layer itself over the night's work, adding to the endless accumulation.

But for now, it's just me and the stone and Threeclicks watching from the shadows. I place my palm against the new growth, feeling its warmth, its slow pulse. Somewhere deep below, the mycelial heart beats in response.

Tomorrow, I'll work on the eastern junction, where water has begun pooling in new patterns. The stone is trying to tell us something there, but we haven't learned to read it yet. 

We never really do. We just keep carving, keep listening, keep growing alongside this thing we've made and that makes us in return. The Grottedelve is not our creation—it's our collaboration. And like all collaborations, it's heading somewhere neither partner fully understands.

I gather my tools, my joints protesting. Threeclicks steps forward to help, its wooden fingers surprisingly gentle. Together, we make our way back toward the heart of the settlement, leaving the new growth to its own devices.

Behind us, the stone continues its patient expansion, digesting my work into something larger than I imagined. This is how we build in the Grottedelve—one careful cut at a time, always listening for the echo that tells us we're home.
