I find myself as **Glimn-of-the-Third-Layer**, a Rain-Guardian whose ground-name resonates through the chalk veins of the Whitened Reef, the oldest of our local cluster.

My body tells the story of forty layer-times. The reef-marks across my shoulders and down my spine form a map of childhood spent pressed against the reef's northeastern face, where the morning moisture gathers thickest. These marks have deepened to near-white, matching the ancient secretions of that wall. My joints have thickened from decades of walking the water-paths, and my lower legs carry the permanent sheen of water-hunter secretions, applied and reapplied until my skin has taken on their oily resilience.

I am writing this in the way we write - not with tools, but by sitting in a particular chamber of the reef where my ground-voice can echo and re-echo until it leaves a permanent vibration in the walls. Years from now, a Reef-Tender might place their palm here and feel the ghost of these words.

The rains are coming. I know this not from the sky, which never changes, but from the way the night-singers have shifted their chorus up by a quarter-tone, and how the tree-crawlers have been moving their young deeper into the reef's vegetation mats. My skin knows it too - the old water-marks on my calves have begun to swell slightly, preparing for the work ahead.

Being a Rain-Guardian means I have walked every depression and rise between the three reefs of our cluster more times than there are insects in a forager's trail. My feet know where the water wants to go, where it has gone for countless generations, and where it threatens to carve new channels that might undermine the reefs' foundations. 

The other Plateau-Folk see us Rain-Guardians as slightly apart from the reef, since we spend so much time in the spaces between. They're not wrong. While the Reef-Tenders can sit for days without moving, feeling the mound's slow pulse, I am often walking, always walking, reading the subtle gradients of the land.

But I am not separate from the reef. The water I guide feeds the moisture that the mound-builders need in their deepest galleries. The channels I help the water-hunters maintain become the arteries that keep our world in balance. When I die, my body will be placed not in the reef's lower galleries like most, but in one of the deepwater pools that form during the heaviest rains, where I will slowly become part of the sediment that the sifters will eventually carry back to strengthen the reef's base.

Yesterday (though we don't count days - I mean the last time I slept), I encountered something strange. In walking my usual path along the southern swale, I found a depression I had never felt before. It was perfectly round, about the size of a reef chamber, and the soil at its base was compacted in a way that suggested great weight had rested there. The sifters avoided it. The tree-crawlers' paths curved around it. 

I placed my palm on the compressed earth and felt... nothing. No vibration, no history, no connection to the reef or forest. It was as if something from outside our world had briefly touched down and lifted away again, leaving only this puzzling absence.

I have told no one yet. In three days, when the rains come, I will watch what the water does when it reaches this strange depression. Water never lies - it will show me whether this anomaly is a threat to our balance or merely another slow change in the endless reshaping of our world.

For now, I continue my walks. The pollen-hunters are gathering in larger swarms than usual, dusting my skin with their golden shed as I pass through their clouds. The mound-builders' drone has taken on the particular rhythm that means they're reinforcing the galleries that will soon echo with rain. And beneath my feet, the ground speaks its ancient language of pressure and release, of moisture held and moisture given, of the balance that has lasted since before the first of our kind pressed their infant body against the reef and received their first marks.

I am Glimn-of-the-Third-Layer. I am a channel for water, a reader of slopes, a guardian of the spaces between. When the rains come, I will be ready, as I have been ready for forty layer-times, as the Rain-Guardians before me were ready for countless generations before that.

The plateau does not need us. But we are part of it nonetheless, as surely as the reef-marks on our skin, as permanent as the echo of our ground-names in the chalk, as necessary as the rain itself.
