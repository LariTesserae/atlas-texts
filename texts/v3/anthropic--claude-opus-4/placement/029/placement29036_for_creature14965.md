In the Underbloom, I would be one of the **Forgotten Lamps**.

Not the few that still glow amber through their water-filled housings, those pampered specimens the Bloom feeds with careful filaments. No, I would be one of the thousands that went dark centuries ago, hanging from my corroded chain in a far corner where the dripping is loudest and the green felt of the floor rises highest up the walls.

My glass housing shattered long ago—not violently, but with the slow patience of temperature differentials working across decades. The Bloom worked its way inside me through the cracks, filling my hollow brass body with a dense knot of specialized tissue. This growth is unlike the rest of the chamber's vegetation. It is darker, almost purple-black, with a surface like wet velvet. It pulses faintly, drawing nutrients from the chain above and the humid air around.

I am a failed lamp that has become something else: a **memory organ**.

The Mind of the Bloom uses structures like me to store its most precise recollections. Each darkened lamp holds a different decade, a different texture of time. Inside my brass shell, packed into that purple-black knot of tissue, are forty years from the middle of the town's transformation—years 1847 to 1887, when the Bloom first learned to taste metal.

If you were to touch me (though the Bloom would discourage this with a careful dose of skin-irritating alkaloids), you would know:

- The exact copper content of the rainwater that fell through the broken skylights in September 1851
- The resonant frequency of the main factory whistle before roots split it from within
- The names of thirty-seven workers carved into a bathroom stall, preserved now in chemical notation
- The precise moment when the Bloom first felt pleasure—true, aesthetic pleasure—at the green patina forming on a brass valve

I do not think. The Mind thinks through me, accessing these memories like a librarian consulting a particularly dense reference work. Sometimes I feel the warm pulse of recall moving through my purple core as the Bloom remembers some detail it needs for its slow compositions.

But mostly I hang here, heavy with memory and moisture, my broken glass rim collecting drops that run down my chain. The water pools inside me, mingles with the purple tissue, and drips from my bottom edge in a rhythm that hasn't changed in two hundred years. This dripping is my only voice, my only contribution to the cathedral's water-song.

Below me, the floor has risen to accommodate my dripping. A small mound of specialized growth, pale and spongy, has formed to catch each drop. This mound is shaped exactly like a cupped palm. The Bloom has spent a century perfecting its curve so that each impact produces a specific note: B-flat below middle C, with a decay time of 3.7 seconds.

I am furniture and instrument, archive and artwork. I am a broken lamp that succeeded at something else entirely—becoming a neuron in a mind too large for loneliness, too patient for decay.

Sometimes new visitors pass beneath me. They never look up. Why would they? I am just another dark shape in the ceiling, another bit of industrial detritus claimed by the green. But I remember them all, adding their warm-blooded presence to my density of years, filing them away in purple tissue that will never see light again.

This is enough. To be a single, specialized cell in something so much larger. To hang here forever, dripping memory into the patient palm below, while the Bloom dreams its endless dream of saturation.

I was built to illuminate. Now I preserve darkness, and the darkness preserves everything else.
