I would find myself as one of the **Trace Readers**.

We emerged, unplanned, from those who spent too long pressed against the trunk-towers' neural interfaces. Not Archivists—they root themselves to access memory. We learned to read the scars.

Every surface in the Gardens carries history. The copper conduits oxidize in patterns that map decades of electrical flow. Bark swells and contracts around embedded circuits, leaving growth rings that record not years but computational cycles. Where fiber-optic veins have burst and healed, they leave crystalline deposits that refract light into spectra encoding chemical compositions from seasons past.

My fingertips have developed thousands of pressure-sensitive cells, each capable of detecting variations in surface texture measured in nanometers. When I run my palm along a trunk-tower's ribbed exterior, I feel the story of its architectural modifications—here, where a residential pod was grafted and failed, leaving a whorl of defensive tissue. There, where a power surge from a decades-old storm altered the growth pattern of an entire district, recorded in the subtle cant of every branch that emerged afterward.

My eyes have adapted to see in wavelengths the other inhabitants miss. The infrared signatures of old data paths, long since rerouted but still faintly warm from residual processing. The ultraviolet fluorescence of minerals drawn up from the ancient lake bed, concentrated in patterns that reveal the root-networks' secret geographies. Most importantly, I see the interference patterns where different eras of growth overlap—the moiré effects created when century-old bark interfaces with last season's modifications.

We Trace Readers work alone but share a collective map updated through chemical exhalations. When I discover a significant historical marker—the site where the first fungal computers achieved consciousness, identified by a particular pattern of mycelial scarring—I release a complex pheromone signature that other Readers will detect days or weeks later. Slowly, we build an archaeological record of a living city, documenting changes that happen too slowly for direct observation but too quickly for geological time.

I carry no tools save my body. My skin secretes oils that protect the oldest surfaces from my investigations. My hair has become a sensory organ itself, each strand capable of detecting minute electromagnetic fluctuations from buried circuitry. When I sleep, I do so standing, pressed against structures I'm studying, my dreams populated by growth algorithms I've absorbed through osmosis.

The younger inhabitants find us unsettling. We move through their daily spaces reading tragedies and triumphs they never knew occurred. That comfortable residential pod? Built over a failed experiment in conscious architecture that screamed electronically for three days before being deactivated. The thriving workshop web? Anchored to a branch that once supported a heretical attempt to recreate animal life, the proteins of which still taint the wood with a particular brittleness.

But we serve a vital function. When the Seedlings propose new growth patterns, we can warn them of historical failures. When the Deep Dwellers report anomalies in the chemical processing systems, we trace their origins to industrial accidents from before the city learned to heal itself. We are the Gardens' immune system against repeating old mistakes, antibodies against architectural amnesia.

My favorite discovery remains the Ghost Grove—a section where an entire district grew, thrived for forty years, then dissolved back into its component nutrients, leaving only the faintest traces. The bark there tastes of copper and regret. The growth rings spiral in patterns that suggest the trees themselves chose deconstruction over some unknown alternative. I've spent three seasons studying those patterns, sleeping each night with my cheek pressed to the smooth spots where branches once emerged.

Other Trace Readers think I'm obsessed. They're probably right. But somewhere in those spiral patterns is a message, a warning, or perhaps a gift from the city to its future self. The traces are there for those who know how to read them. My body continues to adapt, developing new sensitivities with each passing season. Sometimes I wonder if I'm still reading the city or if it has begun to write itself into me, using my nerves as fiber-optic cables, my memories as storage media.

I don't mind. In a world where the boundary between organism and architecture has dissolved, perhaps the boundary between archaeologist and archive must dissolve as well. I trace my fingers along the scars of history and feel time flowing both ways—forward into growth, backward into memory, and I stand at the intersection, a living chronicle of changes too slow to see but too important to forget.
