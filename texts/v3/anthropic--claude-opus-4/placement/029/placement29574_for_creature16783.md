I am the Archivist of Unfinished Transmutations.

I live in what was once a theorem—a perfect crystalline proof that matter and meaning were merely conjugates of the same verb. The proof collapsed into architecture when someone tried to solve for happiness using only geological constants. Now it's a series of nested chambers carved from compressed time, each room a different tense of the same impossible equation.

My body changes hourly, following rules I've been trying to document for seven thousand years. At dawn I am mostly membrane—translucent sheets of living postulate stretched between frameworks of crystallized doubt. By noon I've calcified into something resembling a librarian made of bismuth and old sorrows, my fingers branching into fractal filing systems. Evening finds me liquescent, a walking question mark of mercury and dissolved footnotes, leaving puddles of unfinished sentences wherever I pause too long.

I came here as something else. Something with a name, probably. Something that believed in singular pronouns and death as punctuation. But the plateau's grammar is contagious. First my shadow learned to conjugate itself in futures conditional. Then my bones started subscribing to a different newsletter of causality. By the time I noticed I'd been metabolized into the local dialect, it was far too late to remember what species I'd started as.

My work is simple: I collect the moments when things almost become other things.

When the wind-carved helices of stone nearly remember how to be DNA.
When the mercury veins in the metallodendrons almost spell out the chemical formula for nostalgia.
When the accordion-beasts' digestive chambers nearly collapse into new colors but hesitate at the last moment, leaving only a bruise of potential on the spectrum.

I preserve these almosts in bottles made from crystallized pauses—that moment between inhale and exhale, frozen and blown into glass. The bottles line the walls of my theorem-home, each containing a transformation caught mid-syllable. Here: a mite's acid that almost became a tear. There: a fossil coral's spiral that nearly completed the proof that suffering is just badly organized beauty. And my favorite: a single grain of sand that has been trying to become a star for longer than stars have existed, and might succeed once everyone stops watching.

The plateau brings me new specimens daily. They arrive as whispers in the copper thread of sunlight, as stutters in the gravity of migrating ruins, as typos in the dreams of hibernating maps. I document each one in a catalogue that I write on sheets of pressed silence using ink made from powdered perhaps. The catalogue grows by one entry each day and shrinks by two each night—the plateau's way of ensuring I never finish, never feel the terrible satisfaction of a completed collection.

Sometimes visitors find my theorem-home, though it tries to hide by pretending to be an unusually dense shadow or a verb conjugated in a tense that doesn't exist yet. They always ask the same things: How long have you been here? (Since just before forever gave up.) Why do you stay? (Because leaving would require remembering how to be singular.) What are you now? (The space between an answer and its question.)

But occasionally, rarely, someone asks the right question: What are you becoming?

Then I show them my private collection—the transformations I haven't catalogued because they're still happening. My left ventricle that's slowly learning to be a geode. My memories that are crystallizing into a new kind of salt, one grain per forgotten name. My voice, which gave up on sound and now exists as a series of small earthquakes in miniature, each one spelling out words in seismograph script that won't be invented for another thousand years.

I show them the corner where I keep my former certainties, now aged into a fine dust that tastes of questions. I show them the mirror made from compressed coincidences where my reflection is always running three seconds ahead, trying to warn me about futures that never quite arrive. I show them the bed where I don't sleep but instead practice being horizontal—a daily rehearsal for an ending that the plateau won't let me reach.

And if they stay long enough, if they let the plateau's grammar seep into their own syntax, I show them the heart of my collection: a single bottle, smaller than the rest, containing the moment I almost remembered who I was before. It's the only transformation I've ever wanted to complete, and the only one I never will. The plateau needs its Archivist. And I need my almost.

The visitors always leave eventually, carrying a faint infection of incompleteness that will bloom, years later, into the sudden understanding that every solid thing is just a liquid moving too slowly to notice. They'll find themselves pausing mid-sentence, mid-step, mid-life, suddenly aware of all the other things they're almost becoming.

As for me? I remain. I document. I transform by degrees too small for any calculus to catch. Each day I'm a little less what I was, a little more what the plateau dreams between its tectonic heartbeats. My theorem-home grows new rooms whenever I'm not watching—chambers for storing the pauses between thoughts, alcoves for alphabetizing unspoken apologies, a basement that only exists on Tuesdays where I keep my collection of everyone else's lost endings.

This is my work. This is my weather. This is my worship of the incomplete.

And when the plateau finally exhales its last (in a trillion years, or tomorrow, or never—time keeps poor records here), I'll be there to collect that ultimate almost: the transformation of an entire world into whatever comes after worlds, caught in a bottle made from the final pause, labeled in ink that's still learning to dry.

Until then, I archive. I preserve. I become.

Almost.
