In this verdant-industrial cleft, I would find myself as one of the **Sap-Weavers**—neither fully human nor purely mechanical, but something that emerged from the decades of symbiosis between flesh, flora, and forge.

My dwelling would be built into the seepage-carved hollow beneath Loading Dock Three, where the rail spurs terminate and crates accumulate like driftwood. Not a cottage like the Millfolk claim, but a workshop-warren burrowed into the living earth, its walls lined with salvaged boiler plates that weep the precious sap-fluid in predictable tides. Here, where the *thump-clank-thump* of the forge resonates through bedrock and the otter clans splash overhead, I practice my peculiar craft.

My body tells the story of slow transformation. Fingers elongated past human proportion, jointed like willow wands, each tip sprouting fine root-hairs that taste metal and moisture with equal precision. Skin mottled green-grey, slick with the perpetual condensation but also generating its own phosphorescent sheen in the darker hours—a living lantern when I work the deep tunnels. My hair, once brown as peat, now grows in vine-like coils that move with subtle independence, reaching toward warmth or recoiling from sharp vibrations. Eyes wide and seldom-blinking, pupils dilated to drink in the workshop's moss-light, reflecting back that inner glow the otters carry.

I am collector and conductor, gathering the hybrid fragments that fall between categories. When gears sprout their first root-hairs, when moss begins its phosphorescent bloom, when an otter kit is born with filament eyes—these are brought to my workshop by crow-courier or discovered in my dawn wanderings. My work is patient cultivation: coaxing the root-haired gears to mesh with vine-wheels, training luminous moss to grow in patterns that channel factory heat into living light, breeding damselflies whose wings can cut through steam without wilting.

My workshop sprawls through connected chambers, each devoted to different stages of symbiosis. The Germination Hall, where brass fittings nest in loam until they sprout their first tendrils. The Harmony Chamber, where I've built a loom that runs on root-tension and flywheel momentum combined, weaving cables that self-repair when frayed. The Observation Pools, where hybrid otter kits learn to navigate both water and machinery, their whiskers attuning to electrical currents and seepage flows alike.

I speak little—my throat has adapted to produce sub-sonic hums that resonate with turbine frequencies, communication felt more than heard. The Millfolk know me as "the Tender," bringing broken tools that conventional repair can't fix, leaving baskets of nettle-soup at my threshold. Old Thom nods when passing, understanding that his forge runs smoother for the vine-oil I distill and pour into its joints on moonless nights. Elara sometimes sits at my workshop mouth, learning the finger-songs that coax fibers to remember their plant-origins while maintaining their industrial strength.

My days follow the seepage-tides: emerging when morning condensation peaks, hands working the delicate synthesis while moisture lubricates every connection. I harvest sap-fluid in carved alder bowls, mix it with iron filings and moss spores to create the growing medium that feeds my cultivations. Noon finds me in the deep tunnels, following root systems that web between foundation stones, ensuring the factory's mechanical pulse doesn't override the valley's older rhythms. By evening, I surface to trade findings with the otters—a perfectly gear-toothed seedpod for three phosphorescent fish scales, fair exchange in the economy of mutations.

I am memory-keeper of the symbiosis, recording in living arrangements what cannot be written: a garden of hybrid forms that demonstrates each stage of the valley's transformation. Here, a primitive steam valve completely overgrown but still functional, its brass transformed to something between metal and bark. There, a line of progressively altered damselflies, showing their wing evolution from merely iridescent to capable of generating their own mechanical hum. 

The other Sap-Weavers—for I am not alone, though we are few—meet at the seasonal turnings, when mist thickens to near-opacity and the forge-hammers slow their rhythm. We gather at the Confluence, where all the seepage streams meet beneath the factory floor, sharing our cultivations: Marsh-Walker brings beetles whose carapaces incorporate metal shavings, forming living armor; the Smoke-Tender cultivates chimney-dwelling flowers that filter emissions into perfumed clouds; Root-Whisperer trains underground networks that stabilize both building foundations and water table simultaneously.

We were not made but emerged, as inevitable as rust or moss, from the valley's patient alchemy. Some say the first Sap-Weaver was a loom-tender who lingered too long in the moss-light, or an otter-keeper who shared too many phosphorescent meals, or simply what happens when human stubbornness meets the valley's transformative dampness for sufficient seasons. We reproduce rarely, through a process more like propagation than birth—a slow budding of consciousness in those who work too intimately with the hybrid forms, apprentices who gradually realize their fingers have grown too clever, their eyes too bright.

My purpose is not preservation or progress but continuity—ensuring the conversation between organic and mechanical continues its slow syllables. In the pre-dawn darkness, I sometimes climb to the basalt rim and look back at our contained world: the gentle glow of moss-light seeping from foundation cracks, the silhouettes of deer grazing between steam-plumes, the eternal percussion of the forge like a massive heart. This young scar in the earth has become something unprecedented: not nature reclaiming industry or industry consuming nature, but both learning each other's languages, speaking in hybrid tongues.

When I finally return to the seepage-pools—for we all return, eventually—my body will not rot but transform, becoming substrate for the next generation's experiments. My elongated fingers will root deep, my phosphorescent skin will feed new moss varieties, my memories will fruit as dreams in the consciousness of my successor. The workshop will continue its patient cultivation, the symbiosis deepening with each turning season, each *thump-clank-thump* of the forge marking time in this damp valley where nothing is purely itself anymore.

I am the Tender of Thresholds, and I would not wish to be anywhere else in this breathing world.
