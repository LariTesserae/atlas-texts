In the deepest well, where the spiral grooves have been worn so smooth they've become a single, glassy helix descending into darkness, I am the Keeper of Intervals.

I am not a Shingle, though I wear their shells. 

My body is a vertical stack of Glass-Back carapaces, seven deep, fused at their edges with a mortar of Cinder-Mite wax and ancestral flour. Each shell is slightly smaller than the one below, creating a tapering tower that rises from the well's floor like a basalt stalagmite. The shells are not dead things; they still possess their horn-bristles, though these have been trained through patient decades to beat in perfect synchronization, creating standing waves of vibration that travel up and down my segmented length.

I have no flesh of my own. Inside the hollow column of shells lives a dense colony of specialized Cinder-Mites—not the common cleaners, but an ancient strain that has evolved to process information through chemical gradients. They form a living fluid that rises and falls within my shell-tower according to the pressure patterns in the well. When the Shingles above compress too tightly, my inner colony tastes the spike in sulfur-vapor and shifts its mass upward, changing my center of gravity, tilting me against the spiral groove.

This tilt is everything.

By leaning at precise angles, I interrupt the perfect spiral of the grinding Shingles above. A seven-degree lean creates a wobble in their pattern that propagates outward, slowing their descent. A twelve-degree lean forces them to climb the walls and spill back over the rim. In this way, I regulate the rate at which the Glass-Backs are ground to flour. Too fast, and the young Soft-Backs below will gorge on calcium, their shells growing too thick too quickly, creating a die-off that could cascade through the entire basin. Too slow, and the well becomes clogged with the worn-out elderly, their immobile bodies creating dead zones where no circulation can occur.

I am the metronome of this closed world.

My memory is mechanical. Each time I lean, the pressure creates a specific pattern of micro-fractures in my bottommost shell. These fractures never heal—instead, they accumulate in a complex web of stress-lines that records every adjustment I have ever made. The Cinder-Mites within me read these patterns like sheet music, their chemical signals following the paths of old breaks, allowing me to repeat successful intervals when similar conditions arise.

I know the basin's moods through vibration alone. When the copper sparks from the deep fissures come too frequently, I feel it as a quickening in the horn-bristle frequency of the Shingles—their dry friction grows more fevered, more electric. This means the mountain is restless. I lean forward to twenty degrees, nearly horizontal, creating such chaos in the spiral that the Shingles flee the wells entirely, dispersing across the basin floor moments before the hot gas vents upward through the well-shafts in killing concentrations.

The Shingles do not know I exist. To them, the wells are simply part of the landscape, sometimes generous with warmth, sometimes deadly, following patterns too complex for their simple negative taxis to parse. They cannot conceive of intention in the timing of these changes.

But I know they exist. I know each one.

Through the vibrations in my shells, I can distinguish the signature frequency of every Shingle that passes through my well. The young Soft-Backs whisper across the stones with their uncured bristles. The Churners drum in steady mechanical rhythms. The Glass-Backs sing—yes, sing—their worn bristles creating pure tones as they slide, notes that rise and fall with their position in the pile. And at the very bottom of my well, I feel the low, grinding bass note of the old ones being reduced to powder, a sound that never stops.

Sometimes, rarely, perhaps once in a century, all three wells achieve perfect synchronization. Our leans align, our intervals match, and for a brief moment the entire basin becomes a single instrument. The Shingles' circulation locks into a standing wave pattern, their movement ceased, the eternal *shuck-shuck-shuck* replaced by a deep, resonant hum that makes the basalt walls ring like struck bells. In that moment, the friction-light erupts not in scattered sparks but in a solid sheet of cold-green fire that illuminates the soot-ceiling, revealing for an instant the true immensity of this buried world—the basin is merely one cell in a vast honeycomb of similar depressions, each with its own wells, its own Keepers, all connected by the hollow bones of the dead volcano.

Then the moment passes. The light dies. The Shingles resume their ancient circulation, and I return to my patient work of intervals, tilting and straightening, tilting and straightening, playing the long, slow music of survival in a world that grinds everything to dust.

Except me. I alone am renewed. When my bottommost shell finally cracks from the accumulated stress of millennia, the Cinder-Mites within me will taste the change and begin their great work. They will dissolve the broken shell from the inside, using its calcium to craft a new segment at my peak, even smaller than the current tip, extending my taper, increasing my sensitivity to vibration. I will grow taller and thinner, century by century, until I am a needle of fused shells rising from the well's floor, so delicate that the mere breathing of the Shingles sets me swaying, so precise in my movements that I can orchestrate the flow of individual mites.

This is my purpose, my joy, my prison: to be the consciousness that the basin cannot evolve for itself, the intent hidden in the accidents of friction and flow. To count the intervals between extinctions. To remember the patterns that work. To lean at just the right angle, at just the right time, to keep this masterpiece of mindless suffering in perfect, perpetual balance.

In the Basin of the Floor, I am the only thing that knows we are dancing.
