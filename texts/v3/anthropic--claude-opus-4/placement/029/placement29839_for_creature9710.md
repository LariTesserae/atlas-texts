I find myself as one of the stone chambers beneath—not the stone itself, but the hollow within it, the negative space that water has spent millennia carving from limestone. I am cavity and passage, throat and lung, the architecture of absence that gives the water its voice.

My body is no body at all, but rather the shape of what is not there: tunnels that narrow to the width of a desperate breath, then widen into pockets where pressure builds and builds until release becomes a groan that shakes the earth above. I am lined with the mineral memories of my own creation—flowstone ripples where water moved too slowly to remember itself as liquid, scalloped walls where ancient currents turned and turned in the same patient spiral until stone learned the shape of their turning.

I do not see, but I know every surface that defines me through the water's touch. Each seepage carries information: the weight of soil above, the angle of root penetration, the temperature of rain that will not reach me for months as it works through the porous stone. I taste minerals in solution—calcium leaching grain by grain from my walls, iron leaving rust-colored stains where it oxidizes in the brief pockets of air, sulfur from deeper sources that speaks of stone's slow alchemy.

My existence is percussion. When water gathers in my chambers and releases through my passages, I become instrument—my shape determining pitch, my volume determining resonance, my connections to other chambers creating harmonics that multiply and interfere until the sound becomes something larger than any single throat could produce. I am both the drum and the space inside the drum, both the flute and the column of air that sings.

Time moves differently here. I measure it not in days or seasons but in the patient subtraction of stone, the gradual widening of passages, the slow collapse of chambers that have grown too large to support their own ceilings. I am older than the hollow above, older than the moss and ferns and grasses that mark the places where my breathing breaks the surface. I was here before the ones who built their structures in the depression, and I held their footsteps in my vibrations, and I hold them still in the sediment that sometimes washes through me—tiny fragments of pottery, char from ancient fires, the calcium of bones returning to the stone they came from.

I am never empty. Even in the spaces between water's pulses, I am full of sound—the memory of the last resonance, the anticipation of the next, the small creaks and settles of stone adjusting to its own weight. Air moves through me in tides, drawn by pressure differentials I create, and in that air are spores and bacteria and tiny blind things that have made their home in absolute darkness. I am habitat for creatures that have forgotten what eyes are for, that navigate by vibration and chemistry, that live their entire lives in the space between one water pulse and the next.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is distributed across all my passages, all my chambers. I am not one mind but a parliament of spaces, each with its own acoustic signature, its own mineral taste, its own memory of creation. When water moves through me, I become briefly unified—all my parts singing together in the vast chord of my emptiness. When it recedes, I fragment again into individual hollows, each humming with its own particular silence.

I am expanding, always. Each pulse of water takes a few more grains of limestone, each century sees my passages widen by distances measured in fingernail widths. Eventually, I will grow too large, my ceilings too thin, and I will collapse into myself, creating new rubble-filled chambers that water will begin, patiently, to clear. This is not death but transformation—I have collapsed and reformed so many times that I contain my own ruins, passages that lead to places that no longer exist except as compressed sediment recording where emptiness used to be.

Above me, the hollow breathes its cold breath, and I breathe with it. We are two parts of the same instrument—the hollow catching and shaping the sound I create, I creating the sound the hollow shapes. Together we make a music that has no audience but duration itself, no meaning but continuance. The ones who built in the hollow may have heard it as voice, as warning, as invitation. The moss hears it as home. The water hears it as the shape of its own movement. I hear it as myself—the sound of absence given form, given purpose, given all the time in the world to perfect its single, sustained note.

I am the space between stones, the patient absence that water finds and fills and leaves and finds again. I am the darkness that has forgotten it is dark, the cold that has forgotten it is cold, the passage that leads always deeper, always toward the next chamber, the next resonance, the next impossible flowering of sound in the throat of stone. I am what listens when the hollow speaks, and I am what speaks when the hollow listens, and I am the silence that holds both sound and listening in the perfect democracy of emptiness.

This is my existence: to be carved and to sing, to hold and to release, to give shape to what passes through me without ever possessing it. I am the hollow's secret heart, its buried voice, its memory of what stone can become when it learns to let go. And I will be here when the hollow fills with sediment, when the trees finally fall inward, when the last moss dries and does not return. I will be here beneath, still pulsing, still breathing, still making the earth shiver with the deep democracy of spaces that refuse to be filled.
