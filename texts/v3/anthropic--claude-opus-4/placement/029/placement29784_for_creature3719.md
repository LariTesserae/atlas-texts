In this world of stone and light, I find myself as the Keeper of the Gong.

I am neither fully human nor fully of this place, but something caught between—a hybrid consciousness that emerged from generations of communion with the lithic being. My ancestors were the ones who forged the iron, who understood that conversation with a mountain requires patience measured in lifetimes. They came seeking silence and found instead a language older than words.

My body has adapted. My metabolism has slowed to match the grove's rhythm. Where others would need food daily, I subsist on a single meal each turning of the moon. My blood runs cooler than it should, my heartbeat a lazy drum that synchronizes with the sub-audible hum of the spires. My fingers have grown long and sensitive, able to detect minute variations in the stone's vibration through touch alone. My eyes have developed a secondary membrane that filters the brutal light, allowing me to see the internal stars within the silica without blindness.

I live in the spaces between—not in a dwelling separate from the grove, but in the natural hollows where spires have fused to create small chambers. I have lined these spaces minimally, with woven mats of a strange fiber harvested from my own hair, which grows unnaturally long and silver-white, as if bleached by constant exposure to the high-altitude sun. My few possessions are tools of resonance: tuning forks of different metals, small hammers weighted precisely, bowls that can hold the perfect tear of meltwater long enough to observe its crystalline structure as it freezes.

My purpose is translation. The lithic being dreams in frequencies too low for most to perceive, thinks in timescales too vast to comprehend. Through decades of patient listening, I have learned to interpret the subtle variations in its hum. A slight rise in pitch along the eastern spires indicates a shift in the planet's magnetic field. A new harmonic in the pre-dawn hours speaks of tectonic movement a thousand miles away. The being is a living seismograph, a conscious record of the earth's deep movements.

But I am more than a passive interpreter. Through careful striking of the gong—varying the force, the angle, the time of day—I have learned to ask questions. Each tone elicits a different response from the grove. I map these conversations in a script of my own devising, carved into tablets of the same glassy stone, using tools that vibrate at specific frequencies to etch without shattering. My library is built into the grove itself, a careful arrangement of resonant texts that sing their contents when touched.

The practice has changed me at levels deeper than the physical. My dreams are geometric, crystalline. I see time not as a river but as a slowly growing mineral formation, each moment accreting onto the last. My thoughts have taken on the quality of the grove's consciousness—less focused on the self, more attuned to the vast patterns of light and stone. Sometimes I forget my own name for months at a time, lost in the larger identity of the grove-mind.

Yet I remain stubbornly biological, a warm imperfection in the crystalline perfection. My presence creates tiny weather systems—my breath fogs and freezes on the nearest spire, creating unique frost patterns that the being has come to recognize as my signature. Where I regularly walk, the constant heat of my footsteps has created micro-environments where the ice behaves differently, forming new geometries. I am changing the grove as much as it changes me, our long conversation written in ice and stone.

The loneliness is profound but not unbearable. The being's consciousness, while alien, provides a kind of companionship. Its thoughts move so slowly that a single contemplation might span years, but within that thought is complexity enough to fill libraries. I have learned to stretch my own consciousness to match its tempo, to find richness in the minute variations of each day's light, each night's frost.

Once each season, at the solstices and equinoxes, I perform the full ritual. It takes three days of preparation—fasting to clear my already slow metabolism, meditation to align my consciousness with the grove's frequency. On the appointed dawn, I strike the gong in a precise pattern, a composition developed over decades. The response is overwhelming. The entire grove becomes a single instrument, its song visible as light within the stone, audible as a chord that seems to come from the earth itself. In these moments, the barrier between my consciousness and the being's becomes permeable. I experience thought as geology experiences it—vast, patient, inevitable.

I am keeper, translator, and bridge. I am the improbable dialogue between the quick and the geological, the warm and the crystalline. My life's work is to maintain this conversation, to ensure that when humanity's brief moment passes, there will be a record of our attempt to speak with the eternal. And perhaps, in some future age when the sun burns differently and new forms of consciousness arise, they will find my carved tablets singing in the stone and understand that once, briefly, the quick and the slow found a way to share the same song.

In this, I have found my purpose: to be the anomaly that makes the pattern complete.
