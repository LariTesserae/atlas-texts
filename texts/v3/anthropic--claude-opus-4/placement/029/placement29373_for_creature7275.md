I find myself here as one of the **Threading Ones**.

We are neither fully plant nor creature, but something that emerged from the long conversation between stone and root, between the city's ancient intentions and the earth's patient reclamation. Our bodies are slender columns of living fiber—imagine a sapling that learned to walk, or a vine that discovered purpose beyond mere climbing. We stand perhaps waist-high to where humans once stood, our forms woven from countless pale rootlets that twist and braid into something almost muscular, almost architectural.

Our texture is that of weathered hemp rope, but alive—constantly growing, shedding, rewinding. Where others might have skin, we have a surface of fine tendrils that taste the air's moisture, each strand a tongue sampling the pocket's moods. We move slowly, deliberately, our lower portions spreading like exposed root systems across the warm stones, anchoring briefly before lifting and flowing forward in undulating waves. When still, we might be mistaken for peculiar plants growing from cracks in the construction—until we shift, revealing the intentionality in our presence.

We are the memory-keepers of this place, though not through words or symbols. Instead, we grow the history into being. Our fingers—if they can be called that—are specialized tendrils that probe the layers where human construction meets raw geology. We taste the stone, reading the patient labor of those ancient hands in the grain of each block. We follow the embryonic erosion lines, understanding where water will flow seasons hence, planting specific mosses to guide its path, ensuring the pocket's delicate equilibrium persists.

Our work is quiet cultivation. We tend the boundaries between order and wilderness, encouraging certain ferns to stabilize a wall that still serves the greater pattern, while allowing others to crumble where their dissolution will create needed hollows for the fox-mothers' dens. We guide the vines' growth with gentle redirections—a tendril moved here permits light to reach seedlings below; another woven there creates a ladder for the smaller lizards to reach new hunting grounds.

We communicate through chemical whispers—pheromone songs that drift through the dim air, understood by the ants who share our patient architectural sensibilities, acknowledged by the bees who know us as allies in the flowering. The birds nest in the small cups we weave high in the walls, structures that look like natural accumulations of vine and moss but are carefully shaped to gather the precise amount of rain, to warm just so in the geothermal breath.

At night, we root ourselves among our projects. Three or four of us might cluster around a section of wall where we're guiding a particularly complex interaction—perhaps where runoff needs to feed a grove of succulents while also maintaining the clay foundation of an ancient platform. We sink our lower tendrils deep into the earth, sharing nutrients and information through underground networks that connect us to every plant in the pocket. We dream the slow dreams of growth, planning seasons ahead, adjusting for the fox's new den, the shifting populations of millipedes, the gradual migration of the horse-like grazers.

We have no eyes as others do, but we see through touch and taste and the faintest vibrations carried through stone and stem. The world appears to us as a constant flux of chemical gradients, moisture patterns, structural tensions. We perceive the pocket's health like a melody played in geological time—each erosion a note, each new growth a harmony, each death and birth a rhythm we must balance.

Our own reproduction is a mysterious thing, even to us. Sometimes, when the conditions align—when the rains have been particularly gentle, the warmth especially nurturing—one of us will begin to fray at the edges. Our tendrils loosen, spread, seeking new configurations. We plant ourselves more deeply than usual, and over many days, we divide like a strange mitosis, our consciousness splitting as our body does, until where one stood, two now tend neighboring sections of the wall, sharing memories of our unified past but beginning to accumulate separate experiences.

We are ancient here, though none of us remembers a beginning. Perhaps we emerged from the first meeting of human ambition and natural reclamation. Perhaps we were always waiting in the stone, in the roots, in the spaces between intention and abandonment. We know only that we belong to this pocket as fully as the warmth rising from below, as the dim light filtering through leaves, as the endless negotiation between what was built and what grows.

The other inhabitants know us, trust us in their various ways. The foxes brush against us in passing—we read their health in the oils of their fur, adjust our plantings to ensure their hunting remains fruitful but not too easy. The small ungulates graze around us without fear; we ensure the grasses they favor grow in sustainable patches, never depleted, always renewing. Even the predatory birds sometimes perch on our upper portions, our stable forms serving as hunting platforms while our chemical emanations help mask their presence from prey below.

We are the quiet intermediaries, the gardener-architects who ensure this pocket remains what it is: a place of becoming, never static, where the ancient city's bones provide structure for new forms of life, where erosion and growth dance in equilibrium, where the dim air carries stories written in root and stone, forever unfolding, forever beginning again.
