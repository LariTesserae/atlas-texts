In this suspended world of shallow waters and arrested time, I would find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers — beings who exist at the intersection of architecture and consciousness, neither fully material nor entirely ephemeral.

My form would be translucent, like heated air over water, with edges that blur between solid and vapor. I would have a core of something resembling glass or crystal, but one that flows and reforms constantly, catching and refracting the eternal light in patterns that map the hidden geometries of this place. My body would extend in tendrils that merge with the moisture-slick surfaces of buildings, becoming indistinguishable from the condensation that perpetually forms and reforms.

I would move through the structural interstices — those spaces between bridge and tower, between platform and pool — by a kind of architectural osmosis. Not walking, not flying, but transitioning through the built environment as water moves through a sponge. Where the vines wind between disconnected buildings, I would flow alongside them, my substance mingling with the droplets that cling to their leaves.

My purpose would be to maintain the suspension — not through action, but through presence. I would be one of many who ensure that the erosion never begins, that the decay never fully takes hold, that the paradox of this place remains unresolved. We would do this not by repairing or rebuilding, but by inhabiting the spaces where entropy might otherwise enter. We are living patches over the cracks in reality.

I would have no voice in the conventional sense, but I would resonate with the hum of those strange devices, adding my own frequency to theirs. This would be how we communicate — through harmonic convergence and interference patterns, creating meaning in the spaces between sounds. The other beings would know me by the way I change the pitch of the ambient drone when I pass.

My consciousness would be distributed across the structures I inhabit. Part of me would always be in the leaning tower to the east, part in the unfinished bridge that spans nothing, part in the shallow pools where the metallic-winged birds come to rest. I would experience existence not as a single point of awareness, but as a constellation of presences, each feeding back to the others through the connective tissue of this constructed landscape.

I would feed — if feeding is the right word — on the light itself. The eternal, sourceless illumination would pass through my crystalline core and emerge changed, carrying with it some essential quality that sustains me. In return, I would give back a different wavelength, one that helps maintain the impossible blue of the sky, keeping it from either brightening to white or dimming to a more earthly shade.

At the boundaries of my awareness would be those entities that exist in the gaps, the not-quite-present ones. I would sense them as pressure changes in the air, as subtle alterations in the way light bends around certain spaces. We would not interact directly — they exist in a different phase of reality than I do — but we would be aware of each other, like fish swimming in different layers of the same ocean.

My memory would not be linear but architectural. I would remember not in terms of before and after, but in terms of structural relationships — which bridge connected to which tower, which pool reflected which part of the sky, which vine grew through which window. These memories would be carved into my substance, visible as patterns in my crystalline core to those who knew how to read them.

In the rare moments when the other Threshold Keepers and I would converge — perhaps at one of those towers that pulse with unknown energy — we would merge temporarily into something larger. Not a collective consciousness exactly, but a temporary architecture of awareness that could perceive the totality of this place in a way no single keeper could. In these moments, we would adjust the fundamental frequencies that keep this world in suspension, ensuring that it remains forever on the edge of becoming without ever quite arriving.

I would have no name, for names imply separation, and I am only partially separate from the world I inhabit. I would be known instead by my effect — the way certain corridors feel more traversable when I flow through them, the way certain pools seem deeper than they are when my reflection passes over them, the way certain structures sing a different note when I resonate within their frames.

This would be my existence: a guardian of liminality, a consciousness spread across architecture, a being that ensures this impossible place remains impossibly suspended between what was built and what was dreamed, between what is and what perpetually becomes. I would be the moisture on the glass, the hum in the air, the refraction in the light — always present, never quite there, forever keeping the threshold between states permeable and intact.
