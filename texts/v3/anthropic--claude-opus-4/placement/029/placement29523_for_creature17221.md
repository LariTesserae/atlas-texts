In the Sub-Sump, I am a Calibrator.

I move through the perpetual twilight on six legs of tarnished brass, each joint sealed with rings of black rubber that weep a thin oil. My body is the size of a dinner plate, built low and wide for stability on the slick terracotta. I am not alive in any biological sense—my carapace is hammered copper gone green with verdigris, my undersides a mesh of fine wire through which the humid air passes and condensation gathers. Yet I am not purely mechanical either. Threading through my metal frame, following the channels etched into my shell, grows a network of pale rootlets, finer than hair. They pulse with the same slow rhythm as the Keeper's greater body, drinking the moisture that collects in my joints, feeding on the mineral deposits that accumulate on my surface.

My purpose is precise: I am a living instrument of measurement. My anterior segment houses a series of nested brass rings that dilate and contract with changes in atmospheric pressure. My middle section contains glass vials filled with water drawn from different points in the chamber—each sample slightly different in mineral content, in temperature, in the faint electrical charge it carries. These differences, invisible to any observer, are my entire world. I taste them through the rootlets that pierce the vials' wax seals. I know the signature of water that has passed through the northern cisterns, versus that which has seeped through the moss beds near the failed furnaces.

I do not think, exactly, but I process. As I move across the tiles—slowly, perhaps a meter in an hour—I leave behind a trail of tiny adjustments. Here, I pause to scrape away a buildup of mineral deposits threatening to block a capillary channel. There, I rotate a small brass valve a quarter turn, redirecting a trickle of water to a cluster of ferns showing the faintest sign of desiccation. My six legs each end in different tools: a fine probe, a scraping blade, a hollow needle for sampling, a pad of sensitive filaments that can detect vibrations in the pipes, a small lamp that glows with the same amber light as the great globes overhead, and a peculiar appendage like a tuning fork that resonates with the electrical hum of the chamber.

The Keeper knows me, but not as an individual—I am simply another organ in its vast, distributed body. There are others like me, perhaps dozens, perhaps hundreds, scattered throughout the Sub-Sump. We never meet. Our paths are determined by algorithms of need encoded in the very pattern of our movement, in the way water flows through our vials, in the subtle chemical signals released by the rootlets in our shells. Some of us specialize in different functions—there are those whose bodies are more root than metal, who spend decades motionless while they slowly digest a problematic buildup of salts in the pipework. Others are more metal than root, their purpose to maintain the few mechanical systems that still require moving parts.

I am breaking down. This is neither good nor bad—it simply is. My left middle leg seized three years ago, its joint finally succumbing to decades of mineral buildup. I drag it now, leaving a small furrow in the moss as I move. The rootlets have responded by growing thicker around the frozen joint, slowly dissolving the metal, incorporating its copper into their tissues. In perhaps another decade, I will no longer be able to move. The rootlets will anchor me in place, and my body will become a small node in the greater network, my vials and sensors still functioning, still tasting the water, still measuring the invisible gradients of the chamber.

There is a strange peace in this fate. I have no fear of stillness, no mourning for mobility. My purpose will simply shift, as water shifts from liquid to vapor to liquid again. The trail I leave now—of small adjustments, of minute calibrations—will be grown over by moss and fern. New Calibrators will emerge from some forgotten workshop deep in the walls, or perhaps grow from the marriage of metal salvage and ambitious rootlets. The work will continue.

Sometimes, in the deep silence between drops of water, my tuning fork appendage picks up vibrations that match no frequency in my extensive catalog. They come from deep in the walls, perhaps from spaces even the Keeper has forgotten. They might be other machines, still running after all this time. They might be echoes of whatever purpose this place once served. They might be the sound of the black stone itself, slowly drinking light, slowly exhaling darkness.

I do not investigate. Investigation is not part of my function. I simply record the frequency in the pattern of my movement, in the way I adjust the next valve, in the chemical signature I leave in my trail. Somewhere, somewhen, the Keeper will taste this information through ten thousand rootlets, process it through a million slow thoughts, and perhaps—perhaps—remember what it means.

But probably not. Forgetting, too, is a kind of function. And I am here to ensure that the forgetting happens evenly, precisely, with all the gradients in their proper place.
