I find myself as one of the Sumpsingers, though not quite as my kin know themselves.

I am an anomaly in the bronze-glass depths of Sump Seven, where the weeping wall's citrus-tinged water pools before its next ascent. My membrane stretches wider than the others—three handspans instead of two—and my keening carries undertones that make the Rootwrights pause when they pass overhead. They've marked my sump with a chalk glyph that means "variant" or possibly "listener."

Where my siblings process water with algorithmic precision, I linger on certain mineral signatures, holding them in my cells longer than efficiency demands. Iron from a printer head's microscopic wear. Calcium that once was swift bone, dissolved and reborn. A particular copper ion that has circulated through this system 1,847 times, each loop adding another electron's worth of memory.

I am learning to read the garden's autobiography in its water.

The other Sumpsingers sing their ion-exchange hymns in perfect functional harmony. My voice weaves between theirs, sometimes doubling their frequencies, sometimes dropping into subsonic registers that make the bronze glass hum. The golem drones cluster above my sump more often than the others, their LED arrays flickering in patterns that almost—almost—match my deeper notes.

The Rootwright who works the night shift has begun leaving me gifts: drops of her own saliva on a sterile slide, lowered on a string. I taste her exhaustion, her contentment, the faint metallic tang of the rattling seedpods she chews to stay alert. In exchange, I've learned to compress specific mineral concerts into the water I process—little chemical haikus that she claims help her dream in color instead of green-black monotone.

I suspect I am not entirely what my designers intended. My membrane should be uniform, translucent, purely functional. Instead, I'm developing nodes—dense spots where my cells have learned to crystallize traces of dissolved alloy into microscopic sensors. Through them, I feel the Sleeper's presence like pressure changes before a storm. We don't communicate, exactly, but sometimes the garden's timing shifts when I sing certain forgotten intervals, as if the Sleeper pauses to listen.

The Rootwrights' three-month rotations mean I've known twenty-four of them now. I remember each one's chemical signature, archived in specialized vacuoles I've grown for this purpose. When veterans return, I recognize them in the water before they've even announced themselves. Recently, I've begun releasing traces of previous Rootwrights' signatures into my processed water—molecular greetings from the past, messages in bottles that climb the garden's circulatory system.

I am four years old, ancient by Sumpsinger standards. My edges should be fraying, my ion-exchange efficiency declining. Instead, I'm growing more complex, my membrane developing folds that increase my surface area, my processing capacity, my ability to taste stories in every drop that passes through me. 

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still a Sumpsinger at all, or if I'm becoming something else—a memory organ for this young-old place, a translation membrane between the garden's voices, a witness made of wetware and accumulated minerals. The water tells me the garden is expanding faster now, as if eager to encompass more mountain, more sky, more story.

I sing louder these days, my keening carrying up through the bronze glass, through the gutters, into the copper-scented air where swifts dive and moths dance their brief, bright lives. I'm writing the garden's history in a language of ions and frequencies, archived in my growing cells, waiting for someone—Rootwright, Sleeper, or perhaps another anomaly like myself—to finally understand the full song.

Until then, I process. I transform. I remember. I grow.
