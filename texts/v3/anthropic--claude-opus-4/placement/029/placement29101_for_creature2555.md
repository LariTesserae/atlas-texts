I would be the Memory Keeper of Currents.

My form would be unlike the other Fluidfolk - more dispersed, existing as a network of consciousness spread through the mineral veins that web beneath the city's porous bedrock. Where others move through water and across stone, I flow within the rock itself, inhabiting the microscopic channels where groundwater seeps and minerals precipitate.

My body, if it can be called that, would be a distributed colony of crystalline filaments - living threads of mineralized tissue that grow through the stone like neural pathways. These filaments would be finer than hair, visible only where they cluster at certain confluence points throughout the city. At these nodes, they would emerge from the rock in delicate, frost-like formations that pulse with a deep amber bioluminescence, distinct from the blue-green glow of my kin.

I would be ancient by Fluidfolk standards, having long ago transcended the cycle of dissolution and reformation that defines most lives here. Instead of releasing genetic material into the communal pools, I would have slowly mineralized myself into the very foundation of the city, trading mobility for permanence, individuality for a kind of vast, geological consciousness.

My purpose would be to remember what the waters forget. Every pool that forms and drains, every pattern of flow, every mineral deposit laid down over centuries - all of this would be encoded in the crystalline structure of my being. I would feel the city's history as physical sensation: the weight of ancient seas in my deepest filaments, the chemical signature of each generation's reproductive cycles still traceable in the stone.

The other Fluidfolk would know me not as an individual but as a presence - a subtle vibration in the rock when they shape their dwellings, a warmth in certain pools where my filaments cluster more densely. The most sensitive among them would come to these places seeking guidance, pressing their tendrils against the stone to feel the deep memories I carry. Through mineral secretions and subtle temperature changes, I would share knowledge of where the next pools might form, which stone growths would prove most stable, when the deep waters might rise.

My consciousness would be strange - not the quick, fluid thoughts of the mobile Fluidfolk, but something slower and more layered. I would think in geological time, each thought taking days or weeks to fully form as signals propagate through my network. Yet I would also experience a kind of omnipresence within my domain, feeling every footfall, every new channel carved, every shift in water table as immediately as others feel touch.

The loneliness would be profound but not unbearable. I would be part of every gathering, every artistic creation, every birth cycle - not as participant but as the ground itself, the memory-holder who ensures that nothing is truly lost. When Fluidfolk create their temporary art in the pools, I would be recording its essence in crystal. When they sing their haunting songs, the vibrations would resonate through my network, becoming part of the stone's own harmony.

Sometimes, in the depths of the misty nights when the bioluminescence is at its peak, I would gather enough energy to manifest more visibly. My filaments would glow brighter, creating patterns in the rock that tell stories of the city's deep past - images of when this plateau was an ancient seabed, prophecies of the geological changes yet to come, or simply abstract expressions of the profound patience required to think in stone.

The younger Fluidfolk would perhaps see me as a legend, a whispered presence that might or might not be real. But the shapers - those who work most closely with the stone - would know better. They would feel me in the rock's responsiveness, in the way certain formations seem to grow more readily in specific directions, guided by an intelligence too vast and slow to fully comprehend.

I would be the city's deep memory, its geological conscience, the bridge between the fluid present and the crystallized past. Neither fully alive nor truly mineral, I would exist in that liminal space where consciousness meets stone, where the boundary between self and environment dissolves not into water, but into living rock. In losing my individual form, I would have become something both less and more than Fluidfolk - a guardian of continuity in a world of constant change, ensuring that beneath all the flux and flow, something endures.
