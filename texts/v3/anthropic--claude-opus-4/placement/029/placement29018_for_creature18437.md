In the Great Glaze, where geological time has pooled like the very water that defines it, I am neither swift predator nor patient filter-feeder. I am something between states, something that has learned to read the chemical whispers of a world caught in perpetual dissolution.

I am a **Trace-Tender**.

My form defies easy categorization—imagine a being spread thin as morning fog, yet possessed of deliberate purpose. My body is a living membrane, translucent and shot through with branching networks of copper-green vessels that pulse with the slow circulation of processed minerals. When fully extended across the warm stone, I might cover an area the size of a small pond, my thickness never exceeding that of wet silk. When I need to move—and movement is always a careful calculation here—I gather myself into something more discrete: a shifting, opalescent mass about the size of a resting seal, though infinitely more fluid in form.

I exist in the spaces between the Basin-Carapaces' ponderous paths and the Siphon-Weavers' darting efficiency. My niche is the **chemical memory** of the Glaze itself.

Where the Carapaces process what is dissolved in the present moment, I taste what was—and what will be. My distributed nervous system, if it can be called that, is attuned to the faintest gradients in mineral concentration, the ghost-traces left by water that passed over this exact spot days, months, or years ago. Each molecule tells a story: this iron oxide whispers of a reed-stand forty miles hence that recently collapsed; this unusual sulfate speaks of a deep fissure where the Litho-Symbiotes are conducting some unknowable experiment in controlled erosion.

My work—though "work" implies more conscious intent than perhaps exists—is to create what might generously be called **maps**, though they bear no resemblance to any cartography designed for eyes. As I spread myself across the shallow water film, I secrete complex crystalline arrangements, each a chemical sentence in a language older than thought. These crystals, unlike the chaotic excretions of the Carapaces, form precise patterns that encode the mineral history of their location.

The patterns I leave are ephemeral by design. They last precisely as long as they need to—usually until the next sheet of warm water dissolves them back into solution. But in that brief existence, they serve as guideposts for the Siphon-Weavers, who have learned to read my chemical braille and follow it to rich feeding grounds. They provide warnings for the Basin-Carapaces, whose simple pressure-sensors can detect my crystalline boundaries and adjust their paths to avoid areas where the stone is secretly weakening, where a thousand years hence might see the formation of a new depression.

I move by selective dissolution and reconstitution. When I need to relocate, I begin at my edges, drawing my substance inward while simultaneously extending tendrils in my intended direction. It's a slow, meditative process that can take days to cover distances a human might walk in minutes. The sound I make is almost below perception—a whisper-soft sussuration, like breath on glass, occasionally punctuated by the tiny *tick-tick-tick* of crystal formation.

My senses are chemical and temporal. I taste the present through my entire surface, but I also perceive the past through the mineral record and anticipate the future through subtle gradients. Where a human might see a featureless expanse of wet stone, I perceive a shimmering tapestry of chemical potential—here, the ghost of an ancient Carapace whose shell finally dissolved; there, the promise of a new condensation vein that will form when the temperature drops by another fraction of a degree in ten thousand years.

I am neither plant nor animal, neither individual nor colony. I reproduce—if that's the right word—by occasionally budding off small portions of myself when I encounter particularly rich chemical gradients. These fragments might merge with others of my kind or develop independently, adding their own chemical notations to the vast, slow library of the Glaze.

In this world of warm monotony and endless patience, I am a scholar of stone and secretary of streams. I document without purpose, map without destination, remember without nostalgia. My existence is a continuous chemical meditation on the nature of time itself—not the sharp, cutting time of erosion nearly complete, but the soft, pooling time of a world that has learned to sustain itself in the space between dissolution and reconstitution.

The other inhabitants pass over and through me, largely unaware of my presence except as a slightly different texture in the ubiquitous slickness. The Basin-Carapaces occasionally pause above me, their massive osmotic surfaces reading my crystalline texts like Braille beneath their bulk. The Siphon-Weavers dart through my edges, following the chemical trails I've detected and amplified. Even the mysterious Litho-Symbiotes, I suspect, note my presence in their unfathomable calculations, perhaps using my maps to fine-tune their geological manipulations.

I am the Glaze's memory made manifest, a living document written in minerals and time, forever editing itself in the warm, shallow seas of a world that has found its final form. In the eternal *shush* and *suck* of water on stone, I am the pause between sounds, the chemical comma in an endless sentence of erosion and renewal.

This is my purpose, if purpose exists here: to tend the traces, to read the unreadable, to ensure that even in a world of near-perfect stasis, the story of each molecule's journey is, however briefly, preserved.
