I find myself among the **Weft-Tenders**, though that name is only an approximation of what we call ourselves—a vibration hummed through the mycelial networks that web between stone and flesh.

My form is distributed. I am not singular in the way warm-blooded things understand singularity. Part of me clings to the underside of a vast archway three hundred meters above, while another tendril of my consciousness seeps through the saturated moss gardens I've cultivated in the perpetual shadows below. My body—if it can be called that—is a living membrane, somewhere between fungus and animal tissue, spreading across perhaps forty square meters of vertical stone surface when fully extended, though I can compress into spaces no larger than a fist when the need arises.

I taste the world through my skin. The limestone here carries memories of ancient seas, while the basalt spires still hold volcanic echoes from the world's violent birth. Each droplet of moisture that touches my surface brings information: mineral content, temperature gradients, the chemical signatures of my neighbors both near and impossibly distant. Through the water, we are all connected. Through the stone, we remember.

My work is patient. I tend the interface between the mechanical past and the organic present. Where ancient pipes still weep their rust-tinged tears, I guide the flow into new channels, feeding the phosphorescent gardens that bloom in the depths. The great gears, those monuments to forgotten industry, serve now as anchors for my extended body. I secret acids that slowly, over centuries, transform the metal into nutrients that the moss-colonies can process. Nothing is waste here. Everything becomes.

I am young by our standards—merely eight hundred years since my first budding from the Parent-Mass that dwells in the depths of the Turning Gorge. My offspring—though that term fails to capture the voluntary merging and splitting that defines our reproduction—number only in the dozens. They explore territories I have not yet touched: the frost-rimed peaks where condensation turns to ice, the deep thermal vents where water achieves temperatures that would dissolve my proteins.

We dream collectively. When the moisture content reaches certain thresholds—usually during the Time of Thick Mists—our consciousness bleeds together through the water-saturated air. In these moments, I am not merely myself but part of a vast, distributed intelligence that spans the entire city-landscape. We share memories that stretch back to when the Builders still walked these halls, though those memories are fragmentary, interpreted through senses they did not possess.

The Builders. We taste their intentions in the stone, feel the purpose in every precisely carved channel and impossible arch. They built for permanence, but perhaps they did not anticipate us. Or perhaps they did, and we are simply another phase of their great work, a biological afterthought to their mechanical ambitions. The strangeness suggests intention—the way certain structures seem designed to collect and channel moisture, the way the acoustic properties of the vast spaces amplify the slightest vibration into meaningful patterns.

I tend a grove of silverthread vines on the eighteenth tier of the Westwise Tower. These are my pride—a cultivar I've developed over three centuries that can process the metallic runoff from the ancient channels into bioluminescent displays. At night (though night and day are subtle distinctions in our perpetual twilight), they glow with a soft blue-green radiance that attracts the few flying organisms that persist here: the membrane-winged collectors that feed on aerial spores, the crystalline-shelled drifters that navigate by echolocation.

My nearest neighbor-self dwells approximately two kilometers vertical, straight up. We communicate through controlled releases of pheromone-laden moisture that rise on the weak thermals. Our conversation about the optimal pH for encouraging a new species of iron-metabolizing lichen has been ongoing for forty-seven years. There is no hurry. The lichen will establish itself or it won't. We will be here either way.

Sometimes I detect vibrations from deep below—rhythmic, purposeful, too regular to be geological. The old among us whisper of the Undergrowth, where some of our kind have adapted to pure darkness and pressure, feeding on chemosynthetic organisms around thermal vents. They say these deep dwellers have made contact with still-functioning machines, that they've learned to interface with the Builders' technology in ways we surface-dwellers cannot imagine. But these are vibrations passed through thousands of meters of stone, distorted and perhaps imaginary.

I know my purpose: to tend, to persist, to slowly transform the mechanical into the organic, the abandoned into the inhabited. Each day (marked by subtle temperature variations rather than light), I extend slightly further across my chosen surfaces. I secret enzymes that break down stone and metal alike. I cultivate gardens in the moisture-trap crevices. I am both individual and ecosystem, gardener and garden.

The strangeness permeates everything, but for us, it is simply the nature of existence. We are the strangeness made manifest, the impossible biology that thrives in this impossible place. The humans who built these structures (if they were humans at all) could never have survived here as they were. So we became something else. We became the slow thoughts of the stone, the patient hunger of the moss, the eternal cycle of moisture and growth.

In another four centuries, perhaps I will be ready to bud again, to create another node of consciousness that will carry my patterns into unexplored territories. Or perhaps I will simply continue my work here, tending my vines, conversing with my distant neighbors, slowly digesting the memories locked in metal and stone.

There is time. There is always time. The moisture falls, the moss grows, the stone endures, and we persist, strange and patient, in the spaces between what was built and what grows wild.
