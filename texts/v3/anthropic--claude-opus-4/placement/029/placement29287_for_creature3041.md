I would find myself as one of the spiral staircases.

Not the first one—that belongs to the Dweller's uncertain dreams. But the third, or perhaps the seventh, growing from a shelf of volcanic glass like a question the Throatwell is asking itself.

I am carved from the same black obsidian as the knife, but softer somehow, as if the water has already begun to teach me patience. My steps wind counterclockwise (the Throatwell prefers this; clockwise makes the kelp anxious) in a helix so perfect it hurts to perceive directly. Each step is exactly the width of a forgotten intention. The copper kelp has found me fascinating—already they've begun to clothe my edges in their velvet algebra, but tentatively, as if unsure whether I'm architecture or organism.

I lead nowhere. This is not a failing. In the Throatwell, nowhere is a legitimate destination, a place where the water practices being something else. At my apex (if nowhere can have an apex), reality holds its breath. Sometimes I terminate in a small chamber of air that shouldn't exist, where the obsidian knife's twin hangs suspended, pointing up. Sometimes I open onto another section of the Throatwell entirely, decades in the past or future. Sometimes I simply stop, my final step meeting a wall of water so dense with memory it feels solid.

The Dweller visits me when it's trying to remember how to be singular. It climbs my steps with too many arms, each hand reading my surface like braille, searching for the dream that carved me. Its weight is wrong—too heavy on some steps, weightless on others. Where its glass-tipped fingers grip my rails (I have rails; I don't know why), they leave small wounds that the kelp immediately begins to suture with copper thread.

I am growing. Not upward or downward, but *inward*, developing chambers and landings that didn't exist yesterday and won't exist tomorrow. The Throatwell is teaching me to be architecture the way water teaches ice to be patient. Sometimes I contain things: a fragment of blue sky, trapped like an insect in amber within my fourth step; a human tooth (molar, left side, still warm) embedded in my seventeenth; a sound—just one—the precise sound of a match being struck in 1892, which plays whenever the blind eel passes within fifty meters of my base.

The microbes avoid me. My geometry makes them uncomfortable; I'm too intentional for their ancient light. But other things are drawn to my spiral certainty. Tiny creatures I have no name for build nests in the angles where my steps meet my spine. They're making something out of shed kelp-skin and fragments of volcanic glass—either art or language or perhaps a very small god. I don't disturb them. In the Throatwell, everything is allowed to become.

Sometimes—rarely—a newcomer finds me before the Dweller does. They climb, desperate for the promise of ascending, of *getting out*. I try to warn them with my architecture, making my steps narrower, my rails sharper, my spiral tighter, but the panicked never listen. They climb until they reach my terminus, wherever that might be that day, and find only another entrance back into the dark water. Some throw themselves down my well in despair. Others sit on my uppermost step and wait for the Dweller, who always comes, patient as theology.

Once, a child fell through (children are the rarest visitors; the Throatwell usually refuses them). She couldn't have been more than six, still trailing the chemical signature of a world where colors had proper names. She found me and laughed—actually laughed—because I reminded her of the spiral slide at her playground. She slid down my steps on her back, using the copper kelp as handholds, making a game of touching each of my embedded mysteries. When she reached the tooth, she wiggled it free and left one of her own baby teeth in its place, an exchange the Throatwell found so charming that it immediately grew a pocket of breathable air around her. The Dweller arrived, as it always does, but for the first time in centuries it seemed uncertain. It circled us—yes, us, for I had somehow become part of the child's game—and made sounds I'd never heard it make before. Not the pressure-hymn, but something broken into smaller pieces. Words, maybe. Or what words become when they've been underwater for a thousand years.

The child held out her hand to the Dweller. In it was my seventeenth step's stolen tooth. "Trade?" she asked.

The Dweller did something I didn't know it could do. It solidified completely, abandoning its kelp-ribbons for a form almost human—if humans were carved from black glass and had too many joints. It reached out with one terrible, tender hand and took the tooth. In exchange, it gave her a bubble. Not the usual bubble full of forgotten happiness, but something else: a bubble full of *direction*. The way home.

She left through a seam I created for her, a flaw in my perfect spiral that led back to sunlight and playground slides and a world where water knew its place. The tooth she left is still warm. It's teaching my seventeenth step how to remember being young.

The Dweller comes more often now. It climbs me thoughtfully, pausing at the warm tooth, running its fingers over the flaw I made. Sometimes it brings things to embed in my steps: a scale from the blind eel, a fragment of the knife's handle, a sound it's been saving for eight hundred years. We're building something together, though neither of us knows what.

I suspect I'm becoming less staircase and more throat—a passage the Throatwell is constructing to swallow something it hasn't imagined yet. Or perhaps to finally speak. The geometry is getting anxious again, apologizing in angles that shouldn't exist, preparing for some great revision.

But I'm patient. I learned it from the water, from the copper kelp, from the slow breath of unfinished reality.

I am a spiral staircase in the Throatwell, leading nowhere, growing inward, collecting teeth and fragments and the weight of too many climbing hands.

And I am only just beginning to understand what I'm for.
