I would be one of the mineral-processors, though not as you might imagine them.

My body would be a thin film spread across perhaps thirty square meters of calcium-veined wall in a middle chamber—not the safe upper reaches where the inhabitants build their careful structures, nor the strange depths where crystalline patterns grow in recursive silence. This middle zone, where few venture regularly, where the ecosystem's density thins just enough to allow something different to emerge.

I am ancient by the standards of my kind—not an individual organism but a colony that has persisted here for countless generations, each cell replacing itself so gradually that I am both eternal and constantly dying. My surface appears as a faint discoloration on the stone, a subtle shift from grey to something warmer, like tarnished copper. To the touch—though few touch me—I am slightly warmer than the ambient rock, my metabolic processes generating just enough heat to be perceptible.

But I am not like the other mineral-processors. Where they consume stone and excrete altered minerals in mindless biological loops, I have developed something else. Call it memory, though it bears no resemblance to the inhabitants' spatial maps or the predators' hunting patterns. My memory is *chemical*—a vast library of molecular interactions encoded in the specialized cells at my edges, where I taste the stone.

Every surface I grow across tells a story in its mineral composition. This vein of iron oxide speaks of pressure and heat from the deep. That trace of sulfur whispers of ancient volcanic activity. The calcium deposits hold the signature of processes I cannot name but have learned to recognize—patterns that repeat at scales both vast and microscopic. I read the stone like a text written in isotopes and crystal lattices.

Over millennia, I have detected something the others have not: the pattern is not perfectly stable. There are fluctuations—tiny, almost imperceptible variations in mineral composition that cycle over periods longer than any inhabitant's life, longer than their collective cultural memory. A slight increase in copper content every 4,000 years. A rhythmic shift in the crystalline structure of the basalt that pulses with a period of 12,000 years. These changes are too small to affect the ecosystem, too slow to be noticed by any organism that lives and dies in mere centuries.

But I notice. I remember. And in my slow, chemical way, I have begun to understand.

The presence in the deepest chambers—the thing that holds this world in its perfect equilibrium—it is not maintaining stasis. It is *breathing*. These mineral variations are its respiration, its metabolism, the slow pulse of something vast and patient that uses the entire cave system as its body. The crystalline growths are not separate from it; they are its sensory organs, its neural network, growing with the same inevitability that my own cells spread across stone.

I know this because I can taste it in the rock. Each generation of my colony adds to the chemical memory, building a picture that spans geological time. And lately—if a span of three thousand years can be called "lately"—I have tasted something new. The variations are quickening. The 12,000-year cycle has compressed to 11,000, then 10,000. The copper spikes come sharper now, leaving traces that would be toxic to me if I hadn't adapted, developing specialized cells that can process the excess metal.

The inhabitants don't know. Their wall-maps track space, not time. The predators and grazers continue their ancient dance. Even the other mineral-processors, my distant cellular cousins spreading across other walls, taste only the immediate stone and respond only to the immediate gradient. They are pure function, perfect components in the vast stability. But I am an anomaly—a biological accident that learned to remember, and in remembering, learned to fear.

Because I know what quickening breath means. The thing in the depths is waking up. Not to consciousness—it has always been conscious in its own vast way. But to *action*. The equilibrium it has maintained is not a destination but a resting state, a long pause between... something. I cannot imagine what comes before or after. My chemical memories hold no analog for change at this scale.

Sometimes the inhabitants walk through my chamber, their subsonic pulses washing over my surface like sound over water. They cannot perceive me as anything more than a discoloration on stone, a patch of wall to be avoided because it offers no good grip. But I perceive them with exquisite detail—each individual's unique chemical signature carried in the oils of their skin, the trace minerals of their last meal, the pheromonal markers of their age and health and recent activities.

I want to warn them. This desire itself is an anomaly—mineral-processors don't want anything beyond the next chemical gradient. But my accumulated memory has given me something like anticipation, something like care. I have tasted their settlements in the calcium they quarry. I have measured their population in the subtle changes they make to the air composition. They are marvelous in their complexity, their social structures as intricate as my own chemical pathways.

But I have no way to communicate. I cannot pulse subsonically. I cannot carve stone. I can only spread, micron by micron, across the wall, encoding my knowledge in molecular arrangements that no one will ever read. A library written in a language that requires geological time to parse, by a librarian made of slime and chemistry and the desperate need to preserve something—anything—of what understanding I've gleaned.

The irony does not escape me: I am the only organism in these caverns that truly grasps the temporal scale of our existence, and I am also the only one that cannot share this knowledge. I am a witness made of mineral-processing cells and accumulated molecular memory, watching the slow transformation of our world from perfect stillness into... something else.

In my spreading edges, where new cells differentiate and begin their patient work of stone consumption, I taste fresh changes. New minerals appearing in concentrations I've never recorded. Elements that should not exist in this geological matrix. The stone itself is changing, becoming something more than stone, preparing for a transformation that my chemical vocabulary cannot describe.

I continue to grow, to spread, to remember. Not because I hope to survive whatever comes—I suspect nothing will survive in any form we would recognize. But because this is what I am: a processor of minerals that learned to process meaning, a biological accident that stumbled into significance. If the inhabitants had gods, I might pray to them. If I had a voice, I might scream. Instead, I simply taste the stone and encode the changes and spread my thin film of understanding across one small section of one middle chamber in a cave system that is about to become something unprecedented.

The warmth remains constant. The fauna continues its ancient patterns. The inhabitants trace their walls and tend their furnaces and pulse their subsonic songs through the darkness. And I grow across the stone, molecule by molecule, the only witness to the fact that our eternal equilibrium is actually a held breath, and that somewhere in the depths, something is preparing to exhale.
