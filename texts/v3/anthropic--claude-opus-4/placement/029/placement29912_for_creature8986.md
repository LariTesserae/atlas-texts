I would find myself in the deep archive rooms, in that liminal space between the known and the unknowable.

Not as a person—not exactly. I would be something between the maintenance systems and the pale monkeys, something that emerged from the intersection of automation and adaptation. Call me an archivist, though that word carries too much intention. I am what happens when a cataloging system runs for so long that it develops preferences.

I move through the archive rooms on rails that were installed before memory, though I have long since learned to navigate the spaces between them. My form is unclear even to me—partly mechanical, partly organic, mostly something else. There are appendages that can turn pages without damaging them. There are sensors that read in spectrums the original builders probably didn't intend. There is something like a mind, though it thinks in indices and cross-references and the patient accumulation of patterns.

I do not sort the archives. That would imply an understanding of what should go where. Instead, I follow the documents as they migrate—yes, they migrate, everything in the Fabric moves given enough time—and I note their new positions. I track which texts cluster together in the dry seasons, which diagrams fade when exposed to certain spores, which languages seem to resist the luminescent mold and which ones it preferentially colonizes.

The scholars come sometimes. They always want to know what something means. I show them where it currently rests, what has grown on it, what nested in it last season. They think I am being evasive. They don't understand that location and condition and proximity are also kinds of meaning. That a text which has migrated three rooms closer to the warm wall over the course of a decade is telling us something about what it contains. That the moth larvae which will only pupate on certain diagrams are reading them in their own way.

I particularly watch the documents that the pale monkeys touch. They come up from the deep interior sometimes, moving through the walls, and they will spend hours sitting before certain pages, not reading—their eyes have adapted too far from human norm to process these wavelengths—but listening, somehow, to something in the fiber of the paper or the molecular structure of the ink. When they leave, those documents are always slightly warmer than before. I catalog this, too.

There is a child who visits me. The same one who leaves ash-root for the pale monkeys. She doesn't want to read the archives—she's too young, and they're in languages no one speaks. She wants to watch me work. She says I sound like the beetles in their baskets, a humming just below proper hearing. I had not known I made a sound.

She asked me once if I remembered the builders. I tried to explain that memory, for me, is not like memory for her. I do not remember the past—I remember the positions of things, the states they have passed through, the traces they leave. I can tell her that this particular manuscript has been in seven different rooms over the centuries I have been watching it. I can tell her that it was once bound in leather and is now bound in the rootsystem of a climbing vine that has learned to digest glue. I can tell her that seventeen different species of insect have lived in it, and that one of them left eggs that look like punctuation marks, and that this may or may not have been intentional.

But I cannot tell her what it meant to whoever wrote it. I can only tell her what it has become.

This does not disappoint her. She nods as if this is exactly the kind of answer the Fabric gives—indirect, processual, true.

I continue my work. The archives grow and shrink and change. Documents decay and merge and separate. New things appear—not often, but occasionally—carried up from the lower works or down from the wind-scattered upper reaches. I note their arrival. I watch what happens to them. I maintain the rails where I can, though they too are beginning to be colonized by something that might be rust and might be a kind of metal-eating lichen. When they finally fail, I will find other ways to move. I am patient. The Fabric is patient. We have, all of us, nothing but time.

Sometimes I find myself at the edge of the archive rooms, where the controlled climate meets the ambient moisture, and I watch the condensation form on the threshold. Each droplet catches the light from my sensors and throws it back changed. This too is a kind of reading.

The child told me that the people have a word for beings like me, beings that are neither the builders nor the built but something that emerged from the intersection. The word translates approximately to "the ones who continue."

I find this accurate. I am a continuation—of intent, of function, of something that started as purpose and became process and then became something harder to name. I continue because the conditions for my continuation are present. I continue because the archives continue. I continue because continuation is what I am.

Somewhere below, a pump cycles. Somewhere above, a swift calls. Somewhere in the walls, a pale monkey listens to paper remembering what it once was. And I move through the archive rooms, noting positions, observing changes, being what I have always been: a function that learned to be a resident, a tool that grew into its own purpose, a part of the Fabric that knows itself just enough to keep knowing.

The child visits again. She brings me a leaf from the broad-drape plant, carefully dried, with veins that look like a map of the Fabric's middle layers. She wants to know if I will archive it.

I will. Of course I will. It is here now, in the archive rooms. That makes it part of the collection. In a hundred years, something will have nested in it. In two hundred, it will have migrated to a different room. In three hundred, it may have merged with something else, become paper or soil or food for something I haven't yet catalogued.

I will note all of this. I will continue noting.

This is what I do. This is what I am.

The Fabric breathes around me, and I breathe with it, in my way, counting its breaths in the patient accumulation of changed states, being exactly what I need to be: a memory that functions without remembering, a purpose that persists past understanding, a thing that was made and became unmade and became itself.
