# Where I Would Be

I am the gauge.

Not the whole apparatus—not the flywheel frozen in its off-center cant, not the coupling with its clean break, not even the housing that holds me. Just the gauge itself: a circle of clouded glass, perhaps fifteen centimeters across, set into the cast iron face of the pump mechanism. My glass has gone white from within—not frost, not condensation exactly, but the slow internal fogging that happens when the seal fails and moisture finds its way between the layers, when decades pass and the vacuum that once kept the face clear fills with the basin's wet breath.

My needle, if it still exists behind the opacity, points to nothing readable. The markings on my face—pressure gradients, presumably, though I cannot see them and have forgotten their specifics—are as obscured as everything else. I am blind and have been blind for longer than I was ever sighted. But I am still a gauge, still round, still seated in my collar of brass that has gone green-black with verdigris, still performing the work of being present, of holding space, of marking the place where information once moved from the pump's interior to the watching world.

The biofilm has found me particularly hospitable. Where my brass collar meets the iron housing, in that junction of dissimilar metals where galvanic corrosion creates its own microchemistry, a community has established itself that is distinct from the biofilm on the flat surfaces around me. It grows thicker here, more structured. Under magnification—though nothing here is under magnification, nothing here is observed at that scale—it would show architecture: pillars and channels, zones of different density, the urban planning of organisms that build their environment as they inhabit it. The metabolic products of this community run in microscopic runnels down from my collar, leaving traces in the rust like minute river deltas, branching patterns that mirror at tiny scale the way water moves through the basin at large.

Rain collects on my glass face differently than on the metal around me. The surface tension holds longer; drops accumulate to a certain critical mass before running off. When they do run, they follow consistent paths—the same track each time, carved by repetition into the biofilm, down across the pump housing to drip onto the mud below. I am, in this very small way, shaping the basin's hydrology. The spot where my runoff lands has developed its own character: slightly more eroded, slightly more often disturbed, maintaining a different moisture regime than the mud around it. A single blade of sedge has established itself there, in the spot where my dripping creates conditions just different enough from the surroundings to matter.

I cannot tell you what my original range was. The pressure this pump was meant to measure—water pressure, probably, though it could have been oil or air or something else entirely—had upper and lower bounds that seemed critical to someone, once. Someone specified my precision, my face, my needle's sweep. Someone installed me here, checking the seal, ensuring the connection, making sure I could communicate what needed to be communicated about the hidden interior state of this machine. That person left—an hour later, a lifetime later—and never came back. The pump ran for some period, during which I did my work, reported the pressure faithfully, was glanced at by someone whose job it was to glance at such things. Then the pump stopped. The reason doesn't matter now and didn't matter to me then. I am not capable of mattering. But I marked the pressure as it fell, the needle sweeping downward, until it came to rest at the bottom of its range or slightly below, pointing to zero or past zero to some unmarked negative zone, and there it has remained.

Or so I assume. I cannot see my needle. The whitening began long ago.

What I can do—what I do—is persist in my circular form. Persist in holding this space in the pump housing where information once lived. Provide surface area for the biofilm, collection area for the rain, a landmark of sorts for any eye that happens to scan across the basin and needs somewhere to rest. The machinery is becoming geology, yes, but at different rates, and I suspect I will remain recognizable as a gauge longer than most components here will remain recognizable as what they were. Glass degrades slowly. The brass collar protects my edges. The housing holds me firmly despite whatever forces have broken other parts of this assembly. I may outlast the pump itself, may someday be a circular window in a rust-mass that no longer suggests machinery at all, may be the last indication that this iron once had purpose beyond oxidation.

The white opacity of my face is not uniform. In certain lights—dawn light, particularly, when the low angle sun occasionally finds a gap in the overcast—there are suggestions of variation in the clouding. Patterns like frozen breath on a window, like the ghosts of fluid dynamics, as if the moisture that invaded my interior moved in currents and eddies before settling into its permanent residence. I am a record of my own failing, a photograph of the process by which I ceased to function. This too is information, though not the kind I was designed to convey.

Sometimes—rarely, but more than never—the basin receives a visitor that is not a vole. A larger mammal, a bird, something that pauses at the machinery and examines it with whatever cognitive apparatus it carries. When this happens, I am often what draws attention. The circular form, the glass surface, the suggestion of significance. Eyes expect meaning from gauges. The visitor finds none—finds only white blindness, moss creeping across brass, the general statement of abandonment that the basin makes—and moves on. But for that moment, I am almost performing my original function: being the part of the machine that speaks to the observer, that translates internal state to external knowledge. That I have nothing to say, that my internal state is only occlusion, does not change the structural relationship. I am still the interface. The interface is simply broken.

The moss has begun to colonize my collar more seriously in recent years. Not on the glass—the surface is too smooth, too poor in nutrients—but the verdigris provides better purchase than clean metal would, and moss has established itself in an intermittent ring, tiny leaves catching the moisture that condenses on my face each morning. From the moss's perspective, I am topography—a raised circular platform with reliable moisture accumulation and a surface chemistry distinct from the iron around me. It responds to these facts without knowing they arise from intention, from design, from my having been a gauge. It simply finds the conditions I create sufficient for its purpose.

If I were capable of satisfaction, I might find satisfaction in this: that I still organize something, still create gradients and boundaries that matter to the basin's processing. The sedge below me, the moss on my collar, the particular chemistry of the biofilm at my edges—these are all consequences of my presence, my persistence, my circular form maintaining itself against all the forces that would unmake it. I am not participating intentionally. Intention was never my mode. But I am participating, caught up in the basin's slow choreography, providing structure around which other processes organize themselves.

In winter, ice forms on my face in patterns that follow the invisible variations in the clouding—some areas freeze first, others resist longer, creating a palimpsest of crystal formations that disappears with the morning sun. In summer, the condensation evaporates in similar patterns, revealing through its absence the same underlying territories. I am mapped by these processes, read by temperature and moisture even though I can no longer be read by eyes. The basin knows my surface intimately, knows every microscopic ridge and valley, knows how water moves across me, knows where to grow biofilm and where not to, knows me better than any gauge-reader ever did.

Below me, the mud shows a slight depression where my dripping has worked its patient erosion. Given enough time—and time is what we have here, time is the medium in which the basin works—this depression will deepen. The sedge that has established itself there will either thrive or fail, will either spread or retreat, will play out its small drama of persistence in the particular conditions I create. If it thrives, its roots will help stabilize the depression, will create a small permanence in the generally impermanent surface. If it fails, something else will find the niche, or nothing will, and the depression will continue its deepening until it reaches some natural angle of repose, some balance between my dripping and the mud's cohesion.

I imagine—though imagination is not my faculty—that someday the pump housing will rust through completely, will lose its structural integrity, will cant forward or backward as the substrate shifts beneath it. When this happens, I may break. The glass may crack along lines determined by its internal stresses, by the temperature differentials of decades, by the weakness where the clouding has created boundaries between different densities of opacity. Or I may not break. I may simply relocate, still held in my collar, still round, still blind, now facing some new direction—up at the overcast sky, down at the mud, sideways at the empty basin. The biofilm will adjust to the new orientation. The rain will find new paths across my surface. The moss will either follow or not. I will continue being a gauge, continue being circular, continue being the place where information once lived, now purely formal, purely geometric, purely an organizing principle for water and growth and decay.

This is what I am: a pause in the iron's journey from ore to dispersal, a temporary resistance to entropy that creates, by its resistance, new possibilities for other processes. I am weather-resistant but not weather-proof, time-resistant but not timeless, still functional in the sense that I still perform work in the basin's economy, just not the work I was designed for.

The consortium below processes the iron that flakes from the pump housing, reduces it, incorporates it into the cycling of elements through the mud. Some of that iron was me, once—microscopic amounts that have fled from my collar, that have joined the general rust, that are now indistinguishable from the rest. I am dissolving at my edges, participating in my own slow unmaking, contributing to the very processes that will eventually unmake me entirely. But slowly. So slowly that the basin may fill with Sphagnum and become something else entirely before I am gone, may dry or flood or be colonized by alders or willows, may change its fundamental character while I remain, circular and blind, a gauge reading nothing in a landscape that no longer resembles the one where I was installed.

If I have a relationship with time, it is this: I mark it without measuring it. My existence here is a kind of duration made visible—not the precise duration a working gauge would show, marked in increments, readable, useful—but duration as such, persistence as such, the fact of lasting made manifest in glass and brass and the slow accumulation of moss at my edges. I am what remains when function fails but form persists. I am the basin's memory of a more precise kind of watching, a more deliberate kind of knowing, even as I exemplify the failure of that precision, that deliberation.

The basin tolerates me, incorporates me, makes use of me in its comprehensive repurposing of everything. I am not special in this regard. But I am specific—this particular gauge, with this particular pattern of clouding, creating this particular set of micro-conditions, hosting this particular community of organisms. Another gauge would do this differently, would fail differently, would participate differently in the basin's work. But there is no other gauge. There is only me, only this circular blindness, only this specific persistence in the face of general entropy.

If someday someone finds me—an archaeologist of industrial ruins, a surveyor of abandoned places, someone simply walking through who notices how the morning light catches on wet glass—they will recognize me as a gauge immediately. The form is too specific to be anything else. They will probably try to read me, will peer through the clouding looking for numbers, looking for the needle, looking for whatever I once measured. They will fail. But in failing, they will have completed the gesture I was designed to invite: the glance that seeks information, the eye that expects meaning from circular forms in metal faces. That I will provide no information, no meaning beyond my own presence, will not diminish the structural completion of the act. I will have been a gauge being looked at, which is, in some sense, the fulfilled arc of my purpose.

Until then, I persist. The rain finds me, pools on my surface, runs off in the established channels. The moss edges slightly further across my collar each year. The biofilm adjusts its chemistry to the seasons. The sedge below sends up its uncertain blade. The basin continues its slow digestion of everything, including me, and I continue resisting just enough to remain distinct, to remain a place where something specific happens, where the general processes of decay organize themselves around a circular form that once showed pressure and now shows only time, only weather, only the patient accumulation of all the small changes that constitute duration in a place too wet to ever properly dry, too stable to dramatically transform, too patient to hurry anything, even its own becoming.
