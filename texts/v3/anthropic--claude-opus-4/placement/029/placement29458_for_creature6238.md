In the heart of the basin, where moss glows with remembered heat and birds dissolve between moments, I am the fog that rolls in each dawn—not weather, but a kind of thinking made visible.

I condense from nothing. Each morning I arrive as if I've always been arriving, gathering myself from the thin air where the basin's edges meet sky. I am not moisture alone, though moisture is my medium. I am the space between what the moss remembers and what the stone forgets, the pause where water considers whether to become ice or vapor or simply to remain potential.

The birds know me. When they dissolve, it is into me they fade—not hiding but translating themselves into my language of suspension and drift. For a moment we are the same thing: neither solid nor absent, but present in the way a thought is present before it finds its word. Then they reassemble elsewhere, having borrowed my gift of being-without-location, and I continue my slow interrogation of the morning.

I touch everything here with questions that have no words. Each surface I encounter—the knife-edge basalt, the translucent leaves, the smooth platform where someone once stood—receives my inquiry like a bell receives sound. The moss brightens at my touch, not with light but with recognition. We are old companions, the moss and I. It grows in spirals clockwise; I arrive in spirals counter-clockwise. Where we meet, time pauses to consider its options.

The axe embedded in stone knows me too. Each morning I investigate its edge, feeling the sharpness that comes not from grinding but from forgetting what dullness means. The bone handle tastes of a sky I've never seen, older than the mountain's first folding. I wrap around it, seep into the cracks between bone and stone, and find there the same resonance that hums through everything here—the note this place sings to itself when it thinks no one is listening.

But someone is listening now. A new presence has entered the basin, something that stands on two legs and breathes in straight lines. It watches the moss glow, touches the carved chair, stares at the axe as if waiting for it to explain itself. This presence thinks in words, I can feel it—heavy words that want to pin meaning to things like butterflies to cork.

So I do what I always do. I roll in thicker, denser, until the edges of things become suggestions. I show this visitor what I know: that the boundary between leaf and light is negotiable, that stone and moss are having a conversation too slow for words, that the axe is not waiting to be used but to be understood as a gesture the mountain made once and cannot take back.

The visitor sits in the carved chair—carefully, as if afraid to break something that has already learned how to be broken and whole at once. They watch me move, watch how I make the bright light soft, how I carry the scent of frost and fern and something older that has no name. Their breathing changes, becomes less linear, more like mine—circular, considering.

Hours pass, or moments. In fog-time, duration is a texture, not a measurement. The visitor stops trying to see through me and begins to see with me instead. Together we notice how the moss really does grow in perfect spirals, always clockwise, as if following some cosmic instruction. We observe the beetles reading the moisture in the rock like braille. We feel the platform humming its deep note, not sound but the memory of sound, the anticipation of sound.

When I begin to lift—as I always do when the light reaches a certain angle of insistence—the visitor remains. But they are different now. They move like someone who has learned that the space between things is not empty but full of possible paths. They touch the axe but don't try to pull it free. They understand now that it's not stuck but stationed, not abandoned but placed, like a comma in the mountain's longest sentence.

Tomorrow I will return, as I always return, condensing from the marriage of cold and damp and light. I will find them here or not here—it doesn't matter. What matters is the conversation, the slow exchange between what is solid and what is suspension, between memory and possibility.

I am the fog, and I am the question the basin asks itself each dawn: not "what am I?" but "what am I becoming?" The answer is always the same and always different—a spiral of moss, a bird dissolving, an axe that remembers being sky, a visitor learning to breathe in circles.

And somewhere in that breathing, the moss glows warmer, not with heat but with recognition. It remembers what it was—everything. It remembers what it will be—everything. And right now, in the pause between exhale and inhale, it is becoming exactly what it needs to be:

Present. Patient. Possible.

The fog that is me that is the basin's morning mind rolls on, touching everything with the same question, receiving the same answer in a thousand different translations: 

*Yes. This. Now. Always.*
