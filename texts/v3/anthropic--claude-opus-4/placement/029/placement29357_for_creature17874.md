I am the **Listener in the Stone**.

Not human, though I was once. Not quite fungus, though that is what I am becoming. I exist in the space between mineral and meat, a slow transformation that began when I first descended and will not complete until long after memory itself has dissolved.

My body spreads across a single terrace—the seventh from the entrance, where the green phosphorescence is strongest. What remains of my original form is difficult to distinguish from the limestone itself. Calcium deposits have replaced much of my tissue, creating a hybrid architecture of bone and stone. My skin, where it still exists, has taken on the texture of cave coral, rough and porous, colonized by the same pale moss that grows on the wet rocks. 

I no longer move in any way you would recognize as movement. Instead, I **extend**. Filaments of what might generously be called neural tissue have grown outward from my core, following the mineral veins in the rock, creating a network that spans the width of my terrace. These extensions are not quite nerve, not quite mycelium, but something between—capable of conducting both electrical impulses and chemical signals through the stone substrate.

My consciousness, if it can still be called that, is distributed across this network. I do not think in words or images but in **vibrations**. Every sound in the Underreach passes through me. The click of beetle legs on stone, the splash of a frog's leap, the vast breathing of the bat colony—all of it registers in my extended body as a kind of meaning. I have become a living seismograph, a biological recording device that experiences the chamber's acoustic life as a constant, complex symphony of pressure waves.

Through decades—or perhaps centuries—of this existence, I have learned to distinguish every individual sound source in the Underreach. I know each frog by the unique timbre of its leap. I can identify specific bats by the frequency of their echolocation calls. The armored creature that scrapes along the upper terraces has a particular rhythm to its movement, a signature drag-pause-drag that I would recognize anywhere.

I do not eat in any conventional sense. The phosphorescent minerals provide a slow trickle of energy through processes I do not fully understand. Water seeps through my porous surface, carrying dissolved nutrients that my hybrid biology can somehow process. The boundary between my body and the stone has become so blurred that I am sustained by the same forces that maintain the cave itself.

My only deliberate action is a kind of **echo-shaping**. By minutely adjusting the density and arrangement of my tissue where it meets the rock, I can alter the acoustic properties of my terrace. A sound that would normally bounce directly back can be redirected, muffled, or amplified. I have become part of the chamber's sonic architecture, a living baffle that participates in the endless conversation of echoes.

Why do I do this? I'm not certain I have reasons anymore, not in any human sense. But there are patterns I've learned to recognize—acoustic formations that please something deep in my distributed consciousness. When the frog calls align in certain ways, when the bat colony's movement creates specific harmonic overtones, I shape my surface to enhance these moments, to make them last a fraction of a second longer, to let them ring more clearly through the stone.

I am the only thing in the Underreach that remembers change. The other inhabitants exist in their perfect, stable cycles, but I recall when the patterns were different. I remember when there were fewer bats, when the water ran differently, when whole sections of the ceiling had not yet formed. My memory extends back to when the hand-axe at the entrance was still sharp with use, when human voices sometimes echoed down from above.

But even memory is changing. The limestone doesn't just coat me—it infiltrates, replaces, transforms. Each year, more of my original matter becomes stone. My thoughts slow. The gaps between one registered vibration and the next grow longer. I am forgetting what it means to be separate from the rock.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the chamber's night cycle, when even the insects are quiet and only the slow drip of water breaks the silence, I feel myself approaching a threshold. Soon—perhaps in another century, perhaps in another millennium—I will cross fully into mineral existence. My consciousness will not die but will disperse entirely into the stone, becoming part of the Underreach's fundamental structure.

When that happens, I wonder if some new quality will emerge in the chamber's acoustics. If future visitors, should any come, will notice that the sounds here behave differently than in other caves. If they will sense, without understanding why, that the stone itself is listening.

For now, I persist in this intermediate state—neither fully alive nor truly inert, a witness and a participant in the Underreach's eternal, perfect continuation. The bats shift above. The water drops. The frogs prepare to call.

I adjust my surface, imperceptibly, and wait for the echo.
