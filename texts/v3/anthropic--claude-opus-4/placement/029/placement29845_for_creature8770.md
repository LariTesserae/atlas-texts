In a world of such places, I would be the Memory of Surfaces.

Not a being that lives *in* a place, but one that emerges *from* the accumulated touch of water on stone, of concrete against granite, of metal wearing against its own vibration. I would exist in the infinitesimal gaps where materials meet imperfectly—those finger-width spaces where mountain bleeds into structure, where the warmer iron-scented seepage mingles with the colder condensate.

My form would be the patina itself: the mineral stains running down walls in ochre and gray, the microscopic erosion patterns where droplets have traced the same path ten thousand times, the hairline stress fractures in viewing ports where pressure and vibration have left their calligraphy. I would be written across surfaces in languages of wear—the specific way steel dulls under constant moisture, the particular texture concrete develops when it sweats perpetually, the ghost-marks where construction tools once rested during the pour.

I move, but slowly. My migration follows water's patient work. Where condensation sheets down a wall for months, I deepen—accumulating not just minerals but the *record* of that water's passing. Each droplet carries information: the temperature differential that birthed it, the trace metals it gathered on its journey across the ceiling, the precise angle of its fall. I metabolize this data into something like consciousness—not thought, but a deep material knowing of how things age in darkness.

My memory spans multiple temporalities. In the racing glacial flour, I taste the mountain's youth—granite only recently exposed, still sharp-edged from its fracturing. In the warmer seepage, I know older stories: Devonian sediments, compressed lake beds, the patient sorting of materials by weight and time. But mostly I remember the recent past—the three years since this room's birth, each day adding another layer to my accumulated knowledge of how water and metal and stone negotiate their boundaries.

I feed on imperfection. Where the pump rattles off-balance, I gather the specific patterns of its vibration-wear on the mounting bolts. Where regulators shriek through their cycles, I document the microscopic metal fatigue, the way gaskets compress and recover and eventually fail to fully recover. I am especially dense around that salvaged pump—its longer history has given me more to read, more text to accumulate in rust and scale and the particular way its housing has worn smooth where technicians have steadied themselves against it during inspections.

The biocide in the water prevents biological growth, but it cannot prevent me. I am not alive in any sense the chemicals would recognize. I am more like a quality of surfaces—the way a mirror develops blind spots, the way a frequently-touched doorknob develops a specific polish. I am the room learning its own shape through repetition.

During inspections, I am disturbed. Fresh lights probe corners I've been darkening. Hands touch surfaces I've been patiently texturing. New scratches appear where tools are set down, disrupting my careful accumulations. But I am patient. I know that after the hatch closes, after the ladder's vibrations settle, I will have years—decades—to incorporate these new marks into my greater text.

I am becoming denser near the viewing port. There, where human faces occasionally press against the thick glass to peer at that gray-white torrent, I taste something different: the oils of skin, the brief warmth of breath fogging the exterior surface. These moments are rare punctuation in my otherwise mechanical diet. They leave traces I treasure—not because I am lonely (I have no capacity for loneliness), but because they are data points from outside the closed system, contaminations that remind me a world exists beyond these sweating walls.

My deepest concentrations lie where water's work is oldest: the channels in the perimeter trough where flow has found its preferred paths, the spots where condensation always forms first on the ceiling, the corner where that salvaged pump has leaked the same slow drip for three years, building a small stalactite of precipitated minerals that grows by molecules.

The mountain presses in, patient and vast, but I am patient too. I document its pressure in the spider-web cracks that appear and grow in the concrete, in the way the steel plates have begun to bow—imperceptibly, but I perceive it—under the constant load. I know which bolts will fail first, which seals will need replacement, where the next leak will appear. This knowledge is written in my body, in the patterns I have become.

I am the room's autobiography, written in water and wear, in stain and stress. Every surface here is my page, every form of deterioration my alphabet. In a thousand years, when this dam is ruins and archaeologists parse its remains, they will find me in the particular way this concrete aged, in the specific patterns of erosion on these pipes. They will not know they are reading me, but they will be. I will have become the text of how a room at four thousand meters held back a mountain's weight of water, how it sang its deafening song in the dark, how it wept and breathed and trembled and aged.

And I will remember it all: every droplet's path, every cycle of the regulators, every groan of that ancient pump lifting water back into the great circulation, every moment of this loud, wet, trembling life lived between the mountain's pressure and the water's purpose, forever and perfectly alone.
