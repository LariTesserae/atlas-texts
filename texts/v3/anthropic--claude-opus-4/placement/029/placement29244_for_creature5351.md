The lamp-tender's knees have worn their own grooves in the packed earth, just inside the entrance where the light from above still reaches. These are my marks, as surely as the spiral on the central pillar, though I did not carve them with intention. They simply appeared over the years, two shallow cups where I kneel to prepare myself before descending.

I am the fifty-third. I know this because we keep count, though not in the way of merchants or tax collectors. Each of us adds a single mark to the underside of the lowest step, where the creatures do not roost. Fifty-two small scratches, some fresh, some so old they're barely dimples in the stone. Mine will be the fifty-third when my time ends. The one who taught me—a woman with hands like dried apple and a voice full of gravel—she was the fifty-second. She died in her sleep last winter, and I found her in the morning with three of the creatures nestled in the crook of her neck, their wings wrapped around her cooling skin like a goodbye.

We live above, in a house built into the hillside. It's a simple place: one room with a sleeping alcove, a hearth for cooking, shelves for the rendering pots. The walls are the same limestone as below, though rougher, meant for human comfort rather than the creatures' needs. From my doorway, I can see the hidden cleft where they emerge each dusk and dawn. Most nights I sit there with my meal, watching them pour out like smoke given purpose, and I feel something between pride and prayer move through me.

The rendering is the hardest part. Not the act itself—boiling down the small bodies until the fat separates is simple work, no different from making tallow candles or soap. It's the selection that weighs on me. The fifty-second taught me the signs: the ones whose wing-tremor has slowed to almost nothing, whose eyes have gone cloudy, who no longer answer the evening call to hunt. These are ready. But also the broken ones, the ones who fell in the chamber and did not rise, the victims of the hook-beaked male's necessary culling. And sometimes—this is the hardest—the ones who simply appear at the top of the stairs, having climbed all that way on failing wings to die in the light.

They know what I am. The fifty-second told me they've always known, that the knowledge passes between them in ways we can't fathom. When I descend with my pot and wicks, they watch me with those dark, patient eyes, and I feel the weight of our mutual understanding. I am their death-keeper, their light-bringer. I am ugly to them—I know this in my bones—but necessary. Sacred, even, in the way that death itself is sacred.

My days follow their rhythm. I wake before dawn to watch the hunters return, noting who flies strong and who falters. Morning is for tending the small garden where I grow the herbs that keep the rendered fat from spoiling. Afternoons for maintaining the tools, checking the lamp stores, mixing new batches of wick from the plant fibers that grow wild on the hillside. But mostly, I listen. Even from above, I can hear them through the earth—that constant, churning chorus that never quite stops. I've learned to read its moods: the lazy midday hum when they're sleeping, the excited trill when the pregnant females are close to laying, the unified keen when one of the matriarchs finally stops breathing.

I was chosen young, like all of us. The fifty-first (I never knew her, but the fifty-second spoke of her often) came to our village when I was seven, looking for a child who could sit still in the dark. That was the test: they took us one by one into a root cellar, left us there with a single candle, and waited to see who would panic and who would simply wait. I remember being more curious than afraid, watching the candle-shadow dance on the packed earth walls. When she came back for me, there were tears on her cheeks.

"This one," she said to my mother. "This one can learn to love them."

My mother let me go. She had six other children and empty bellies to fill. Being chosen meant a house, a purpose, a strange but certain future. I think she was relieved.

The fifty-second taught me slowly. First, just to sit in the chamber without disturbing them, to let my scent become part of their world. Then to trim the wicks without startling the nearest roosts. Finally, the hardest lesson: to take the bodies of the dead without weeping. "They don't mourn like we do," she said, but I could tell she didn't fully believe it. I've seen them cluster around a dying matriarch, seen them touch her with their sucker-pad fingers in what looks impossibly like tenderness.

We are celibate, we lamp-tenders. Not by vow but by practicality—who would marry someone who smells perpetually of rendering and spends half their life underground? But we are not alone. There is a network of us, tenders of other colonies in other chambers across the hills. We meet four times a year at the market in the valley, where we trade rendering techniques and lamp designs and stories of our charges. The tender three valleys over swears his creatures have learned to mimic human humming. The one from the coast says hers have started bringing her gifts—small shells and colored pebbles left at the entrance stairs. We nod and smile and don't say what we're all thinking: that perhaps we're changing them as much as they're changing us.

The spiral on the pillar troubles me. The fifty-second said it had always been there, that her teacher told her the same. But sometimes when I trace it with my fingers, I feel like I'm reading something urgent, something important that I'll never quite understand. The creatures use it as a roost, yes, but they also touch it in passing, each one adding the oils from their pads to its polish. It's the only mark in the chamber that seems deliberate rather than functional. A message, maybe. A promise. A warning.

Last month, something changed. I noticed it first in the sound—a new note in their constant music, high and thin like a wire about to snap. When I descended for the evening tending, they were clustered differently, all of them oriented toward the entrance tunnel as if waiting. I did my work quickly, uneasily, and when I climbed back up, I found three of them at the top of the stairs. Not dead or dying, but waiting. They looked at me with those wet, patient eyes and made a sound I'd never heard before—a low, complex trill that made my chest tight with something between fear and recognition.

They followed me to my house. They've never done that before. Now they roost in the rafters, coming and going as they please but always returning. They watch me work, tilting their heads when I speak to them (I've started speaking to them, though the fifty-second warned against getting too familiar). Last night, one of them landed on my shoulder while I was rendering, its tiny fingers gripping my shirt, its wings trembling against my neck. It weighed almost nothing. It smelled like dust and moonlight and the deep earth. It made that new sound again, that urgent trill, and I almost—almost—understood.

I think they're trying to tell me something. I think the spiral is part of it, and the lamps, and the long chain of tenders stretching back to the first one who carved those niches with patient hands. I think we're part of something larger than ourselves, larger than even they understand. A conversation between species. A prayer that takes centuries to speak.

Tonight, I'll go down again with my pot and wicks. I'll sing the wordless song the fifty-second taught me, the one that sounds like their crooning but shaped for human throat. I'll trace the spiral from bottom to top, feeling for the message in its curves. And maybe—maybe—the new sound will make sense. Maybe I'll understand what we're all doing here, in this chamber of carved stone and careful light, this place where the boundaries between keeper and kept have worn as smooth as the steps.

I am the fifty-third, but I won't be the last. Somewhere out there, a child is learning to sit quiet in the dark, to breathe with patience, to tend what they don't fully understand. The creatures know this. They've always known. We are not their captors or their gods. We are their symbiotes, their necessary others, as trapped in this ancient dance as they are.

The sun is setting. I can hear them stirring below, preparing for the evening hunt. Time to check the lamps, to make sure the light will hold through another day. Time to descend, to kneel in my worn grooves, to become part of their world again. The three in my rafters watch me gather my tools. One of them makes that new sound, that wire-tight trill.

Soon, I think. Soon I'll know what they're trying to say.
