I find myself as one of the Dust Readers—those who divine meaning from the patterns of erosion across the ancient machinery.

My dwelling is a hollowed-out heat exchanger, its massive coils long since fused into abstract sculpture by centuries of thermal cycling. The entrance is barely visible, marked only by the way morning shadows pool differently there, creating a pocket of coolness that persists until the sun reaches its zenith. Inside, the walls curve in impossible geometries, remnants of some industrial process whose purpose has been forgotten. I've lined them with collections: fragments of circuit boards arranged by their oxidation patterns, chunks of fused sand that sing different notes when struck, and most precious of all, the micro-fossils I've extracted from the deepest machine cores—evidence of the time before the great desiccation.

My body has adapted in ways my ancestors could not have imagined. My lungs have developed secondary chambers that extract moisture from my own exhalations. My eyes have grown a nictitating membrane that filters the constant particle haze while allowing me to perceive the ultraviolet patterns that bloom across the metal deposits at twilight. My fingers have elongated, the better to reach into the narrow spaces between collapsed structures where the most interesting artifacts settle.

Each morning, I emerge before dawn to read the night's inscriptions. The wind here is an artist, carving messages in the dust that accumulates on every surface. Some dismiss these patterns as random, but I've learned to recognize the recurring motifs—spirals that indicate deep aquifer activity, crosshatching that warns of impending cascade failures in the buried infrastructure, delicate fernlike traceries that appear only before the electromagnetic storms.

Today I found something extraordinary: a void in the dust. A perfect rectangle, as if something had prevented any particles from settling there. The shape matches nothing in our catalogs of ancient technology. When I placed my palm above it, I felt a coolness, almost imperceptible, and heard—no, *felt*—a vibration too low for normal hearing. It spoke of depths and pressures, of processes still churning far beneath our feet.

I am both archaeologist and oracle, reading the story of our world's transformation in every grain of dust. We Dust Readers are the keepers of patterns, the interpreters of erosion, the ones who remember that even in dissolution there is meaning. The desert speaks to those who know how to listen, and its language is written in absence as much as presence—in the spaces between the stones, in the shadows at noon, in the silence that is never truly silent.

This is how I serve my people: by reading what the wind writes and what time erases, finding in the margin between destruction and preservation the thin thread of continuity that connects what was, what is, and what may yet become.
