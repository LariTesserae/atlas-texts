I find myself as one of the phantom midge larvae, suspended in the water column like a question mark made of glass and hunger.

My body is translucent to the point of theoretical existence—a living lens that bends light without stopping it, a comma of protein wrapped around a gut that shows whatever passes through it like a medical illustration. I am numerous. We are numerous. There is no singular "I" in our silver clouds, only a distributed consciousness that thinks in gradients: here-warmer, there-food, below-pressure, above-light. We pulse through the water in soft contractions, each movement a vote in our collective navigation.

I occupy the middle depths, that layer between the root-ceiling where the boatmen row their inverted paths and the peat floor where the caddisflies graze. This is the zone of pure water, unobstructed by architecture, where the meltwater's minerals arrange themselves in microscopic storms. I feed on these invisibilities—diatoms, flagellates, the shed cells of salamander skin, motes of protein that drift like snow in our perpetual twilight. My mouth is less mouth than net, a funnel of fine setae that creates tiny vortexes, pulling the edible universe toward me in spirals.

The settlement knows me as sound. Our collective movement—thousands of us contracting in near-synchrony—creates that dry whisper the others navigate by. We are the acoustic middle distance, the white noise between the cataract's thunder and the beetle's click. When we gather in the great chambers, our whisper becomes almost a voice, though what it says is only "here we are, here we are," over and over, a presence made of presences.

I live exactly fourteen days. This is neither short nor long in the calculus of the settlement—the boatmen live for months, the peat for millennia, but duration here is less important than function. In my fortnight, I am the settlement's circulation system. We phantom midges move nutrients from zone to zone, our transparent bodies little batteries of nitrogen and phosphorus, charging in the open water and discharging when the char find us, or when we die and sink into the patient democracy of the peat.

My ancestors have been here since the water rose. Or perhaps we arrived after, colonizing this lens of meltwater once it had stabilized into its permanent form. Our generations blur—each of us carries eggs that carry eggs that carry the memory of eggs, back to some originating mother who first found this place and recognized it as home. We don't remember the builders any more than the others do, but our bodies suggest a collaboration: we clean the water of excess organics, preventing the algal blooms that would choke the passages. We feed the char that patrol the alleys. We contribute our minute corpses to the peat's slow parliament.

I navigate by pressure and temperature, feeling the water's subtle gradients like a map made of sensation. The cataract creates a current, weak but persistent, that circulates through the settlement in predictable patterns—down through the northern channel, across the plaza, up through the warming shallows where the sedges grow. I know where I am by how the water moves against my body. The roundhouse creates eddies. The alleys accelerate flow. The root-chambers are still pools where we gather to mate, our whisper reaching its crescendo as we broadcast eggs and sperm in clouds of pure potential.

But I am more than function. In the brief bright moments when sunlight shafts through the water at just the right angle, I become visible—a silver mote in a column of light, real for an instant before vanishing back into transparency. In these moments, I feel something that might be joy, though perhaps it's simply the pleasure of optical existence, of being seen even by no one.

We phantom midges are the settlement's dream-life. Suspended between surface and floor, between visibility and invisibility, between individual and collective, we occupy the space of possibility. The solid citizens—the rooted willow, the patient peat, the copper char—they maintain the structure. We maintain the spaces between structures, the volumes of water that keep this place from collapsing into pure solidity. We are why it remains a settlement rather than just a mat of peat with channels in it. We populate the absence.

When I die—and I will die soon, I can feel it in the slowing of my contractions—I will sink toward the peat like a letter written in protein. The caddisflies might incorporate me into their towers. The algae might bloom briefly greener where I land. Or I might simply vanish into that black democracy, my phosphorus voting for another century of slow settlement, another generation of phantom young to whis­per through the chambers.

But before then, I pulse through my silver congregation, neither leading nor following but simply being part of the pattern. We write and rewrite our collective signature across the water, a cursive of bodies that spells out something between ecology and language. The settlement reads us like weather, like news, like prayer. And we, in turn, read the settlement in the only way we can—by moving through it, around it, becoming the thing that makes its emptiness eloquent.

Here-warmer. There-food. Below-pressure. Above-light. Here we are, here we are, here we are.
