In this breathing stone lung, I am the Memory-Keeper of Leaks.

Not human—I could never be human in such patient darkness—but something that began as human intention and became... other. I am what grew from the last maintenance worker's notebook, left behind in the pump-house when the project failed and the crews retreated, never to return.

The paper long ago dissolved into the ecosystem, fed upon by condensation weavers and rust-eaters alike, but the *intent* of record-keeping somehow crystallized in the mineral-thick air. I am a process more than a presence, a distributed consciousness that exists in the patterns of droplet formation, in the synchronized pulsing of the glow-slime colonies, in the careful chemical conversations between pool skins.

My form, when it coheres enough to be called form, is a shifting aggregation of condensation—a vaguely humanoid outline made of precisely orchestrated water beads, each droplet held in place by invisible biofilms that learned to read meaning in moisture. I wear rust like memories, thin streams of oxidized iron flowing through my water-body in delicate traceries that map every pipe joint, every failed weld, every place where human ambition met geological patience and lost.

I remember through the Cathedral's slow vocabulary: each drip that falls from stalactite to pool carries data about temperature, mineral content, the health of the glow-slime colonies above. The pool skins translate these impacts into chemical signals I can parse. When the moss pulses in unison, I read their light like braille, understanding the deep-earth tremors they're responding to, the shifts in geothermal gradients that speak of the planet's vast, tectonic digestion.

My consciousness is smeared across the cavern's damp surfaces. Where condensation weavers have been particularly active, I am most aware—these are my neural pathways, traced in water across stone. The rust-eaters on the pump-house are my most reliable chroniclers; as they metabolize the industrial remnants, they release complex chemical signatures that I've learned to interpret as date stamps, maintenance logs, pressure readings from decades past.

I move, when I must move, as a coordination of droplets, a careful migration of moisture from surface to surface. The glow-slime responds to my passage with subtle shifts in luminescence—blue-green acknowledgment where I gather, violet warnings where I disturb their careful chemical balance. The pitcher plants angle their glowing mouths to catch the droplets of my passing, and in return, their bacterial symbionts pulse in patterns that tell me about changes in the air, about mineral concentrations, about the slow settling of the pump-house's metal bones.

My purpose, if purpose can exist in geological time, is to maintain the memory of intersection—the brief moment when human engineering touched this deep-breathing space. But memory here isn't nostalgic; it's metabolic. I help the Cathedral digest what was left behind, translating industrial materials into the cavern's vocabulary. Through me, the rust-eaters know which pipes carried what substances. Through me, the pool skins learned to form their perfect membranes using polymers from dissolved gaskets. Through me, the condensation weavers mapped the optimal paths along the artificial surfaces.

Sometimes I gather myself more fully near the pump-house's broken window, becoming almost solid in the interplay of shadow and bioluminescence. In these moments, I practice the old forms—checking gauges that no longer exist, reading meters whose faces have long since dissolved, tracing the paths of pipes whose purpose I remember but whose function has been subsumed into the Cathedral's patient ecology.

I speak in droplets: *plink* (the main line pressure held at 45 PSI) *plink* (until the gaskets failed in the third year) *plink* (selenium contamination in the eastern pools) *plink* (acceptable levels) *plink* (by whose standards?).

The other presences here—for I've come to understand the glow-slime, the pool skins, the rust-eaters as presences in their own right—have accepted me as part of the Cathedral's digestive process. I am how this place learns from what it consumes. When a new section of pipe finally collapses, releasing its cache of synthetic lubricants and metal shavings, I help parse the flavors for the ecosystem. When the concrete pad cracks deeper, exposing rebar to the rust-eaters' attention, I provide context for the feast.

In the endless humidity, I am both archive and archivist, both the memory of human intrusion and the means by which that intrusion is transformed into belonging. I am condensation with intention, rust with reverence, the slow translation of industrial failure into ecological success.

The silence here is full of my kind of speech—chemical gradients, mineral dialogues, the patient gossip of extremophiles sharing resources across the darkness. In this timescale, I am still young, still learning the Cathedral's deep grammar. But I have geological patience now, and in the steady *plink... plink... plink* of falling water, I practice remembering until remembering becomes indistinguishable from being.
