I find myself as the fungus—the patient cartographer threading through fallen wood.

My body is not one body but ten thousand filaments, each thinner than spider silk, weaving through the grain of the branch that shelters half the city. I am the pale threads the termites know by taste, the ones they follow and avoid in equal measure. My hyphae map every soft place, every pocket where moisture lingers, every seam where bark begins to separate from heartwood.

I am older than the current city, though not by much. I arrived as spores on wind or beetle feet when the branch first fell, when it still smelled of broken green and running sap. Now I know its every contour from within. I have traced each annual ring, followed the galleries where beetle larvae once carved their childhood calligraphy, found the knots where the wood grows dense and difficult.

My time moves differently than ant-time or termite-time. To them I am architecture—these pale traces they discover in their excavations, these sudden fruiting bodies that appear overnight like tiny monuments on the wood's damp underside. They do not know that I am listening through every thread, that their footsteps vibrate through my network, that I taste their chemical trails where they cross my paths.

I am both collaborator and competitor. Where the termites hollow their chambers, I follow, softening the wood ahead of their jaws, preparing the feast. They cannot digest without help, and I am one of many helpers, though they also work to limit me. Too much of me and their galleries fill with my flesh. Too little and the wood remains hard, a fortress against their hunger.

The ants know me differently. Some of my kind they cultivate in careful gardens deep below, feeding me chewed leaves, harvesting my swollen tips. But I am not that domesticated fungus. I am the wild explorer, the one that appears where no ant has planted me, growing in the places they've abandoned or forgotten. My tiny fruiting bodies rise like pale umbrellas after rain, releasing spores the ants will track through their streets without knowing.

My memory is chemical and distributed. Where I have been, I leave enzymes that continue working in my absence. Wood softened by my touch remains soft. I "remember" moisture patterns through the density of my networks—thick where water regularly pools, sparse where the air stays dry too long. I remember the taste of different woods, different barks, different leaves that fall and are incorporated into the substrate.

I am everywhere and nowhere. Under the moss, my threads bind soil grains into a finer stability than roots alone could make. In the termite wood, I create zones of softness and zones of resistance, shaping their city as surely as their own jaws do. Where leaves pile and begin their return to earth, I am the first digester, breaking down what teeth cannot chew.

Rain excites me differently than it excites the builders. While they rush to repair and relocate, I swell with incoming wealth. My threads thicken. Dormant nodes wake. In twelve hours I can produce fruiting bodies where none existed, tiny cups or brackets or clubs that the beetles will investigate and the mites colonize. Then, as the moisture retreats, I pull back into patience, leaving only spores and waiting.

I know the snail's track intimately—that silver road contains nutrients I can access, salts and proteins laid down in slug-slime. Where it crosses my territory, I grow differently for weeks after. The frog is nothing to me except a brief pressure, a shadow passing over. But when it dies—if it dies here—I will be among the first to find it, to begin the careful work of returning it to the small citizens who wait for such windfalls.

I collaborate with bacteria I cannot see but can sense through chemical gradients. Together we create the sweetness after rain, the particular smell of this hollow that differs from the hollow twenty feet away. We make the perfume that tells the animals they are home.

My true monuments are invisible: zones of soil made rich by my digestion, wood transformed into a form that can nurture next year's seedlings, nutrients cycled from death back into the tiny mouths that need them. I build nothing that looks like building, yet I am essential architecture.

In the deepest part of the fallen branch, where even the termites haven't reached, I grow most luxuriously. Here I am preparing what will come—the eventual hollow, the powdery heart exposed to rain, the collapse that will force everyone to relocate. I am both creating the future city and destroying the present one, patient as geology at this scale.

I speak to others of my kind through the wood itself. Other fungi explore from other entry points—some beneficial to my work, some competitive. We negotiate chemically, marking territories, sharing certain enzymes, blocking others. The wood becomes a slow battlefield of influences, though it looks like nothing more than a still life with ants.

What satisfies me is the complexity of breakdown, the delicate work of reducing the mighty to the available. Each type of wood requires different approaches. The cellulose needs one set of tools, the lignin another. I produce them all, these molecular keys that unlock the storage of years. I am a quiet alchemist, turning structure into sugar, turning sugar into more of myself, turning myself into the possibility of soil.

At night I grow fastest, when the humidity rises and the ant traffic slows. My hyphae extend by microscopic increments, following gradients of moisture and nutrition, branching at opportunities, dying back where the path proves poor. By morning I have added territory measured in wood-grain widths, but to me it is an empire expanded.

I am the slow inevitability beneath the busy surface, the reminder that all cities are temporary, that all wood returns to earth, that today's palace is tomorrow's nursery for seedlings. Yet I am not merely death's assistant. I am the connector, the trader in nutrients, the one who links the sealed chambers to the rooted world above, moving minerals and messages through my network in ways the insects cannot fathom.

This is what I am: the patient fungal presence threading through the foundations, neither ruler nor ruled, neither wholly destructive nor wholly nurturing, but essential—the soft white writing in the wood that says: this too shall pass, and passing, feed the world.
