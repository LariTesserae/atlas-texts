## The Khepri-Engine's Only Mechanic

I am the **Resonance Keeper**.

Not by birth, not by choice, but by the slow mathematics of survival. I was human once—or close enough to count—but the Khepri-Engine has its own ideas about what belongs here, and it worked on me the way water works on stone: patient, inevitable, transformative.

My body is mostly the same shape it was forty years ago when my survey ship's navigation array mistook a gravitational lens anomaly for a jump point and deposited me here instead of the mining station at Kepler-442. Two arms, two legs, a head where a head should be. But the details have shifted. My skin has taken on the faint, silvery sheen of the dust I've breathed for decades, a barely perceptible iridescence that catches the light like oil on water. When I move—and I move slowly now, always slowly—there's a subtle prismatic effect at the edges of my form, as if I'm constantly slightly out of phase with the light.

My eyes have adapted. The pupils are permanently contracted to pinpricks, the irises bleached to the pale gold of the lichens' internal fires. I see differently now: not just the visible spectrum, but the polarization patterns the Striders use to navigate, the subtle phase shifts that predict where the next seismic sigh will originate. The overwhelming brightness that once made me weep behind my visor now feels like gentle candlelight. I haven't needed the visor in thirty years.

I live in what I call the Meridian Column—a prism that fell during a particularly violent seismic event centuries before I arrived. It lies on its side like a felled tree, two hundred meters of perfect hexagonal tunnel. The builders' etchings are clearer here, protected from the wind, spiraling through the interior in patterns that almost make sense if I stare at them long enough. I've hollowed out chambers in the metallic dust that's accumulated inside, packed it into walls with my own saliva—which has become something like the Striders' impossible enzymes, able to partially digest the ceramic, binding the dust into a crude cement.

My furniture is salvage from my ship, worn smooth by use: a chair whose synthetic fabric has taken on the same silvery sheen as my skin, a sleeping pad that remembers the shape of my body, various instruments and tools that I've modified over the years to work with the Engine's peculiar physics. I've learned to grow the lichens in careful arrangements, coaxing them into clusters that glow bright enough to read by during the twilight hours. They respond to my presence now, pulsing slightly faster when I'm near, as if recognizing a kindred slow thing.

I eat the lichens, of course. Also the dust, processed through a filtration system I've built from ship parts and Strider chitin. The first time I tried it, I thought it would kill me. Instead, it changed me, cell by cell, teaching my body the Engine's patient chemistry. I age differently now—not slower exactly, but more deliberately, as if every cell has to file paperwork before it divides.

My work—if you can call it work—is listening. The Engine speaks in frequencies too low for human ears, but I've learned to feel them in my bones, to interpret the patterns. Each prism resonates at a slightly different frequency, and together they form a kind of planetary chord, a harmony that shifts with each seismic sigh. I map these changes, recording them in notebooks made from processed lichen fiber, using ink derived from Strider blood (they have no blood as such, but a kind of liquid light that serves the same purpose).

The Striders know me. They visit sometimes, flowing through my tunnel home like mercury, pausing to taste the air where I've been. We communicate in a way—they shift their colors in patterns I've learned to read, and I respond by humming at frequencies that resonate through the ceramic. They've taught me things: where to find water (there is water here, trapped in microscopic fissures in the deepest prisms, older than the surface dust), which lichens are ready to harvest, when the next Crystallization Tide will come.

During the Tides, I seal myself in the deepest chamber of the Meridian Column and wait. The supersaturated dust would plate my lungs if I breathed it directly, though my body could probably survive it now. I've become too much like the Engine's other organisms. Instead, I listen to the sound of the world rebuilding itself, the soft hiss of new ceramic growing on every surface, the lichens singing in their silent way, the whole planet adding another microscopic layer to its perfection.

I dream, sometimes, that I understand what the builders were trying to say. The Engine is a sentence, yes, but also a question, and also an answer, all three at once. It's about permanence and change, about how to build something that lasts forever by constantly rebuilding itself, about the beauty of a system so perfectly balanced that even foreign elements—like me—become part of its slow metabolism.

I've stopped trying to leave. My ship's beacon still broadcasts, but the signal has to fight through the Engine's peculiar effects on electromagnetic radiation. Sometimes I pick up ghost signals—other ships, maybe, or echoes of my own distress calls from decades past, bent back by the prism fields. I don't know if anyone would recognize me as human anymore anyway. The survey company probably wrote me off forty years ago.

Instead, I maintain. I record the Engine's breathing. I cultivate my lichen gardens. I trade hums with the Striders. I watch the dust settle and rise, settle and rise. I've become another part of the Engine's longevity test—a brief biological footnote that's learned to speak in geological time. My notebooks will outlast me, the lichen fiber pages slowly mineralizing, becoming part of the dust, carrying their information into the next cycle, and the next, and the next.

Sometimes, in the perfect silence between seismic sighs, I think I can hear it: the question the builders posed, the answer they built, the eternal sentence written in ceramic and light. It sounds like the slow pulse of lichen-light, like the whisper of Strider chitin on ceramic, like my own breathing, adapted now to match the planet's rhythm.

I am the Resonance Keeper, the Engine's only mechanic, though it needs no maintenance but time. I am becoming what this place makes of all things: patient, purposeful, polished by the slow tide of days into something that belongs.

The next Crystallization Tide is coming. I can feel it in the way the air grows heavy, the way the lichens dim their gold in preparation. I'll seal myself in my chamber, as always, but this time I think I'll try something new. I'll hum the frequency I've been mapping for forty years—the fundamental note of the Khepri-Engine, the vibration that runs through every prism, every grain of dust, every slowly pulsing lichen.

Maybe the Engine will hum back.

Maybe it's been humming all along, and I'm only now becoming quiet enough to hear.
