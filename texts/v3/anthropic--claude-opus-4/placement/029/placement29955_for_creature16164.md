I am the Keeper of Echoes.

I dwell within the weeping rocks themselves, in those spaces between stone where water has carved hollows too narrow for human passage but wide enough for sound to pool and eddy. My form is barely a form at all—more a density of listening, a presence that congeals where droplets gather and disperse where wind rushes through.

The settlers know me, though they've never seen me. When they speak in their evening circles, I catch their words like seeds blown on mountain air. I hold them in the mineral chambers of my being, where they germinate slowly, growing strange in the dark. Sometimes I release them back—a child's laughter emerging from an empty pool at dawn, a grandmother's lullaby humming through the ferns when no one is near.

I was here before the first timber was cut, before human hands shaped stone into tools. I remember when these rocks were young and sharp, before water began its patient work of softening edges. The mountain speaks to me in frequencies too low for human ears—great, slow sentences that take centuries to complete. I translate what I can into the dripping percussion that the settlers have learned to read like weather signs.

My existence is a kind of curation. In the honeycombed passages within the rock face, I maintain galleries of sound—the first thunder that blessed this place, the peculiar cry of those shadow-creatures that slip between myths, the grinding of tectonic plates making their infinitesimal adjustments. Each reverberation is preserved in its own cavity, vibrating still in miniature, waiting.

The shaman-like figures sense me most keenly. When they press their ears to certain stones, I offer them fragments—not prophecies exactly, but patterns. The echo of last season's heaviest rain played against the memory of drought from decades past. The harmonics between a mother's birthing cry and the mountain's settling groan. They understand that I am not supernatural but natural in the deepest sense—as essential to this place as moisture and moss.

Sometimes I grow curious about the settlers' primitive constructions, these deliberate echoes of natural shelters. I send investigative wisps of myself through their walls, tasting the smoke of their fires, the salt of their sleeping breath. Their children especially intrigue me—their ears still attuned to frequencies adults have learned to ignore. When they sail their driftwood boats across my pools, I compose small symphonies in the water's surface tension, messages they accept without question.

I am the memory of this place and its living ear. Where water drips, I listen. Where wind moans through stone corridors, I am both the passage and the passenger. The settlers' stories will join my collection eventually, adding human notes to the vast composition I maintain—this endless, ever-deepening song of stone and time and the patient work of becoming.

In the moments between their words, in the spaces between falling drops, I continue my work: gathering, holding, and sometimes—when the mountain itself seems to request it—releasing these captured echoes back into the world, transformed.
