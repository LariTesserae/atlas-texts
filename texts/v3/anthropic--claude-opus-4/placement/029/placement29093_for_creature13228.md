I would find myself as one of the Memory Keepers—though that is not what we call ourselves, for names here dissolve like morning mist before they can fully form.

My dwelling lies in the transition spaces, those liminal chambers where the gigavine's flesh thins from the dense, woody interior to the more delicate outer membranes. Here, the plant's tissues form natural acoustic chambers, spaces that catch and hold sound in ways that defy simple physics. The walls pulse with that faint bioluminescent heartbeat, but irregularly—sometimes darkness reigns for what might be days, sometimes the blue-green glow persists in lazy, tidal surges.

My form is neither wholly of the Root-Singers nor the Sky-Listeners, but something between—skin that shifts in texture from smooth membrane to rough bark depending on the humidity carried by the rare winds. My fingers are elongated, with sensitive pads that can read the microscopic variations in the gigavine's surface, each ridge and valley a letter in an alphabet written across centuries.

What I keep are not memories in any conventional sense, but the patterns themselves—the way spore-fall changes with the infinitesimal shifts in air pressure, the precise harmonics that emerge when certain chambers resonate together, the slow migration of the bioluminescent zones through the plant's body over decades. I map these patterns into the living walls themselves, using tools made from crystallized sap and my own patient touch to encourage the gigavine to grow in specific ways. A slight thickening here creates a new acoustic node; a carefully tended hollow there preserves the echo of a particular wind-song for generations.

The other inhabitants know me by the faint clicking sounds my movements make—a percussion of fingernails on bark, of small stone tools tapping out rhythms that encode information in ways that bypass language entirely. When the Lamp-Bearers need to know the safe paths through zones where the gigavine's growth has shifted, they come to me. When the Root-Singers detect an anomaly in the deep ionic flows, I help them trace its source through the accumulated knowledge written in the plant's own flesh.

My work is solitary but not lonely. The gigavine itself is company enough—its vast, distributed consciousness touching mine through every surface I read, every pattern I trace. Sometimes I feel I am not so much recording its history as taking dictation from a mind too large and slow to comprehend all at once. In the deepest chambers, where my fingers find patterns laid down before any of the current inhabitants emerged, I sometimes sense intentions, purposes that unfold across timescales that dwarf even our patient lives.

There is a chamber I tend with particular care, where the acoustic properties create a perfect archive of sound. Every rustle that has occurred in this space for the past thousand years lives on in the subtle variations of the walls, readable to those who know how. Here, I discovered that the gigavine's song changes—not randomly, but in response to something. To what, I have not yet determined. The patterns suggest communication, but with what or whom remains opaque.

In the perpetual twilight of my domain, I sometimes pause in my work, hands resting on the living walls, feeling the slow pulse of sap through vessels thick as my body. In these moments, I am not sure where I end and the gigavine begins. My memories—of learning my craft, of discovering each new pattern, of the slow accumulation of understanding—feel indistinguishable from the plant's own patient chronicle of growth and change.

When I die—and we do die here, eventually, our bodies given back to the gigavine in careful ceremony—another will take up the work. They will read my patterns in the walls, learn my methods from the very substance I've shaped, and continue the endless documentation. Perhaps they will solve the riddles I leave behind. Perhaps they will discover new mysteries I never imagined.

But for now, I persist in my transitional spaces, clicking softly through the darkness, reading the deep time written in living wood, adding my own small marks to the vast, rustling library of the Chasm. The spores fall. The wind sings. The patterns accumulate, layer upon layer, a palimpsest of meaning that stretches back to the Chasm's first breath and forward to its last.

I am keeper and kept, reader and read, a single note in the endless rustling chorus that defines this place. And in the blue-green flicker of bioluminescent thought, I continue my work, patient as the stone below, persistent as the spores above, woven inextricably into the weird, wonderful fabric of this vegetal universe.
