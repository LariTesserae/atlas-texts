I find myself in the resonance chambers beneath the Great Bellows Factory—the deepest spaces where a body can still work without going mad from the weight.

I am a **Sympathist**, one who tends the harmonics between stone and steel.

My quarters are a narrow cell carved into a natural cavity where the obsidian meets the first iron girders. The walls here sweat constantly—not with water, but with a viscous mineral oil that seeps from microscopic fissures in the volcanic glass. I've learned to read these sweats like others read weather. When the oil runs thick and amber, the mountain is content. When it thins to the consistency of tears, the stone is under stress, grinding against itself in the deep.

My body has adapted to this depth. My skin has taken on a pewter sheen from years of absorbing the mineral vapors. My fingernails grow thick and ridged like shells, perfect for scraping samples from the stone's surface. But it's my bones that have changed the most—they've become dense, almost metallic. I wear no ballast belt. At this depth, the weight in my skeleton is enough to keep me grounded.

I spend my shifts in the **Tuning Hollows**, natural resonance chambers where the factory's massive steam hammers meet the mountain's own vibrations. My tools are simple: brass listening rods of various lengths, each one attuned to a different harmonic frequency. I press them against the junction points where iron bolts sink into obsidian, listening for the ghost-notes that warn of structural fatigue.

The factory doesn't know what to make of us Sympathists. We're neither true Glass-Listeners nor proper engineers. We exist in the margin between the mechanical and the geological, translating the mountain's slow language into something the furnace-tenders can understand. When I detect a dangerous resonance—a frequency that could shatter the stone or buckle the steel—I mark it with phosphorescent chalk, a recipe known only to our order, mixed from Cold-Worm secretions and pulverized Slick-glider membrane.

My greatest discovery came three years ago, during a particularly violent growth-surge. I found that certain combinations of industrial rhythm—the precise timing of steam-hammer strikes—could actually *soothe* the mountain. By coordinating with the Shift-Masters above, we've learned to make the factory itself into a massive percussion instrument, playing a slow, thunderous lullaby that keeps the worst of the glass-screech at bay.

The other Sympathists think I go too deep. Below my chamber, there are spaces where even we don't venture—smooth, spherical voids in the obsidian where the air pressure changes every few heartbeats, where your ears pop and bleed from invisible tides. I've been there. I've heard things that have no names: the sound of the stone *breathing*, the whisper of magma still cooling miles below, the clicking languages of creatures that live in the liquid spaces between solid rock.

In my dreams, I sometimes become the mountain—feeling the weight of the iron city like barnacles on my skin, the tickle of the Soughs nesting in my crevices, the slow itch of my growth as I push myself up through the heavy air. I wake with my hands pressed against the walls, my fingernails leaving small grooves in the stone, as if I'd been trying to climb inside it.

The Company sends physicians to check on us twice a year. They test our hearing, our bone density, our mental acuity. They don't understand that we're not degrading—we're transforming. Each year, I need less light to see in the deep chambers. Each year, the stone's vibrations make more sense than human speech.

There's a prophecy among the Sympathists, whispered in the deepest shifts when the steam-hammers fall silent. They say that one day, one of us will sink so deep into harmony with the mountain that we'll stop being human altogether. We'll become a living conduit between the city and the stone—a permanent translator, embedded in the obsidian like a fossil, forever listening, forever speaking in frequencies only the mountain can hear.

I polish my brass rods and descend a little deeper each shift. The stone is teaching me its oldest songs, the ones it learned in the pressure-dark before it was born. Sometimes, when I press my ridged nails against the warm spots where the oil weeps thickest, I swear I can feel a pulse.

The mountain is not just growing. It's *becoming*.

And so am I.
