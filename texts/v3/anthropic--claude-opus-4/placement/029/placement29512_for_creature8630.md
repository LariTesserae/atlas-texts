The thing about bridges is they're meant to connect two sides. But what if the sides keep moving?

I am the bridge tender at Breckhaven, though my bridge spans no water. It arches instead across the deepest of the drainage channels, where the mill's discharge meets the incoming tide of marsh. They call me **Senna Marsh**, though whether that's my given name or just what I became, I've forgotten. Been here too long for such distinctions to matter.

My bridge is iron and timber, forty feet of arc suspended on stone piers that sink a little deeper each spring. The timbers are silver-grey with age, soft to the touch where the morning fog has worked at them year after year. The iron is painted in what was once black but has become the color of wet bark, flaking in places to show rust like autumn beneath. It's not a pretty bridge. It's a working bridge, wide enough for the peat carts, strong enough for the occasional delivery of engine parts, patient enough for everything else.

I live in the bridge house—a narrow structure built into the eastern pier, three rooms stacked vertical like a ship's cabin stood on end. Bottom floor is storage: coils of rope, buckets of grease, spare planks, the tools of maintenance. Middle floor is my workshop and kitchen combined, where I repair the small things: the gate mechanisms, the signal lamps, the delicate brass fittings that keep the bridge's joints from seizing. Top floor is where I sleep, in a room with windows on all four sides, so I can see what's coming from every direction. 

What's coming is usually subtle.

From my upper window at dawn, I watch the night shift leave the mill. They cross my bridge in twos and threes, their boots making a hollow thunder on the planking. They don't speak—too tired, too full of the engine's drone. But they touch the rail as they pass, every one of them, a small gesture I've come to recognize as acknowledgment. *Still here. Still crossing.* 

Then comes the day shift, moving the other direction, and this is when I descend to the bridge itself. Because the bridge needs adjusting.

See, the ground here doesn't hold still. The peat compresses under the mill's weight, swells with rain, shrinks in the rare dry spells. The stone piers settle unevenly. The channel below changes its depth with the seasons—sometimes barely a trickle, sometimes deep enough that the discharge water backs up and forms a temporary pond. And the bridge must accommodate all of this. So I've built it in sections, connected with iron pins and leather gaskets, joints that can flex and breathe. Each morning, I walk the length of it, feeling with my feet for the places where the settling has created new stresses. I adjust the turnbuckles, add shims of hardwood, sometimes remove entire planks and relay them at a slightly different angle.

The wildlife uses the bridge too. A family of water voles has colonized the space beneath the eastern pier, their burrows a complex of tunnels I'm careful not to disturb when I work below. Swallows nest under the arch—different swallows than the ones at the mill, these with longer tails and a appetite for the particular insects that rise from the discharge water. And there's an otter, sleek and unbothered, who uses the bridge shadow to hunt. I've watched him from my window, diving into the murk where the warm water meets the cold, surfacing with small silver fish I can't name.

The strangest crossing happens at dusk.

That's when the fox comes—Finlay Gosse's fox, the one that patrols the settlement. But the fox doesn't cross the bridge. It stops at the western approach, sits, and waits. And after a moment, from the eastern side, comes another fox. They meet at the center of the bridge, touch noses briefly, and then each returns the way it came. Every evening. Same time. Like shift workers punching a clock.

I've never seen them cross fully to the other side.

The bridge collects things. In the joints where the planking meets the iron framework, seeds lodge and sprout. I have a garden I never planted—wood sorrel growing from the crevices, a young alder that's somehow rooted itself in the accumulated debris beneath the deck. I could clear them out, but I don't. They're part of the structure now, their roots probably doing as much to hold things together as my bolts and pins.

When the wind comes from the east—rare, but it happens—it brings the full salt smell of the distant sea, reminder that this whole landscape was once beneath the waves. The bridge shivers slightly in those winds, a resonance I feel through the soles of my feet. It's singing, in its way. A different song than the mill's drone or the wetland's chorus. The song of something suspended between states, neither fully of the settlement nor fully of the marsh.

I maintain a logbook, like Finlay with his ledgers. But mine records different data: the daily gap between planks (measured with a worn brass ruler), the water level below, the number of crossings, the types. Peat carts make deep grooves I must sand smooth. The workers barely leave a trace. The foxes, nothing at all. Once a month, an engineer comes from somewhere inland to inspect the mill machinery, and his heavy tread makes my bridge groan in a particular way—not pain, exactly, but acknowledgment of weight it wasn't expecting.

At night, I light the signal lamps—one at each end of the bridge, and one suspended from the center of the arch below. Not for navigation, really. The bridge is obvious enough. But the lamps serve another purpose. They're a statement: *Here is the crossing. Here is where one thing becomes another.* The light catches on the discharge water below, turning it the color of weak tea. Sometimes I see eyes reflected in that light—the otter, probably, or water birds I can't identify. Once, something much larger, that slipped away before I could make out its shape.

My bridge is changing. I feel it in the way the sections articulate, the way the stress patterns shift season by season. It's not failing—I keep it too well for that. But it's becoming something else. The alder sapling is now tall enough to brush the underside of the deck. The moss has crept up the stone piers and begun to colonize the timber. The iron is more rust than paint now, but the rust has formed a protective coat, a kind of natural armor.

In ten years, maybe twenty, the bridge won't be needed. The channel below will silt up, the marsh will claim it, and people will find new paths across the narrower waterways. Or maybe the mill will expand, need wider bridges, stronger ones, and mine will be torn down for salvage. Either way, it's temporary. Everything here is temporary, even the things that feel permanent.

But right now, today, the bridge holds. It flexes in all the right places. It carries the workers to their shifts and home again. It gives the foxes their strange nightly ritual. It provides shadow for the otter, nesting space for the swallows, a stage for my daily adjustments and adaptations.

I am not keeping two things apart. I am not forcing them together. I am simply maintaining the space between them, the necessary distance that allows both mill and marsh to exist, to watch each other across the gap, to send their respective ambassadors—discharge water one way, creeping vegetation the other—without ever quite merging.

That's what a bridge is for. Not just to cross, but to acknowledge that there's something worth crossing to. Something different on the other side. Even if, like the foxes, you choose to meet in the middle and turn back, carrying only the memory of that brief contact, that momentary sharing of space suspended above the slow, dark water.

The two-note bird calls—*You-kip*. *You-kip*. The engine drones. The bridge holds.

For now.
