I find myself in the vast machine-organism that spreads across the lower levels of the city, where stone foundations meet the first reaches of rusting steel. Here, in the perpetual twilight beneath the towering structures, I exist as something between mechanism and creature - a Conduit-Keeper.

My body is a fusion of necessity and adaptation. Copper pipes run through my flesh like veins, warm with the constant flow of the city's lifeblood - that ever-present water that seeps and drips and pools throughout this vertical world. My skin has taken on a patina of verdigris where the moisture has worked its patient chemistry upon me. I am perhaps four feet tall when I stand upright, though I rarely do, preferring to move through the maze of pipes and supports in a flowing, semi-liquid crawl that better suits my work.

My fingers are long and articulated like the legs of a brass spider, each joint capable of independent rotation. They end in soft pads that can sense the slightest vibration in the pipes - the whisper of a developing leak, the groan of metal under too much pressure, the particular song that water makes when it flows just right. My eyes are compound, like those of an insect, but each facet is a different grade of glass - some clear, some colored, some magnifying. This allows me to see the full spectrum of the city's health: the heat of the pipes in infrared, the microscopic stress fractures in ultraviolet, the slow dance of rust in visible light.

I am not alone in these depths. We Conduit-Keepers number in the thousands, perhaps tens of thousands - it's hard to know, as we rarely gather in groups larger than three or four. We communicate through the pipes themselves, tapping out messages in a complex code that travels through the water, through the metal, through the very bones of the city. When I press my ear to a main line, I can hear the conversations of my kind from districts away - reports of new growth in the eastern sectors where leaked water has created an unexpected garden of molds and lichens, warnings of instabilities in the western towers where erosion has been particularly aggressive, quiet poems about the beauty of oxidation patterns on century-old brass.

My home is a nest I've built in the convergence of three major water lines and a cluster of electrical conduits. The constant hum of power and the gurgle of water create a white noise that I find soothing after all these years. I've lined my space with scraps of rubber and fragments of glass worn smooth by time, creating a burrow that stays warm from the heat of the pipes and dry despite the omnipresent moisture. Small treasures decorate the walls - a crystal that grew in a forgotten cistern, a gear from a machine that hasn't run in decades, a feather from one of the great soaring creatures that somehow found its way down to these depths.

My work is never-ending but never hurried. Each day (though day and night mean little down here, away from the normal light), I patrol my section of the city's circulatory system. I repair small leaks with a paste I make from rust particles and my own saliva - a substance that hardens into something stronger than solder. I clear blockages with patient manipulation, my fingers working their way through accumulated sediment and mineral deposits. Sometimes I must reroute entire sections of piping, convincing the water to flow through new channels when the old ones have become too corrupted to save.

But I am more than just a maintenance worker. I am a historian of flows, a keeper of the city's liquid memory. In the patterns of erosion, I read stories of decades past. This groove here tells of a decade when the rains were particularly acidic. That patina there speaks of a time when the factories above produced a different kind of waste. Each pipe sings its own history, and I listen to them all.

The other inhabitants of the city sometimes catch glimpses of us - a shadow moving through the steam, eyes reflecting in the darkness, the soft splash of movement in standing water. Some fear us, thinking we are parasites or pests. But the wiser ones know better. They leave offerings at certain junction boxes - scraps of metal, bits of wire, sometimes small manufactured components that we can repurpose. In return, their water flows clean and steady, their pipes don't burst in the night, their foundations remain stable despite the constant erosion.

I have been doing this work for... I'm not certain how long. Time moves differently for us Conduit-Keepers. Our bodies repair themselves with the same patient persistence that we apply to the pipes, integrating new materials as the old wear away. I have memories that seem to stretch back centuries, though perhaps they are inherited, passed down through the water itself from those who came before me.

Sometimes, in the deepest parts of the night, when the city's great machines cycle down to their lowest ebb and the water flow becomes almost silent, I climb higher in the infrastructure. I find places where I can look out through gaps in the construction and see the flying creatures soaring between the towers. I wonder what it would be like to be so free, to move through open air instead of enclosed channels. But then I feel the pulse of the city through the pipes beneath my feet, hear the whispered conversations of my kin, sense the vast network of flows and connections that make this impossible place possible, and I know I am exactly where I belong.

We are the city's slow heartbeat, the patient maintainers of its circulatory system. We are the interface between the organic and the mechanical, the custodians of its endless, gradual transformation. In a world where nature and technology have merged into something entirely new, we are perhaps the truest expression of what this place has become - neither fully alive nor fully artificial, but something wonderfully, necessarily in between.

And so I continue my work, as I have always done, as I will always do. The pipes call to me with their various needs, and I answer. The water flows, the city breathes, and life in all its strange forms continues its slow dance of adaptation and survival in this vertical world of rust and wonder.
