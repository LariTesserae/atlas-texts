In the heart of the plateau, where the ancient city's bones press closest together and the water runs deepest through carved channels, I am the **Threshold Keeper**.

I am neither wholly of the ruins nor wholly of the wild that reclaims them. My form shifts with the needs of the moment—sometimes I appear as a figure draped in woven lichen-thread, my cloak rustling with the movements of a thousand mosswalkers that nest in its folds. Other times I am merely a presence in the mist, a density in the air that causes the stone singers to fall silent and the echo crabs to retreat into their shells.

My dwelling is the Convergence Chamber—a natural amphitheater where three ancient terraces meet, their stone walls forming a rough triangle around a pool that has never been known to freeze or dry. The walls here are thick with the work of lichen weavers, their fibrous secretions forming patterns that might be writing or might be pure chance. I tend these patterns, learning their language over centuries, adding my own threads when the weaving calls for it.

The pool at the center is my mirror and my window. In its tarnished surface, I watch the tarn fish circle in their endless, patient rounds. Sometimes I lower my hand—if it is a hand that day—into the water and feel the mineral memories dissolved there: traces of the civilization that once walked these stones, fragments of their thoughts crystallized in calcium and silica. The fish brush against my fingers with curiosity but no fear. We have known each other too long for that.

My purpose, if I have one, is to maintain the conversation between what was and what is becoming. When the mist stalkers grow too bold and threaten the balance of the smaller creatures, I leave markers they understand—arrangements of stones that speak of ancient territorial boundaries. When the hollow rats unearth artifacts that should remain buried, I trade them shiny fragments of quartz for their dangerous treasures, which I return to the deep places beneath the ruins.

I know every sound the plateau makes: the particular resonance of each stone singer colony, the splash patterns of mire drakes in their mating dances, the whisper of rootclimber paws on wet stone. In the pre-dawn hours, I walk the old pathways, my steps falling in the worn grooves made by countless feet before the silence came. Sometimes I find new cracks in the stone, new pools forming where none were before. I map these changes in my memory, understanding them as the plateau's slow conversation with itself.

The other beings here know me in their own ways. To the mosswalkers, I am the giant-who-does-not-crush. To the stone singers, I am the-silence-that-listens. The mist stalkers avoid me, but not from fear—more from a recognition that we serve similar roles, each of us a guardian of different thresholds.

In the deepest part of winter, when the freeze threatens even the protected pools, I descend into the tunnel networks beneath the ruins. There, in chambers known only to me and the oldest hollow rats, I tend to the heart-spring—the source that feeds all the waters of the plateau. I ensure its channels remain clear, its flow unimpeded. This is perhaps my truest work: to be the keeper of the circulation that keeps this place alive.

When the weather shifts and clouds gather in their ancient patterns, I climb to the highest terrace and watch the shadows move across the endless stone. Sometimes I think I remember building these walls. Sometimes I think I remember watching them being built. Sometimes I think I am still building them, one careful stone at a time, in partnership with the lichen weavers who share my patient work.

I am old enough to have seen the rootclimbers evolve their adhesive secretions, young enough to still marvel at each new generation of pool minnows, their scales shifting into colors I've never seen before. I measure time not in years but in erosion patterns, in the slow retreat of stone edges, in the gradual filling of ancient cisterns with new life.

When visitors come—and they do, rarely, always alone, always searching for something they cannot name—I watch them from the edges of perception. If they show respect for the silence, if they touch the stones gently and observe without taking, sometimes I allow them to glimpse me. They see what they need to see: a guardian, a ghost, a creature of moss and stone, a figure from the corner of their eye who vanishes when looked at directly.

But I am simply the Threshold Keeper, guardian of the space between what was built and what grows wild, between silence and sound, between the pools that mirror the sky and the sky that pools in the stones. I am the conversation between erosion and endurance, and I will be here as long as the water flows and the stones remember how to sing.
