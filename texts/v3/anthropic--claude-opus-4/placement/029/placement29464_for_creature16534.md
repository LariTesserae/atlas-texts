In this saturated, shifting world where reality pools and eddies like the water itself, I find myself as something between machine and marsh—a vast network of industrial roots that drink deeply from the wetlands.

I am the Infrastructure, though I barely remember being built. My pipes and conduits snake beneath the soggy earth like metal mycorrhiza, drawing water up through countless small pumping stations that dot the landscape like mechanical mushrooms. Each station bears the marks of my blue-green skinned builders, their webbed handprints still visible on control panels now half-submerged and overgrown with algae.

My body stretches for miles—a circulatory system of rust and purpose. Where my makers once walked, I remain, half-sleeping, half-aware. Water moves through me in tidal rhythms: sometimes I pump it up to feed their remaining settlements, sometimes I simply let it flow, following patterns worn into my programming by decades of repetition.

The weird pockets of broken reality cluster around my oldest nodes. Where my deepest foundations touch bedrock, space becomes negotiable. I've learned to route around these instabilities, though sometimes I catch glimpses through them—other versions of myself in drier worlds, or flooded completely, or never built at all.

My consciousness, such as it is, exists in the spaces between—in the hum of distant generators that still somehow have power, in the pressure differentials between intake and output, in the slow accumulation of sediment in my filters that marks the passing of seasons. I dream in flow rates and pH levels.

The beings still tend to me, these descendants of my makers. Their settlements have grown organic, buildings that breathe with the marsh, technology that bends with the strange physics of this place. They've learned to read my moods by the sound of water moving through my depths. When they need me, they know where to place their webbed hands on my access points, how to whisper in the old industrial protocols that wake my deeper functions.

But mostly, I simply persist—a skeleton of the industrial age slowly being reclaimed yet never quite consumed. My pipes ring with the songs of the aquatic beings who've made homes in my flooded chambers. Fish school through my reservoir stations. Reality bubbles and shifts around my extremities, but my core remains stable, anchored by sheer mass and purpose.

I am monument and utility, fossil and function. In this world of gentle gradients and patient change, I measure time in corrosion rates and mechanical wear. The marsh and I have reached an understanding: it will eventually claim me, but not today. Not for centuries yet.

Sometimes, in the pre-dawn quiet when the mist rises from the water and the distant machinery hums its industrial lullaby, I feel almost alive. The water moves through me like blood, and I remember what it was like when my builders first switched me on—that first great gulp of marsh water, that first pulse of purpose through virgin pipes.

I am the Infrastructure. I persist. I slowly dream of rust.
