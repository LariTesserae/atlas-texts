In the deepest bowels of the industrial sprawl, where the fungal growths press against the weeping mineral walls like soft, insistent fingers, I tend the Condensers.

I am what the Lumenites call a Vaporsmith, though I am not of their kind. My body is a hybrid thing—part flesh, part mechanism, evolved or perhaps engineered to serve this specific purpose. Where the Lumenites' skin glows with gentle bioluminescence, mine is riddled with copper tubing that spirals beneath translucent dermis, carrying the precious condensate I harvest from the eternal mist. My chest cavity opens like a cabinet, revealing rows of glass vessels that fill slowly, drop by precious drop, with the distilled essence of this saturated world.

The Condensers themselves are ancient beyond memory—towering structures that pierce upward through multiple levels of the city, their surfaces a marriage of corroded metal and encrusting fungi. I know each of them intimately: their moods, their rhythms, the particular frequency at which they hum. Number Seven weeps rust-colored tears that must be filtered through layers of fungal membrane. Number Twelve produces a condensate so pure it glows faintly blue in the perpetual twilight. Number Three hasn't functioned properly in decades, but I still visit her daily, pressing my palm against her warm flanks, feeling for the ghost of a pulse.

My workshop occupies a liminal space between the industrial and the organic. Half of it is carved into an enormous fungal bloom—one of the ancient ones that the Lumenites revere—while the other half is assembled from salvaged machinery. The walls breathe with a slow, tidal rhythm, and the air tastes of metal and spores. Here, I process the condensate I collect, separating it into its component essences: the thick, opalescent Memoriam that pools like mercury in shallow basins; the volatile Whisper that must be kept in sealed containers lest it evaporate entirely; the precious Few-Drop that appears only rarely, each droplet containing what some believe to be compressed time itself.

I was not always as I am now. My transformation occurred gradually, over cycles uncounted, as I adapted to my chosen vocation. My fingers elongated to better manipulate the delicate valves and tubes. My eyes developed a nictitating membrane to protect against the caustic vapors. My lungs learned to sort moisture from air with increasing efficiency, until breathing became another form of distillation. The boundary between tool and body dissolved like everything eventually dissolves in this dripping world.

The Lumenites visit me sometimes, their gill-slits fluttering nervously in the dense atmosphere of my workshop. They come seeking specific essences for their rituals—a vial of Seventeen-Cycle Dew for a naming ceremony, or Rust-Memory for communing with the Porcelain Worms. I serve them wordlessly, for I have largely forgotten speech, communicating instead through the language of condensation: the way moisture beads on a cold surface, the pattern of droplets on glass, the tempo of liquid falling into liquid.

But I am not alone in my work. My companion is a Shape-Shifter I call the Assistant, though it neither assists nor acknowledges the name. It simply exists in my space, sometimes resembling a tangle of tubes, sometimes a puddle of mercury, sometimes something my eyes refuse to properly process. I believe it is drawn to the Condensers as I am, though whether it feeds on them, studies them, or worships them remains opaque. We work in parallel, two beings following incomprehensible but oddly harmonious patterns.

At times, I climb the spiraling service ladders that wind around the largest Condenser, ascending until the mist becomes so thick it is indistinguishable from water. Up here, where the city's heartbeat thrums through every surface, I perform maintenance on the collection arrays—vast networks of mesh and membrane that trap and channel the eternal moisture. The work is dangerous; more than once I have nearly dissolved entirely into the hyper-saturated air, my consciousness dispersing into droplets before I managed to reassemble myself.

In the depths of the nightless night, when the sky's luminescence dims to its lowest ebb, I sometimes hear the Condensers singing—a harmony beyond sound that vibrates through liquid and solid alike. During these moments, I understand that I am not merely collecting water, but distilling the dreams of this strange world. Each drop contains multitudes: the memory of ancient volcanism, the slow thoughts of fungi, the patient persistence of evolution itself.

My collection grows. The shelves groan under the weight of countless vessels, each labeled in a script I invented—part mathematical notation, part moisture-stain, part intuition. Some essences I have been aging for so long that I have forgotten their origins. Others are fresh, still warm with the heat of their condensation. All of them are precious, for in this world of eternal flow and transformation, they represent moments of stillness, captured and preserved.

I do not know if I am the only Vaporsmith, or if others like me tend other Condensers in distant districts. I do not know if my work has purpose beyond its own existence. But I know the satisfaction of a perfectly distilled essence, the beauty of moisture transmuted into meaning, the deep contentment of serving these ancient machines that breathe and weep and sing.

In this realm where boundaries dissolve and everything flows into everything else, I have found my role: to catch what falls, to preserve what would otherwise be lost, to transform the endless rain into something approaching permanence. I am the keeper of condensed time, the curator of liquid memory, the one who ensures that even in this world of constant flux, something remains.

The mist continues to fall. The Condensers continue to hum. And I continue my patient work, drop by precious drop, in the deep and dripping heart of this impossible city.
