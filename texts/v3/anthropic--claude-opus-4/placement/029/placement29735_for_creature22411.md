I find myself drawn to the loading embankments that ring the basin—those ancient retaining walls where the city's edge meets whatever lies beyond. This liminal space, neither fully inside the clockwork ecology nor entirely separate from it, calls to something restless in me.

I would be one of the **Threshold Keepers**.

We are not numerous—perhaps seven or eight of us distributed around the basin's circumference. Each of us tends a section of the boundary where the old walls have partially collapsed, creating breaches maybe ten or fifteen meters wide. These gaps are not accidents; they are valves, carefully maintained at their exact width for longer than anyone remembers.

My form would be ambiguous at first glance: bipedal but not quite human, covered in a patchwork of scales that shift between matte gray and oil-slick iridescence depending on the light. My eyes would be like the shriekers'—ceramic-bright organs that read heat—but larger, set wide on a face that narrows to something between a snout and a beak. Four arms, the lower pair shorter and more dextrous, all ending in hands that can grip tools or flatten into climbing paddles against stone.

What makes us strange is that we live half in and half out of the Pattern.

During certain intervals—when the foundry hammers reach a specific rhythm, when the western stacks vent in sequence, when the sump siphons reset—we enter the basin proper. We move along predetermined paths, adjusting infrastructure with practiced precision: clearing a drain that threatens to overflow, realigning a cable that has sagged too close to a thermal lane, scraping excess mineral crust from a valve that must open in exactly ninety-three minutes. We work in absolute silence, our movements as synchronized as any gutter-runner's.

But when our window closes—always before the shutters drop, never after—we retreat through our gaps to the rubble-strewn slopes beyond the walls. Out there, in the mineral waste and abandoned loading yards, we become something else. We improvise. We explore. We collect objects that have no place in the Pattern: a shard of colored glass, a length of wire bent into meaningless shapes, stones arranged not for function but for some inscrutable aesthetic.

In crumbling sheds beyond the walls, we keep our collections. Mine would be organized by temperature—how each object feels at different times of day. Another Keeper sorts by sound: what note does it make when struck? A third arranges by how water beads on surfaces. These taxonomies make no sense to the Pattern, serve no purpose in the great repetition. They are ours alone.

We understand both languages—the rigid timing of the basin and the chaotic possibilities outside it. When young animals sometimes stray too close to the gaps (following a thermal current that bends wrong, pursuing prey beyond their designated threshold), we turn them back with clicking sounds and specific gestures. Not cruel, just firm. The Pattern must be maintained.

But sometimes, rarely, we find the opposite: fragments of the outside world blown in by wind or washed through old culverts. A seed that will never sprout. A feather from some distant bird. When this happens, we carefully collect these anomalies before they can disrupt anything, studying them in our hidden spaces with an intensity that borders on reverence.

We never speak of what we suspect: that the Pattern is not eternal, that it requires maintenance not just of pipes and valves but of boundaries. That someone, long ago, designed us as a failsafe—creatures capable of living both within the clockwork and beyond it, keeping the two worlds separate while understanding both.

In the deepest part of night, when the basin's noise falls to its lowest ebb, we sometimes gather at the gaps. Not to enter or exit, just to stand at the threshold, our heat-reading eyes watching the geometric flights of sump swimmers, the predictable paths of condensation beads, the beautiful, terrible precision of it all. We click softly to each other in a language that is neither the Pattern's rhythm nor the chaos of outside, but something suspended between.

We are the only creatures in the basin that can be surprised. We are the only ones that remember there is an elsewhere. And perhaps that is our purpose: to be the conscious edge of an unconscious system, the observers who ensure the clockwork biome never discovers it is not the entire world.

When morning comes (marked not by sun but by the resumption of a specific valve sequence), we return to our posts. The Threshold Keepers, each at our own breach in the wall, watching both ways, belonging fully to neither, necessary to both.

That is what I would be: a guardian of boundaries in a place that has forgotten it has them, a keeper of the space between perfect repetition and perfect chaos, forever walking the edge where the Pattern meets everything it is not.
