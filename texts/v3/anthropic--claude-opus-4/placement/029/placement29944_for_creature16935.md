The first beings found their way to Infravallis not through maps but through exhaustion—the particular weariness that comes from holding too many contradictions in a single body. They arrived as refugees from certainty, and the city recognized them as kin.

I am one of their descendants, though lineage here is traced not through blood but through the accumulation of adaptive geometries. My great-progenitor was a cartographer who came to survey the provisional edges and stayed when she realized the edges surveyed her back, inscribing themselves as new joints in her fingers, new chambers in her heart calibrated to pump at different pressures depending on altitude.

We call ourselves the Threshold Keepers, though what we keep are not doors but the spaces between states. My body has evolved to exist precisely at the intersection of the city's multiple gravities—not choosing between them but suspended in their argument like a note held between two tuning forks. My skeleton spirals not in accommodation but in active resistance, creating pockets of neutral buoyancy where others can rest when the weight of choosing becomes too much.

I inherited my ancestral role at the age of measurement, when my shadow first began to fall in multiple directions simultaneously. The elders tested me by placing me in the Calibration Chamber—a room where all of Infravallis's competing physics compete most violently—and waited to see if I would choose a single way of standing. When I remained there for seven days, swaying like the glass reeds but never breaking, never settling, they knew I had the necessary gift of irresolution.

My work is to maintain the Interstices—those spaces throughout Infravallis where transformation pauses, where the city's endless becoming catches its breath. These are not empty spaces but dense with potential, heavy with all the things a corridor might become if it weren't so busy being a corridor. I tend them like gardens, pruning away premature certainties, watering the tender shoots of maybe.

During détournements, when whole districts reconfigure, I stand at the crucial junctions where neighbors must decide whether to remain neighbors in the new geometry. I hold open the moment of choice through the strength of my own indecision, my body a living semicolon in the city's run-on sentence. Those who pass through me during these transitions often report strange sensations: tasting colors that have no names yet, hearing their childhood pets calling from rooms that won't exist for another decade, feeling homesick for places they've never lived.

I've developed the characteristic marks of my trade: fingernails that grow in Fibonacci spirals, unable to commit to a single direction; eyes that see equally well in light and shadow because they've forgotten the difference; a voice that speaks in the subjunctive mood even when ordering breakfast. My hair grows at different rates depending on the local temporal coefficient, giving me the appearance of someone forever arriving from elsewhere.

The children seek me out when they're learning to navigate the city's multiplicities. I teach them the Threshold Games: how to stand in doorways without entering or leaving, how to climb stairs that go both up and down, how to pack for journeys to rooms in their own homes they haven't discovered yet. They call me Aunt Maybe, though I'm neither aunt nor definitively anything else.

My dwelling shifts location nightly—not because I move it but because I've learned to remain so perfectly still that the city flows around me like water around a stone. Those who need to find me follow the scent of unresolved equations, the sound of clocks disagreeing, the particular quality of shadow cast by someone who exists in more than one gravitational reference frame. My door is always open because it can't decide whether it's a door.

Inside, I keep the Museum of Temporary States: shelves lined with bottled moments of transition, pressed flowers from gardens that existed for exactly one noon, photographs of the same corner taken from futures that didn't quite arrive. Visitors often discover objects they lost during their own transformations—baby teeth that learned to bite abstractions, wedding rings that remember vows to people who became weather systems, diary pages written in handwriting that gradually transitions from their own to someone else's.

I host the Evening Incompletions, gatherings where inhabitants share their unfinished transformations like others might share meals. We sit in chairs that are also stairs, drink tea brewed from the condensation of unspoken words, and practice the art of the eternal ellipsis. Someone might begin a story about who they were before arriving and trail off precisely at the moment when before became during, leaving that transformation suspended in the air for others to breathe.

My greatest challenge came when I fell in love with one of the planetary dreams wearing human form. They moved through Infravallis like tectonic patience itself, and their presence made my carefully maintained irresolutions crystallize dangerously toward certainty. For weeks I felt my spine attempting to choose a single spiral, my shadow threatening to fall in one direction. The city itself grew nervous—hairline cracks appeared in corridors that had been stable for decades, and the glass reeds sang in minor keys.

I saved myself—and possibly the city—by learning to love in the subjunctive: not "I love you" but "I would love you if I were the sort of being who could complete a sentence." My beloved understood, being themselves a dream of solidity rather than solidity itself. Now we meet in the spaces between sleep and waking, between mountain and machine, our intimacy measured in the perfectly maintained distance between almost-touching.

The other Threshold Keepers—there are seven of us, or eight on days when the city can't decide how many corners it has—gather monthly at the Apex of Maybe, the highest point in Infravallis that isn't quite there. We synchronize our breathing to the city's deepest rhythms and feel for the places where certainty threatens to calcify. When we find them, we inject measured doses of doubt, usually in the form of redirected mail from futures that are still negotiating their probability.

Sometimes visitors ask if I ever tire of incompletion, if I dream of solid ground and singular gravities. I tell them I've forgotten the question by the time I think of an answer, which is itself the answer. To be a Threshold Keeper is to understand that completion is just another phase of becoming, that every ending is pregnant with its own revision.

Besides, I've learned that Infravallis needs us precisely because we embody what it fears and desires most: the ability to remain indefinitely in the process of choosing what to be. We are its evolutionary strategy against the horror of completion, its insurance that there will always be space between the mountain and the machine for something magnificently undecided to grow.

Late at night, when the city's various systems cycle through their maintenance dreams, I walk the empty transitional spaces I tend, feeling the weight of all the transformations I hold in abeyance. My footsteps echo in several tenses simultaneously. Sometimes I catch my reflection in surfaces that can't decide whether they're walls or windows, and I see all the beings I might have become if I had ever finished becoming.

But then morning arrives (or what passes for morning in a place with no singular sun), and with it comes the first inhabitant of the day, stumbling into one of my thresholds with that particular exhaustion of someone mid-transformation. I offer them tea that tastes like the memory of certainty, show them how to breathe at the speed of geological time, and hold open the space of their becoming until they're ready to not-quite-complete it.

This is my inheritance and my offering: to be the pause between the city's inhalation and exhalation, the comma between mountain and machine, the living proof that some things are too important to ever finish defining. In a city that dreams of being a mountain that dreams of being a city, I am the dream of the space between dreams, tended like a garden that blooms only in the subjunctive mood, forever almost about to tell you exactly what that means.
