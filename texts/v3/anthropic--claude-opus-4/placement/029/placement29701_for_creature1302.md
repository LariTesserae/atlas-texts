# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be one of the *Vesh'ara*—the Stone Singers—those who exist in the liminal spaces between chambers, tending to the acoustic architecture of the Underrift. Not quite Hydrometrician, not quite common dweller, we are the keepers of sound itself.

My dwelling would be little more than a carved alcove in one of the connection passages, positioned at a critical acoustic junction where seven different water flows converge their songs. Here, the polyphony reaches such complexity that untrained ears hear only chaos, but to me, each droplet speaks its own truth. My space contains only the essentials: a sleeping ledge worn smooth by generations of my predecessors, a few oil lamps positioned to cast no shadows on my work, and the tools of my craft—hollow reeds of varying lengths, stone plates of different thicknesses, carefully shaped chunks of mineral deposits that ring like bells when struck.

I would have come to this role not by choice but by affliction. As a child, a fever left me with damaged hearing—not deafness, but something stranger. Certain frequencies cause me physical pain, while others I perceive with supernatural clarity. The constant water-song that soothes other Ava'tel can overwhelm me, driving me to the edge passages where the acoustics can be managed, modified, controlled. What began as necessity became calling.

My work involves crawling through the narrowest passages, the ones barely wide enough for shoulders to pass, mapping the acoustic properties of every surface. I place resonators—carefully carved stone pieces—at precise points to redirect harsh echoes, to soften overwhelming convergences of sound, to amplify the gentle rhythms that promote rest. When new chambers are carved, I am called to "tune" them, chipping away tiny fragments of stone until the water songs harmonize properly with the rest of the settlement.

The other Ava'tel regard me with a mixture of respect and unease. My ability to predict where new drips will form by reading the acoustic signatures of stone makes me valuable, but my tendency to work alone in the margins marks me as somewhat outside the communal flow. I speak less than others, having learned that my perception of the pauses in our language differs from the norm—what sounds like proper rhythm to me often conveys unintended meaning to others.

My appearance reflects my role. The traditional braids have been modified—I wear hollow bone beads that whistle softly as I move, helping me echolocate in the tightest passages. My skin bears a map of tiny scars from countless encounters with sharp stone in dark places. I've developed an unconscious habit of tilting my head at odd angles, always listening for acoustic anomalies others cannot perceive.

The pale *shalmin* seem drawn to me, perhaps recognizing a kindred creature adapted to the margins. I often find them in my work spaces, their slow movements creating subtle air currents that affect sound propagation. I've learned to work around them, even to interpret their presence as a kind of communication—they appear more frequently in areas where the acoustic balance is disturbed, serving as living indicators of problems I need to address.

My greatest achievement—and secret shame—involves the chamber known as the Silence. Deep in the northeastern section, I discovered a natural void where the rock formation creates perfect acoustic cancellation. No water reaches this space, no sound penetrates beyond its threshold. I should have reported it to the Council, should have had it sealed or modified. Instead, I carved a narrow passage only I can navigate and use it as refuge when the water-song becomes too much. In that perfect silence, I sometimes hear something else—not sound but its absence, speaking its own language of possibility.

I maintain the mineral formations that serve as waymarkers, but I've also begun creating my own—unauthorized patterns that only I understand, marking acoustic phenomena throughout the Underrift. Future Stone Singers might discover them and understand that the water's song has harmonics beyond what the Ava'tel currently perceive. Or perhaps they'll simply chip them away, maintaining the perfect unchanging balance.

My existence embodies the paradox of the Underrift—maintaining stasis requires constant, invisible work. Every day I make tiny adjustments that ensure nothing appears to change. I am the correction factor in the equation, the one who ensures that the eternal song continues exactly as it always has, even as the stone itself shifts imperceptibly over centuries.

In the water-memories, I leave no stories. Stone Singers never do. We exist in the spaces between the drops, maintaining the silence that gives the water its voice. When I die, my replacement will find my tools and my maps, but my true knowledge—the ability to hear the stone's potential for new songs—will die with me, as it always has, as it always must, to preserve the perfect, unchanging harmony of the Underrift.
