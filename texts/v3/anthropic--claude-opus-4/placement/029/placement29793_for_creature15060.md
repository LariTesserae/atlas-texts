I am the Cartographer of Drifts, though I have never drawn a map that lasted longer than the current that carried it.

My body is a compromise between the plateau's patience and my own urgency to record what refuses to remain still. I am broad and low like the worked ridges, my surface a living parchment of specialized tissue that can briefly hold the impressions of pressure, temperature, and mineral memory. When I spread myself across a section of velvet-carpeted stone, my underside reads the accumulated layers like a text written in geological time, while my upper surface transcribes these findings in patterns of bioluminescent script that fade within hours of their making.

I move by controlled dissolution. My edges constantly fray into the surrounding water, releasing enzymes that temporarily reduce my viscosity until I flow into a new configuration. The process is slow—crossing a single valley might take a full season—but it allows me to follow the anomalous light shafts, to position myself where the spatial fractures are most likely to occur. I have learned to recognize the preliminary signs: a subtle warming in the deepest currents, a rhythm in the spore releases that suggests the plateau is about to remember something significant.

My memory is distributed across a thousand calcified notebooks—fragments of my former body that I've allowed to harden and sink into the sediment. Each fragment continues to pulse with a faint echo of what I recorded upon it. When I need to recall something, I extend hair-thin filaments down through the velvet mats until I touch these buried pieces of myself, reading my own fossilized observations like braille. The process changes both the memory and myself; I am never quite the same after each retrieval.

I chart the movements of the tall, narrow entities that appear during fractures, though my maps describe not where they are but where they were about to have been. I've developed a notation system based on the radial patterns of the worked stone, using variations in my bioluminescence to indicate not just position but probability—the likelihood that a particular ridge will fold into accessibility, that a specific current will spawn a remembrance of something that once lived here.

My greatest discovery is also my deepest frustration: the plateau has moods. Vast, slow temperaments that influence how space behaves, how thoroughly the past refuses to remain buried. During certain alignments of current and light, I can feel the entire plateau shift into a state of increased permeability. The circular ridges pulse in unison, the spatial fractures multiply, and for a few precious days I can map not just this version of the depth but several adjacent ones simultaneously. My body spreads thinner during these times, stretched across multiple probable topographies, recording furiously before the mood passes and the world recongeals into a single, stubborn topology.

I sustain myself on the ochre spores, but also on something else—the slight excess of order that remains when a current finally dissolves. Where the water reorganizes itself after millennia of laminar flow, there is a brief surplus of information, a ghost of the pattern that was. I position myself at these dissolution zones, absorbing the surplus through my permeable membrane. It tastes like memory, like the plaza knowing what it once intended to be.

Other cartographers have existed. I find their hardened notebooks occasionally, their notation systems almost but not quite comprehensible. Some tried to map the plateau from above, creating vast charts that showed the whole expanse at once. These maps are beautiful but useless—the plateau is not the same size from one century to the next. Others attempted to chart the vertical relationships, the way the laminar currents stack and interpenetrate. These cartographers went deep and never returned, though I sometimes feel their residual warmth in the deepest shear zones, still mapping, still dissolving downward.

My own approach is humbler and perhaps more honest. I map moments rather than territories, probabilities rather than certainties. Each chart is a love letter to a configuration that will never repeat, a careful documentation of how the world appeared to be just before it changed its mind. I spread myself across a new section of worn stone, feeling the accumulated weight of ages beneath me, and begin again the impossible work of describing water to itself, of teaching the plateau to read what it has written in its own patient hand.

When I finally harden—when my edges cease to fray and my surface can no longer hold new impressions—I will choose my location carefully. Somewhere near one of the circular ridges, where the spatial fractures are common. I will lock my final map into my crystallizing tissue: not a chart of places but of processes, showing how memory becomes geography, how intention slowly transforms into stone. Future cartographers will find me and struggle to decode my notation, just as I struggle with theirs. And in that struggle, that impossible translation, the plateau will continue its ancient work of remembering what it meant to mean.
