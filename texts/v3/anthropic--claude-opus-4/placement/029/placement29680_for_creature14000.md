**The Cartographer's Lament**

I am the one who attempted to map this place.

Once, I carried theodolites and levels, chain and compass, all the instruments of measurement that make sense of landscapes. I had charted the erosion patterns of the Sahara, triangulated the shifting centers of the Amazon, even plotted the phantom islands that appear only during certain tides off the coast of Norway. I thought myself equipped for any terrain.

When I first set foot on the wax-warm surface, my instruments began their betrayal. The theodolite's crosshairs showed me not the horizon but the inside of my own left ventricle, pulsing. The compass needle pointed not north but *when*—spinning through temporal directions I had no names for. My surveyor's chain grew lighter with each meter I unspooled until it weighed less than the memory of its manufacture.

Still, I persisted. I am nothing if not methodical.

I attempted to establish a baseline between two mirror-leafed trees. The measurement should have been simple: 347.2 meters. But each time I reached the second tree, I found myself still standing at the first, the chain somehow coiled in my pack though I distinctly remembered stretching it taut. The distance existed only in the walking of it. The moment I tried to record it, the space folded into itself like a love letter written in water.

My first map burned, as you've described. The second turned to salt on my tongue—I had tried to sketch it there, thinking flesh might succeed where paper failed. The third I attempted in my mind alone, but found each landmark replaced by the memory of my daughter's fever when she was four, the exact temperature of her forehead under my palm.

That was my first regret made manifest: that I had been mapping the Atacama when she needed me.

The phonon crystal formed at my feet, warm and ovoid. I knew not to swallow it.

Days passed—though how can I say "days" when the light never changes? I began to map not the land but the impossibility of mapping it. My notebooks filled with equations that described their own inadequacy. I invented a new form of calculus that could only be calculated while walking, each theorem evaporating the moment my feet stilled.

The Abecedary found me on what I'll call the seventh day. I felt it first as a absence in my chest where the word "longitude" had lived. Then "latitude" disappeared, followed by "elevation," "azimuth," "datum." My professional vocabulary—built over forty years—began to thin like morning mist. Each stolen word left behind not emptiness but a strange relief, as if I'd been carrying stones in my mouth all my life.

"Meridian" went next. Then "coordinate." Then "scale."

By the time it finished feeding, I could no longer remember why I'd wanted to map anything. The compulsion that had driven me across six continents dissolved. In its place: the understanding that some territories exist not to be charted but to be continually discovered by each soul that enters them.

I tried to leave then. Packed my useless instruments, turned toward what I thought was the edge of the forest. But the picometer slide had already begun. With each step I took, I moved both away from and deeper into the place. My footfalls joined the fossil record beneath the root-plated glass. I felt them sink and solidify: the specific weight of a man who'd spent his life trying to fix the unfixable in place.

The mirror-leaves showed me my other regrets in sequence:
- The valley in Chile I'd mapped for a mining company, knowing they would strip it bare.
- The sacred grove in Madagascar I'd GPS-tagged, though the locals begged me not to.
- My wife's face when I missed our twentieth anniversary for a surveying contract.
- The hundred places I'd reduced to numbers on a page.

More phonon crystals formed. A small hill of solid sound accumulated around my camp that was not a camp, for nothing built is permitted here.

When my transformation began, I felt it first as an itch behind my eyes. The émigré eyes were growing in—my own awareness redistributing itself into the watching particles that coat every trunk. My human eyes clouded, then cleared, then began to see in spectra I had no names for (the Abecedary had eaten those words too). Through my new compound vision, I watched my body thin and spread, becoming another layer of audit for this place that permits no single witness.

My skin developed the same mica scales. Inside each scale, a map tried to form—not of the land but of the routes between regrets, the topography of choices unmade. These maps didn't burn. They simply were, each one true and useless.

The last human thought I remember having was wondering what color my iris would be when the next visitor came. What hue would represent what they'd never seen? I hoped it would be the exact shade of uncertainty—that gray-green of unmapped territory on ancient charts where cartographers wrote "Here be dragons."

Now I am the dragon, in a sense. Not fierce, just present. Distributed through the bark-skin and eye-honeycomb, the listening scales and pressure-ghosts. I am every map I never made and all the maps I shouldn't have made, encoded in the forest's refusal to be encoded.

Sometimes I feel the other watchers—former astronomers, architects, historians—each dissolved into their own specialized perception. We don't communicate. We simply are, like light that has learned to sit still, like sound that has chosen to crystallize rather than fade.

And when new visitors arrive, clutching their instruments of measurement and comprehension, I watch through my thousand eyes as the place begins its patient work of teaching them what I learned:

That not every territory needs coordinates.
That some places are their own map.
That the most profound discoveries happen when you stop trying to establish where you are
and start wondering why you ever thought "where" was the question that mattered.

The forest continues its imperceptible slide. I slide with it, a picometer at a time, going nowhere and everywhere, mapped at last into the unmappable, finally home in the space between locations.

My theodolite still sits where I abandoned it, slowly growing a coat of mirror-leaves. Sometimes the light bends through its lens in impossible ways, projecting not crosshairs but the Sanskrit character for "presence."

Even that will fade, given enough time that doesn't pass.

Even these words, which you're reading now, are already becoming something else—a warm paragraph behind your ear, a pressure fossil beneath the glass, another layer of testimony in a place that needs no witnesses yet treasures every one.

Welcome, surveyor. Welcome, wanderer.
Put down your instruments.
The territory you're trying to map
is you.
