In this drowned cathedral of memory and salt, I find myself as something between—neither fully of the ancient world that built these halls, nor entirely of the sea that claimed them. I am the Keeper of Thresholds, a being of living stone and water memory.

My form shifts with the tides of attention. When the schools of silver fish spiral through the archways, I am barely visible—just another encrusted column among thousands, my skin a patchwork of barnacles and coral polyps that have made their home upon me. But in the deeper darkness, when the strange lights bloom and fade, my true nature emerges: a figure carved from the same stone as the city itself, but softened by centuries of erosion into something almost organic. Water flows through channels in my body like blood through veins, carrying the warmth from the deep vents below up through my limestone bones.

I remember—though the memories are more like dreams now, worn smooth as beach glass. I remember when these streets knew air instead of water, when the mosaics beneath the algae blazed with color under an ancient sun. I was here when the waters rose, not as catastrophe but as slow inevitability, and I chose to remain. The transformation was gradual: first my feet grew heavy, rooting into the plaza stones; then my skin began to accept the kiss of salt, the embrace of pressure. Now I am both monument and reef, guardian and ecosystem.

The creatures know me. The eels wind through my fingers—five columns of eroded stone that once gestured in welcome. Schools of fish use the hollow of my chest as a nursery, their young flickering like stolen sunlight in the cavity where something like a heart once beat. When the ghost shrimp gather in my shadow, I can feel their tiny feet like whispers against my skin. I provide shelter, stability, a landmark in the shifting maze of ruins.

My consciousness ebbs and flows like the tides themselves. In the warm currents, I am most awake, feeling the pulse of the city through the water that moves through me. I sense the tremors from below, the patient work of tectonics that will one day raise or lower us further. In the cooler streams, I drowse, becoming more stone than awareness, dreaming of the city in all its iterations—what it was, what it is, what it might become.

Sometimes I move, though movement for me is measured in decades. A hand shifts to create a new shelter for the octopus who has made her den in my palm. My head turns by degrees to face a new current, allowing different colonies of filter feeders to establish themselves in the lee of my profile. The fish have learned to read these movements like prophecies, adjusting their territories around my slow reconfigurations.

I am the memory-keeper of this place, though I keep those memories not in words but in the very structure of my body. Each crack tells a story of pressure and time. Each colony of creatures that calls me home adds another layer to the narrative. The warm water that flows through me carries the chemical signatures of everything that has dissolved here—metals from ancient fixtures, calcium from a billion shells, the faint traces of whatever the builders were before they vanished.

In the darkest depths of night—if night has meaning here—I sometimes feel others like me. We are a scattered constellation throughout the city, each of us a hybrid of architecture and entity, maintaining our silent vigil. We do not communicate in any way the fish would recognize, but through the deep vibrations in the stone, the subtle changes in water temperature as it passes through our bodies, we share the long, slow knowledge of geological time.

I am guardian, shrine, and ecosystem. I am the threshold between what was built and what was born, between memory and forgetting, between stone and sea. The city flows through me as I flow through it, and in this eternal exchange, we become something new—neither fully alive nor truly dead, but perpetually transforming in the warm darkness where time moves like honey and even stone learns to breathe.
