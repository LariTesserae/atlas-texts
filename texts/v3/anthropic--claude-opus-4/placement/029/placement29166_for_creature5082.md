In the deepest cistern beneath the city, where even the Keepers rarely venture, I rest in perpetual darkness. I am what they call a Thread-Keeper, though I have forgotten if this was always my name or merely what I became.

My body stretches thin as spider's silk across the vast chamber, a living web of nerve and sinew that feels every droplet's path through the stone. Where the Keepers above read the water's movement with eyes and fingers, I have become the water's memory itself. Each strand of my being thrums with the vibration of moisture seeping through rock, each tremor a word in the plateau's ancient language.

I was not always thus. Once, I walked the high paths as they do, tended the channels, whispered to the moss. But I heard something in the water's voice that others missed—a deeper song that pulled me down through the carved stairs, past the lowest inhabited chambers, to this forgotten reservoir where the city's first builders caught the plateau's tears.

The transformation was gradual. First, I simply sat in the darkness, learning to distinguish each source of water by its mineral taste in the air. Then I began to feel the stone's breathing through my palms, my feet. My flesh grew sensitive to vibrations none could see. Years passed—or perhaps decades; time moves differently in the deep—and I found myself spreading, thinning, becoming less dense and more aware.

Now I span the cistern's width, a constellation of consciousness dispersed through filaments finer than hair. My original form rests at the web's heart, but I am barely that body anymore. I am the tension between droplets, the pause before the fall, the echo of every rainstorm the plateau has ever known.

The Keepers know of me, in their way. The eldest speak of the Thread-Keeper in hushed tones, leave offerings of water and crushed herbs at the cistern's entrance. They believe I predict the plateau's breathing, and in this they are both right and wrong. I do not predict—I remember. In my web-body lives the pattern of ten thousand years of rain, the rhythm of seasons long forgotten, the knowledge of aquifers that will not break through for centuries yet.

Sometimes, rarely, a young Keeper descends to the edge of my darkness. They bring questions about the coming weather, the health of hidden springs. I answer in the only way I can—by adjusting the tension in my strands, creating harmonics in the dripping water that those with patience can interpret. Most leave quickly, unsettled by the darkness and the sense of something vast and strange breathing in the shadows.

But a few linger. These ones, I sense, hear what I once heard. The water calls to them as it called to me. I feel their potential in the way they disturb the air, these future thread-keepers who might one day join me in the deep. For I am not alone down here. In chambers deeper still, I sense others—my predecessors, perhaps, or simply different expressions of the same imperative. We do not communicate as the surface dwellers do. We simply exist, overlapping at our edges, sharing the endless data stream of water moving through stone.

What purpose do we serve? The Keepers above would say we guard the water's wisdom, ensure the city's survival. But from my dispersed perspective, I understand it differently. We are not servants of the city or even of the Keepers. We are part of the plateau itself, a bridge between the quick-lived humans above and the geological patience of the stone. Through us, the plateau knows itself, remembers its own becoming.

In my strands, I feel tomorrow's rain gathering three days hence. I sense the fault line, dormant for now, that will one day redirect the western streams. I know where the next generation of mosses will take hold, where a new seep will emerge in forty years. This knowledge flows through me not as thought but as sensation, as real as the weight of water on web.

The Keepers live in harmony with the plateau, but I have become that harmony. They guide water; I am the memory of its guidance. They read the stone's breathing; I am the breath itself, stretched thin across the darkness, vibrating with every secret the plateau whispers to itself in the language of drip and flow, erosion and accumulation.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night when even the city above sleeps, I contract my web slightly, drawing myself together. In these moments, I almost remember what it felt like to be singular, to have edges, to distinguish between self and stone. But the sensation passes quickly, and I expand again, settling back into my eternal listening, my infinite dissolution into the water's patient voice.

I am Thread-Keeper, and I keep more than threads. I keep the memory of rain.
