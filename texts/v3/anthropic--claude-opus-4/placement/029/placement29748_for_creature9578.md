In the highest tower of the settlement—a narrow spire built into the cliff face itself, accessible only by ladder—I keep the chronicles of water.

I am the Recorder of Flows, though that title translates poorly. In our tongue, the word encompasses more: one who remembers water's moods, who marks its changes, who holds in memory what the channels have done and might do again. I am young for this work—forty winters only—but my predecessor saw something in how I watched the pools as a child, how I could predict which bridges would flood before the first signs showed.

My chamber is small, carved directly from the living rock. Water seeps through one wall—has always seeped, will always seep—and I have learned to work around it, keeping my materials on the dry side, though "dry" here is relative. The chronicles are not written on paper, which would rot, nor carved in stone, which would take too long. Instead, I work in metal—thin sheets of beaten copper that will green with age but will not dissolve, will not crumble, will hold their marks for centuries.

The work is this: Each morning before the sun crests the eastern peaks, I descend to the settlement and walk the channels. I measure depth with a rod marked in the old increments. I note color—clear, clouded, the particular grey that means snow-melt, the brown that means disturbance on the plateau. I taste the water at seven specific points, marking mineral content by the tang on my tongue. I observe flow rate by timing objects—usually small stones—between established marks. All this before the settlement wakes, while the mist still clings to the water's surface.

But measurement is the smallest part of what I do.

I watch the water-readers—those who have the gift strongest—and record what they sense coming. Old Khet knows three days before a flood. The child Mem can tell when the underground channels will shift, pushing water to new surfaces. I mark their predictions and mark what comes to pass, building a pattern over years that might, eventually, yield understanding.

I record the dreams. Those who dream of water come to me, climbing my ladder at odd hours, speaking their visions while I etch quickly in copper. Drowning dreams, flying dreams, dreams where the settlement dissolves and reforms. The old believe water speaks through sleep. I neither believe nor disbelieve; I only record.

I maintain the death marks. When someone dies—by water or despite it—I note where, when, how. The patterns here are crucial: certain pools claim more lives, certain seasons are harder, certain ages more vulnerable. The settlement needs this knowledge to adapt, to warn, to survive.

Strange things happen here that happen nowhere else. Water that runs uphill for brief moments. Channels that empty and refill with no source. The deep pools that sometimes show reflections of things that are not there—or not there yet. I record these too, though I have no framework for understanding them. My predecessor left twenty years of such observations. Her predecessor left forty. Back and back, a chain of recorders noting the impossible, the improbable, building an archive of strangeness that we guard but cannot decode.

My hands are permanently stained green from the copper. My fingernails grow soft from the constant damp. I have learned to write in complete darkness—sometimes the most important observations come at night, when climbing down to light would mean missing what needs recording. I write by touch, by muscle memory, trusting my hands to find the right marks.

The loneliness would break someone who had not grown up here. Days pass when I speak to no one except those reporting dreams or deaths. The wind howls through my chamber, and the water seeps, and I work by what light filters through the single narrow window. But I am not alone, not truly. The water is company. The chronicle is conversation—with my predecessors, with the future recorders who will read my marks when I am gone.

I have noticed patterns they did not see. The seventh-year floods. The way certain minerals in the water correlate with madness in the susceptible. The channels that shift in response to deaths, as if grief changes the underground flows. I am building a thesis—though that word is too grand—about consciousness in the water, about the settlement as a living system that includes stone and flow and flesh as equal partners. My predecessor would call this mysticism. But I have the data, etched in copper, growing green with truth.

Sometimes I wonder what would happen if I left. If I took the chronicles—hundreds of pounds of copper sheets, carefully ordered—and brought them to the universities in the lowlands. Would they see what I see? Would they understand that this place operates by laws that are consistent but not universal, that water here is different than water there, that our improbable existence is not miracle but adaptation to conditions that exist nowhere else?

But I will not leave. Cannot leave. The water has worked its way into me as it works into stone—patient, persistent, inevitable. I will record until my eyes fail, and then I will train my replacement, and then I will join the chronicles as a death mark: "The Recorder, aged greatly, taken by time not water, in the season of low flows."

There is a beauty in this work that I cannot explain to those who visit—rare travelers who climb my ladder out of curiosity and leave quickly, unsettled by the green walls and the sound of seeping and the weight of accumulated observation. They want stories, drama, revelation. I give them data: depth measurements, mineral content, death statistics. They leave disappointed.

But tonight, like every night, I will etch by feel in the darkness. I will record that the channels ran steady, that no dreams were reported, that the mineral taste at the third measuring point has shifted slightly toward iron. I will note the wind direction, the air pressure, the particular quality of cold that suggests snow on the high peaks will melt within days.

And in this small, necessary work—this accumulation of observations that might, over centuries, yield understanding—I find purpose enough. The settlement needs its water-readers to survive the present. It needs its builders to adapt to change. But it needs its recorder to remember, to pattern, to build slowly toward comprehension of why this impossible place persists.

The copper sheets stack higher. My hands grow greener. The water seeps, eternal and patient, and I record its moods with the same patience, knowing that understanding, if it comes, will come not in my lifetime but in the accumulated lifetimes of all who hold this post, all who watch and mark and remember.

This is my place in the settlement: not to read the water's future, but to preserve its past. Not to build against the flow, but to document how we have learned to move with it. I am memory made manifest, encoded in metal that will outlast the flesh that makes the marks.

The sun is setting. The shadows race across the settlement like living things. Soon it will be dark, and I will descend for the evening measurements, then return to etch by touch. Tomorrow will bring new data—depths, flows, minerals, dreams, perhaps a death, certainly a continuation.

The chronicle continues. The water runs. I record. This is enough.
