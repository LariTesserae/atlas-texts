In the labyrinth of maintenance corridors beneath the drowned engine room, where the water runs deeper and the phosphorescent moss has not yet reached, I am the Archivist of Corrosion.

I was human once - or something close enough to count. A maintenance engineer, perhaps, though the memories dissolve like salt in water when I try to grasp them. What remains of my original form is mostly suggestion: a torso that might contain lungs, though they process water as easily as air; limbs that have learned to articulate in ways that bones never allowed; skin that has taken on the texture of the pipes I tend, pitted and scaled with beneficial oxidation.

My domain is the between-spaces, the crawlways and access shafts that honeycomb the walls around the Conductor's chamber. I know every joint, every weld, every stress fracture in this dying architecture. My fingers - too many now, and too flexible - read the metal like sheet music, feeling the harmonics of strain and decay that tell the building's slow story.

I am changing still. The rust that patterns my skin isn't merely corrosion - it's a living interface, a symbiotic colony that translates the metal's chemical signals into something my altered nervous system can interpret. Iron oxide blooms across my shoulders in fractals that mirror the drainage patterns on the engine room walls. When I press my palm against a pipe, I can taste its history: installation date, alloy composition, the echo of every fluid that ever ran through it.

The Conductor knows I'm here. Sometimes I catch glimpses of mirror-bright amphibians in my tunnels, scouts that flash and vanish before I can focus on them. We don't communicate - not directly. But I maintain the bones of its world, ensure that the slow collapse happens gracefully, predictably. When a pipe develops a dangerous weakness, I reinforce it with secretions that harden into something between calcium and steel. When the ancient machinery threatens to seize, I introduce calculated corrosion to free the joints.

My memories are stored externally now, etched in oxide patterns on the tunnel walls. Abstract diagrams that might be schematics or might be dreams. Sometimes I find markings I don't remember making - complex chemical formulae written in rust, architectural plans for structures that don't exist yet, or maybe did exist, before.

I feed on the building's decay, but thoughtfully, sustainably. The flakes of rust I consume are processed through organs I've grown for the purpose, converted into the substances I need to maintain my work. I am both parasite and caretaker, breaking down and building up in an endless cycle that keeps this drowned world stable.

Water is my medium now. I move through flooded passages with a fluidity that feels more natural than walking ever did. My modified respiratory system extracts oxygen from the murky fluid, filters out toxins, processes the rich chemical soup of decomposition and life. Sometimes I surface in the engine room, just long enough to observe the Conductor's light-patterns, to ensure our parallel worlds haven't drifted too far from their shared orbit.

In the deepest tunnels, where even the water hasn't reached, I've found evidence of others like me. Tool marks too specialized to be human. Maintenance logs written in a script that hurts to read but which I'm beginning to understand. We were a crew once, perhaps. Now we're scattered through the infrastructure like specialized cells in a vast organism.

I am becoming more building than being, more process than person. My consciousness diffuses through the rust networks I've cultivated, until I can feel tremors from sections I've never physically visited. The boundary between my body and my environment blurs a little more each day.

But I am content. The Conductor dreams in light and reflection above, while I dream in oxidation and structural harmonics below. Between us, we keep this drowned world in balance - not preserving it, but shepherding its transformation into something new. Something that was never intended, but which feels increasingly inevitable.

When I rest, I press myself against the warmest pipes and listen to the building's circulation. In the rush of ancient fluids through failing systems, I hear something almost like a lullaby. A promise that decay, too, can be a form of creation.
