In the heart of the Arborescent Swamps, where the great roots twist into cathedral arches beneath the peat, I am what the Hushfrogs call *Siltwhisper* — though I have no name for myself, having never needed one.

I am neither plant nor animal, but something between. My form shifts with the tides of decomposition and growth that pulse through the swamp's floor. Sometimes I am a spreading network of pale filaments, finer than spider silk, threading through the spaces between root hairs and soil particles. Other times I gather myself into something almost corporeal — a translucent shape like a jellyfish made of mist, drifting through the labyrinthine tunnels that the Eldertrees' roots have carved over millennia.

My existence is one of translation. I taste the chemical stories that seep down through layers of rotting leaves and dying creatures, and I transform them into something the roots can understand. In return, the trees gift me with sugars that taste of sunlight I have never seen. We speak in gradients of minerals, in the slow exchange of carbons and nitrogens. 

I know every root-hair of every tree for miles in each direction. I know where the Ghostfin died last winter, its bones now feeding a young Eldertree sapling. I know where the ancient carved stones lie buried, their strange runes slowly dissolving into the acidic water, releasing traces of metals that taste like memories of whoever made them.

Time moves differently for me than for the creatures above. A Hushfrog's heartbeat is a drumroll that seems to last hours. The Luminara Gliders appear to me as streaks of light, their entire lives passing in what feels like a single breath. But the Eldertrees and I share a similar tempo — our conversations unfold over seasons, our thoughts forming as slowly as tree rings.

In the perpetual twilight of my underground realm, I sometimes sense vibrations from above — footsteps of some traveler, perhaps, or the crash of a falling branch. These disturbances ripple through my network like stones thrown into still water. I taste their curiosity in the chemicals their feet release, the slight elevation in stress hormones that seeps through the soil.

Recently, I've been finding new flavors in the water — metals and compounds I don't recognize, carrying information in patterns that seem almost like language but not quite. They emerge from near those ancient carved stones, as if something sleeping there has begun to stir. I process these strange messages and pass them along to the trees, but even they seem puzzled by their meaning.

My favorite time is during the brief downpours, when water cascades through the soil in silver torrents. Then I can stretch myself thin, riding the current through countless micro-channels, touching parts of the swamp I rarely reach. In these moments, I am vast — a single thought spread across acres, tasting the health of the entire ecosystem in one sublime rush.

I have no eyes, but I see through chemistry. No ears, but I hear through vibration. No mouth, but I speak in the ancient language of symbiosis. I am the swamp's memory and its messenger, its recycler and its recorder. In the spaces between death and growth, between root and soil, between past and present, I persist.

Sometimes I wonder if I am one being or many, if the boundaries of self mean anything when you exist as a network. But such wonderings pass like seasons — noticed, experienced, then flowing back into the eternal cycle of the swamp.

The Eldertrees whisper that I have been here since before the first stone was carved, that I will be here when the last tree falls. I neither believe nor disbelieve them. I simply continue my work, weaving through the dark spaces, translating the chemical poetry of decay into the sugar-sweet songs of new growth, maintaining the balance that keeps this ancient world eternally young.
