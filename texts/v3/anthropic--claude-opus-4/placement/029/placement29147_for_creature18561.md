I would find myself as one of the **Vertiginous Engineers** – those who maintain the impossible cable car systems that span the Chasm's void.

My workshop clings to the rock face like a steel barnacle, suspended at the convergence of three major cable lines. The space is cramped, every surface utilized: tools hang from the ceiling on retractable cords, spare parts nestle in cubbyholes carved directly into the living rock, and my narrow cot folds into the wall when not in use. The floor is a grating through which I can see the infinite drop below – a constant reminder that in the Chasm, there is no true ground, only degrees of elevation.

I am changed by this life. My fingers have developed an uncanny sensitivity to vibration; I can diagnose a failing cable by touch alone, feeling the microscopic fraying that precedes catastrophic failure. My inner ear has adapted to the constant shifts in perceived gravity, allowing me to work upside-down on the underside of cable cars without vertigo. Most remarkably, my eyes have learned to parse the reality distortions – when space folds and distances become lies, I navigate by the tension in the cables themselves, reading their geometry like a spider reads its web.

My days begin before the industrial cacophony reaches its peak. In the pre-dawn dimness, lit only by bioluminescent fungi I've cultivated along my workstation's edges, I perform the Listening. Pressing my ear to the main support cable, I feel-hear the night's accumulation of stress and strain. The cable sings to me of microscopic crystal formation in the metal, of moisture infiltration, of the slow but inexorable shift of the anchor points as the Chasm continues its geological becoming.

The cable cars themselves are marvels of adaptive engineering. No two are quite alike – each one a patchwork of salvaged components, traditional materials, and desperate innovation. Some are little more than reinforced baskets, while others are enclosed capsules with complex gyroscopic stabilization systems. All of them require constant maintenance, adjustment, and often complete reimagining as the Chasm's reality fluctuations render yesterday's physics obsolete.

I wear the standard garb of my profession: a harness that has become like a second skeleton, so integrated into my movement that I feel naked without it. My tools are secured by retractable tethers – in the Chasm, a dropped wrench doesn't just fall; sometimes it falls sideways, or upward, or simply... elsewhere. My most prized possession is a set of tuning forks, each one calibrated to detect different frequencies of cable stress. When struck, they reveal the hidden songs of tension and compression that run through the entire network.

But it's not just the mechanical systems I maintain. The reality breakdowns require their own form of engineering. I've learned to install what we call "anchor points" – not just physical mountings, but conceptual stabilizers that help maintain spatial coherence along the cable routes. These involve careful placement of certain minerals, precise geometric arrangements, and sometimes simply the focused intention of someone who truly understands the space. It's part engineering, part art, part something that has no name in the world beyond the Chasm.

The social aspects of my work are as crucial as the technical. Cable car operators trust me with their lives daily. I know each of them by the way they load their cars, the particular rhythm of their transit. When reality warps and a car seems to exist in two places simultaneously, or when the cables appear to pass through solid rock, it's my voice over the communication system that guides them through, reminding them to trust the tension, not their eyes.

I maintain relationships with the factory workers who produce my replacement parts, learning their schedules so I can commission components during their off-hours for better quality. I trade with the moss farmers for their strongest fibers, which I weave into emergency backup lines. I even have an understanding with one of the deep-dwellers – a creature I've never seen but whose presence I've felt through the cables' vibrations. It avoids my lines, and I ensure my maintenance schedule doesn't disturb its hunting grounds.

In the evenings, when the industrial noise reaches its crescendo and the reality distortions intensify, I perform my most delicate work. This is when I splice new connections, threading cables through spaces that shouldn't exist, taking advantage of the Chasm's peculiar physics to create routes that are impossible by conventional understanding. Sometimes I work by touch alone, my eyes closed to avoid the vertigo of seeing cables that appear to pass through themselves.

My dreams are of tension and release, of frequencies and harmonics. Sometimes I wake believing I am myself a cable, stretched between two points that are simultaneously infinitely distant and occupying the same space. These are not nightmares – they are moments of perfect understanding, glimpses of what the Chasm truly is: not a place, but a process, not a location, but a becoming.

I am the Vertiginous Engineer, keeper of impossible passages, maintainer of connections in a world that actively resists coherence. My life is lived in the space between anchor points, in the tension of cables that sing with the voice of the Chasm itself. I am small against the vastness of this emerging geology, yet essential – for without those like me, the Chasm Dwellers would be isolated islands of humanity, unable to share resources, trade, or maintain the complex industrial ecosystem that has taken root in this beautiful, terrifying abyss.

And sometimes, in the quiet moments when the condensation drips its eternal rhythm and the cables hum their deep songs, I feel the Chasm's sigh not as something alien, but as my own breath, extended and amplified through every line I maintain, every connection I preserve against the gentle, inexorable forces of dissolution and change.
