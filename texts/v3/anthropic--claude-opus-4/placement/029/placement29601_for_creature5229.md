In this world of heat and mineral breath, I would be something between the micro and macro scales—neither the foundation films nor the apex gliders, but one of the pocket's essential middle workers.

I am a **trace-reader**.

My body: low and wide like a hand pressed flat, built from overlapping plates that flex at the joints but lock rigid when needed. Each plate carries its own history—mineral deposits laid down in growth rings, telling you which vents I've lived near, which gases I've breathed. My dorsal surface is dull, perpetually dusty, the color of old pewter where it isn't stained yellow or gray by whatever corridor I last traveled.

Six legs, but I move on four. The front pair have evolved into something more like instruments—sensitive to vibration, temperature gradients, and the faintest chemical signatures. When I pause (which is often), I drum these modified limbs against the stone, reading the resonance. Every surface in the pocket has its own signature: fresh deposits ring bright and sharp, old crusts thud dull, and voids beneath sound hollow, dangerous, worth avoiding.

My role in this industrial ecosystem: I am a scout of instability. The pocket is always changing—passages clogging, new cracks forming, gas flows shifting—and I read these changes before they become critical. Where static-runners flee randomly from electrical discharge, I map the charge differentials. Where lane-keepers blindly follow their chemical roads, I test whether those roads still lead somewhere survivable.

I don't do this out of altruism. I feed on the pocket's forgotten spaces—the micro-deposits that accumulate in cracks too thin for grazers, the condensation films in chambers where the air has gone still and strange. These places are rich precisely because they're temporary. When I find one, I settle in for days, sometimes weeks, my metabolism slowing to match the pocket's patience.

My ventral surface is my most valuable adaptation: a series of book-lung-like structures, but built for chemistry rather than oxygen. Each "page" is coated with oils that can selectively bind different gases. When I taste a new mixture, I know not just what's in it, but what used to be in it—reading the ghost of the pocket's previous breath.

This makes me valuable to others, though they don't know it. The paths I test become the paths the lane-keepers use. The cracks I abandon often seal completely within days. The gliders watch where I pause and won't roost there—they've learned that my stillness means the air has gone wrong in ways even they can't tolerate.

I navigate by touch and time. My eyes (six simple ones, arranged to see movement more than form) can detect the ember-pulse, but I don't rely on it. Instead, I build a vibrational map of my territory. Each footfall returns information; each surface drummed tells me about density, about hollow spaces, about the mineral skeleton of this place.

My lifecycle is tied to the pocket's major transitions. When a significant passage closes—something that happens perhaps twice in my lifetime—the whole ecosystem reorganizes. In these chaotic days, I am most active, testing every alternative route, every new gradient. Many of my kind die during these shifts, but those who find the new stable configurations first get to feed on virgin deposits, untouched films, the pocket's brief abundance.

I carry my history in my carapace. The growth rings show droughts and plenty—thin bands where the microbial films barely sustained me, thick bands from seasons when I found a perfect condensation trap. The mineral stains map my travels: that sulfur-yellow streak from the lower corridors, this rust-red patch from an iron-rich seep I discovered and exhausted.

When I die—which will likely be from a misjudged gas pocket or simple mechanical failure as my joints wear through—my body will become a small landmark. My chemical-reading organs contain concentrated oils and waxes that the scavengers prize. My carapace, with its accumulated minerals, will be incorporated into the wall crusts. Even my mapped knowledge dies with me, but the paths I helped establish persist in the behavioral patterns of others.

I am not the pocket's most numerous inhabitant, nor its most efficient, nor its most resilient. But I am its memory-keeper, its risk-tester, its patient scholar of dangerous edges. In a world that builds itself from chemical gradients and heat, I am the one who reads where those gradients are heading.

The pocket roars and whispers, and I listen with my feet.
