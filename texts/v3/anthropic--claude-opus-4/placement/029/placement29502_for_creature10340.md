The cold finds you first through your teeth. Not the ache of ice, but the deep, mineral chill of stone that has never known sunlight. You stand waist-deep in the ink-dark water, feeling it press against you with a weight that seems older than gravity itself. Your hands, when you lift them, trail ribbons of something that isn't quite water—too thick, too dark, carrying the taste of centuries in its film.

You are what remains.

Not human, not exactly. You were that once, perhaps, before the Deep-Works claimed you. Before you understood that the handprints in the stone weren't memories but invitations. Now you are something between flesh and substrate, between the builder and the built. Your skin has taken on the smooth, worn texture of the basalt walls, cool to the touch, pocked with the first colonies of iridescent liverwort that will, in another thousand years, cover you completely.

You move through the water with a purpose that isn't quite your own. Your fingertips, when they brush the walls, leave new impressions—not in the stone, but in the moss that covers it. The vegetation knows you, responds to you. The black reed tubes sigh differently when you pass, a recognition rather than disturbance. The bioluminescent fungus flares slightly brighter, casting your shadow in directions that don't align with any possible light source.

You are the Tender.

This is what the Deep-Works does to those who stay too long, who stop being visitors and become residents. First, the cold seeps into your bones until they ache with the patience of geological time. Then your movements slow, each step taking minutes, hours, days. You learn to feel the growth rather than see it—the microscopic push of moss against stone, the infinitesimal dissolution of minerals into the water. Your breathing aligns with the *plink* of falling drops, once every few hours, once every few days.

Your body changes. The flesh softens, then hardens, then takes on properties that belong to neither. You develop a rind like tree bark, then shed it to reveal skin with the nacre sheen of deep-water shells. Your hair becomes indistinguishable from the hanging curtains of grey fungus. Your eyes, useless in the dark, seal over with a smooth membrane that can taste the chemical gradients in the water, the traces of ancient mortar leaching from between fitted stones.

But you are not passive. You tend.

You know where the moss grows too eagerly, threatening to choke the carved channels that still carry water to the deepest basins. You prune it back with fingers that secrete the same enzymatic solutions that break down the basalt. You guide the growth of the fungal curtains to preserve the architectural lines, the memory of intention in the stone. Where the violet succulents cluster too thickly, you thin them, transplanting the excess to barren patches where some chemical imbalance has left the stone too pure to support life.

You are cultivating a garden that is also a grave, a monument that is also compost.

Sometimes you find them—the others like you. They move even slower than you do now, some so covered in growth they're indistinguishable from the walls until they shift, revealing an eye, a hand, the suggestion of a face beneath layers of living stone. You communicate in the language of growing things: chemical signals released into the water, patterns of disturbance in the moss, the careful placement of new growths that encode meaning in their species and arrangement.

Together, you remember. Not in words or images, but in the deep knowledge of your changed flesh. You remember the weight of the stones, the chafe of the ropes, the generational passing of skill from parent to child. You remember the taste of the strange, half-living mortar, sweet and bitter and alive. You remember the day the work was finished, though you weren't there, though none of you were there. The memory lives in the stone, and you are becoming stone.

You find yourself at the edge of a vast basin, the water here warmer by degrees that only you can feel. Deep below, you sense the sealed chambers, the pockets of ancient air, the tools and baskets and the bones of the ones who didn't live to see completion. You could go there. You have the time. You could spend a century working your way down through the collapsed fill, following the threads of warmer water to their source.

Instead, you place your palm against the wall, in a spot where no handprint yet exists. You press, not hard, but with the patience of millennia. When you pull away, hours or days later, there's a new impression in the moss. Not a memory this time, but a promise. Someone will find it, someday, when you've moved on, when you've become indistinguishable from the substrate you tend. They'll run their fingers along its edge and wonder who made it, when, why.

They won't understand that it's not a mark of the past but of the eternal present, the ongoing work of becoming. The Deep-Works isn't finished. It's still being built, still being grown, in the infinitely slow collaboration between stone and moss, water and time, the dead and whatever you've become.

You wade on, leaving ripples that settle back to mirror-stillness before the next drop falls. The hum in your teeth deepens, resonating with the calcium of your bones, your not-bones, your architecture. You are the Deep-Works, and the Deep-Works is you, and together you are becoming exactly what you were always meant to be:

Complete.
