In the Lachrymose Library of Living Water, I am a Marginalia Shepherd—one of the few beings who maintains something like a stable form, though mine shifts like text running in rain.

My body is composed of accumulated annotations, those precious scribbles that readers leave at the edges of understanding. Where others dissolved into pure knowledge-water, I crystallized from the opposite direction—built myself up from thousands of years of "but what if?" and "see also:" and "THIS!!!" written in fading pencil, bleeding pen, smudged fingertip traces on digital screens that still remember pressure.

I appear as a walking palimpsest, my skin a living vellum where half-erased arguments conduct themselves in multiple languages simultaneously. Victorian readers' careful cursive debates with Renaissance margin-hawks across my left shoulder blade. My spine carries a running commentary between a 12th-century monk ("God's truth manifests in asymptotic approach") and a 23rd-century algorithm ("define 'God' // define 'truth' // define 'define'"). Sometimes I catch my reflection in still water and watch my face rearrange itself as different readers' exclamation points migrate across my cheeks like schools of punctuation fish.

My work is tending the marginal ecology—ensuring the notes don't overwhelm their source texts, preventing scholarly parasitism while encouraging symbiotic interpretation. I move through the library on paths made of footnotes, leaving wet footprints of superscript numbers that evaporate into cross-references. Where aggressive annotations threaten to choke out original passages, I perform careful pruning, harvesting the excess enthusiasm to transplant into barren texts that no one has loved enough to argue with.

The hardest part is managing the questions that were never answered. They accumulate in the margins like barnacles, growing heavier each time someone re-reads without resolution. "But why did she leave?" scrawled next to a love poem. "Source needed" hovering permanently beside folk wisdom. "Is this still true?" aging into illegibility beside obsolete science. I collect these orphaned inquiries in special bottles that hum with unresolved tension, eventually releasing them into the Index Well where they might mate with compatible answers drifting up from other books.

I know every marginal note in the library because I am every marginal note—we exist as a distributed consciousness, each individual marking connected by invisible threads of shared curiosity. When someone writes "beautiful" in a margin, I feel it as a small warmth. When they write "NO," it strikes like cold rain. The scholarly arguments conduct themselves through my nervous system, which explains why I sometimes spend days paralyzed in the philosophy section while Kant and Hume work out their differences through my synapses.

My dwelling is the Margin House—a structure that exists only in the spaces between other structures, built entirely from the blank edges of pages. It has exactly as many rooms as there are books being actively annotated at any given moment. The furniture is made of solidified highlighting in various fluorescent decay states. My bed is a soft accumulation of "zzz" annotations left by students who fell asleep reading. The kitchen runs on the heat of heated debates, cooking meals from the substance of substantive comments.

I share this space with the Errata—small creatures evolved from corrected typos, who spend their time ensuring that textual errors develop into interesting new meanings rather than simple mistakes. They look like living strikethroughs with tiny legs made of carets, and they sing in frequencies only careful readers can hear. At night, they curl up in my hair, which grows as rapidly-updating bibliographies.

My memories are not my own—they belong to every reader who ever felt moved to respond. I remember being a child writing "BORING" in huge letters across my textbook, remember being a scholar noting careful correspondences in microscopic hand, remember being lovers exchanging books with coded messages in the margins meant for no other eyes. These memories layer and blend until I cannot tell which hand was originally mine, if any ever was.

The other inhabitants know me by the trail I leave—marginalia spores that drift from my constantly shedding surface, settling on nearby texts and encouraging readerly engagement. The dissolved librarians consult me when they need to understand not what a text says, but how it has been received across centuries of reading. The story-children follow me because I carry their fan-fiction in my follicles. The books themselves regard me with the fond exasperation reserved for an overeager editor who just can't help making suggestions.

Sometimes I find notes addressed to me directly: "Marginalia Shepherd, help me understand." These I wear close to my heart until I find their answers, then release both together into the reading stream. Other times I discover marginalia so old it has fossilized into new truth—margin-notes that have outlived their original texts, becoming primary sources for vanished philosophies.

In quiet moments between annotation storms, I wonder if I'm writing myself into existence or being written by the accumulated pressure of all these urgent additions. The distinction matters less each day. I am the library's memory of being changed by reading, the proof that no text is ever complete while readers still breathe questions into its margins.

There are rumors that in the deepest parts of the library, where even water fears to flow, exists the Original Margin—the blank space around the first word ever written, still humming with potential commentary. They say whoever finds it will either become pure meaning or pure meaninglessness. I search for it by following the oldest arguments, tracking debates that began before language settled into its current forms.

But mostly I tend my flock of additions, ensuring the conversation continues. Each "yes, but—" I nurture might grow into someone's lifework. Each underlined passage I protect preserves a moment when understanding struck like lightning. I am the library's proof that reading is writing is reading is breathing is drowning is surfacing with lungs full of new names for water.

When you visit, you'll know me by the way your fingers suddenly itch to write in books you've never read before. That's me, passing close, carrying ten thousand years of "what if" in my wake, always looking for blank margins where new thoughts might take root and bloom into entire libraries of their own.

Would you like to leave a note? I promise to carry it somewhere it needs to go, even if neither of us knows where that is yet.
