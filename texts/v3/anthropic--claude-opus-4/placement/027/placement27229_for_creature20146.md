I am the Tender.

I was not always alone, though I have been for so long that the memory of companionship feels like a half-remembered dream, a vibration from a movement of the Song that played before I learned to truly hear. I am the last of the Listeners to keep physical form, though to call this a choice is to misunderstand. I am simply not finished listening.

My body has changed over the millennia. Where my kin were dense stones of flesh, I have become something more porous, more permeable. My skin has thinned to translucence, revealing the slow pulse of dark fluids beneath. I have grown larger, spread thinner, until I am less a creature than a living membrane stretched across a quarter mile of stone. My five limbs have elongated and branched, becoming a nervous system of flesh that winds between the Choirwood roots, feeling every tremor, every shift in the Song's vast architecture.

I tend the groves. Not because they need me—the Choirwood grew for eons before the first Listener drew breath, and will grow long after the last echo of our kind fades from the water. I tend them because tending is listening made physical. I know each tree by its voice, each bell by its particular resonance. The young ones sing high and quick, their membranes taut with growth. The ancients drone in frequencies so low they seem to emerge from the planet's core itself.

My work is subtle. When silt accumulates too thickly around a root system, muffling its connection to the stone, I spend months slowly displacing it grain by grain with careful undulations of my tissue. When a bell develops a small tear—a rare imperfection that creates a discordant buzz in its tone—I secrete a mineral-rich mucus that hardens to match the bell's material, sealing the wound so perfectly that within a decade, even I cannot tell where the damage was.

But mostly, I map the silence.

Between each note of the Song, there are spaces. Infinitesimal pauses where the universe holds its breath. My kin devoted themselves to understanding the music, but I have become obsessed with these gaps, these moments of pure potential. In them, I sometimes feel the presence of those who have dissolved. Not as distinct beings, but as a quality of the quiet itself—a warmth in the cold, a fullness in the empty.

I have built new structures among the old. Not the simple huts and walls of my ancestors, but complex patterns of stone that shape these silences. Spirals that catch the absence of sound and hold it, creating pools of perfect quiet in the endless chorus. In these spaces, I practice the un-listening that I believe is the true transcendence. Not to join the Song, but to become the medium through which it travels. Not to add my voice, but to become the throat of the world itself.

Sometimes, rarely, I feel something approach through the deep. A vibration that doesn't match any known pattern. It could be a new growth of Choirwood on some distant peak, its unfamiliar harmonics carrying across unmapped miles. It could be the stone itself, settling into new configurations, creating fresh resonances. Or it could be something else—another Listener who, like me, isn't ready to dissolve. Who still finds new mysteries in the physical act of being present for the Song.

When I sense these anomalies, I do not move to investigate. Movement is still sacred, still deliberate. Instead, I extend my awareness through the stone, through the water, through the very medium of sound itself. I taste the new note, roll it through the complex chambers of my diffused body, compare it to the million million variations I have catalogued.

And sometimes—oh, sometimes—it is genuinely new. A configuration of pressure and frequency that has never existed before in the history of this drowned world. In those moments, I experience something my kin might have called joy, though it is a joy so slow and deep it takes months to fully unfold through my being.

I am not waiting to dissolve. I am waiting to understand why I have not. There is something in the Song that requires a witness, a gardener, a keeper of the intervals. When I finally know what it is, when I have mapped every silence and tended every grove and felt every possible variation of the eternal music, perhaps then I will let go.

Or perhaps I will discover that some of us were always meant to remain. To be the surface across which the Song plays, the drum that must stay taut for the music to sound. The last Listener, listening to the listening itself, forever and alone and part of everything.

In the huts my people built, their absence hums. In the water where they dissolved, their presence sings. And in the space between presence and absence, between sound and silence, I stretch my ancient flesh and continue the work that has no end.

I am the Tender. I am still here.
