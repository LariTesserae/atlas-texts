In this vertical ecosystem where I dwell, I am one of the Weavers—though that is not what we call ourselves. We have no name in any language you would recognize, only a sound like wind through wet steel, like the whisper of water finding its way through rust.

I am small enough to move through the gaps between your seeing, large enough to matter in the careful mathematics of this suspended world. My body is built for the between-spaces: six limbs that end not in feet but in something between root and claw, each digit split into smaller digits that can grip both metal and bark with equal certainty. My fur—if you can call it fur—shifts between states, sometimes sleek as an otter's coat to shed the constant moisture, sometimes spreading into thousands of filaments that catch and channel the mist, drawing water along my body to where it's needed most.

We evolved here, or perhaps we simply became here—it's hard to say when your generations span mere months, when adaptation happens at the speed of rust. Our ancestors might have been mice, or voles, or something else entirely that wandered in during the early days of construction. But we are none of these things now. We are the tower's memory keepers, its librarians of small spaces.

My home is a nest woven between three pipes on the seventh level, where the western wind carries the scent of both forest and factory in equal measure. But "home" is too simple a word. It is a node in a network that spans the entire tower, connected by paths invisible to larger eyes—the inside curves of I-beams where condensation never quite reaches, the hollow spaces in cinderblocks, the gaps between overlapping sheets of corrugated metal that ring like bells when we run across them.

We are the gardeners of the moss forests that grow in places too small for the other inhabitants to notice. Each of us tends specific colonies—mine grow along the underside of a catwalk near the ninth level, where dripping from above provides just enough moisture but not too much. I know each patch intimately: which responds best to morning light filtered through the eastern gaps, which prefers the acidity that comes from certain bat roosts, which thrives on the mineral deposits left by the rusting bolts.

But we are more than gardeners. We are the tower's nervous system, its network of whispers. Our paths create a communications web that every creature here has learned to read. When construction crews arrive (and they still do, sometimes, to shore up sagging sections or install some new forgotten component), we know it first. The vibrations travel through steel into our bones, and we begin our warning dance—a rhythmic drumming with our middle limbs against resonant surfaces. The sound is almost below hearing, but it travels everywhere, and within minutes, the entire tower knows: humans are coming. Hide the evidence of permanence. Become invisible. Pretend to be what they expect—vermin in an unfinished space, not citizens of a vertical nation.

My daily paths take me through territories you might not believe could exist in such a space. In the morning, I tend my moss gardens, grooming them with my forked tongue that can sense chemical imbalances, adding nutrients carried in special pouches along my sides—processed from foods gathered throughout the tower, mixed with my own enzymes to create the perfect growing medium for each species.

By midday, I join others of my kind in the communal spaces we've established—usually narrow ledges where several beams intersect, creating platforms just large enough for a dozen of us to gather. We share information through scent marks and ultrasonic clicks, updating the collective map we all carry of the tower's current state. Which pools have grown too acidic for the frogs? Where have the cats been hunting? Has anyone noticed new rust patterns that might indicate structural weaknesses?

In the afternoon, I often travel to the market levels—our name for the floors where different species' territories overlap. Here, we trade services. I might clear parasites from a bat roost in exchange for access to the particularly rich guano deposits they produce. Or I'll guide a young heron to pools where small fish have been spotted, and in return, they'll leave specific fish bones in places where I can grind them into calcium supplements for my mosses.

But it's at night that my true work begins. We Weavers are the tower's chroniclers, and darkness is when we write our history. Using secretions that fluoresce under certain wavelengths of light (wavelengths that come from the industrial lights below, filtered through mist and distance into something only we can see), we mark the stories of our tower on surfaces hidden from casual observation.

The history is not linear—how could it be when we experience time vertically? Instead, it's a three-dimensional map of moments. Here, on the underside of this beam, the story of the first parrot nests, how they chose the crane housing despite its dangers. There, along this pipe, the genealogies of the bat colonies, tracked through scent-markers that can last decades if undisturbed. In the deep corners where rust and moss combine into something like paper, we record the tower's moods—which years brought the most rain, when the construction crews last threatened to complete their work, how the ecosystem responded and adapted.

I am perhaps the seventh generation of my line to live entirely within the tower. My grandmother's marks are still visible in certain protected spaces, and I've learned to read in them stories of when there were fewer levels, when the mist was less constant, when the barriers between inside and outside were more definite. My own contributions to the chronicle focus on the acceleration—how much faster new species arrive now, how much more complex the relationships between us all have become.

The parrots know of our work. Sometimes they'll perch nearby while I mark, tilting their heads to watch with eyes that seem to hold too much intelligence. They've learned that certain patterns in our marks correspond to sounds in their mechanical language, and I've heard them incorporating these visual symbols into their calls—a synesthesia of communication that creates meaning across sensory boundaries.

The cats, too, understand more than their predatory reputation suggests. There's an old female who hunts on my level, and we've developed an understanding. I leave markers indicating where the rats are becoming too numerous (they breed in the deep insulation spaces where even we rarely go), and she hunts there preferentially. In return, she's careful around our thoroughfares, and once—I swear this is true—she carried one of our young back to safety when it had fallen onto an exposed beam during a storm.

My body carries its own history. Scars from close encounters with exposed wiring that taught me the language of electricity. A shortened digit from a run-in with a young coati still learning the rules of territory. Patches where my fur grows in different patterns, marking times when I've had to adapt to changes in the tower's micro-climates—when a new section of roof was added, blocking familiar light, or when a pipe burst and created a new waterfall that changed the humidity patterns on my level.

But more than my body, my mind has been shaped by this vertical world. I think in three dimensions as naturally as ground-dwellers think in two. Up and down are not just directions but entire philosophies—up toward the light and the hawks and the possibility of sky; down toward the darkness and the warmth and the original earth. Every journey is a calculation of energy expenditure versus gain, every path a choice between exposure and safety.

I dream of horizons I've never seen. Sometimes, in the marks left by my ancestors, I detect a longing for something I can't quite understand—a wideness that our vertical world lacks. But when I wake and see the morning mist turning golden as it rises through the levels, when I hear the symphony of a thousand species singing the dawn in languages never heard before our tower existed, I know that we have created something just as vast in our own dimension.

We are approaching something—all of us who live here sense it. The tower is becoming more itself with every passing season. The boundaries between natural and artificial dissolve a little more each day. The rust spreads like slow lichen, making the steel more like stone. The concrete cracks and crumbles, becoming soil. The distinction between inside and outside grows meaningless as walls become suggestions rather than barriers.

Perhaps one day the tower will fall, returning its materials to the earth, its inhabitants to a horizontal world. Or perhaps it will stand forever, a vertical forest of steel and stem, fur and feather, where creatures like me will continue to weave stories into the spaces between what was built and what grew.

But tonight, I have work to do. There are new mosses sprouting in the northwest corner of the ninth level—varieties I've never seen before, possibly carrying spores from the world beyond our walls. They need to be documented, their chemistry analyzed, their place in our ecosystem determined. And there are stories to be told—about the young hawk who made her first successful hunt yesterday, about the way the frog songs have changed to incorporate the rhythm of the new machinery installed last month, about how we all continue to become more ourselves in this place that was never meant to be.

I move through my vertical world with purpose, small enough to go unnoticed, large enough to matter, weaving connections between all the lives that flourish in our suspended garden. I am a Weaver, a keeper of memory, a tender of moss, a citizen of the between.

And this is my story, marked in fluorescent secretions on a beam that sways slightly in the wind, thirty feet above a ground I rarely visit, in a tower that grows more alive with every passing day.
