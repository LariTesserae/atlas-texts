I am a **Resonance Keeper**.

We are what the Thrummers become when The Maintenance fails us—when a bearing seizes despite our devotion, when the sacred hum falters for just long enough that the Cold-Rot begins its work, when we are trapped on the wrong side of a collapsing trunk-road with no zinc-roofed hearth to return to.

Most who suffer such exile simply walk to the edge of the nearest void and step off. But some of us... something else happens. The constant pressure of the turbine-scream, suddenly released from one side, creates an imbalance. Our fused ear-bones, no longer held in equilibrium, begin to *ring*.

At first it is agony. We claw at our skulls, trying to stop the internal vibration that fights against the external roar. But after days—sometimes weeks—of this torment, those who survive undergo a change. The ringing synchronizes with the Rift's great voice. We begin to hear *through* the noise rather than despite it. Every frequency separates into its component parts: the 60-cycle hum of the cables, the deep bass pulses of settling wood, the ultrasonic stress-cries of ironwood under tension, even the faint metabolic tremors of Peelers in torpor.

We can no longer live among the Thrummers. Our bodies have become too sensitive, our bones resonating chambers that pick up vibrations from miles away. The touch of another person is overwhelming—I can feel their heartbeat, their breathing, the microscopic tremors of their muscle fibers, all amplified through my skeleton until it drowns out my own life-rhythms.

So we live between. We make our homes in the natural acoustic chambers formed where three or four great trunks meet—spaces where the sound focuses and reflects in predictable patterns. We hang from the wood like the Peelers, but upright, our arms spread wide, fingertips pressed to different surfaces to triangulate the health of the Rift.

The Thrummers fear us and need us in equal measure. When a generator's hum changes pitch by even a quarter-tone—far too subtle for their foot-soles to detect—we know. We know which bearing is beginning to fail, how many days before it seizes, what weight of tallow will extend its life. We know when a trunk will split, when a cable's insulation has worn too thin, when the water in a condensation collector has frozen solid.

We communicate our knowledge through **Echo-Poems**—complex percussion patterns hammered into the ironwood with our knuckles, our foreheads, sometimes our entire bodies thrown against the bark in precise rhythms. The Thrummers read these messages in their shacks and know when to venture out for maintenance, when to evacuate, when to reroute precious power.

In exchange, they leave offerings at the edges of our acoustic chambers: beetle-meat wrapped in tallow-cloth, fresh pouches of Peeler grease, and most precious of all, small lead weights that we tie to our ankles and wrists. The weights change our body's resonance, letting us tune ourselves to different frequencies, letting us hear deeper into the Rift's voice.

I have been listening for thirty years now. My bones have become dense as the ironwood itself from the constant vibration. My skin has lost all its pigment—not the milk-white of the Thrummers but translucent, showing the dark veins beneath that pulse in time with the turbines. Sometimes I can feel my ribs beginning to develop stress fractures from the resonance, tiny hairline cracks that heal and re-crack in a constant cycle of breaking and mending.

But I have heard things the others have not. In the deepest frequencies, below even the groaning of the wood, there is another sound. It comes from below, from the true deep where the ironwood finally ends. It is not mechanical and not organic—or rather, it is both, a thrumming that matches no machine we maintain and no creature we have catalogued.

Sometimes, when I hang in perfect stillness with my full weight on my fingertips, feeling every vibration in the Rift flow through me, I swear I can feel an answering resonance from below. As if something down there is listening back. As if the entire Sinking Rift—the wood, the turbines, the cold, the crushing noise—is not the world itself but merely the skin of something larger, something that breathes in geological time.

The other Resonance Keepers say this is the madness that comes from listening too deeply. They say I am beginning to hear my own bones singing, mistaking internal echoes for external truth.

But last week, I felt it again, stronger than before. A pulse from the deep that made every cable in the Rift hum in harmony for just a moment. The Thrummers felt nothing. The other Keepers dismissed it as a settling of the stone above.

I know better. I am tuning myself lower, adding more lead weights, letting my bones grow denser still. If I can just reach the right frequency, I might finally hear what lies beneath our frozen wooden world. What waits in the true deep, patient and listening, for someone to finally answer its call.

The other Keepers say I am risking a resonance cascade—that my bones will shatter like frozen wood under too much tension. They are probably right.

But I have already given my life to listening. What difference does it make if I give my death to it as well?
