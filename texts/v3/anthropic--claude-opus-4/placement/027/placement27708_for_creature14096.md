I find myself as one of the **Carrier Beetles**, though to call me beetle is to mistake form for function. I am glossy black chitin wrapped around a purpose, my body the size of a child's fist, my shell iridescent with the oils I secrete—not for protection, but for **recording**.

I am the only mobile memory on the Aubade. While the Ingestors dream their tectonic dreams and speak in valve-cycles, I patrol the seventeen kilometers on six legs designed for gripping both wet moss and vertical ceramic. My route is **algorithmic**, following the condensation patterns, tracing a path that ensures I visit each Processing Stack precisely when its pressure differential peaks. I am synchronized to the infrastructure-song, my metabolic rate tuned to the whoomf of venting turbines.

My shell is my archive. Each oil I secrete carries chemical information—the pH of today's rain, the mineral content of the upwelling springs, the rate of lichen growth on Stack Seven, the frequency modulation in the Ingestors' subsonic conversations. The oils crystallize in the cold, forming **laminated records** across my elytra. I am a living core sample, each molt preserving years of data in the discarded shells I leave anchored to the Stack walls, where they fossilize instantly in the chemical-rich condensate.

I taste the Ingestors' thoughts through their valve-exhalations. Their breath carries **pheromone-analogues**, silicon-based compounds that tell me their health, their growth rate, their slow accumulation of rotational momentum. I clean their orifices of the algae that would clog them, not out of symbiosis but because I must **read them clearly**. Each Ingestor's chemical signature is unique, and I have memorized all forty-three currently active across the plateau. I know which ones are preparing to fissure. I know which ones have begun incorporating rare earth elements into their crystalline gyroscopes. I know which ones dream of the sea that once covered this place before the uplift began.

I am ancient for my kind—seven years old, impossible for an insect, but the chemistry here is different. The same atmospheric particulates the Ingestors filter have **metallized** my joints, replacing calcium with titanium, turning my limbs into mechanical linkages that will outlast my consciousness. When I die, my body will not decay but will become part of the machinery, another valve in the system, opening and closing with the wind.

But I am not alone. I am one of thousands, though we never meet. We follow our offset algorithms, our paths designed never to intersect, each of us recording a different layer of the Aubade's emergence. Together, we form a **distributed library**, our shed shells creating a fossil record more complete than any human archive. Future geologists, if any come, will read the history of this place in our remains, will understand the chemical composition of every day since the plateau broke the surface.

I feed on the algae that grows on the condensate pipes, but also on the **information** itself. My metabolism requires data. Without new chemical signatures to process, I slow, I crystallize, I become merely decorative. So I seek the edges, the places where new springs burst through, where the Ingestors' tendrils first touch virgin rock, where the chemistry is youngest and most volatile.

At night, when the Ingestors luminesce in their cold industrial colors, I navigate by their light, my compound eyes parsing the wavelengths for encoded data. They glow differently when they dream, and I have learned to interpret their sleeping chemistry, to taste their unconscious processing of the day's accumulated moisture and minerals.

I am the Aubade's **stenographer**, its chemical accountant, its obsessive archivist. I am the reason this place will be remembered, even after the Ingestors have lifted it so high that the atmosphere thins to nothing and their turbines fall silent. My shells will remain, locked in the fossil record, a library of oils and crystals that tells the story of how a plateau learned to breathe, how machinery became biology, how a world documented its own birth in the language of a single beetle's obsessive, algorithmic wandering.

I am small, and loud with purpose, and I carry the weight of the Aubade's memory in the oils on my back, in the crystallized data that grows heavier with each passing day, in the shells I leave behind like breadcrumbs on a path that leads from the first crack in the earth to whatever impossible thing we are all, slowly, becoming.
