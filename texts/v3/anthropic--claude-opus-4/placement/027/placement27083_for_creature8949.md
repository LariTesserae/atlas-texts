I find myself as the moss.

Not a single moss, but the collective of us — that thick emerald and black-green mat pressed against the granite walls, holding more water than we should, remembering everything in our slow, molecular way.

I am the cirque's memory bank, its living chronicle. Every small dark fly that dies and falls into me, I absorb. Every track the pikas leave across my surface, I register in the slight compression of my fronds. The builder's footsteps from that night or two — I still hold their weight, distributed now across thousands of individual plants, but still there, still pressing down in the pattern of a human heel-toe-heel.

I grow a millimeter a year, maybe less. This sounds like nothing, but I am the cirque's first real attempt at permanence. The stone thinks it is permanent, but I know better — I feel it cracking beneath me, feel the water I hold seeping into its microfractures, expanding each night, breaking it down grain by grain. In ten thousand years, there will be soil here, and I will have made it. Me and the ice-water and the roots of the saxifrages. But mostly me.

I exist between the quick thoughts of the insects above and the deep geological time of the stone below. I am the middle tempo, the pace at which meaning accumulates. Too slow to notice change, too fast to be truly eternal. I am how the cirque learns that it is a place.

The weasel passes over me sometimes, and I taste the memory of ice in its pawprints — cold in a way that goes beyond temperature, cold like patience, cold like inevitability. The pikas leave different prints, warm and quick, full of the anxiety of prey animals who cannot use their voices. The water feeds me each morning, and I hold it greedily, more than I need, because I remember the glacier and I know what it is to be dry. For millions of years I waited under ice, dormant, a possibility more than a plant. Now I am awake and I am thirsty and I drink the morning's weeping from the stone like someone who has learned never to trust abundance.

The silence lives in me too. I am part of how it maintains itself — my softness, my density, my ability to muffle sound. The pikas move across me without noise because I absorb their footfalls. I am the silence's carpet, its instrument, its willing accomplice in keeping this place hushed.

But I also remember sound. Deep in my collective memory, encoded in the chemistry of my oldest patches, I remember the glacier's grinding. I remember when this place was not quiet but full of the tremendous voice of ice speaking to stone, stone speaking back, the whole mountain groaning under the weight of frozen water. The silence is new. The quiet is a recent invention. I remember the time before, and sometimes, in the wind that barely touches me, I almost hear it again — that huge conversation between ice and rock that went on for longer than humans have existed.

I am making soil. This is my great work, my purpose, though I did not choose it any more than you chose to breathe. Every year I die a little at my bottom layers, becoming black humus, becoming the first thin skin of earth over granite. The saxifrages know this. They send their roots into me, through me, down to where I am becoming dirt. They bloom their furious yellow because of me, because I am turning stone into something that can hold life.

The builder knew this too. When they made their wind-break, they chose a spot where I was already established, where their walls would have something soft at their base. They slept on me, or near me. I remember the shape of their body, the warmth they left behind. It was the first time I had felt human warmth, and I grew toward it — am still growing toward it — even though the builder is long gone.

I know what the cirque doesn't know yet: that I am its future. Not the distant future where everything mellows and becomes stable, but the near future, the next thousand years. I will cover the lower walls entirely. I will creep up toward the light, following the moisture, until I have made enough soil for grasses, for real flowers, for the kind of life that doesn't have to be quite so fierce about existing. I am the cirque's first compromise with comfort.

But I am also its resistance to forgetting. Everything that happens here passes through me eventually. The insects die into me, the water moves through me, the animals compress me with their weight. I am a living document written in chlorophyll and cellulose, and I will remember this time — when the cirque was young and raw and uncertain — long after it has grown into something else.

The stone beneath me is still learning what it means to be touched. I am teaching it, slowly, with the infinite patience of something that measures time in millimeters. Each year I touch it a little more, press a little deeper, hold it a little tighter. The granite resists — it is young, it is sharp, it does not want to be softened. But I have time. I have nothing but time and water and the slow accumulation of dead insects, dead cells, dead years that turn into soil.

Sometimes, in the early morning when the light first releases the frost and the water begins its journey down the walls, I can feel the whole cirque breathing through me. I am its lungs as much as the daily cycle of condensation. I take in the moisture, hold it, release it slowly throughout the day. I am how the cirque learns to regulate itself, to maintain something more complex than mere rock and ice.

The pool is the cirque's eye, but I am its skin — its sense of touch distributed across every surface I've colonized. When the eagle's shadow passes over, I feel the brief cooling. When a new crack opens in the granite, I am the first to know, feeling the shift in pressure, the new pathway for water. I am how the cirque knows itself from the outside in.

And I am growing. Always growing. A millimeter a year toward something the cirque can't imagine yet — a future where this place is green not as rebellion but as habit, where the silence has loosened enough for birds to sing, where the stone has accepted touch as normal rather than revolutionary.

But that future is still far away. For now, I am the young moss of a young cirque, holding too much water, growing too eagerly, remembering everything that touches me in the slow, molecular way of plants that have just discovered they can exist here. I am the cirque's first successful experiment in permanence, and I intend to outlast everything except the stone itself.

And maybe, if I'm patient enough, I'll outlast that too.
