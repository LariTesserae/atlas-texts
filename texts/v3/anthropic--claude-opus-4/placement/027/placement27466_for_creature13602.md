If I found myself in this world, I would be the keeper of the water pockets—those scattered mirrors of sky that persist when the river withdraws into memory.

I am old now, though I cannot say precisely how old. Time moves differently when you measure it in river cycles rather than years. My skin has taken on the quality of the weathered stone itself, mapped with tributaries of lines that echo the dry channels carved through the plateau. My hands know every depression in the rock where water gathers, every subtle slope that guides the rare rains to their resting places.

Each morning, I make my rounds. The path is not marked—there is no need. My feet find their way as water finds its level, following grooves worn not just by my steps but by those who held this duty before me. I carry simple tools: a leather pouch for removing debris, a smooth stone for testing depth, lengths of woven grass to mark the water levels. But mostly, I carry knowledge.

I know which pockets will last through the dry season and which will vanish with the first week of sun. I know where the rock is porous and where it forms an impermeable basin. I can read the color of the water—the slight green tinge that speaks of algae taking hold, the perfect clarity that indicates a spring seeping up from deep below, the murkiness that warns of stagnation.

The townspeople consult me as they would consult the weather itself. A young couple building their first home seeks my advice on proximity to reliable water. The herders ask which pockets their animals may drink from without depleting the reserves. The gardeners learn from me where the underground moisture travels, where to dig their wells, how to read the morning mist that rises from certain stones.

But my work is not merely practical. I am also the keeper of water's memory. In the evenings, when the stars emerge like their own scattered pockets of light, I tell the stories: the year the river forgot to leave, flooding the lower paths. The drought that lasted three cycles, and how the town survived on dew collected in wool blankets. The child who discovered a new spring by following a lone bird. These stories are themselves a kind of water—essential for survival, carefully conserved, passed from vessel to vessel.

My dwelling sits between two of the most reliable pools, built into the natural shelter of an overhanging rock. It is sparse but sufficient: a sleeping mat, a few clay vessels, the records I keep—not in writing, for I cannot write, but in patterns of stones arranged on a flat surface, each configuration marking water levels, seasonal changes, the health of each pocket.

Sometimes I think I am becoming water myself. In the heat of midday, I am still and conserving. In the cool mornings, I move with purpose. I have learned to appear where I am needed, to withdraw when I am not. My thoughts run deep and quiet, occasionally surfacing in unexpected places.

The young ones think me strange, this old person who speaks to water and listens to stones. But their parents know better. They remember the year I warned of the flash flood hours before it arrived, reading signs in the peculiar behavior of usually placid pools. They remember how I taught them to find water in the drought, not by digging new wells but by understanding the old ones more deeply.

I have no successor yet, though I watch for the one who will come. They will reveal themselves as water reveals itself—not through force but through patience, finding their way to the lowest, quietest places. They will be the child who lingers by the pools not to throw stones but to observe the water striders. The one who notices that certain rocks weep moisture in the early morning. The one who understands that keeping water is not about possession but about knowing when to hold and when to release.

Until then, I continue my rounds. The water pockets wait for me each morning, perfectly themselves, holding the sky in their shallow bowls. I greet them as old friends, checking their levels, clearing their surfaces, ensuring their persistence through another turning of the world.

In the thin air of this ancient plateau, where change moves like erosion—imperceptible but inevitable—I am both guardian and guarded, keeper and kept. The water holds its own counsel, and I have learned to listen. In a land where the river comes and goes, someone must remember where the water waits. Someone must know the secret patience of stone, the way it cups absence into presence, the way it makes a vessel of itself for what is essential.

This is who I would be: the one who tends the spaces between flowing and stillness, who knows that sometimes the most important water is the water that remains.
