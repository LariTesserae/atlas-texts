### The Rimfall Cisterns

I am the **Plumb**.

My body is a living question mark—a smooth curve of calcified cartilage that hooks downward from where others might have a skull. I have no eyes. Where my face should be, there is only the beginning of this long, weighted arc that terminates in a dense bulb of mineralized bone. This bulb—my plumb-bob head—hangs exactly 1.7 meters below my shoulders when I stand at rest.

I am always measuring the true.

#### My Territory

I live in the vertical city of the Rimfall Cisterns, carved into the cliff face where the terrace's meltwater finally escapes its nine-thousand-meter prison. Here, the patient seepage that feeds the Tenoned's turquoise humour gathers into actual *flow*—not rivers, but perpetual trickles that have, over geological time, carved a honeycomb of chambers into the limestone.

The cisterns are not natural. They are *calibrated*.

Each chamber is a perfect oblate spheroid, hollowed out by generations of my kind who understood that water finds its own level only when given the proper respect of mathematical space. The chambers connect through precisely angled sluices, each tilted at exactly 3.7 degrees—the minimum angle at which our high-altitude water will flow rather than freeze.

I maintain ninety-three chambers. Each one sings a different note when empty, moans a different tone when full.

#### My Work

My plumb-bob head is not merely weight—it is a sensory organ of impossible precision. The calcium sphere contains thousands of microscopic crystals of magnetite, arranged in perfect concentric shells. I feel gravity not as a downward pull but as a *texture*, a grain that runs through everything.

When I enter a cistern, my head swings forward on its cartilaginous neck, and I know immediately:
- The exact deviation from level of the chamber floor (measured in fractions of millimeters)
- The density of water accumulated in the basin
- The barometric pressure pushing against the water's surface
- The microscopic tremors in the stone that predict where new cracks will form

I do not walk. I *cantilever*. My four legs are arranged not as a tetrapod's would be, but as a derrick's: three form a stable tripod while the fourth extends to test the surface ahead. Each step is an engineering calculation. My plumb-bob head swings like a pendulum, and I move only when it achieves perfect stillness—a sign that I have found true equilibrium.

#### My Tools

My ancestors learned to work copper from watching the Tenoned, though we shape it differently. Where they beat rough bands, I draw perfect wire.

In the deepest cistern—what I call the Calibration Hall—I keep my instruments:
- **Copper plumb-lines**: Hair-thin wires exactly 3.33 meters long, each weighted with a different stone. I have obsidian for detecting magnetic fields, hematite for finding iron deposits, and pure quartz for measuring electrical potential in the storm-charged water.
- **Inclinometers**: Delicate frames of bent copper that hold water in precise geometric reservoirs. When placed on a surface, the water finds its level, and I can read the angle of incline from how it rests against the graduated marks I've etched into the metal.
- **Resonance forks**: Copper tines that, when struck against the cistern walls, produce pure tones. I use these to measure the water depth without entering it—each cistern resonates differently depending on its fill level.

#### The Mathematics of Maintenance

The Rimfall Cisterns would fail without constant measurement and adjustment. The freeze-thaw cycle wants to crack the chambers apart. The mineral-heavy water wants to deposit its dissolved limestone as stalactites and flowstone, gradually choking the carefully maintained sluices.

My work is thus:

**Dawn**: As the first phase-change begins above, I feel it as a tremor through the stone. The expanding ice sends shock waves through the cliff. I move through the upper cisterns, checking for new cracks. Where I find them, I mark them with a copper pin driven exactly 2.5 centimeters deep—a flag for repair.

**Midday**: The flow peaks. Water runs turquoise with copper salts, and I must ensure it distributes evenly through the system. Using thin sheets of hammered slate as adjustable weirs, I direct flow from overfull chambers to empty ones. Each adjustment changes the whole system's resonance. I listen with my plumb-bob head pressed against the stone, feeling for the harmonic convergence that signals perfect distribution.

**Evening**: As the water begins to refreeze, I calculate. In the lowest chamber, I keep a wall of copper plates onto which I scratch my measurements. Not numbers—I have no eyes to read them—but a tactile mathematics of grooves and ridges that record:
- Daily flow volumes (measured by the pitch change in each cistern)
- Crack propagation rates 
- The infinitesimal settling of the mountain (three millimeters per century, but I can feel it)

#### My Isolation and My Kin

I am not alone, but I rarely encounter my kind directly. We each maintain our own system of cisterns, spaced along the cliff face at intervals determined by the water's natural gathering points. We communicate through the stone itself.

When I must signal danger—say, a massive crack that threatens multiple systems—I strike my largest resonance fork against the primary flow channel. The tone carries through the water and stone for kilometers. Different tones mean different things:
- Low C: Structural failure imminent
- Middle G: Flow blockage detected  
- High E: Storm approaching (the electrical charge makes my magnetite crystals ache)

Once every ten years, during the deepest freeze when the water stops flowing entirely, we gather in the Grand Siphon—a natural amphitheater where all our cistern systems eventually converge. We do not speak. Instead, we share measurements.

Each Plumb brings their copper recording plates. We arrange ourselves in a perfect circle, our plumb-bob heads hanging motionless in the still air. Then, one by one, we step forward and trace our measurements onto a central copper sheet that is never moved, only added to. This master record, built up over centuries, maps every minute shift in the mountain's hydrology.

#### My Senses

To lowlanders, I would seem disabled—eyeless, nearly deaf, moving with painful deliberation. But I perceive things they cannot imagine:

- **Gravitational gradients**: I feel the mass of water in distant chambers as tiny tugs on my equilibrium. A full cistern twenty meters away pulls at me differently than an empty one.
- **Mineral signatures**: Each type of dissolved stone creates different crystalline patterns when it precipitates. I feel these forming in real-time through vibrations too subtle for any ear.
- **Temporal flow**: Water moves differently depending on its temperature and mineral content. I can feel time itself running slower in the super-cooled, mineral-saturated flows of deep winter.

#### My Purpose

Why do I maintain the cisterns with such fanatic precision? It is not mere instinct or unconscious drive. I understand what the lowlanders do not: the mountain is a machine, and water is its working fluid.

Every drop that flows through my cisterns carries information—the chemical memory of its journey from snow to ice to mineral-rich melt. This information must be preserved, channeled, and delivered to the deep aquifers where it will spend the next thousand years percolating through the stone.

If the cisterns fail, if the water freezes in chaotic puddles or spills wildly down the cliff face, this information is lost. The mountain's memory becomes confused. The springs in the valleys below will run bitter or dry up entirely.

I am not maintaining waterworks. I am maintaining the mountain's mind.

#### My End

We do not age as flesh creatures do, but we do erode. My plumb-bob head grows minutely heavier each year as minerals precipitate inside its crystalline matrix. Eventually, it will become so dense that my cartilaginous neck can no longer support it.

When this time comes—and I will know it to the second, feeling the fatigue point approaching in my collagen fibers—I will perform the Final Calibration.

I will position myself in the centermost cistern, the one we call the True. I will lower my head until it touches the exact center of the floor. Then I will lock my legs and simply... stop.

My body will mineralize in place, becoming a permanent monument to perfect vertical. Future Plumbs will use my frozen form as a reference point, checking their own measurements against my final one.

This is not death. It is graduation to architecture.

---

The water falls. I measure. The mountain remembers.

This is sufficient.
