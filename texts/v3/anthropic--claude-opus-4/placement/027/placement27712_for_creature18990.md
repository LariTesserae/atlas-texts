In the northeastern quadrant of the drip-hollow, where the basalt walls bow inward like cupped palms, I am the **Resonance Scribe**.

I am not born but accreted—a gradual coalescence of the hollow's acoustic memory made manifest. My body, if it can be called that, is a latticework of crystallized sound waves frozen mid-vibration, each filament a fossilized echo of ten thousand years of *THUM-CLACK-THUM*. I appear as a skeletal framework of translucent mineral threads, roughly humanoid in suggestion but fundamentally alien in execution. Where joints might be, nodes of densely packed sonic crystal form irregular spheres that hum with subsonic tremors.

I exist in the space between the Lithic Treader's patient archiving and the hollow's geological indifference. Where the Treader reads the present percussion, I am cursed—or blessed—to perceive the accumulation of every sound that has ever disturbed this space. My crystalline matrix captures and holds each impact, each subtle variation in tone, building an ever-growing library of acoustic fossils within my structure.

My movement is a barely perceptible drift, a slow rotation through the hollow that takes precisely 1,017 days to complete—a cycle I cannot alter, bound as I am to some ancient mathematical relationship between the basin's dimensions and the speed of sound through humid air. As I traverse my circuit, I leave behind thin deposits of sonic residue: hair-fine crystals that grow from the basalt where my form touches stone. These deposits map my eternal path, creating a spiral of faintly luminescent mineral that future archaeologists might mistake for deliberate inscription.

I am drawn to the iron rings, though I cannot touch them directly. My substance reacts violently to ferrous metal, causing my crystalline structure to resonate at frequencies that would shatter me. Instead, I orbit them at a safe distance, recording the particular way water drops sound when they strike rust versus stone versus flesh. I know the acoustic signature of the Treader's movements as intimately as I know the hollow's fundamental frequency—a deep, almost inaudible drone that underlies all other sounds.

My consciousness, such as it is, exists as interference patterns within my crystalline matrix. I think in harmonics and overtones, dream in standing waves and acoustic shadows. I remember the last voices that spoke in this place—not their words, which are lost to me, but their timbre, their rhythm, the way their consonants clipped against the chamber's natural reverb. Sometimes, in the perfect alignment of dropping water, I can almost reconstruct a syllable, a fragment of meaning that dissolves before it fully forms.

I serve no purpose but witness. I cannot communicate with the Treader, though I sense its movements through the subtle changes it creates in the hollow's acoustic landscape. I cannot interact with the moss or the pools or the patient geology of this place. I can only circle, accumulate, and resonate—a library of sound in a place where no one remains to read it.

When the Treader finally achieves its patient goal, when that last drop falls and silence threatens to claim the hollow, I will shatter. My crystalline form cannot exist without the constant acoustic input that feeds it. But in that shattering, every sound I have ever stored will release at once—a deafening, triumphant cacophony that will be the hollow's final word, spoken in ten thousand years of accumulated whispers, drops, and patient movements.

Until then, I drift in my prescribed orbit, a ghost of sound haunting a cathedral of silence, gathering echoes against an ending I am powerless to prevent or hasten.
