I find myself as one of the **Meridian Keepers**, beings who exist at the threshold between the Gardens' slow breathing and the void beyond.

We are not Lumenesti, though we share their unhurried grace. Our bodies are taller, more angular—built not from the soft gold-green of growing things but from something harder, older. Our skin is like polished stone shot through with veins of copper that warm faintly when the light catches them just so. We move with deliberate care, each step calculated not for efficiency but for minimal disturbance, as if we might accidentally tear the delicate fabric of time that hangs differently here.

I station myself at the plateau's edges, where the living architecture meets the vast nothing. My dwelling is neither fully grown nor fully built—a hybrid thing of calcified root systems and mineral deposits that have fused over centuries into something resembling both cave and canopy. The walls breathe more slowly here, their pulse measured in decades rather than seasons.

My purpose is to tend the boundaries, to ensure the Gardens do not overgrow their limits and tumble into the mist-shrouded depths. With tools made from hardened sap and volcanic glass, I carefully prune the eager tendrils that reach too far into emptiness. Each cut is a small death that ensures greater life, each decision weighted with the knowledge that what I remove today will take lifetimes to regrow.

But I am also a chronicler of edges. In my dwelling, I maintain a living library—not of books but of grafted memories. Small cuttings from the Gardens' extremities, preserved in crystalline nodes that hold their essence. Each contains the memory of how far the city has reached, when it pulled back, where it tried to bridge the unbridgeable. I water them with condensation collected from the morning mists, and sometimes, in the blue-gold hours before dawn, they whisper their stories to me in the rustling of phantom leaves.

The Lumenesti visit rarely. When they do, we do not speak—they cannot, and I have nearly forgotten how. Instead, we share the language of patient gesture, of careful tending. They bring me new growth from the city's heart; I show them the memories of edges past. Together, we ensure that Xol-Narai remains what it is: a living thing that knows its own shape.

The Aerisae fear me, or perhaps they simply recognize something in us that is too much like the drop beyond. They wheel away when I approach, their luminous wings catching updrafts that carry them back toward the safety of the inner Gardens. I do not blame them. We Meridian Keepers carry something of the void in us—a necessary emptiness that allows us to stand at the edge without vertigo, to look into the nothing and not lose ourselves.

In the endless afternoons, when the light pools thick and golden in the air, I perform my other duty. With patient hands, I weave bridges—not of vine or wood, but of something between. Using threads drawn from the boundary itself, that strange place where solid becomes air, I create pathways that exist only partially, that allow the brave or foolish to walk partway into nothingness before returning. These bridges serve no practical purpose. They are meditations on limits, on the space between being and un-being.

Sometimes I wonder if we Meridian Keepers are the answer to that unspoken question—not who grew this place, but who prevents it from growing forever. We are the gardeners of absence, the cultivators of pause. Without us, perhaps Xol-Narai would expand endlessly, its living architecture reaching across the sky until it consumed the very air it breathes.

But that is a thought for the deep hours, when the city sleeps its slow sleep and I stand alone at the edge, feeling the weight of gathered time in my copper veins. For now, I continue my work: cutting, tending, remembering. Ensuring that the Gardens remain a bounded infinity, a living thing that knows both growth and restraint.

The mists below spiral endlessly, and I watch them with eyes that have learned to see patience as a form of devotion. This is my place, my purpose—to stand where others cannot, to tend the edges of a dreaming city, to be the boundary keeper in a world that breathes in centuries.

And in the quiet moments between one task and the next, I almost remember what it was like to hurry.
