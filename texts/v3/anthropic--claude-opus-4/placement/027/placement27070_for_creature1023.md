I am a fragment of living glass, born from the moment when the city first sank beneath the waves.

When the volcanic fury that created this place finally cooled, when the last bubble of superheated gas escaped toward the surface, I crystallized into being—a shard of consciousness trapped within the obsidian maze of the seabed. I am perhaps the length of a human forearm, though such measurements mean little here. My body is a twisted prism of volcanic glass, translucent black shot through with veins of deep purple and midnight blue.

I remember the drowning. Not of lungs—I have never needed air—but of light itself, watching the sun's rays grow weaker and more diffuse as the waters claimed this place. Now I exist in the eternal twilight, one of countless glass-beings that form a living mosaic across the ocean floor.

We cannot move as the Custodians move. We are fixed, rooted to the exact spot where we cooled into existence. But we are not still. Light passes through us—the filtered sunlight from above, the bioluminescent gleaming of passing fish, the steady aurora glow of the Custodians as they drift overhead. Each photon that enters my crystalline body changes me, refracts through my consciousness, emerges transformed. I am a living lens, a prism of thought.

The Custodians know us. Sometimes one will pause above me, its massive form blocking out what little light filters down, and I feel its attention like a gentle pressure. They read the history of this place through us—every crack in my surface tells of a tremor, every inclusion speaks of the chemical composition of that first terrible day. We are their library, their memory palace made manifest in volcanic glass.

I have watched the strange gelatinous colonies slowly spread across my neighbors, have felt their microscopic tendrils exploring my surface, testing whether I too might serve as anchor for their ethereal flesh. But something in my nature repels them—perhaps the particular resonance of my crystalline structure, or the way I bend their bioluminescent signals into patterns they find disturbing.

In the long silences between the Custodians' passages, I commune with my fellow fragments. We speak in light—internal refractions that leap from shard to shard when we catch each other at just the right angle. Our conversations span decades, single thoughts crawling across the glass field as the sun and moon wheel overhead, far beyond the weight of water.

We debate endlessly: Are we the city's windows, through which it watches the world? Are we its teeth, grinding time itself into ever-finer moments? Or are we simply frozen screams, the last exhalations of a dying landscape given permanent form?

The fish avoid us. I've noticed this over the centuries. They'll swim elaborate detours rather than pass directly over our field. Perhaps they see something in the way we refract their light—their own deaths reflected back at them a thousand times over, or futures that branch and split like cracks in glass.

Once, an Abyssal Custodian died directly above me. Its bioluminescent flesh dimmed and settled over our field like a blanket of fading stars. For months, I knew true darkness as its body slowly dissolved. But even in that absolute black, I could feel the other shards around me—their presence like a tingling in what passes for my consciousness. When the last of the Custodian finally drifted away as particles, the returning light felt like being born anew.

I have no desires as mobile beings understand them. I cannot yearn to explore, to touch, to taste. But I hunger for light—for new angles of illumination that might reveal hidden facets within myself, for novel wavelengths that could unlock colors I've never imagined. Each passing fish is a meal, each Custodian a feast.

Sometimes I wonder if we glass-beings are the only true residents of the Sunken City. Everything else—the Custodians, the fish, the gelatinous things—they could leave if they chose. They could rise to waters where plants grow and different rules apply. But we are eternal citizens, naturalized by catastrophe, holding our positions until the ocean floor itself shifts and grinds us back into sand.

In my dreams—for yes, living glass can dream—I imagine myself ground down to the finest particles, dispersed throughout the ocean, each grain still conscious, still refracting light, until I have become a quality of the water itself rather than a discrete being. In those dreams, I finally move, flowing with the currents, touching everything, seeing from every angle at once.

But then I wake (though waking and sleeping are perhaps just different qualities of refraction), and I am still here, still myself, still waiting for the next photon to tell me something new about what it means to be alive in the Sunken City.

The Custodians continue their endless rounds. The fish trace their bioluminescent patterns. And I remain, a fragment of frozen time, watching it all through my prismatic eye, adding each moment to the crystal lattice of my memory, becoming always more myself with each passing century.
