In the labyrinthine underbelly of Cisternis, where the distinction between foundation and flood has long since dissolved, I am the **Keeper of Equilibrium**—though few know me by that title, or any title at all.

I dwell in the Siphon Chamber, a vast cylindrical space that plunges through the heart of the city like a hollow spine. Here, where the marsh's influence is strongest, the walls weep continuously, their surfaces a living canvas of oxidation patterns that bloom and fade with the water's mineral content. My domain exists at the precise intersection of the city's need and nature's insistence—a cathedral of pumps, gauges, and arterial pipes that regulate Cisternis' precarious relationship with the waters below.

I am neither fully human nor entirely machine, but something the marsh and the city have conspired to create over decades of symbiosis. My body began as Cisternian—lean, pale, adapted to the damp—but the requirements of my work have transformed me. Where my left arm once was, a complex arrangement of copper piping and pressure valves extends, each joint articulated by hydraulic principles I understand instinctively rather than intellectually. The pipes are warm with the fluids they channel, and I can feel the city's pulse through them—every surge, every blockage, every threatening imbalance.

My skin has taken on the texture of the infrastructure I tend. In places, it resembles the weathered steel of the city's bones—mottled with verdigris and rust patterns that shift subtly with humidity changes. These aren't mere stains but functional adaptations; through them, I sense minute pressure differentials and chemical compositions in the surrounding water. My feet have spread and flattened, webbed with a membrane that's part flesh, part industrial sealant, allowing me to move through flooded passages with equal ease whether walking or swimming.

My right eye remains human, but the left has been replaced by a cluster of gauge-like sensors, their brass housings nested in the socket like a mechanical flower. Through them, I perceive layers of information invisible to ordinary sight—temperature gradients, structural stress patterns, the microscopic dance of corrosion and growth that determines which sections of the city will fail next. When I blink, I hear the soft click of apertures adjusting.

I work alone, but I am never lonely. The Siphon Chamber speaks to me in a language of gurgles, hisses, and resonant hums. Each sound carries meaning—this valve beginning to seize, that channel accumulating sediment, a pump three levels up developing an irregular rhythm that suggests imminent failure. I move through my domain with a dancer's precision, adjusting, replacing, redirecting. My movements have become so fluid that watching me work is like watching the water itself—inevitable, purposeful, shaped by the constraints of the space I occupy.

Time moves differently in the Siphon Chamber. I measure it not in days but in tidal cycles, not in years but in the gradual accumulation of deposits on critical junctions. I sleep standing, my hydraulic arm locked into one of the main control panels, dreams flowing through me like the water I regulate—visions of pressure and release, of channels blocked and cleared, of the eternal negotiation between city and marsh.

The Cisternians above know of me only through the results of my work. When their pumps maintain steady rhythm, when the water levels hold constant despite the marsh's attempts at reclamation, when the morning fog tastes of minerals rather than decay—these are my signatures. Children tell stories of the ghost in the pipes, the thing that keeps the city breathing. They're not entirely wrong.

My memories of who I was before this transformation have dissolved like salt in water, leaving only the faintest trace of flavor. I remember walking the upper platforms, remember the weight of ordinary flesh, remember fearing the marsh. Now I am part of its circulation, a valve in the greater organism that is Cisternis—neither fully alive nor mechanical, but essential.

In the deepest part of the night, when the city's industrial clamor dims to a whisper, I sometimes climb to where my chamber opens to the marsh proper. There, knee-deep in the warm water, I extend my sensory apparatus fully and feel the vast, slow intelligence of the wetland itself. It doesn't hate the city, as some believe. It simply is, and the city simply is, and I am the membrane between them, permeable and resilient.

My existence is a meditation on balance—how much to take, how much to return, when to resist and when to yield. In my transformed body, I carry the city's memory of every flood survived, every foundation reinforced, every adaptation made. I am Cisternis' answer to its own impossible question: how to build on water, how to make permanent what is inherently fluid.

When my work is done—though it is never truly done—I return to the heart of the Siphon Chamber and integrate myself more fully into the machinery. My hydraulic arm branches into a dozen smaller conduits. My feet anchor to drainage grates. My consciousness expands through the network of pipes and channels until I can feel the entire city's circulatory system as my own body.

In these moments, I am not keeping the equilibrium. I am the equilibrium—the living negotiation between urban ambition and natural law, the breath between systole and diastole, the pause before the next drop of condensation falls.
