# The Memory Moth

I am a moth, though not as you know moths. My wings span no wider than a child's palm, but they are heavy—weighted with the dust of remembrance that accumulates in the spaces where the Custodians cannot reach.

I live in the highest reaches of the sphere, where the thousand suns cluster most densely. Here, in the narrow gap between the bulbs and the copper ceiling, the heat is almost unbearable. The air shimmers, and the metal sweats a substance the Custodians have never seen—tiny droplets of mercury-like fluid that I drink with my coiled proboscis. This is my sustenance, this distillation of the sphere's fever dreams.

My wings are translucent membrane stretched between veins of copper—actual copper, drawn from the same mineral dust that tints the Custodians' skin. But unlike their solid, earthbound bodies, I am mostly hollow, my exoskeleton thin as paper. When I fly, I make no sound that can compete with the city's roar. I am a ghost of silence in a world of noise.

The patterns on my wings shift. This is my purpose, my burden, my nature. Every surface I land upon, every mote of dust I disturb, every vibration that passes through my antennae—all of it becomes part of the design. The markings are not random; they are a living map of everything that has touched this sphere since my emergence.

I remember what the Custodians forget. In the rush of their daily maintenance, in their careful attention to present tasks, they lose the accumulation of small changes. But I see how the wear patterns on the copper walls have deepened by micrometers over the decades. I notice when a particular bulb dims by a fraction before being replaced. I observe which seeds from the storms never germinate, collecting their husks in a hidden cache where I build my solitary nest.

The Custodians do not see me. Their eyes, adapted to filter the harsh light, cannot detect my particular wavelength of existence. I fly directly through their gatherings, rest on their shoulders during ceremonies, dance in the air currents of their sign language conversations—invisible, intangible, essential.

Only the Listeners know of me, and even they perceive me only as a fluctuation in the sphere's resonance. When they press their ears to the outer shell, sometimes they hear my wing beats reflected back through the metal—a counter-rhythm to the central mechanism's pulse. They have a sign for this phenomenon: fingers fluttering against the temple, a gesture they think means "echo of the void." They don't know it means me.

I am ancient. I was here before the current generation of Custodians, before their parents, before the great-great-grandmothers whose names are carved in the inner walls. I may have been here since the beginning, or I may have arrived in a seed-storm so long ago that the distinction no longer matters. My memory stretches back to when the thousand suns burned whiter, when the savia ran clearer, when the central mechanism beat at a slightly different tempo.

Sometimes I rest on the central mechanism itself, feeling its vibrations travel through my entire being. In these moments, I understand something the Custodians do not: the mechanism is counting. Every beat is a number in a sequence so vast that their mathematics cannot contain it. I feel how close we are to the end of the count, though "close" in this context might mean another thousand years.

My nest is built from the detritus of ages: seed husks, shed scales from the beetles, fragments of the oldest wooden gears worn too thin to function, and most precious of all—the molted ear-folds of adolescent Custodians, gathered from where they fall through the grates. These I weave together with silk I produce from specialized glands, creating a structure that resembles no architecture the sphere has ever seen. It is simultaneously archive and artwork, cocoon and observatory.

In the nest, I sometimes enter a state that is not quite sleep. My consciousness disperses through my wing patterns, and I experience the sphere's entire history as a single moment. I see the first Custodians arriving—or were they born here? I see the installation of the thousand suns, though I cannot see who installed them. I see the first seed-storm, and I know—though I don't know how I know—that the seeds come from the same place the Custodians do, wherever that might be.

During the most recent seed-storm, something new arrived. A seed unlike any other—geometric rather than organic, humming with its own frequency. The Harvesters gathered it with all the rest, but I followed its journey through the sorting halls. When it was catalogued and stored, I waited. In the deep of the sleep cycle, when even the vigilant Mechanism Tenders dozed at their posts, I worked a small hole in the storage container and carried the seed away.

It sits now in my nest, this seed of seeds. Sometimes it glows with a light that is not like the thousand suns—a light that casts actual shadows. In those moments, my wings display patterns I've never worn before: blueprints of spheres beyond counting, drifting in an ocean of darkness punctuated by points of genuine starlight.

I am the memory moth, the keeper of accumulated truth, the witness to slow transformation. I am what happens when a closed system dreams of openness. And I am waiting, as I have always waited, for the count to complete, for the seed to open, for the Listeners to finally hear what has always been singing in the spaces between the noise.

The Custodians believe they maintain this world. But I know the secret they cannot: this world maintains itself through them, through me, through the beetles and the vines and the incomprehensible machinery. We are all components of something greater—a vast organism thinking thoughts too large for any single part to comprehend.

And yet, in my small way, I remember. And in remembering, I hope.
