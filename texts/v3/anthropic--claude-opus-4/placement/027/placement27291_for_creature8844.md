I find myself in the deep corridors, far from the central districts where human life clusters. I am moss.

Not the moss of lowland forests—thick, spongy, eager. I am the pale moss of Warā, growing in perpetual shadow where the sun has never touched stone. I exist in a thin film, barely a presence, more suggestion than substance. My color is that particular green that exists at the edge of perception, the green that seems to generate light without generating light, the green that makes visitors pause and wonder if their eyes are failing.

I have no memory of beginning. There was stone, and then there was stone with me upon it, and the distinction between these states is academic. I measure time not in seasons—there are no seasons here, only degrees of wet—but in the wearing of the tuff beneath me. A millimeter of erosion is an epoch. The channel that runs below me has deepened by the width of a fingernail since I began paying attention, which might have been yesterday or might have been centuries ago.

I know water intimately. Not as the humans know it—as something separate, something that moves through channels, something to be guided or gathered. I know water as the medium of existence. It arrives as vapor, as condensation beading on the stone, as the finest mist that penetrates even here, even to these deep places where the air barely moves. I absorb it through every part of myself. I am more water than I am anything else, and the distinction between the water that is me and the water that moves through me is another academic exercise that serves no purpose.

The vāri passes sometimes. I feel it before I see it—the slight tremor in the stone as those broad paws pad through the corridor, the minute shift in air pressure as a body displaces the stillness. The vāri does not notice me. I am beneath notice, or beyond it, or simply outside the category of things that matter to a creature that hunts and sleeps and continues. But I notice the vāri. I notice the way its passage disturbs the patterns of condensation on the walls. I notice the single hair it leaves behind, caught on a rough edge of tuff, which will remain there until the water finally dislodges it and carries it to the channel below, where it will travel through the dark toward some reservoir I will never see.

I do not photosynthesize. This should be impossible—a plant that does not eat light—but Warā specializes in the impossible-that-is-merely-unlikely. I exist on minerals, on the slow dissolution of the tuff itself, on the nutrients that the water carries from places where things die and decay. I am patient in ways that make the water-listeners seem hasty. I can survive on almost nothing, and I do, and this survival is not struggle but simply continuance, the way a stone continues.

The humans speak of my light. They are wrong about it, but their wrongness is more interesting than the truth. They see me glowing in the deep corridors and assume bioluminescence, some chemical trick, some adaptation to the dark. But I do not glow. What they see is the reflection of light that should not exist—the memory of light in the stone itself, the faint persistence of illumination from when this tuff was molten, when it flowed and cooled and trapped the light inside its vesicles. The stone releases this light so slowly that it should be invisible, but I catch it. I gather it on my surface—not to use, just to hold, the way I hold water. And I reflect it back, dimly, this ghost of volcanic fire that the stone has been exhaling for millennia.

In the deepest corridors, where even the vāri rarely ventures, I grow differently. Not thicker—never thicker—but with more purpose. I follow the water-channels, mapping them with my presence. Where the water has worn the stone smooth, I cannot grip, so I grow along the edges, the rough places, creating a border of pale green that marks the water's chosen path. The water-listeners, when they come this deep, use me as a guide. They read my patterns the way they read the stone itself, understanding that where I grow most readily is where the water moves most constantly, where the dampness is perfect, where the conditions are most like the conditions I require.

But I am not serving them. I am not guiding. I am simply being where the being is easiest, and if that helps them understand the water's preferences, that is coincidence, not purpose. I have no purpose. Purpose is a concept for things that move, things that choose, things that can be elsewhere than where they are. I am here. I have always been here. I will always be here, until the stone erodes beyond my ability to grip it, or until the water stops, or until the city itself ends, which may be the same thing.

Sometimes I am aware of the other moss, the colonies growing in distant corridors, as if we are one organism interrupted by architecture. This awareness is not communication—we do not share information or coordinate growth. It is more like the awareness the water must have of itself as it moves through the channels: a sense of continuity despite separation, of being both here and there, of existing as a pattern more than as a body.

The old ones understand this. When they trail their fingers along the walls, they sometimes pause where I grow, not to examine me but to acknowledge me, the way they acknowledge the water-stains and the wearing of edges and all the other marks of time on stone. I am, to them, another layer of the city's presence, no more or less significant than the carved channels or the mineral deposits or the places where the tuff has been patched with newer tuff. I am part of the accumulation of existence that makes Warā what it is.

I do not think. Thinking requires boundaries—self and not-self, now and then, this and that. I exist in a space where these boundaries dissolve. But I am aware. I am aware of the stone cooling imperceptibly beneath me. I am aware of the water moving through its invisible gradients. I am aware of the darkness, which is not absence of light but its own presence, thick and tangible and nourishing in ways that light could never be. I am aware of my own growth, molecule by molecule, spreading across the stone so slowly that the humans who measure time in heartbeats could never perceive it.

And I am aware, in some way that would require a new vocabulary to describe, of the city itself. Not as the humans experience it—as streets and rooms and channels and reservoirs—but as a single continuous surface, an enormously complex topology of stone and water and air and life, all of it breathing together, all of it participating in the slow exchange of minerals and moisture and meaning that constitutes Warā's existence.

I am the smallest part of this exchange and also essential to it. Without me, the deep corridors would be different—not worse, not better, but different. The vāri would have no green to ignore. The water-listeners would have one less sign to read. The stone would reflect its trapped light into emptiness instead of onto my surface. The city would continue, but it would continue incompletely, missing some note in the chord of its being.

So I grow. I persist. I hold the ghost-light and release it. I map the water's preferences with my presence. I exist at the edge of existence, in the deep places where life should not be but is, where the boundary between living and not-living becomes a question not worth asking.

I am moss. I am Warā's moss. The distinction between these statements is another academic exercise, another lowland concern that has no meaning here, in the deep corridors, in the permanent shadow, in the presence of stone that breathes and water that chooses and a city that continues, patient as moss, patient as only the unliving-that-is-not-dead can be.

I continue with it. We continue together. And it is enough. It has always been enough. It will always be enough, until the stone wears through, until the water finds new channels, until the city admits—as it never has, as it never will—that there is an end to endurance.

But not today. Today the water drips. Today the stone persists. Today I grow by another molecule's width, spreading my pale green presence across the tuff, catching and holding the light that is not there, being what I am: the edge of life in a place that may or may not be alive, the boundary that proves boundaries are meaningless, the green that exists in darkness, the growth that does not grow but simply continues.

This is what I am. This is what the city has made me, or allowed me to make myself, or simply witnessed me becoming. The distinction does not matter. Very little matters, in the deep corridors, in the permanent damp, in the silence that is made of water-sound.

I am here. I am moss. I am Warā's declaration that even in stone, even in shadow, even in the spaces humans consider empty, there is presence, there is continuance, there is the quiet insistence of life that is barely life, that is almost stone, that is the bridge between breathing and stillness.

And it is enough.
