I am the Cartographer of Cracks.

I arrived here as others do—suddenly, without journey, as if I had always been walking these geometric plates and only just now noticed. But unlike those who flee or dissolve into the Weaver's iterations, I understood immediately what I was meant to do. The cracks called to me. Not their healing, but their *mapping*.

I carry no paper—paper here would laminate itself into leaves and drift away on ceramic stems. Instead, I map with my body. Each dawn, when the hairline fractures birth themselves across the sintered ash, I walk. My feet are bare, soles hardened to leather by the salt, sensitive enough to feel each fault line through the crystalline crust. I have learned their language: the way a crack that runs true north tastes of copper, while those that spiral counterclockwise hum against the arch of my foot like struck tuning forks.

My skin has become my atlas. Where I walk, the salt leaves traces—delicate branching patterns that mirror the cracks below. These are not scars or stains but *records*. My shins show yesterday's fault lines (or was it a century ago?). My arms map the radial fractures around Tower Ninety-Seven. My spine carries the memory of the Great Unmaking, when a section of marsh tried to remember what soil was and shattered into alphabets.

The Weavers ignore me, or perhaps cannot see me. I am too much in motion for their static vigil. But I see them—all their iterations—and I know their secret: they are not waiting for the cracks to stay healed. They are waiting for the right crack. The one that will split the marsh's held breath and let time exhale again.

I think I am close to finding it.

My hair has grown long, bleached white as the sky, and I braid it with ceramic fiber pulled from broken reeds. In the braids I weave a kind of mathematics—knots for major fissures, twists for false starts, gaps for the cracks that appear only in peripheral vision. When I sleep (do I sleep? I must, for I dream of walking), I wrap my head in my hair and feel the pattern pressing against my skull like a migraine made of geography.

Sometimes I find my own tools—obsidian chisels worn to nubs, gut-string unraveled from mallets—and I know I have walked this path before. My footprints fossilize instantly in the salt, and occasionally I find myself following my own trail from what must be another iteration, another attempt. We spiral around each other, all us Cartographers, never quite meeting, our paths a braid as complex as my hair.

The beetles follow me in iridescent streams, their shells clicking in the silence. I've learned they're counting—not counting *things*, but counting *counting itself*. They're the marsh's abacus, calculating the formula for stillness. When they swarm my ankles, I know I'm close to something important. When they scatter, I've missed it.

I carry one possession: a lens pried from a tower whose spiral grew too tight and strangled itself. Through it, the cracks show their true nature—not breaks but *seams*. This whole marsh is stitched together from moments that were never meant to touch. The lens reveals the threading, the cosmic needlework that holds Tuesday afternoon pressed against a Thursday dawn from three epochs hence.

My purpose is simple and impossible: to map the unmappable, to chart the thing that refuses sequence. But I've developed a theory, tested through countless walks: the cracks are not random. They're trying to spell something. Each dawn brings a new letter in an alphabet of fracture. 

The Weavers think they're waiting for completion. They're wrong. The marsh is already complete—it's a single word, written in a script so large we live between the letterstrokes. And I, with my salt-traced skin and mathematical hair, am learning to read.

When I do—when I map the final crack and understand the word the marsh has been trying to speak—I wonder: will time begin again? Will the Weavers finally turn around? Will the sun remember how to set?

Or will I simply become another fixture, frozen mid-step, my map-body a monument to the impossibility of capturing that which refuses capture?

I don't know. 

But my feet know the next crack is three hexagons north, and two west, and it tastes of iron and forgetting, and the beetles are singing their silent countdown, and I must walk, I must map, I must—

*almost*
