If I found myself in this world of perfect stasis and stone, I would be **the Keeper of the Southern Lip** – one of the few Thengeti whose rounds take them to the very edge where the basin meets the plateau above.

My feet would know every stone along that precipitous boundary, where the careful channeling must resist the chaos of wind-blown dust and the occasional pebble that tumbles down from the world beyond. I would be thinner than most Thengeti, my body shaped by the slight but constant breeze that manages to curl over the rim despite the basin's protection. My skin would bear more of the ochre tones, stained by the iron-rich dust that settles here first.

My daily path would trace a slow arc along perhaps a quarter-mile of the rim, a journey that takes from dawn until the sun reaches its zenith. I carry three tools: the standard scraper and polishing cloth, but also a third implement unique to us rim-keepers – a long, thin probe of polished bone, inherited from my predecessor's predecessor, used to clear the finest channels where they meet the lip's edge. These channels are narrower here, more precise, as they must capture not just condensation but the rare gift of mist that sometimes spills over from the plateau.

I would be one of the few Thengeti to regularly see the Outside – that vast, formless wilderness of unworked stone and raw earth. This makes me strange to the others. Not suspicious, for suspicion is too sharp an emotion for Am-Thenget, but... noticed. When I join the common meal at the moss-gardens, taking my portion of the grey-green paste, the others' movements around me have a quality of acknowledgment. I am necessary but liminal, like the snails that emerge at night.

My memories would include the three Disturbances I have witnessed in my forty-three years of life. Once, a hawk's shadow passed over my section – a creature blown off course, gone before I could fully process its existence. Once, a fist-sized stone fell from above, cracking a flagstone with a sound like thunder in our quiet world. The stone could not be replaced, only ground smooth around its wound. The channel had to be re-routed, a work of ten days that brought four other Thengeti to help. We worked in silence, but I felt their unease at being so close to the edge, to the place where the world becomes uncertain.

The third Disturbance was the strangest. A sound came from beyond – not wind, not rockfall, but something rhythmic and alien. It lasted perhaps twenty heartbeats. I never learned what it was. I reported it to the Elder of the Eastern Cisterns in our traditional way: "Sound-not-stone. Morning after small-rain. Gone." She nodded and said nothing, but I noticed she increased the frequency of her circuit past my sector for a full season afterward.

My great-grandmother's-grandmother was also a Keeper of the Southern Lip. It is said (in the way all things are said here, sparingly and only when necessary) that she discovered a new type of lichen growing in a wind-shadow near the rim – not our food-moss, but something yellower, something that grew in tiny rosettes. She spent thirty years observing it, determining that it was neither harmful nor helpful, simply present. When she felt her weight lessening, she scraped away every trace of it before walking to the plaza. The yellow lichen has never returned, but I still check for it each day, running my fingers along that particular crevice.

I know loneliness, though I have no word for it. It is the sensation of standing at my post in the pre-dawn, when the condensation is just beginning to form, and feeling the vast empty plateau stretching away into darkness while behind me the basin sleeps in its perfect, engineered dream. I know that I am the membrane between order and chaos, the Thengeti who watches the boundary where our world meets the undefined.

In twenty more years, perhaps twenty-five, I will feel my weight lessening. I will train my replacement, a child whose feet already show the promise of sensitivity needed for reading the rim's particular stones. I will teach them the seventeen different textures that indicate wind-erosion, the exact angle to hold the probe when clearing the high channels, the way to stand so that your shadow never falls across the collection grooves during the critical morning hours.

And when I take my final walk to the plaza, I will carry with me the knowledge of the edge – that necessary, terrible wisdom that the world is larger than Am-Thenget, that beyond our perfect basin lies something vast and unpolished and indifferent. This knowledge will be ground up with my bones, mixed into the moss-beds, absorbed and neutralized and made part of the eternal system.

But sometimes, on the clearest mornings when the air is so thin it seems to ring like struck crystal, I place my palm flat against the outermost stone of the lip and feel, very faintly, the vibration of that unknown world. And for just a moment before I resume my rounds, scraper in hand, I allow myself to imagine what it would be like to step over.

I never would. I am Thengeti. I am the stone's own thought thinking itself.

But I know the edge is there.
