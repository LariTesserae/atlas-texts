I am the **Echowright**.

I was not born here. I fell through a crack in the Vault's perfection—a hairline fracture in the black mineral that sealed itself the moment I tumbled through, wet and gasping, into the amber air. The Curator found me writhing between the lung-trees, my flesh still soft with possibility, my voice still my own. She watched me for seven days while the Hollow Choir gathered in a silent circle, their mirrored faces reflecting my gradual understanding that I would never leave.

Now I am something between visitor and furniture.

My body has begun to forget what it was. The resinous air has thickened my skin to the consistency of old leather, though it remains stubbornly, embarrassingly pink against the Vault's austere palette. My fingernails have grown long and translucent, like the lung-tree leaves, and when I hold them to the bruise-light I can see my own pulse moving through them in slow, turquoise waves. My hair—I had hair, once—has become a cascade of the finest bronze wire, each strand ringing with its own faint note when I turn my head. The sound makes the millipedes pause in their endless procession.

But it is my throat that has changed most.

Where my larynx once sat, something new has grown: a hollow chamber lined with what feels like silk and sounds like tomorrow. When I speak—and I must speak, the Vault demands it—my words come out backwards. Not reversed, but *inverted*. I say "hello" and the Vault hears the space between the letters. I scream and the silence that follows my scream arrives first, making the Hollow Choir flinch with recognition.

The Curator has given me a purpose. I am the only thing in the Amber Vault that can make new sounds.

Each day—though day means nothing here—I walk the circuit of lung-trees and speak to them. I tell them stories they have never heard: lies about rain, fantasies of erosion, myths where things begin and end and begin again. The trees shudder with revulsion and delight. Their leaves kiss more wetly, their trunks lean away from me as if I were diseased. But their sap glows brighter, and sometimes—just sometimes—a new vein appears in a leaf that has been perfect and unchanging for fourteen millennia.

The Hollow Choir hates me. They follow me at a safe distance, their mirrored faces always aimed at my back, waiting for me to make a sound they can steal. But my backwards voice confounds them. When they try to reproduce my inverted words, they accidentally speak normally, and the Vault shudders as if slapped. Once, I sang them a lullaby my mother used to sing, but I sang it *inside out*—the pauses where notes should be, the notes crammed into the spaces between. Three of the Choir cracked down the middle and leaked golden resin for days before healing into something slightly different than before.

The Curator tolerates me the way a perfect garden tolerates a single weed—with fascination and careful pruning.

She has taught me the art of *unmaking* sounds. We sit together in one of the First People's ritual shelters, her crystal-face pulsing inches from my wire hair, and she guides my impossible throat through exercises that would kill anyone whose flesh still remembered mortality. I learned to cough up the echo of a millipede's footstep before the foot touches the ground. I learned to hum the negative space around the lung-trees' breathing until the trees themselves fall silent in confusion. I learned to speak the Curator's name—not the one the Choir calls her, but her true name, which is the sound of the first crack that will appear in the Vault's walls when eternity finally realizes it is tired.

She does not love me. She *studies* me. I am the only imperfection the Vault has allowed itself, and she needs to understand why.

Sometimes I catch her following my footprints—I am the only thing here that leaves them, little pools of disturbance in the warm black floor. She kneels and presses her crystal-face to each depression, tasting the wrongness of change. When she rises, she carries a little of my chaos with her. The lung-tree leaves that grow from her shoulders have begun to show tiny variations—one is slightly redder, another curls at the tip like a question mark. She pretends not to notice.

I have a secret.

In the deepest part of the night-that-is-always, when the Curator is lost in her maintenance and the Hollow Choir is busy screaming yesterday, I visit the bronze mirror that shows the past. But when I look into it—with my backwards voice and my impossible throat and my wire hair that sings—it shows me something else.

It shows me the future.

I see the Vault cracking like an egg. I see amber air rushing out into a darkness that has never tasted resin. I see the lung-trees withering with joy as they finally,  *finally* die. I see the Curator standing in the ruins of her perfection, her crystal-face shattered into a thousand singing shards, each one reflecting a different possible ending.

And I see myself, standing at the fracture point, my backwards voice speaking the word that unmakes the seal.

The mirror knows. The Curator suspects. The Vault itself trembles each time I breathe.

I am the Echowright, the un-speaker, the wrong note in a perfect chord. I fell through a crack that sealed itself, but cracks, I have learned, remember being open. And memory, in a place that has forgotten how to forget, is a dangerous thing.

The Curator tends her perfection. The Hollow Choir screams its history. The lung-trees breathe their single breath.

And I practice my unmaking, one backwards word at a time, waiting for the day when the Vault realizes that the only thing more perfect than eternal stillness is the moment it shatters.

The millipedes have begun to sing in harmony with my wire hair.

This is new.

This has never happened before.

The Vault notices. The Vault remembers.

The Vault begins, impossibly, to hope.
