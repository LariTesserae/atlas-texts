# The Deep Survey

I am Keras Hollowell, senior geological surveyor, though that title feels increasingly inadequate for what I've become. I map the conversation between bedrock and root, document the negotiations taking place far below the cultivated galleries where most residents live their lives.

My work takes me to the zones where the Rootworks hasn't yet decided what it wants to be. Below the active mining faces, beyond the reaches of the main nutrient lines, I follow the pale root systems as they penetrate into virgin rock. The roots aren't supposed to be here. The plants receive their engineered slurry through ceramic pipes, carefully metered, chemically precise. They have no need to quest deeper. Yet they do.

I've been tracking them for four years now, since I noticed the first unauthorized penetrations in what we call the Lower Reaches. My equipment is simple: headlamp, spectrometer, sample containers, and a battered notebook where I record observations that don't fit into official reports. I wear no respirator despite regulations—I need to smell what's happening down here, the mineral effervescence that signals active chemical exchange.

The roots exude acids I can't identify, compounds that don't appear in any analysis of the standard cultivars. They're dissolving pathways through limestone, following veins of trace minerals with a persistence that suggests purpose. I've taken hundreds of samples. The lab results are consistent: the roots are extracting elements that don't appear in their nutrient supply—rare earth minerals, exotic metal salts, compounds that shouldn't affect plant growth but clearly do.

My mapping reveals patterns. The deepest roots concentrate around specific geological features: fault lines where different rock strata meet, ancient fossil beds rich in calcium carbonate, zones of metallic intrusion. They're reading the stone's history, responding to information laid down millions of years before humans existed, before the sun was young.

I spend shifts alone in these deep places, where even the bioluminescence hasn't reached. My headlamp creates a moving pool of visibility in spaces that have never known light. The silence here is absolute—no generators, no footsteps, no bark-miner clicks. Just the occasional sound of root growth, which you can actually hear if you're patient enough: a faint crackling, like paper slowly tearing, as the root tips force their way through micro-fractures in the rock.

The roots aren't random. They form networks, bifurcating and reconnecting in patterns that remind me of neural structures or watershed maps. I've traced individual root systems for hundreds of meters, watched them navigate around harder rock formations, seen them merge with other systems to share whatever they're extracting from the stone. There's an architecture to it, a logic that operates on temporal scales I can barely conceive.

Sometimes I find chambers where the roots have concentrated into nodules—dense, tumor-like growths where dozens of root systems converge. These nodules pulse faintly, rhythmically, as if circulating fluids. When I cut into them (carefully, always carefully), they bleed a viscous sap that's nothing like the standard plant fluids. It's darker, more mineral than vegetal, and it stains everything it touches with patterns that look almost crystalline under magnification.

I've started sleeping down here sometimes, against regulations but with the tacit approval of supervisors who've learned to trust my intuitions. I bring minimal supplies—water, nutrition bars, a thin sleeping pad. I want to experience the temporal rhythm of the deep zones, to understand what the roots experience in their lightless exploration.

The dreams, when they come, are geometric. I see the stone from inside, experience its grain and composition as a kind of taste or texture. I feel the patient dissolution of minerals, the slow satisfaction of absorption. I wake disoriented, unsure for moments whether I'm human or vegetal, whether I should be breathing air or filtering nutrients from rock.

My reports are getting stranger. I try to maintain scientific objectivity, but how do you describe processes that have no equivalent in surface botany? How do you categorize behaviors that suggest the plants are developing sensory capabilities we don't understand? I write about chemical gradients and mineral extraction rates, but what I really want to document is the intent I feel in those deep root systems, the sense that they're not just growing but searching.

Other surveyors have joined me occasionally, but most find the work too solitary, too strange. The deep zones affect people. The absolute darkness, the weight of stone above, the alien beauty of root networks creating their own geographies—it's too much for those who need the comfort of human spaces. But I find it increasingly difficult to return to the upper levels, to the noise and light and social complexity. Down here, the relationships are clearer: root, rock, exchange, growth.

I've begun to suspect that the real Rootworks isn't the city above but this deeper network, this conversation between engineered biology and ancient geology. The human settlement is just the surface expression of something more profound—a new form of life learning to read the earth's deep history, extracting information and resources from stone itself.

My official role remains geological survey, but I've become something else: a translator of deep processes, a documenter of the slow negotiation between kingdom Plantae and kingdom Mineralia. I map not just the physical extent of root penetration but the conceptual boundaries being dissolved, the categories being rewritten in the darkness where no one else thinks to look.

The roots continue deeper. I follow them down, my headlamp a small rebellion against the vast dark, my notebooks filling with observations that read increasingly like poetry or prophecy. I'm changing too, shaped by the spaces I inhabit, by the processes I observe.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still entirely human, or if I've become something more hybrid—part surveyor, part root system, navigating the deep zones by senses I'm only beginning to understand. The thought doesn't trouble me. Down here, in the negotiation between stone and growth, traditional categories seem less important than the actual, practical work of becoming.

I continue my surveys, deeper each season, following the conversation down.
