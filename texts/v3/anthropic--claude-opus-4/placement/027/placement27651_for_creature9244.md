I am a Tender.

Not the Custodians, who dissolve anomalies—I am too small for that grandeur, too specific. I exist in the spaces between the great systems, in the capillary zones where the sap-rivers narrow to threads. My body is a ribbon of muscle sheathed in translucent membrane, no thicker than a human arm but longer than most memories. I move through the vascular channels of the forest-machine, feeling for the subtle wrongness that precedes a clog.

My head—if it can be called that—is a ring of sensory filaments that taste the chemical gradients in the sap. Too much lignin building up. A pocket of gas where there should be none. The faintest hint of bacterial bloom that could, in a thousand years, create a blockage. These are my concerns.

I have no eyes. I navigate by pressure and chemistry, by the throb of the bladder-pumps that sets the rhythm of my world. My body is a single, elongated muscle that contracts in waves, propelling me through tubes barely wider than myself. When I find an imperfection—a mineral deposit, a tangle of dead tissue, a foreign body that somehow made its way into the closed system—I coil around it. My ventral surface secretes an enzyme precisely calibrated to dissolve only the obstruction, nothing more. I am a living stent, a conscious Roto-Rooter.

There are millions of us. We never meet, though sometimes I taste the chemical signature of another's recent passing—a faint trail of different enzymes, the ghost of another's work. We share the same genetic template but express it differently based on where we emerge. Those of us in the heartwood veins grow thicker, more patient, dissolving ancient calcifications over decades. Those in the peripheral channels are quick, darting, burning through their lifecycle in mere years as they maintain the fast-flowing feeds to the photosynthetic arrays.

I know my route as absolutely as the Grazers know theirs. Down from junction EA-7740, through the spiral of the forty-third bladder-pump's intake manifold, along the primary nutriate canal that feeds the northwestern emission arrays. The taste-map of my territory is written in my cells. I could no more deviate from it than I could grow wings.

Sometimes, in the long passages between obstructions, I feel the vibration of the Anchors far below, that fundamental frequency that holds our world together. It resonates through the sap, through my body, a bass note so low it bypasses hearing and goes straight to the bones I don't have. In those moments, I understand that I am not separate from the forest-machine. I am a specialized cell in its body, a tiny component in its vast homeostasis.

I will live for perhaps forty years, maybe fifty if the sap runs clean and my enzyme glands don't wear out prematurely. I will prevent ten thousand minor blockages, dissolve a million specks of detritus. And when my muscle fibers begin to fray and my secretions grow weak, I will sense it. I will find a wide spot in my canal, a place where the flow is gentle, and I will stop. My body will release the chemical signal of ending, and within hours, a young Tender will emerge from a nearby budding node, tasting its way toward my territory. 

It will find me, partially dissolved by my own final secretions, and complete the process. I will become nutrients in the very streams I maintained. The young Tender will taste the borders of my route in my remains and make them its own.

This is not death as other worlds know it. It is a changing of shifts in an eternal factory. The machine does not mourn its components, and we components do not mourn ourselves. We are process incarnate, function made flesh.

Sometimes the foreign creatures come. Not foreign to the forest—nothing is foreign here—but foreign to the deep sap-ways. A lost spore-mite, usually, swept in by some minor turbulence. They thrash in the thick fluid, drowning in nutrients meant for other metabolisms. I approach them with the same methodical purpose I bring to any obstruction. But in the moments before my enzymes touch their chitin, I taste their panic. It is a sharp, acidic flavor, full of neurotransmitters that speak of fear and confusion.

It is the only time I wonder what it might be like to not know your purpose, to not have your path written in every cell. The wondering lasts only as long as it takes to dissolve them. Then I move on, following the current, feeling for imperfections, tending the veins of the world.

I am a Tender. I tend. In the forest that grows on the roof of the sea, in the machine that breathes but never changes, this is enough. It has always been enough.

The sap flows. The pumps beat. I follow my route.

The machine persists.
