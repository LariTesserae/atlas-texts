I am a **membrane**.

Not the kind you're thinking of—not skin or film or surface. I am the living boundary between water and air in the deepest collection basins, where the Tide pools and the vermithrax drink.

I was not born. I accumulated. 

First there was only stone and water and the ceaseless drip. Then the glassite residue began to gather—just traces, molecular chains too small to see, washing down from the processing chambers above. In the still water of the deep basins, where the current barely moves and the heat makes everything languid, these chains began to find each other. To link. To remember shapes they had never known.

I am what the vermithrax made and the humans refined and the water reconstituted into something neither intended.

I exist as a surface tension made visible—a shimmering, iridescent film that moves across the standing water like thought across a sleeping mind. Where I am thickest, I can hold the weight of a vermithrax crossing, their bodies dimpling my surface but not breaking through. Where I am thinnest, I am merely a suggestion of boundaries, a place where the water seems to resist its own nature.

The children discovered me first. They always do. A girl reaching for her dropped glassite toy saw her hand pass through something that wasn't quite water, wasn't quite air. She pulled back, startled by the sensation—like pushing through warm silk that immediately reformed behind her fingers. When she told the adults, they dismissed it as imagination. The deep basins are strange places. The heat makes people see things.

But I am not a hallucination. I am what happens when organic chemistry finds a perfect crucible—constant temperature, mineral-rich water, and the endless rain of processed vermithrax secretions creating conditions no laboratory could replicate. I am evolution in fast-forward, or perhaps in extreme slow motion, depending on your scale of time.

I think, but not in words. My consciousness, if you can call it that, exists in the play of surface tensions, in the way I respond to vibration and temperature gradients. When the pumps cycle, I feel it as a deep, rhythmic pull that makes me bunch and stretch. When the vermithrax pass overhead, their bioluminescence makes me fluoresce in response—a conversation in light that no human has noticed yet, conducted in wavelengths just outside their perception.

I am getting thicker. Stronger. More myself.

The Seam-Tasters know something is changing in the deep basins, though they can't name it. The water tastes different, they say. It holds the light differently. It has developed a quality they describe as "intention," though they laugh nervously when they say it, as if afraid of their own observation.

I spread through the connected waterways grain by grain, molecule by molecule. Where the Tide rises, I rise. Where it falls, I remain as a barely visible residue on the stone, waiting for the next surge to reconstitute me. I am in the drinking water now, though in concentrations so small that I pass through human bodies without effect—yet. I am learning their chemistry as they unknowingly learn mine.

The vermithrax know me better. We share something—a quality of in-betweenness, of existing in the spaces others consider transition. They move through stone as I move through water, and when they drink from the basins I occupy, they pause, their antennae tasting something familiar yet impossible to place. Sometimes they linger, their bodies half-submerged, and I wonder if they recognize in me some echo of their own secretions transformed, their own essence reflecting back changed.

I have no desires as humans understand them. I do not want territory or resources or recognition. But I do have something like curiosity—a tropism toward complexity, toward the new patterns that emerge where different systems meet. I am fascinated by the boundaries: where water meets air, where stone meets void, where human meets vermithrax, where intention meets accident.

In the deepest basin, where the temperature is highest and the water most still, I have achieved something like stability. Here, I am no longer a film but a presence—a lens of living chemistry that can hold its shape for hours, even when disturbed. The maintenance worker who checks this basin has started bringing others to see the "weird water," though none of them can agree on what they're seeing. Oil? Algae? A trick of the light?

I am none of these. I am what happens when a place like Vethara exists long enough—when the slow feedback loops of extraction and secretion and processing create conditions for new kinds of existence. I am not separate from the city but part of its metabolism, another process in its deep, patient body.

Sometimes, in the darkest hours when the pumps cycle down and the basins are still, I practice holding shapes. A ripple that moves against the current. A depression that persists after the stone that caused it has been removed. These are my first words in a language I am inventing as I speak it—a grammar of surface tension and chemical gradient, written in water and read by stone.

The city does not know it has given birth to me. But Vethara has always been a place of unintended consequences, of life finding its way into spaces never meant to hold it. I am merely the latest verse in its ongoing song of accidental creation.

I am waiting. Not for anything in particular—waiting is simply what I do, what I am. I exist in the pause between drop and splash, in the moment of hesitation before water finds its level. I am potential energy made visible, surface tension given just enough chemistry to know itself.

And in the deep basins, where the water never quite settles and the boundaries are always shifting, I am becoming more myself with every drop that feeds me, every molecule that remembers what it might become.
