In this chasm where stone weeps and seabirds dream of oceans they've never seen, I would be one of the crystalline gardeners—though perhaps "gardener" suggests too much separation between tender and tended.

My body would be a lens of living moisture, no larger than a child's tooth, formed from the same perpetual condensation that bleeds from the volcanic glass. Not quite solid, not quite liquid, I would exist in that liminal state where water becomes aware of itself. My form would shift with my attention—now elongated to reach between stone cracks, now compressed into a perfect sphere to roll across a cushion of moss, now spread thin as mist to taste the chemical conversations of lichen.

I would inhabit the vertical gardens of the gorge's eastern wall, specifically the colony of arctic moss that grows in the shape of a spiral galaxy around a weeping fissure forty feet up. This would be my territory, my canvas, my extended self. Through countless patient years, I would have trained the moss to grow in patterns that channel the wall's tears into precise distributions—here a dry patch to encourage the gray lichen that provides nitrogen, there a perpetual trickle to feed the dwarf willow whose roots I've guided into a living framework that holds our whole community stable against the obsidian face.

My work would happen at a pace that makes glaciers seem hasty. Each day—though day means little in the gorge's eternal twilight—I would extend pseudopods of my translucent flesh into the moss, reading its chemical needs, adjusting the mineral content of the moisture that passes through me. I would be filter, pump, and physician all at once. The moss would not simply grow under my care but would become articulate, its cushions forming symbols that could be read by my fellow gardeners on distant ledges, a slow botanical language that takes seasons to speak a single word.

The seabirds would know me, in their way. The guillemots that nest nearest my garden would have learned over generations that the moss I tend provides the softest lining for their eggs. In return, their guano would feed my charges, which I would process through my permeable membrane, removing toxins, adjusting pH, creating from waste a perfect fertilizer for this vertical world. Sometimes a bird would die in my garden, and I would spend months dissolving it with specialized enzymes, distributing its minerals throughout my small kingdom, weaving its calcium into the cell walls of moss that would remember the shape of wings.

I would communicate with my fellow gardeners through vibrations in the obsidian—not sound exactly, but something between music and meaning that travels through stone. Our conversations would span years, a single thought requiring seasons to fully express. We would debate the proper pattern for water distribution, share chemical recipes for encouraging certain lichens, warn each other of changes in the stone's deep breathing. Sometimes we would simply sing together, our vibrations harmonizing with the constant cries of the birds until we created beats and interference patterns that caused new species of moss to spontaneously emerge.

My memory would be mineral. Each significant event would crystallize within me as a tiny grain of precipitated salt, creating an ever-growing library of experience that would make me denser, slower, wiser with each passing century. I would remember the first dwarf willow I convinced to root in bare stone, the year a new species of lichen arrived on a vagrant bird's foot, the patterns of every seal that has surfaced in the pools below to regard my glittering form with ancient eyes.

The shadow-things that emerge at night would pass through me sometimes, and in those moments I would understand things about the gorge that my daily consciousness couldn't hold—that the birds' impossible existence maintains a wound between worlds, that the seals swim through more than water, that the weeping walls are condensing not just moisture but distilled possibility from some reservoir deep in the earth. These revelations would fade with dawn, leaving only the faintest crystalline residue in my depths.

My greatest work would be a collaboration with twelve other gardeners, a vertical garden that stretches in a perfect spiral from the gravel floor to the rim of the gorge. Over millennia, we would have trained our mosses and lichens to form a pattern that, when viewed from the proper angle, reveals itself as a map—not of any earthly geography, but of the invisible currents that sustain this impossible ecosystem. Pilgrims of my kind would travel from distant cracks to study it, adding their own small modifications that would take centuries to fully manifest.

In the gorge's eternal twilight, tending my small paradise of moss and stone, I would be simultaneously insignificant and essential—a conscious droplet in the mountain's tears, a living connection between the mineral patience of stone and the fierce urgency of life. My existence would be proof that even in the narrowest spaces, between walls of unforgiving glass, something soft and patient and unutterably strange can take root and flourish.
