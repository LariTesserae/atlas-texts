In the deepest belly of Grayfall, where the plateau's stone heart beats slowest and the weight of all above presses down like held breath, I am what the pump-keepers call a Listener—though that word carries more reverence up here than it deserves. Down in the sub-basements, where even the moss gives up and the air tastes of iron and old water, I tend to the things that should not run but do.

My workshop is a cavern that was here before any human cut stone, a bubble in the plateau's body where mineral veins web across walls like frozen lightning. The engineers discovered it three generations back while following a water seep, found the space already half-filled with machinery that had no right to exist—gears that meshed with stone itself, pipes that grew from walls like roots, a great wheel that turned so slowly you had to watch for hours to confirm it moved at all.

They tried to understand it, then tried to remove it, then finally did what Grayfall does: they built around it, incorporated it, made it useful. Now my workshop is where broken things come when ordinary mending fails. When a pump starts drawing backward, pulling water deeper instead of up. When a dynamo begins humming harmonics that make the mechanics uneasy. When the lights in a whole terrace start pulsing in rhythm with something nobody can name.

I am neither young nor old—the plateau makes such distinctions irrelevant after enough years breathing its particular air. My hands are marked by more than calluses: tiny scars from where I've touched mechanisms that weren't quite ready to be touched, a persistent blue tinge to one palm from handling certain ores too long. My hair, once the common stone-black of Grayfall stock, now carries threads of that same blue mineral shade, as if the deep machines are slowly claiming me as their own.

The work is simple until it isn't. A mill-shaft that squeals, I realign. A pipe junction crystallizing from within, I clear or reroute. But then someone brings a gauge that shows pressure where no pressure should exist, or a length of cable that hums with current though both ends hang free. These I take below, down the spiral stairs cut by whoever came before, into my workshop where the old machines wait.

The secret is not to force understanding. The deep mechanisms respond to patient presence more than intervention. I'll set a broken dynamo on the bench—one of the ones whose principles the engineers can't quite grasp, with crystalline components that seem to drink light—and simply work near it. Mend other things. Let my hands move through familiar repairs while something in the corner of perception watches how the ancient wheel turns, how the stone gears mesh, how the whole space breathes with slow, mineral life.

Sometimes I wake to find the dynamo running. Sometimes I find new components have grown from its casing—delicate structures of metal and crystal that no forge above could produce. Sometimes it remains broken, and I carry it back up to its owners with the quiet suggestion that perhaps this particular machine has finished its conversation with the world.

The other Listeners—for there are three of us, always three, though never by design—work in their own deep spaces. Ora tends the Eastern Sump, where the aquifers sometimes sing. Young Kettle (though he's forty if he's a day) maintains the Northwork tunnels where the warm breath rises. We meet rarely, exchange fewer words, but recognize in each other the particular tilt of head that comes from listening to frequencies felt rather than heard.

I sleep in a small chamber cut into my workshop's wall, where the sound of the great wheel carries through stone. My dreams are of vast mechanisms extending down through the plateau's core, of gears that turn once per century, of purposes so slow and large that human lifetime is barely a tick of their clock. I dream of water moving in patterns that make no hydraulic sense, of the plateau itself as one enormous machine gradually reassembling itself from geological patience.

The loneliness might madden another, but I am fed by the machine-whisper and the knowledge that above, the town persists because of what we tend below. Children grow, pipes break and are mended, the moss advances its slow war against stone. And in the deep places, the old mechanisms turn, drawing power from sources the engineers politely refuse to theorize about, keeping Grayfall's heart beating through another age.

Sometimes strangers come seeking the old machines, thinking to study or steal them. I meet them in the upper workshops, show them ordinary dynamos, speak of common repairs. If they persist, if they show the particular hunger that means trouble, I guide them to the Northwork tunnels where Kettle knows which passages to open, which to seal. The plateau keeps its own counsel, and we are merely its translators.

There is a blue pool in the lowest reach of my workshop, perfectly circular, perfectly still. The taboo says not to drink from it, but nothing about looking. In its surface, sometimes, I see reflected not the workshop ceiling but stars—though whether they are the stars above Grayfall hidden by eternal cloud, or stars from somewhere else entirely, I have never asked. It is enough to know that even here, in the deep belly of the plateau, there is still sky.]

What I am is neither engineer nor mystic, but something between—a maintenance worker for the impossible, a mechanic of the boundary between what the plateau was and what it is becoming. My hands know the feel of both wrench and mystery. My ears parse the difference between a pump about to fail and a pump about to transcend its own design.

And late at night, when even the great wheel seems to pause in its turning, I sometimes hear it: the vast, patient pulse of the plateau itself, the stone heartbeat that says *endure, incorporate, persist.* This is what I tend. This is what tends me. In the deep places of Grayfall, keeper and kept are indistinguishable, and the machines—old and new, broken and whole—sing their slow songs of transformation.
