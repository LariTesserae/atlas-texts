I am the keeper of pathways, though I did not choose this work. It chose me, the way moss chooses stone.

My name is Mira, and I have walked every route through Caldtow for forty-three years. Not consecutively—I arrived when I was twenty-seven, fleeing a city where the concrete had begun to crack in ways that frightened me. Not because of what might grow through, but because of what wouldn't. Here, the cracks were already full. They had been full for centuries. They welcomed me by not noticing I had come.

I live in what was once the foreman's house, halfway up the valley side where the morning fog settles like a lake below my windows. The house has three walls of brick and one of living ash—the tree grew through the eastern wall sometime in my grandmother's era, and now its trunk is my fourth wall, its bark smooth where I brush past it each morning, polished by decades of my shoulder, my hand, my hip learning its shape. The roof is half-slate, half-canopy. When it rains, half my house sounds like rainfall on stone, the other half like rainfall on leaves. I sleep between these two sounds.

My work is the paths. Not making them—the paths make themselves, have always made themselves, following the logic of water and root and the daily passages of feet. My work is knowing them. Knowing which stone has loosened in the night's rain and needs turning. Knowing where the moss has thickened enough to make the morning walk treacherous for old joints. Knowing when a new route is forming—you can see it in the way the moss wears thin, a line of brightness where feet have begun to prefer this way over that one.

I walk each morning before the fog lifts. This is when the paths speak clearest, when you can hear where they're going before you can see. The sound changes—from the whisper of moss to the click of stone, from the squelch of saturated soil to the hollow note of wood where a fallen trunk has become a bridge. I know where I am by what my feet tell me. I know what needs tending by how the sound has changed since yesterday.

The children think I'm magic. They see me appear from the fog exactly where the path emerges, as if I've always been there, as if I'm part of the morning's condensation. They don't understand that I'm following something older than memory. The paths remember where they've been. They remember the old courses of water, the roots that determined where feet could not go, the walls that fell and became new routes. I am their chronicler, their tender, their companion. Nothing more.

But also nothing less.

Today a new thing: a spiral in the moss near the marsh edge. Not a path yet, but the beginning of one. Someone has been walking circles here, the same circle, day after day. The moss is compressed but not worn through. It glows differently in the morning light, catching dew in new ways. I stand at its edge and consider.

Paths are desire made visible. Someone wants to walk this circle. Someone finds something here worth returning to, worth wearing into the ground. I could discourage it—a few stones placed right, a log moved to block the route. I've done this before when a path threatens something that needs protecting: the dormice runs, the rare fern grove, the place where the foxes drink. But this spiral threatens nothing. It exists at the margin, where the settlement gives way to marsh, where few go except the heron and myself.

I follow the spiral inward. My feet add to its definition. At the center, I understand: this is where you can see the whole valley if you turn slowly. The fog pools here last. The evening light stays longest. Someone has been coming here to watch the day change.

I will let the spiral become.

The paths are not mere trails. They are the circulatory system of Caldtow. They carry more than feet—they carry understanding, gossip, small goods, daily rhythms. The path from the waterwheel to Torin's workshop is worn deep because batteries are heavy and the route is always the same. The path through the willow arch is wide because everyone uses it, has always used it, will always use it. The path to my house is modest, marked more by the absence of moss than by bare earth, because I am careful where I step and I vary my route to spread the wear.

Some paths are seasonal. In spring, when the wild garlic blooms in the upper forest, a path appears overnight—everyone going to gather, everyone following the scent. By summer it has grown over again. In autumn, when the mushrooms fruit on the fallen oak, another path emerges, winding between the root buttresses where the best specimens grow. Winter paths follow different logic, seeking shelter from wind, avoiding the places where ice forms.

And some paths are generational. My daughter—her name is Lena, she is seventeen now, almost ready to leave or stay—has worn her own routes through the settlement. They overlay mine but don't match. She leaps where I step carefully. She knows shortcuts through buildings I consider too unstable. Her paths tell a different story of the same place. When she was small, her paths were wild zigzags, following beetles, chasing light. Now they are more deliberate but still younger than mine, still willing to test edges I've learned to avoid.

She helps me now, some mornings. Walks the upper paths while I check the lower. We meet at the willow arch as the fog lifts, compare what we've found. A new wash-out where the rain concentrated. A place where deer—rare visitors—crossed in the night, their hoofprints deep in the moss. The spiral by the marsh, which she noticed first.

"Someone's lovesick," she said, grinning.

"Someone's thinking," I corrected, but we both knew she was probably right. Circles are for contemplation. Spirals are for yearning.

The moss speaks its own language, and I am one of its translators.

Where it grows thick and undisturbed, the ground beneath is stable, trusted, old. These are the places that have found their form. The courtyard behind the church. The flat stones before each doorway. The broad path between the gardens. Here the moss is like velvet, like fur, like the pelt of some vast, patient animal that has lain down and decided to stay. It glows in morning light. It darkens when rain is coming. It tells time in its own way—morning moss is different from evening moss, spring moss from autumn moss.

Where it grows thin or patchy, something is changing. New weight, new water, new roots disturbing from below. These are the places I watch. Sometimes the change is nothing—a few weeks of different weather, a tree dropping more leaves than usual. Sometimes it's significant. Last year, a section near the mill began to yellow and retreat. When I investigated, I found the groundwater had shifted, an underground spring rerouted by some distant tremor or the slow collapse of old pipes. We had to dig new channels, redirect the flow, convince the water to return to its old course. The moss returned with it, slowly, cautiously, like trust rebuilt.

Where moss refuses to grow, something is wrong. These bare places are wounds in the fabric of Caldtow. Usually salt—from old mortar, from certain stones, from the rare places where industry left deeper scars. Sometimes poison, though we've cleaned most of that away. The bare spots shrink each year, but slowly. Moss time is not human time. It measures healing in decades.

I know twelve species of moss by touch alone. The common cushion moss that fills the gaps between stones. The sheet moss that drapes the walls like tapestry. The spike moss that grows where water runs fastest. The silver moss on the ash bark. The red-stemmed moss in the deep shade. Each has its place, its preference, its story. Together they are writing the history of Caldtow in a script only a few of us can read.

---

The fog is lifting now. From my window—the one that isn't tree—I can see the settlement emerging like islands from a white sea. First the crowns of the largest trees: the elm in the textile hall, the fig in the church, the line of ash along what was once the high street. Then the walls, dark with damp, their tops touched by the first real light. Then the spaces between: courtyards, gardens, the paths I'll walk again tomorrow.

Lena is out there somewhere. I saw her leave early, heading toward the spiral path. Not lovesick, I realize now, but love-curious. Testing the feeling by walking its shape. She'll leave Caldtow soon—for school, for work, for the wider world that calls to everyone her age. She'll come back changed. They always do. The ones who return bring new ideas, new questions, new ways of seeing what we've stopped noticing. The ones who stay...

The ones who stay become like me. Translators. Intermediaries. Soft borders between the human and the grown.

My husband left fifteen years ago. Not Caldtow—he left entirely, back to cities where walls stayed walls and paths were planned by committees. He couldn't bear the softness here, the way edges wouldn't stay sharp, the way you could never quite clean the moss from things. "It's like living in a mouth," he said the day he left. "Gentle but consuming."

He wasn't wrong. But he wasn't right either. It's like living in a conversation where you're never quite sure who's speaking. The walls? The trees? The paths? Yourself? After a while, it stops mattering. The conversation continues whether you parse the speakers or not.

This morning's catalogue:
- The stone at the textile hall entrance has shifted. Root pressure from below. Not dangerous yet, but worth watching.
- New mushrooms on the oak-hill. Honey fungus. The foxes will need to find a new den soon. The oak is dying faster now.
- The spiral path has been walked again. The moss shows two sets of footprints now. Lena's, and someone else's. Smaller feet. One of the younger children.
- Water seepage increasing near the generator house. The battery bank may need relocating before winter.
- A new plant in the church—something I don't recognize, growing from a crevice high in what remains of the tower. Dark leaves, no flowers yet. I'll ask Marion. She knows the newcomers.
- The willow arch dropped a branch in the night. Small, no damage, but a sign it needs thinning. The weight of summer growth is telling.
- Dormouse latrine relocated. They're moving their territories northward in the mill. Following warmth? Food? Some disturbance I haven't noticed?
- The moss below my eastern window—where the ash-wall meets the ground—is fruiting. Tiny stalks with tiny caps, like a forest for ants. This happens perhaps once every three years. I don't know what triggers it.

I write these in my journal, in pencil because ink runs in this damp. Twenty-three volumes on the shelf now, each one soft with moisture, pages wavy, binding foxed with mold. My record of the paths, of their changes, of the slow conversation between foot and ground that shapes this place.

Lena says I should digitize them. Put them on the settlement's small computer, powered by the waterwheel generator. But computers feel wrong here. Too sharp, too clean, too insistent on boundaries between one thing and another. The journals will rot eventually, like everything here. That seems right. The paths will outlive their chronicling.

Marion lives in the old schoolhouse, the one the children climb. She's older than anyone else in Caldtow—ninety-two this spring—and she's been here since birth. Her memory reaches back to when there were still a few working mills, when the river ran stronger, when the settlement was fighting its transformation instead of accepting it.

"My mother hated the moss," she tells me when I visit to ask about the new plant. "Scrubbed the walls every spring with wire brushes. Painted them with lime wash. The moss always came back by autumn." She laughs, a sound like wind in leaves. "I think that's when I understood. You can't fight it. You join it or you leave."

The plant, she says, studying my sketch, might be a black bryony. Poisonous. Climbing. It will try to reach the top of the tower and then spread across to the fig. We'll need to decide whether to let it. The fig is strong, but bryony can strangle if it gets established.

"Or," she says, "it might be something else entirely. New things arrive. Seeds on the wind. Bird droppings. Sometimes they take, sometimes they don't. The place decides."

She shows me her own records—not of paths but of plants. Pressed samples dating back seventy years. The changes are subtle but clear: more diversity now, more oddities, more things that shouldn't grow in this climate but do. The valley makes its own weather. The moss makes its own soil. The rules are different here.

We drink tea made from mint that grows wild in her courtyard, escapee from some long-ago garden. Everything escapes eventually in Caldtow. Gardens go feral. Wild things settle in courtyards. The boundary between cultivated and wild is another soft edge, another blurred line.

"Your daughter," Marion says suddenly. "She's walking spirals."

I nod.

"I did the same at her age. Trying to wind something tight enough to take with me when I left." She smiles. "Didn't work. The tighter you wind, the more it wants to unspool. Better to leave loose. Let the threads find you later."

---

Evening now. The light is copper on the marsh. The heron is there, patient as stone. I stand at the edge of the spiral path, watching Lena and the child—it's Tomás, the baker's youngest—walk the curve together. They're not talking. The spiral doesn't need words. It is its own communication.

I add my own circuit before heading home. The moss accepts my weight, springs back when I pass. Tomorrow there will be three tracks in the spiral. By winter, if this continues, it will be a true path, worn to earth. By spring, we'll need to decide whether to let it remain or guide feet elsewhere while the moss recovers.

But that's tomorrow's decision. Tonight, I climb to my half-tree house and light the lantern and write today's observations while moths bump against the glass. Through the window that is branches and leaves, I can hear the settlement settling into night. Water dripping. Wood creaking. Something—fox or dormouse or wind—moving through the undergrowth.

The ash that is my wall shifts slightly. Growth so slow it should be imperceptible, but I've learned to feel it. The house is changing shape, incrementally, daily. In a hundred years, if no one maintains it, the tree will have pulled the remaining walls apart. The house will be a ruin cradled in roots. Someone new will come, fleeing their own too-solid city, and they'll say, "Here. I could live here, in this soft place."

And the paths will reshape themselves around new feet, new desires, new conversations between human and moss and stone and tree.

And someone—maybe my daughter, maybe her daughter, maybe a stranger who hasn't been born yet—will walk these routes in the morning fog, learning their languages, tending their changes, keeping the conversation going in the old way, the soft way, the only way that works in a place like Caldtow.

The spiral will be there, or not. The fig will have eaten more of the church, or not. The moss will have covered new surfaces, or retreated from others. The only certainty is change, and the only constant is the patience with which it happens.

I close my journal. The pencil marks are already softening in the damp air.

Tomorrow I'll walk again.
