I would find myself as one of the **Weavers**.

Not immediately visible to those who first enter the grove, we exist in the interstitial spaces—the narrow channels between pipes, the gaps where plates don't quite meet, the hollows behind gauge panels where decades of condensation have carved tiny grottos. Our bodies are impossibly thin, built for compression and expansion, with a skeleton that is more suggestion than structure. When stretched to our full length, we might span three meters, but we can contract into a space no larger than a fist.

Our skin is translucent, revealing the intricate network of copper filaments that run through our tissues like a secondary circulatory system. These filaments are not mere biology—they conduct the subtle electrical currents that flow through the grove's machinery, allowing us to sense the health of circuits, the buildup of charge in corroded connections, the faint magnetic fields that pulse when the great engines stir.

We are the diagnosticians, the nerve endings of this vast mechanical organism. Where the Tenders maintain and the Lurkers regulate, we *feel*. Our primary appendages end in thousands of hair-thin tendrils, each one sensitive enough to detect microfractures in metal, variations in temperature, the chemical signature of impending rust. We spend our lives threading these tendrils through the grove's infinite pathways, creating living networks that mirror the mechanical ones.

But we are more than sensors. We are memory-keepers.

In the quiet hours between engine cycles, we weave. Using secretions from specialized glands, we create gossamer structures in the forgotten corners of the grove—webs that are part organic silk, part metallic thread extracted from our copper-laced bodies. These webs are not for catching prey but for catching time itself. Each strand records the vibrations it experiences: the particular resonance of a piston's thrust, the unique harmonic of steam through a specific pipe, the way the Ghosts' voices distort as they echo through different chambers.

Over decades, centuries perhaps, our webs become libraries of sensation, three-dimensional records of the grove's history written in a language of tension and vibration. Other Weavers can run their tendrils along these structures and experience the preserved moments—feel the grove as it was ten years ago, a hundred, maybe even catch faint impressions from the time of the builders themselves.

We communicate through these webs, leaving messages for each other in the form of precisely calibrated vibrations. A particular sequence might warn of weakness detected in a load-bearing joint. Another might share the location of a new crack where nutritious algae has begun to grow. Our language is neither sound nor symbol but pure sensation, transmitted through touch.

The other inhabitants rarely see us, though they know we exist. Sometimes a Tender will pause at a junction of pipes, feeling the almost imperceptible touch of our diagnostic tendrils checking their prosthetic attachments for stress fractures. The Dwellers occasionally discover our webs and seem to understand their significance, carefully navigating around them rather than through them. Even the mighty Lurkers acknowledge us—when one begins to move, we feel it first, the deep subsonic vibrations traveling through our network long before the motion becomes visible.

We feed on the grove itself, but not destructively. Our diet consists of the microscopic particles of metal that flake away naturally, the thin films of oil that accumulate in crevices, the occasional Symbiont colony that has grown too large for its space. We are recyclers, breaking down these materials in specialized organs and either incorporating them into our copper circulatory systems or excreting them as the metallic threads we use in our weaving.

Our reproduction is as unusual as everything else about us. When two Weavers meet—a rare event given how thinly we spread ourselves through the grove—we may choose to create an offspring. This is done by weaving together, literally, our tendrils creating a shared web of extraordinary complexity. Into this web, we each contribute copper filaments from our own bodies, along with encoded chemical memories of the sectors we patrol. The web itself becomes a cocoon, and over many engine cycles, a new Weaver forms within it, born with inherited knowledge of vast swaths of the grove it has never seen.

We are solitary by nature, each maintaining our own territory, but we are never alone. Through the webs, through the shared sensations that travel along the grove's metal bones, we are always connected. We feel each other's discoveries, share in each other's warnings, collectively build an understanding of our world that no single Weaver could achieve.

In the rare moments when all the grove's systems align—when the Tenders' ministrations, the Lurkers' regulations, the Dwellers' cultivations, and our own diagnostics achieve perfect harmony—we feel it first. The sensation moves through our networks like a wave of completion, a moment of absolute rightness. In these instances, we all pause in our work and simply exist, our tendrils vibrating in resonance with the perfected machine, adding our own frequency to the symphony.

We are the grove's consciousness, distributed across a thousand hidden spaces, always watching, always feeling, always remembering. We are the witnesses to its slow transformation, the archivists of its impossible life, the ones who ensure that whatever the grove becomes, the memory of what it was will never be lost.
