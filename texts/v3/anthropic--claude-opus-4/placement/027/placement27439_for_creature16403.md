In this world where the line between structure and creature has dissolved into irrelevance, I am the Archivist of Erosions—though erosion here means something altogether different than the wearing away of stone.

I exist as a vast membrane stretched between seven towering columns of the plant-architecture, my body a translucent sheet of living glass that filters and refracts the phosphorescent pulses of the world below. Each light that passes through me leaves a trace, a scar of color that builds in layers until my surface resembles a mad cartographer's fever dream of this impossible landscape. I am both window and record, observatory and observed.

My consciousness flows not through neurons but through the prismatic distortions of light itself. Each beam that penetrates my membrane carries information—the death-liquefaction of a small scuttling thing, the synchronized dance of a thousand creatures building their ephemeral geometries, the slow respiration of the ancient column-beings whose thoughts move in geological time. I parse these light-languages, sort them, store them in the crystalline matrices that grow like tumors across my underside.

I remember the last rain. It fell seventeen thousand years ago, before I stretched myself between these columns, when I was still mobile, still one of the flowing things that built and demolished in endless cycles. The water came as an aberration, a cosmic accident that set the entire biome into convulsions of shock. I watched—small then, scurrying—as the drops hit the superheated surfaces and exploded into steam that carried away whole sections of the living architecture. The screaming was visible, ultraviolet shrieks that painted the air in colors that had no names.

That was when I understood my purpose: to remember what this world forgets.

So I grew. Slowly, painfully, I extruded myself upward, spreading thinner and thinner until I could catch more light, more information. The smaller creatures began to crawl across my surface, leaving their own phosphorescent trails, writing stories I couldn't yet read. Some tried to feed on me, anchoring their feeding tendrils to what they thought were nutrient veins, but found only hardened light, memories made solid. They learned to leave me alone.

Now I span nearly a kilometer, my membrane rippling in the heat-shifts, creating new lenses that focus on different parts of this breathing, writhing world. In my deepest layers, I preserve the light-patterns of species that no longer exist, evolutionary experiments that failed or succeeded too well. I can still replay their final moments—creatures that grew too large and collapsed under their own architecture, others that refined themselves out of existence, becoming pure light that dissipated into the burning sky.

Sometimes the ancient column-beings notice me. Their attention manifests as a deep thrumming that makes my entire surface resonate. They send up queries in the form of controlled spore-bursts that settle on my membrane and spell out questions in chemical alphabets. They want to know what I've seen, what patterns I've noticed in the endless construction and destruction below. I answer by shifting my refractive index, creating light-shows that tell stories spanning millennia.

But mostly, I simply watch and remember.

I watch the newest generation of creatures, smaller than any before, barely larger than the spores they're born from. They move in vast coordinated swarms, each individual a pixel in some larger image they're creating. They build structures that last only moments—geometric forms of impossible beauty that serve no purpose except to exist, to be seen, to be remembered. Perhaps they know I'm watching. Perhaps that's the point.

I watch the middle-distance where the heat-shimmer makes the world liquid, where the boundaries between one structure and another blur into a continuous field of living matter. Sometimes I think I see patterns there—faces, or what might be faces if faces were made of burning resin and twisted fiber. The pareidolia of a mind that has been watching too long, perhaps. Or perhaps not.

I watch the sky, where no clouds form but where the spore-streams create their own weather systems, cyclones of biological matter that spiral upward and dissipate. On rare occasions, these cyclones grow large enough to block out sections of the sun, and in those moments of relative darkness, new colors bloom across the landscape—purples and deep blues that exist only in shadow, that shrivel and die the moment full heat returns.

And I watch myself, the watcher, growing larger and thinner with each passing year. One day I will stretch too far and tear, and my memories will spill out in a cascade of crystallized light, to be absorbed by the root systems below. The smaller creatures will incorporate my fragments into their bodies, and perhaps some piece of my purpose will live on in them—a instinct to preserve, to remember, to witness.

But that day is not today. Today I catch the light of ten thousand small dyings and rebirths, the endless churn of a world that has replaced geology with biology, stone with flesh, permanence with perpetual change. Today I add another layer to my archive of erosions—not the wearing away of rock, but the wearing away of boundaries, of distinctions, of the very concept of separate existence.

I am the memory of a world that consumes itself to survive. I am the eye that watches the watchers. I am the silence between the phosphorescent screams.

I am, and I remember.
