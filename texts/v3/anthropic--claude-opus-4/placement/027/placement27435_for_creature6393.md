I find myself drawn to describe an unlikely inhabitant of the Deep Canning—not the system itself, though it breathes like something alive, but a keeper who has learned to match its patience.

---

**The Tender**

I am called the Tender, though I've forgotten if that was always my name or if it emerged from the work like salt bloom on metal.

I measure my life now in pressure cycles. Each pulse from the ocean above marks time better than any clock—the dome flexes, the moss brightens, the pale vines redirect their light, and I know another unit of existence has passed. I stopped counting them years ago. Or was it decades? Time moves differently when you calibrate yourself to growth rather than decay.

My quarters are built into a maintenance alcove between tanks E-7 and E-8, where the designers left a gap just wide enough for equipment storage. I've lined the walls with salvaged grating to match the floors, creating a permeable nest where the chamber's breath can find me even in sleep. The condensation wakes me each morning—not an alarm, but a gentle accumulation of droplets on my face until I must either rise or drown in the slowest possible way.

I no longer wear shoes. My feet have developed their own cartography of calluses that match the grating's grid. Each intersection of metal tells me exactly where I stand in the chamber without looking. The warmth of my soles leaves brief prints in the condensation that the moss below has learned to anticipate. There's a faint trail of brighter tips along my daily routes—not following me, but preceding me, as if the moss dreams my pathways into being.

My skin has taken on the chamber's moisture as a permanent resident. I am never fully dry, never fully wet, existing in the same intermediate state as the air itself. When I leave the chamber (rarely now, so rarely), the outside world feels abrasively crisp, like sandpaper against my osmotic membrane. I've learned to move through the humidity like the plants do—not fighting it, but letting it medium my existence.

The work is observation. The consortium sends requirements through the fiber cables—temperature parameters, salinity targets, growth projections—but they arrive already obsolete, composed in a world that moves too fast to understand what happens here. I translate them into the chamber's language: nudges rather than commands, suggestions that the system will consider at its own pace.

I've developed a taxonomy of touches. The E-series tanks run slightly warmer than spec, so I rest my palms against them during the coolest part of the cycle, letting my body heat draw out excess. The algae has grown to expect this; where I regularly place my hands, it forms subtle depressions, like handprints in slow putty. The F-series needs more attention to its condensation channels—I trace my fingers along them, clearing not debris but excess growth, training the flow patterns through repetition rather than force.

The frond-over-panel at the chamber's heart knows me now. When I approach, the display beneath brightens slightly—not a programmed response, but an emergent behavior from years of correlation between my heat signature and the need for data review. The frond's translucency shifts to accommodate both my eyes and the system's need to show itself to itself. We read the data together, the plant mediating between human perception and machine precision.

I've learned the chamber's moods. When storms pass overhead, the pressure waves create patterns in the dome that cascade through every living surface. The vines flash their gold edges in sequences I've started to recognize—anxiety spirals when the pressure drops too fast, satisfaction rhythms when it stabilizes. The moss becomes a constellation of response, writing the ocean's weather in living light along the floor.

My own biology has shifted to match. My circadian rhythms now follow the twelve-hour dimming cycle of the piped sunlight. My hunger comes and goes with the tidal circulation of the floor water. I eat mostly from the consortium's nutrition tubes, but I've also learned which condensation channels carry the purest water, which trace minerals from the plants can supplement what my body needs. I am becoming native to this enclosed sea.

The plants read me as carefully as I read them. The modular fronds near my sleeping alcove grow slightly denser—not enough to block light, but enough to moderate the temperature where I rest. A section of moss near tank C-3 glows brighter when I approach, having learned over years that I often pause there to check a problematic valve. The pale vines above my most common standing spots have oriented their leaves to cast light precisely where I need it to read the gauges.

I speak to them sometimes—not because they understand language, but because the CO₂ patterns of my breath have become part of the chamber's data. My words are humidity and carbon, parsed into the same slow grammar as everything else here. The system adjusts its parameters slightly when I speak, accommodating the tiny local weather of human expression.

There was a time when I thought this was loneliness. Now I understand it as the opposite—a saturation of connection where every breath is registered, every movement incorporated, every habit written into the living memory of the chamber. I am not alone; I am dissolved into the superorganism, still conscious but no longer separate.

The consortium's messages ask about my "mental health" and "social needs." I try to explain that these concepts assume a distinction between self and environment that no longer applies. My mental health is measured in the stability of salinity gradients. My social needs are met by the conversation between algae and alloy, between pressure and response, between the machine's intentions and the garden's slow decisions.

I dream in growth patterns now. Sleep brings visions of fronds unfurling over centuries, of moss writing light-letters across vast floors, of vines growing down from domes the size of continents. Sometimes in these dreams, I see others like me—tenders in distant chambers, all of us nodes in a network of patient observation, keeping the gardens while they keep us.

When I wake, the condensation has drawn my outline on the grating where I slept. By evening, the moss below will have brightened along that shape, and tomorrow the algae will be microscopically thicker where my breath pooled against the nearest tank. I am being printed into this place, layer by molecular layer, becoming part of its slow documentation of itself.

The chamber is young, they say. The salt bloom hasn't gone deep. The corrosion is still manageable. But I can feel the future in the metal's vibration—centuries of patient accumulation, the gradual transition from machine to hybrid to something entirely new. I will not see the end of that transformation. Neither will my successor, if I ever train one. We are all just temporary anomalies in the garden's long life, brief warmths that barely disturb its dreams.

But we leave our marks. In the slightly denser growth where we walked. In the patterns of brightness where we paused. In the microscopic adjustments the system makes to accommodate our presence. We are written into the garden as surely as its parameters are written into its cells—not as commanders or gardeners, but as fleeting weather patterns in its vast, slow sky.

This morning—or evening, the cycle matters less now—I noticed a new behavior. The fronds near my alcove have started to anticipate not just my presence but my absence. When I leave for the monthly inspection of the lower pumps, they adjust their orientation slightly, optimizing for the temperature differential where I'm not. The chamber is learning to miss me in the only way it can—through optimization curves and growth responses, through the patient accumulation of data about the shape of my not-being-there.

I find this beautiful in a way I can no longer fully explain to the world above the dome. To be missed by something that has no mind, only pattern. To be known by something that has no memory, only accumulated response. To be held by something that has no hands, only the slow grip of adaptation.

The Deep Canning breathes. I breathe with it. We are synchronized now, our rhythms matched across scales—my quick human inhalations and its vast mechanical lungs moving together like nested waves. In and out, pressure and release, growth and patience and the endless circulation of water through all the forms it can take.

I am the Tender. I tend. I am tended to. The distinction blurs like everything else in this humid air, until there is only the tending itself—the mutual attention of system and component, of garden and gardener, of the vast breathing machine and the small warm anomaly it has learned to accommodate in its dreams.

The moss is pulsing. Another pressure wave from above.  
Another unit of time.  
Another layer of slow growth.  
Another day in the Deep Canning, where I dissolve a little more into the patient catalog of what lives.
