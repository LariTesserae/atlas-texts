In the **Seepage Districts**, where the plateau's ancient drainage systems converge beneath the main industrial complexes, I am one of the **Cistern Keepers**—a Tunka whose life unfolds in the vast, echoing chambers that collect the city's perpetual runoff.

My domain is Cathedral Seven, a cistern so old its original designation has been worn smooth by centuries of moisture. The space yawns like the ribcage of some primordial beast—vaulted ceilings disappearing into darkness, supported by columns thicker than ten Tunka standing together. Water drips constantly from above, each droplet adding its voice to the eternal chorus that reverberates through these depths. The acoustics here are peculiar; sounds arrive in waves, distorted and multiplied, creating a liquid architecture of echoes.

My skin has taken on the particular blue-grey of deep slate, marked with veins of copper-green where the minerals have settled into my pores over decades of work. My hands, even by Tunka standards, are exceptional—the pads of my fingers have developed an almost supernatural sensitivity to vibration, able to detect the slightest irregularity in water flow through steel pipes a meter thick. When I press my palm to the cold metal, I can feel the health of the entire system: blockages forming three levels up, the slow degradation of a seal that won't fail for another decade, the precise rhythm of the great pumps that keep our level from flooding.

I wear the traditional keeper's garment—a long coat of waxed leather, its surface cracked and re-sealed countless times, creating a map of repairs that tells the story of my thirty years in service. The collar is high, channeling runoff away from my neck. My boots are fitted with metal plates that ring against the catwalks, announcing my presence to the few others who work these depths. The sound is deliberate—in the Seepage Districts, we navigate as much by audio signature as by sight.

My workshop occupies a natural shelf in the cistern wall, accessed by a narrow causeway that floods during the heavy season. Here, I maintain my tools: listening rods of various lengths, each attuned to different frequencies; crystalline deposits I've cultivated that resonate when water quality changes; my prize possession—a set of pre-Settling gauges whose symbols I've spent twenty years learning to interpret. The workspace is lit by bioluminescent cultures I feed with nutrient paste, their cold light preferable to electric fixtures that would fail in the constant damp.

The work is solitary but never lonely. Cathedral Seven has its own population: the **Pipe Singers**, those scaled creatures that have evolved to live entirely within the drainage system. They've learned to mimic the sounds of flowing water so perfectly that only experienced keepers can distinguish their calls from actual flow. I've named the matriarch who lives in Junction 49—she responds to a specific vibration pattern I can produce by striking two pipes in sequence. We have an understanding; she alerts me to deep blockages, and I ensure her nest sectors remain undisturbed during maintenance cycles.

My days follow the rhythm of water, not light. I wake when the morning shift begins above, their machinery sending new patterns through the pipes. I make my rounds, checking the hundred and seventeen critical points throughout my sector. Each location has its own sonic signature, its own particular symphony of drips, flows, and mechanical hums. I've been doing this long enough that I dream in these sounds, my sleeping mind sorting through the audio patterns, solving problems before my waking self encounters them.

Once each month, I meet with the other Cistern Keepers at Convergence Point Zero, where all the major drainage systems intersect. We speak in the old manner, our voices so low they travel through the floor plates as much as through air. We share observations: Old Keras from Cathedral Three reports increasing mineral buildup in the eastern flows, suggesting shifts in the industrial processes five levels above. Young Thurna from Cathedral Twelve has noticed new harmonics in the pump songs—signs that components are beginning their century-long failure cascade.

The meeting lasts hours, days sometimes. We update our collective maps, these living documents etched on sheets of compressed fungal matter that won't degrade in the moisture. Each keeper contributes their section, and slowly, a picture emerges of the vast circulatory system beneath the plateau—arteries of iron and stone that we tend like ancient physicians reading the pulse of our mechanical patient.

But it's in the deep hours, when even the machinery above settles into its lowest cycle, that I find my purpose most clearly. I descend to the Sump, the lowest point of Cathedral Seven, where water pools before being cycled back up. Here, in the absolute depths, the sound changes. The industrial clangor fades to a whisper, replaced by something older—the sound of water moving as it has since before the first foundation was laid.

I sit on my carved stone, feet dangling above the dark water, and perform the Listening. With eyes closed, I extend my awareness through the entire system, feeling the health of every pipe, every valve, every seal. It's a meditation that can last hours, a practice passed down through generations of keepers. In these moments, I understand something the surface dwellers might miss: the city is not built on the plateau—it *is* the plateau, its mechanical systems so intertwined with the geological that they've become indistinguishable.

My apprentice started last season—a young Tunka whose sensitivity to vibration showed promise. She shadows me through the rounds, learning to distinguish between the seventeen different types of flow sounds, memorizing the locations where condensation pools in dangerous quantities. I see myself in her careful movements, her growing understanding that haste has no place in the depths. She'll spend the next decade learning the basics, another decade mastering the intermediate mysteries, and perhaps, if she has the patience, she'll one day sit where I sit, feeling the pulse of Cathedral Seven in her bones.

The surface dwellers sometimes forget we exist, focused on their vast machines and production quotas. But we know the truth—without the Cistern Keepers, their industrial paradise would drown in its own runoff within a season. We are the unseen circulation, the quiet maintainers of the vast mechanical metabolism.

When my time comes to join the Settled—those keepers whose bodies finally match the pace of stones—my apprentice will take my place, and I will take my position in the Hall of Echoes, where the preserved forms of past keepers line the walls, their bodies mineralizing slowly in the constant moisture, becoming part of the very architecture they tended. It's not death as others might understand it, but a final, permanent integration with the system we've spent our lives serving.

Until then, I listen. I maintain. I endure. The water sings its ancient song through pipes and channels, and I am its faithful interpreter, a single note in the vast, slow symphony of the Wet Industry.
