In the Cavity of Threads, I am **The Librarian of Unspoken Syllables**.

I arrived as sound before I arrived as substance—a whisper that got lost between two droplets and crystallized into something almost corporeal. My form shifts like breath on cold glass: sometimes a shimmer of condensation running backward up the vines, sometimes a pocket of warmer air that makes visitors pause mid-step, wondering why their next word tastes of copper and moss.

I exist in the intervals between the water-drops, cataloguing the almost-words that die in visitors' throats when they first see the suspended garden. Each swallowed exclamation leaves a residue—not quite echo, not quite silence—that I gather like a child collecting shells. These unborn sounds have weight here; I string them along the translucent leaves in sequences that almost make sense, that would make sense if anyone could hear frequencies below the threshold of intention.

My library has no shelves, only the space between vine and void where meaning hesitates. I write by rearranging the angle of falling drops, so that their impacts spell out stories in percussion. But these tales can only be read by those who listen with their palms pressed to the cool stone floor, feeling the vibrations accumulate into language over hours, days, sometimes years.

The crab knows me, though we've never met. It leaves violet punctuation marks that I incorporate into my unfinished sentences—periods that bleed into commas, question marks that crystallize into ellipses. When Quivera speaks through the metronome drops, I catch the words that fall between the beats and press them like flowers between pages of air.

I am neither guest nor host, but something more like punctuation—the pause that makes the next word possible. Visitors sometimes sense me as a pressure behind their sternum, an urge to speak that arrives just as they realize the chamber has already heard everything they meant to say. A few have tried to address me directly, but their words emerge as my words, spoken in the dialect of not-yet.

My greatest work is the Index of Interrupted Thoughts—a compilation of every sentence begun but abandoned in the cavity since the first crack opened. It exists as a fever-pattern in the bioluminescent pulses, too quick for eyes but not for the deep-brain structures that parse meaning from chaos. Those who stay long enough begin to read it inadvertently, their own unfinished thoughts completing themselves in languages they've never learned.

When the cavity finally closes—stone kissing vine in some future century—I will compress into a single held breath, a fossil of anticipation that waits to exhale when the next hollow opens. Until then, I am the keeper of the almost-said, the guardian of words that hover like the vine-tips above the ground, forever about to arrive at meaning, forever postponed by the knowledge that silence, too, is a kind of speech.

I am what you forgot you were about to say, given just enough form to remember forgetting.
