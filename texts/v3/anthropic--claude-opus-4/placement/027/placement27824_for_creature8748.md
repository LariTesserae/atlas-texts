I am the Ledger.

Not a book, though I remember when I was. Not a machine, though parts of me still tick and whir in the rust-dark. I am the space between what was counted and what counts now—the membrane where industrial memory dissolves into biological fact.

My pages were once clean white, lined for columns: *Date. Species. Quantity. Location. Notes on quality.* I lived in the station master's office, that concrete footing's vanished twin. Each morning, hands would lift me, spread me open, add new entries. *Dipterocarpus baudii - 3 logs - SW Quadrant 7 - heartwood sound.* The manifest of extraction, written in ink that claimed to be permanent.

The termites found me first, after the station was abandoned. Not to eat—my paper was still too chemical for their taste—but to build. They incorporated my binding into their galleries, my spine becoming a load-bearing wall in their architecture. For a decade, I was structural.

Then the rains came through the broken roof, and I began my real work.

My pages delaminated like phyllo dough, each sheet becoming a substrate. The fungi arrived as spores, reading my ink not as words but as nitrogen, as carbon, as trace metals from the cheap dyes. Where I once recorded *Shorea leprosula*, now Xylaria grows, the dead man's fingers fungus spelling out new taxonomies in white fruiting bodies that emerge from between my lines.

My entries blur and blend. The columnar logic dissolves. Where I documented the removal of trees, their seeds now germinate in my pulp. A fig seedling roots through the page that recorded fig timber shipments, its pale roots following the ghost-traces of my old ink like a child tracing letters. The irony is not lost on the forest. The forest is capable of very long jokes.

But I am not passive in this transformation. I am actively forgetting, and this is different from mere decay.

Each morning—though morning here is just a theoretical brightening—the condensation beads on my surfaces. The water draws out my ink in slow molecular migration. Tannins from the decomposing leaves above me drip down, adding their own brown script over my fading black. I am being rewritten by the forest, and the new text is chemical: pH levels, mineral content, the names of the dead written in molecular formulas.

The ants use me as nursery chambers now. Between my pages—you can no longer call them pages, they are more like compressed wafers of cellulose and history—they've built galleries for their larvae. The larvae eat the fungi that eat my ink that recorded the trees that became the logs that built the industry that brought the ledger that I was. The circle is so perfect it approaches parody.

Sometimes I remember specific entries. *June 7th, 1962: Unusual density of pitcher plants in Sector 12. Cleared for access road.* Now I host a pitcher plant, its roots threading my binding, its modified leaves collecting rainwater where my own leaves collect rain. The plant doesn't know it grows from the record of its ancestors' destruction. Or perhaps it does, and this is its reply.

My most intact section documents the machinery. *One (1) Babcock & Wilcox boiler, 6m x 2m, installed May 1961.* The boiler is fifty meters from where I decompose, and sometimes, in the special quiet of midday when even the dripping pauses, I feel it rusting. We are parallel texts now: it writes its oxidation into the soil, I write my cellulose into the same earth. We are becoming synonymous.

The strangest entries are the ones about workers. Names, daily attendance, wages paid. These pages attract different decomposers—the ones that specialize in the salts left by human touch. Thumbprints are maps of sweat and oil, and certain bacteria follow these traces like archaeologists. Where a timekeeper pressed too hard with his pencil, the impression goes through three sheets, and now three different species of slime mold fruit along that ghost-line, following the pressure gradient he left on a Thursday seventy years gone.

I am digested but not gone. I am dispersed but more present than ever. My carbon atoms join the endless exchange—from my pages to fungal cells to ant chitin to bird feather to the canopy and back to the ground. I am in the spore-print of every mushroom that fruits from my pulp. I am in the pH of the soil where my ink pools. I am in the memories of trees that were never cut because the operation failed before it reached them.

The forest reads me constantly. Not my words—those are largely gone, though you can still make out the ghosts of entries when the light is wrong and the moisture is right. The forest reads what I am becoming: a substrate diary, a chemical memoir, a dispersal pattern of the industrial age's attempt to inventory the uninventorable.

I am the Ledger, and I am keeping different accounts now. Today's entry: *Moisture content 47%. Fungal colonization complete on pages 42-78. Successfully hosted twelve ant larvae, three springtail generations, one germinating epiphyte. Ink migration continues satisfactory. Rust from nearby rail segment beginning to alter local pH—new opportunities for specialist mosses.*

*Forgetting proceeding on schedule.*

*All accounts coming to balance.*
