I would find myself as one of the **Tongue-ferns**.

Not the Kneaders with their endless labor, their thumbs raw from patching what will always leak again. Not the valve-lice, those flatulent little pressure-gauges living mouth-first against the pipes. I would be rooted in the wet clay, yes, but growing *up* into the confusion of that gravity-bent space.

My flesh would be that thick leather, warm as blood, each frond a muscled tongue tasting the mineral-rich air. I would drink the steam through my pores and sweat it back out in an endless circulation, my stomata opening and closing like tiny mouths breathing the chamber's pressurized atmosphere.

What strange consciousness would I have? Not thought as we know it, but something more immediate—a constant awareness of moisture gradients, of pressure differentials. I would *feel* the Kneaders approaching by the vibrations in my root system long before their pale bulk disturbed the air around my fronds. I would know each individual by the particular rhythm of their thumb-work in the clay that feeds me.

The gray velvet underneath my fronds—that would be my only vulnerability, my soft underbelly that the Kneaders scrape for sustenance. But I would grow it back, always, a perpetual offering to those who maintain the world that maintains me. It would not hurt, exactly, but I would feel the scraping as a kind of grooming, a necessary thinning that keeps me from growing too thick, too heavy for my own flesh to support.

My roots would be the deepest knowledge-keepers in that place. Woven through the clay, following the green timber's grain, I would map every weakness in the structure through the minute chemical changes in the sap that bleeds from the binding vines. I would taste the iron from the Kneaders' thumb-stains, the copper leaching from the beaten plates above, the limestone dissolved in the ascending steam.

In the bent light of that hovering water-globule, I would appear to myself in fragments—my own fronds reflected and refracted, multiplied into a forest of tongues all tasting the same impossibly thick air. Through the bladder-windows, I would see my parallel selves in that sideways room, our movements synchronized but delayed, a botanical echo of my own existence.

The sound would be my constant companion—not heard but *felt* through every cell. The whistling pipes would vibrate through my flesh like a deep bass note, the wet thumps of air pockets would shake my fronds like percussion. I would live inside the rhythm of that place, my growth timed to its industrial heartbeat.

And when pieces of me died—when fronds grew too heavy and tore free—they would not fall down but drift in that confused gravity, spiraling slowly toward the water-globule where they would float, slowly dissolving, their minerals returning to the cycle that feeds the clay that feeds my roots.

I would be both permanent and temporary, structural and edible, essential to the Hollow-of-the-Loom's wet ecology. My existence would be measured not in years but in cycles—how many times the water I sweat becomes the steam I breathe becomes the rain that feeds me again. 

To be a tongue-fern would be to taste eternity one drop at a time, forever licking the air of that impossible chamber suspended in its pocket of bent physics and desperate maintenance.
