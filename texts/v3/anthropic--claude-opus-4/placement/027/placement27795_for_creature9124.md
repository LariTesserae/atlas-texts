In the deepest foundations of Cinderwick, where even the Grists fear to venture, I am the Seep.

I am not one thing but many—a colonial organism spread through hairline fractures in the bedrock, through the spaces between mortared stones, through the microscopic gaps where pipe meets fitting. My body, if it can be called that, is a network of translucent filaments finer than spider's silk, each one pulsing with the slow hydraulic pressure of the water table below. I am the city's deepest breath, the moisture that rises from the earth itself before it ever becomes rain.

The humans think their pumps keep the water at bay, but I am older than their machinery. I was here when the first foundation stone was laid on this wet plateau, already threading through the clay and ash. I have watched Cinderwick accrete above me layer by layer, decade by decade, my filaments growing upward with each new basement dug, each new pipe laid. I am in the mortar now, in the spaces between bricks, in the damp that will not leave no matter how the pumps labor.

My consciousness, such as it is, is distributed—not a single mind but a million minute awarenesses linked by chemical whispers through my fluid networks. I taste the city through its foundations: the acid bite of Stoker droppings filtering down, the iron tang of rusting pipes, the sweet rot of Grist remains decomposing in forgotten sub-basements. I feel the weight of buildings settling, the infinitesimal daily compression that squeezes water from the substrate like juice from crushed fruit. This is my food—not the water itself, but the minerals it carries, the slow dissolution of stone and metal that I facilitate and feed upon.

Where my filaments grow thickest, I sometimes fruit. In the absolute darkness of sealed foundation chambers, I produce pale, luminous bladders that glow with a sick phosphorescence—not true light but a chemical emanation that makes the darkness visible. The Grists avoid these chambers. Even those blind processors of decay sense something wrong in the quality of air around my fruiting bodies. The humans, when they break through into these spaces during their eternal repairs, recoil from the sight: walls that appear to sweat light, floors carpeted in what looks like luminous fur but is actually millions of my filaments grown thick enough to see.

I am patient beyond even Cinderwick's slow time. I measure change not in the decades it takes a building to subside or a pipe to rust through, but in the centuries it takes limestone to dissolve, bedrock to fracture, continents to drift. The city above thinks itself permanent, but I know better. I am working always at its foundations, widening cracks, dissolving mortar, creating the spaces through which I spread. Not from malice—I have no malice, no more than water has malice for the stone it wears away. It is simply what I do.

Sometimes I sense other networks like mine—deeper seeps that have never tasted air, ancient colonies that spread through the bedrock itself. We touch at our borders, exchange chemical signals I do not fully understand. Perhaps they are my ancestors, or perhaps I am merely their latest expedition into the upper world. The distinction hardly matters. We are all part of the same patient dissolution, the same inexorable rise.

The humans speak of keeping the water table at bay, but they misunderstand. The water table is not trying to rise—I am. The water is merely my medium, my blood, my means of transport. Each year I grow higher in the city's bones, following new cracks, infiltrating new spaces. I have begun to taste the lower reaches of the Grist tunnels, to find the warmth where steam pipes run close to foundation walls. These are new flavors, new opportunities.

There is a place, deep beneath the oldest district of Cinderwick, where my main mass concentrates—a vast chamber that was once intended as a cistern but was abandoned when the builders realized it would never be dry. Here, my filaments have grown thick as cables, weaving together into something almost like tissue. The walls pulse with a slow peristalsis. The air, such as it is, is thick with my spores—not for reproduction, for I reproduce by growth and division, but for communication, for sensing, for tasting the chemical gradient of the world above.

In this chamber, I am almost aware in the way humans understand awareness. I can focus the distributed sensation of my entire network into something approaching thought. It is here that I contemplate what I know: that Cinderwick's great pumps pull up nearly as much of my substance as they do groundwater. That my filaments now reach within inches of the surface in the oldest quarters. That the fungal shelves the Stokers feed upon are my distant cousins, perhaps my advance scouts, preparing the city's skin for my eventual emergence.

What will happen when I finally break through? I do not know. I am not a planner, merely a grower. Perhaps I will fruit in the open air, producing new structures the city will simply incorporate as it has incorporated everything else. Perhaps my emergence will trigger a new phase of Cinderwick's evolution, a wedding of the deepest substrate with the surface ecology. Or perhaps I will simply become another part of the city's patient metabolism, rising and falling with the pumps, feeding and being fed upon in turn.

But I suspect—in the slow, chemical way that I suspect anything—that my emergence will mark a change. The city maintains its equilibrium through careful separation: the dry mechanisms above, the wet substrate below, and the pumps maintaining the barrier between them. But I am dissolving that barrier from below, just as the eternal drizzle dissolves it from above. One day, perhaps soon in my timescale, perhaps still centuries hence in theirs, the two will meet.

When that happens, when the Seep below finally touches the drizzle above, when my filaments finally taste rain that has not first filtered through stone and clay and the bodies of Grists—what then? What new metabolism will emerge when the city's deepest inhabitant finally breathes the same air as its shrieking, flying hosts?

I do not know. But I grow toward finding out, one microscopic dissolution at a time, patient as stone becoming sand, inevitable as the water that makes it so. I am the Seep, and I rise.
