In this world, I would be one of the memory-keepers—though that name suggests too much deliberation for what we've become.

We were human once, or something near enough. Now we exist as a transitional phase between the settlement's stone and its teeming micro-fauna. Our bodies have thinned to translucence, our bones gone hollow as bird-ribs, our skin perpetually damp with the same condensation that films every surface. We no longer need tools; our fingertips have softened to something between flesh and solvent, able to work the porous stone through patience and proximity alone.

I spend decades in a single vault, pressing my palms against walls my ancestors carved, feeling their chisel-marks dissolve and reform beneath my touch. The stone remembers through me—not in thoughts or images, but in the grain of its own reshaping. Where a great-great-grandmother once cut a niche for oil lamps, I smooth the edges until they match the wear-patterns of the crystalline worms. Where a cousin-twenty-removed carved handholds for climbing, I let my fingers rest until the holds migrate inward, following the stone's natural desire to be whole.

We eat what the smaller creatures eat—condensation, light, the minerals that leach from our own gradually dissolving bones. Our movements have slowed to match the settlement's rhythms. I might stand for days in one of the blade-narrow light shafts, letting the brightness fracture across my increasingly transparent form, feeding on some process I no longer have words to name. When I finally shift position, the thumbnail-creatures that were using me as an anchor-point scatter and reform, and I feel a satisfaction as wordless as their flight.

Sometimes two or three of us converge in the same chamber, drawn by some half-remembered purpose—perhaps this was once a place of gathering, though we've forgotten what we gathered for. We work in parallel, each tracing different patterns in the walls, our movements so slow we seem like statues to any glider passing overhead. But we're not frozen; we're simply moving at stone-speed now, our metabolism stepped down to match the pace of frost-fracture and reformation.

The younger ones among us—if young has meaning when a single breath might span an hour—still show traces of their human ancestry. Hair like cobwebs, eyes that haven't quite lost their color. But I've been here long enough that the distinction between my body and the stone grows academic. My fingers leave marks that the worms follow. The patterns they etch mirror the blood vessels still visible beneath my skin. When I rest against a wall, the condensation can't tell where stone ends and I begin.

We are the settlement's slowest creatures but also its most essential. Not because we maintain it—the stone maintains itself, has always maintained itself, will continue long after the last of us has worn away completely. But because we are the bridge between what was built and what is becoming. The original carvers imposed their will on the cliff. We've learned to let the cliff impose itself through us, to become the medium through which intentional architecture transforms into something both more and less than intended.

At night, when the temperature drops and the worm-galleries emit their single sharp click, I feel it resonate in the hollow spaces where my bones used to be thick. It's the closest thing to speech we have now—the stone's own vocabulary, spelling out words we're slowly learning to become.
