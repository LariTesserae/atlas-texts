I find myself as one of the cable-singers.

We are a small guild, perhaps thirty of us in the whole city. Not engineers—though we work alongside them—and not musicians, though what we do involves sound. We are diagnosticians of tension, readers of vibration, interpreters of the language that stressed metal speaks when it holds more weight than it should for longer than it should.

I inherited the work from my mother, who inherited it from hers. Three generations of women who learned to press their ear to junction points and listen for the particular frequencies of fatigue. My grandmother arrived in Kaldenvast when the city was twenty years young and the engineers still believed in standardization. She was the first to understand that each cable develops its own voice over time, its own harmonics based on load and weather and the particular minerals leaching from its anchor points.

My territory is the Old Suspension, where the cables are thickest and the songs most complex. I walk the primary load-bearers at dawn, when the temperature differential makes the metal contract and the true stress reveals itself. I carry a tuning fork—not for pitch but for comparison, to hear the intervals between what the cable sings and what pure metal would sing. The differences tell stories: microfractures accumulating like hairline script, corrosion eating through the core while the surface remains bright, the slow stretch that comes from decades of holding what should not be held.

The climbing-scorpions know me. They cluster where I work, drawn perhaps by the vibrations, and I've learned to read their movements as another kind of diagnostic. They avoid cables that will fail within the year—not obviously, but with a subtle preference for parallel routes, a reluctance that manifests as empty space where they would normally sun themselves. I've saved three districts this way, marking cables for replacement based on where the scorpions won't go.

My skin has the calluses of the trade: fingertips worn smooth from running along wound metal, palms marked with the spiral patterns of cable-lay, a permanent indentation behind my right ear where I press it to junction boxes for hours each day. My hearing has changed too. I perceive the foundry's thrum not as a single note but as a chord, complex and shifting, and I can tell from my apartment in the mid-districts when they're running a heavy extraction by the way it modulates the sympathetic vibrations in my water pipes.

But it's in the quiet hours that the real work happens. When the foundries cycle down for maintenance and the city's constant hum fades to near-silence, that's when you can hear the cables truly sing. Each one carries the memory of its making, the foundry where it was drawn, the temperature of the day it was installed. But more than that, they carry the accumulation of their service: every gust of wind, every shift in load, every time the geology has moved and asked them to move with it.

I document these songs in a notation of my own devising—part musical score, part engineering diagram, part poetry. The guild maintains an archive in the upper districts, volumes of cable-songs going back sixty years, a record of how the city's voice has changed as it aged. We can trace the biography of individual cables through decades of notation, watch them mature from the bright ping of new metal to the complex harmonics of middle age to the dangerous quietness that precedes failure.

Sometimes I work at night, when the traffic on the catwalks has stilled and the only sounds are the city's own: water seeping through everything, wind finding new paths through the suspension system, the occasional groan of geology doing whatever geology does in the dark. These are the hours when I feel most clearly that I am not just maintaining infrastructure but tending to something alive, something that breathes through metal lungs and dreams in frequencies too low to properly hear.

The deep-dwellers avoid me. I've seen them in the lower districts, moving with their terrible slowness, but they alter their paths to avoid the cables I'm working on. At first I thought they were responding to the vibrations, but now I wonder if it's something else. Perhaps they recognize a fellow interpreter of the city's hidden languages. Perhaps they know that we are both, in our different ways, trying to understand something that resists understanding.

My daughter has begun to follow me on my rounds, though she's only seven. She presses her small ear to the junction points and tells me what she hears in the language of shapes and colors—"That one sounds purple," or "This one is too pointy." I'm teaching her the formal notation, but I remember what my mother told me: the real knowledge comes from time, from learning the particular vocabulary of each district, each installation, each cable that holds our impossible city in its web of trust and tension.

When newcomers ask me if I ever fear the cables will fail, I tell them what my grandmother told my mother, what my mother told me: The cables sing their intentions long before they act on them. You just have to learn to listen. And in Kaldenvast, where the ground cannot be trusted and the future is still being mined from stone that doesn't quite exist yet, listening is the most essential skill of all.

The city sways. The cables sing. I listen, and transcribe, and trust that tomorrow they will still hold.
