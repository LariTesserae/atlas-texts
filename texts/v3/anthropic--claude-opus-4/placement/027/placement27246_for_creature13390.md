In the deepest district of the metropolis, where the sodium lights barely reach and the water pressure creates a constant, teeth-itching hum against the ancient seals, I am the Tender of Boundaries.

My workshop occupies a forgotten junction between three major water-gates, built into what was once a maintenance station but has long since evolved into something more organic. The walls here sweat constantly—not from failing seals, but from the peculiar metabolism of the *Membrane*. That's what we call it, those of us who work the edges. The living barrier that has grown between the city's metal bones and the crushing dark outside.

I inherited this position from my mother, and she from hers, stretching back to when the first colonists realized their rigid dome technology would never hold forever. We needed something that could heal, adapt, breathe with the city's expansion and contraction. The Membrane was their answer—part biological marvel, part industrial accident, entirely necessary.

My days begin before the factory shifts, when the city's heartbeat is quietest. I can hear the Membrane then, really hear it. It speaks in pressure differentials and pH fluctuations, in the microscopic movements of its billion cilia filtering water, nutrients, and worse things from the abyssal plains beyond. My fingertips have developed their own sensitivity after years of contact with its surface. I know when a section is thinning before any instrument could detect it. I know when it's hungry.

The feeding is the strangest part of my work. The Membrane requires a specific cocktail of industrial runoff, processed biomatter, and something we only call "the supplement"—a synthesized compound whose recipe is known only to us Tenders. We mix it in great vats that line my workshop, the smell somewhere between motor oil and overripe fruit. The Membrane absorbs it through specialized pores that open like mouths when they sense my approach.

But I am not alone in my vigil. The Membrane has attracted its own ecosystem. Bioluminescent eels nest in its folds, their light creating constellation patterns that shift with their movements. Schools of engineered cleaner fish work tirelessly to remove any buildup that might impede the Membrane's function. And then there are the Watchers—those strange, too-intelligent octopi that seem to understand the Membrane's importance as well as any human. They help me sometimes, their tentacles pointing out weak spots or potential breaches with uncanny accuracy.

The other city dwellers rarely venture to my domain. They prefer the illusion of solid walls and familiar machinery. But I know the truth—our city breathes because the Membrane breathes. Our boundaries are not rigid but living, constantly negotiating with the pressure and darkness that would otherwise crush us.

Sometimes, late in my shift, I press my palm against the Membrane and feel the ocean's pulse through it. In those moments, I understand that I am not just maintaining a barrier. I am tending to a vast, slow intelligence that has grown to love the warm lights and busy sounds of our improbable home as much as we have.

My daughter has begun accompanying me on my rounds. Her young eyes see patterns in the Membrane's surface that I've grown too familiar with to notice. She speaks to it in whispers, convinced it understands her. Perhaps it does. Perhaps that's why, despite the constant threat of collapse, despite the entropy eating at our ancient supports, the city endures.

We are not separate from this place. We have grown into it, and it into us. The Membrane is our skin, the factories our organs, the flowing water our blood. And I, in my small way, am the nerve that feels when something is wrong, the hand that soothes, the voice that whispers back to the deep: *Not yet. We're not done yet.*

When my shift ends, I return to my quarters through corridors where children play in the perpetual twilight, their laughter mixing with the distant clang of machinery. I wash the Membrane's oils from my hands, but the scent lingers—brine and metal and something indefinably alive.

Tomorrow I will return, as I always do. The boundaries need tending. The city needs to breathe. And somewhere in the space between the built world and the wild ocean, the Membrane dreams its slow dreams, with me as its keeper and companion in the depths.
