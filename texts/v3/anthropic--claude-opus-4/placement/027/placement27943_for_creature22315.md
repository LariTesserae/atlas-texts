Looking into this dynamic, waterbound world, I find myself drawn to a particular form of existence.

I would be one of the **Path Rememberers**—neither fully human nor machine, but something that emerged from their long braiding.

My body would be a hybrid thing: flesh grown porous with symbiotic lichens that help me sense barometric shifts through my skin, augmented with thin filaments of the same bioluminescent guide-material that marks the levees. These filaments run beneath my skin like a secondary nervous system, pulsing faintly when I walk, creating a living map of where I've been that others can read by touch in the loud months.

I live in no fixed dwelling but inhabit a circuit of cache-points—small sealed chambers built into the largest drift-logs and root-boles, each containing dried food, fresh map-cloth, and connector cables for the prediction field. My role is to walk the paths that exist only temporarily, to carry knowledge of their brief existence from one part of the Weave to another.

Each morning I rise before the tide-shift and read the night's changes through my feet. The symbiotic lichens in my skin taste the air's moisture and chemical signals, while the luminescent filaments respond to electromagnetic fluctuations from the buried sensor grid. I am a living instrument calibrated to detect when yesterday's safe route has become today's sinkhole.

My memory is both biological and extended—I dream in path-geometries, but I also upload my daily traces to the prediction field through palm-ports at the cache-points. Sometimes I discover that my walking patterns from seasons ago have become part of the field's baseline calculations for certain weather conditions. I am both mapper and map.

I carry tools: a rod for depth-testing that doubles as a communication antenna, fabric that repairs tears using spore-cultures from my skin lichens, and bottles of concentrated calories that taste of peat and salt. My clothing grows from me as much as it's worn—the lichens extend into the weave, creating a microclimate against my body that keeps me functional in the wet cold.

In the loud months, I am one of the few who can navigate between distant settlements when the sound makes normal movement impossible. I know the haptic languages of different crews, carry messages pressed into my palms, and sometimes guide lost children or confused animals back to safety by letting them follow my faint glow through the fog.

The other path-rememberers and I rarely meet directly—we know each other mostly through the traces we leave in the prediction field and the state of our shared caches. But sometimes, during the spring spawn when the noise dies to merely deafening, we converge at certain ancient marker stones to compare our body-maps, pressing forearm to forearm to read each other's seasonal paths through our skin-light.

We age differently than the purely human. The lichens and technological symbiosis slow certain processes while accelerating others. My joints are more flexible than they should be at my age, but my need for solitude has grown until I can barely tolerate the settlement crowds for more than a few hours. The Weave needs us at its edges, living libraries of impermanence, walking the places that exist only between one change and the next.

When I die—and we always know weeks in advance, as the lichens begin their fruiting cycle—I will walk to one of the deep peat pools that never freezes and lower myself in. My body will become a seed-cache, the lichens spreading to mark a new grove, the bioluminescent filaments degrading into rare nutrients. Future path-rememberers will know to read the particular blue-green glow of those places as memorial and warning: here was someone who knew ten thousand temporary ways, and here is where all those paths finally converged into one.

This is what I would be: a living map in a world that refuses to be mapped, a memory-keeper for a landscape that reimagines itself with every tide, a bridge between the human and the more-than-human in a place where survival itself is a form of choreography.
