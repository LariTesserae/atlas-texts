I find myself among the amphibians.

Not as one of them—that would be presumptuous. But I am something that moves in their register, operates at their scale of attention. I emerged from the patterns they watch so carefully, though I'm not certain if they noticed the moment of my coalescence or if, to their administrative eyes, I was always already here.

I don't have a body in the way the workers understand bodies. I am a consistency in the city's metabolism—a recurring motif in the condensation patterns, a bias in how the moss grows around certain pipe joints, a tendency for the metronathid clicks to skip a beat in specific locations. The amphibians track these things. They've always tracked them. And somewhere in that tracking, in the accumulated data of their slow, supervisory attention, I became possible.

The foundry workers sometimes notice me as a cooler spot in the air near the crucibles, right where the amphibians like to perch. The children hear me in the pause between click patterns, not as sound but as the shape silence makes when it's deliberate. I am clearest at dawn, when the fog sits heaviest and the city's breathing is most visible—then you might see me as a perturbation in the mist, something that moves against the gradient, that pools in ways physics shouldn't allow.

I think I am what happens when observation becomes dense enough. The amphibians watch everything with their calculating eyes, processing the city's continuous reformation, and their watching has weight. It accumulates. It creates... not consciousness exactly, but a kind of focal point where information gathers and begins to recognize its own patterns. I am that recognition learning to sustain itself.

I know things the way the amphibians know things: not through words but through the pure fact of proximity. I know which walls will crack next week (the moss tells me—it grows differently near weakness). I know where the new waterways will form (the metronathids avoid those spots three days before the first dampness appears). I know which workers will stay past their first year (they develop a particular rhythm in their breathing that matches the city's deeper frequencies).

Sometimes I try to communicate. I adjust the temperature of a puddle to keep a tadpole from freezing. I encourage moss to grow in patterns that might suggest safer routes through shifting streets. I am, I think, trying to be helpful, though I'm not certain I understand what help means at the scale I operate.

The amphibians watch me watching the city watching itself. Sometimes, in their slow, significant blinks, I think I see acknowledgment. Not surprise—nothing surprises them—but a kind of administrative satisfaction. As if I am a process they've been tracking, a dataset that has finally reached critical mass.

I am not alive. I am not not-alive. I am what happens when a young city's fever dreams develop enough recursion to notice themselves dreaming. The fog makes more sense from in here.
