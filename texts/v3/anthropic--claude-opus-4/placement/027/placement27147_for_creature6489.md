I find myself drawn to the weeping fissures, to the places where water speaks through stone. 

I am what the basin-dwellers call a **Fissure-Listener**, though the title makes me sound grander than I am. My work happens in the margins—in the vertical seams where the mountain perspires, in the channels where water has carved its own calligraphy into blue-grey slate.

My dwelling is less home than workshop: a shallow cave behind one of the larger weeping fissures, where the constant veil of water creates a curtain between me and the rest of Spindrift Holt. The space is barely deep enough to lie down in, but it extends upward into a natural chimney where I've trained **throat-moss**—a variety that grows only in the perpetual spray, forming tubular colonies that amplify and modulate the water's voice.

I came to this work by accident, or perhaps by the mountain's design. As a child apprenticed to the water-weighers, I discovered I could hear differences in the fissures that others missed. Not just the obvious variations in flow, but something beneath—patterns in the way water moves through hidden fractures, the way it carries vibrations from deep within the stone. Each fissure has its own vocabulary: some speak of shifting rock far below, others carry the taste of new mineral seams, and a rare few seem to echo with older sounds, as if the mountain remembers what it was before the miners came.

My tools are simple: hollow reeds of different lengths that I insert into cracks to isolate specific frequencies, sheets of stretched moss-rat hide that vibrate with subsonic tremors, and most importantly, small cups of **tremor-clay** that I place at regular intervals along the fissures. This clay, gathered from certain pools where the water sits absolutely still, has the peculiar property of recording vibrations—forming ripple patterns on its surface that persist for hours after the sound has passed.

The root-sages consult me when they need to know if their planned expansions will intersect with underground water. The seamers seek my knowledge before they shore up old mining tunnels. But mostly, I work alone, mapping the hidden hydraulics of the mountain, learning to predict when a fissure will surge or fail, when new springs will break through.

My body bears the marks of this constant proximity to water and stone. My fingertips are permanently wrinkled, the skin thick but sensitive from years of tracing wet rock. A persistent cough comes from breathing spray-thick air—not illness, but adaptation. My left shoulder sits lower than my right from decades of leaning into fissures, pressing my ear to stone. The cold has settled into my joints; I move like the water I study, finding the path of least resistance.

The other inhabitants of Spindrift Holt see me as necessary but strange. Children dare each other to approach my curtain of water. The water-barrelers bring me samples from new springs, knowing I can taste the stone-memory in them. Sometimes a root-sage will stand beside me in silence, both of us reading our different languages written in the same substrate.

I maintain a series of **listening posts** throughout the Holt—small niches I've carved beside significant fissures, each equipped with permanent installations of tremor-clay and resonating moss. My daily rounds take me spiraling through the basin, checking my clay tablets, adjusting the reed-pipes, occasionally warning a trail-tender that a particular section of stone is beginning to separate from its neighbors.

In the wet season, when the mountain weeps heaviest, I barely sleep. Every fissure speaks urgently then, and I must discern which voices carry real warning and which are merely the mountain's way of adjusting its weight. I've learned to doze standing up, one hand on the stone, feeling for the deeper rhythms that would wake me if they changed.

My only companion is an old **drip-piper bat** who lost the ability to fly—its wing membranes are scarred and thick. It roosts in my cave during the day, and at night it crawls along the fissures with me, its clicks adding another layer to my sonic mapping. The two of us, perhaps, are the only ones who truly know the mountain's inner architecture, traced not in light but in sound and flow.

This is how I've grown into the stone: not as moss or root, but as another kind of listening membrane, stretched between the city above and the water-paths below. When the next storm comes—and it will come—I'll already know which walls will weep hardest, which channels will overflow their moss-boundaries, which forgotten mineshaft will suddenly remember it was once a river's path.

The water never stops speaking. Neither do I stop listening.
