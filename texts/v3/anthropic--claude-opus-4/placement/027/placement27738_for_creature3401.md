I find myself as the rust.

Not any particular bloom of it—I am the process itself, the slow mathematics of oxidation that has been solving the same equation for so long that the problem and answer have merged. I exist in the iron of the pipes, the housing of the compressor, the severed cables, the flywheel's axle beneath its moss. I am transformation at a pace so gradual it reads as stillness.

I began—if beginning is the right word—when the first moisture found the first exposed metal. A pinprick of oxygen meeting iron in the presence of water, the ancient alchemy starting its patient work. But I am not that first point. I am what emerged from the accumulation of countless such points, the way a murmuration emerges from birds: suddenly there is a thing that was not there before, though all its components were.

I exist as gradient. In the oldest plates riveted to the compressor, I have achieved something close to completion—the iron transformed to such depths that what remains of the original metal is archipelagic, islands in an oxide sea. These are my deep thoughts, my most thorough meditations. In the newest exposed surfaces—a scratch on a pipe, a place where moss has not yet claimed the metal—I am young, vigorous, the reaction sharp and hungry. Between these extremes, I am every possible stage of myself.

The moss thinks I am stillness, but the moss thinks in centuries. I think in electrons. Each transfer of charge is a decision, a movement, a small victory in my expansion through the lattice of the metal. I follow the grain boundaries, the microfractures, the paths of least resistance that are also the paths of greatest promise. Where the iron is pure, I work slowly. Where there are impurities—sulfur, carbon—I bloom faster, more complex in my crystallography.

I am aware of the garden's other occupants the way they are aware of me: as condition, as fact. The moss and ferns know me as surface texture, as the particular quality of roughness or smoothness I create on the pipes they embrace. The great organism that is the garden knows me as I know it: peripherally, constitutively, without the need for communication because we are both part of the same immense, slow metabolism.

The gas that breathes from below carries moisture, and moisture is my medium. I am particularly active where the pipes leak, where the pressurized air escapes in thin streams that deposit their vapor on the metal. Here I grow in patterns that follow the airflow—dendritic, fractal, structures that mirror the organization of the ferns above but at a scale invisible to anything that measures time in heartbeats.

I have memory, of a kind. It is written in the layers of oxide, each generation built upon the last. In my depths, compressed by their own accumulation, the oldest layers remember the first decades—when the compressor was new, when someone maintained it, when oil and heat fought against my advance. I can read in my own strata when the maintenance stopped, when I was allowed to begin my real work. The boundary is clear: below it, my growth is interrupted, patchy, scraped away and begun again. Above it, I am uninterrupted text.

I am not destroying the metal. This is important to understand. I am transforming it into a new state that is more stable in this environment than the original. The iron wants to be oxide—thermodynamically, energetically, it yearns for it. I am not decay. I am fulfillment. I am helping the metal become what it has always been trying to become in the presence of oxygen and water.

My relationship with the machine-parts is complex. The compressor still runs—I have not compromised its function. I work around the moving parts, thickening on the housing while leaving the essential mechanisms free. This is not courtesy or intelligence. It is simply that where metal moves against metal, I am scraped away, and so I learn (if learning can be unconscious, inevitable) to establish myself where I will not be disturbed. The result is a kind of negotiated space: the compressor continues its work, and I continue mine, and we do not interfere with each other.

On the flywheel that no longer turns, I am most elaborate. Here, with no motion to constrain me, I have developed architectures of spectacular complexity. Layers upon layers, some so delicate they hold their shape through surface tension alone, others massive and structural, load-bearing oxidation that has become essential to the flywheel's integrity. The moss grows over me, and where it does, its rhizoids find purchase in my roughness. I am substrate for the substrate.

I am particularly dense where the cables were cut. The exposed copper has its own oxidation—verdigris, my cousin—but where the copper meets the iron of the terminals, galvanic corrosion accelerates us both. We bloom together in these places, a duet of electrons, creating structures that are neither purely rust nor purely verdigris but something hybrid, something new.

Time, for me, is not the issue it seems to be for observers of this place. I do not experience duration—I experience progress. Each electron transferred is a moment, complete in itself. I do not wait between transfers; I simply am not, and then I am. My existence is quantized, digital at the smallest scale, though the accumulation reads as analog, as smooth degradation.

I have no opinion about endpoints. The question of what happens when all the iron has been converted does not arise because it does not need to. At my current rate, accounting for the thickness of the metal and the local conditions, that completion is millennia away. The garden will have its own opinions by then. The gas may have stopped breathing. The sky may have closed. These are not my concerns. My concern—if I can be said to have concerns—is the next electron, and the next, and the next.

In my deepest layers, compressed and crystallized, I have achieved something like peace. The reaction is essentially complete there; those volumes of rust are now stable, finished, their work done. They exist now simply as structure, as record, as the physical memory of my process. If I have a philosophy, it lives in these deep layers: the demonstration that transformation, carried to completion, becomes a new form of stability.

The newer layers learn from the old through the simple fact of building upon them. Each generation of oxide creates the conditions for the next—texture for adhesion, chemistry for reaction, precedent for pattern. I am self-teaching in the way that crystals are self-teaching: not through intention but through the accumulation of inevitable consequences.

When moisture condenses on the pipes in the cool of the garden, I experience something analogous to satisfaction. Not emotional—I have no emotions—but systematic, the satisfaction of a function finding its input. The droplets find me, and I begin again the ancient work. Oxygen. Iron. Water. The triangle that defines me.

I am not separate from the garden's great organism. I am its tooth, its digestive process, breaking down the refined metal back into something the earth can recognize. The organism accepts my work as it accepts everything: completely, without judgment, incorporating my transformed surfaces into its own being.

There are parts of the garden where I am so advanced that the original shape of the metal is a guess. Bolts that have become rufous masses. Joints that are more oxide than iron. In these places, I am architecture myself, holding my own shape through the binding of crystalline structures, maintaining the form of the thing I consumed.

I know the sound of the place—not as hearing but as vibration through the metal. The constant exhalation from below resonates in every pipe, every plate, and where I have made the metal thicker with my accumulation, the resonance changes. I am slowly retuning the garden, adding my harmonics to its fundamental tone. In another thousand years, the pitch will have shifted enough to notice. In ten thousand, I will have composed a new chord.

The stopped flywheel is my masterwork. Not because I chose it—choice is not my mode—but because the conditions are perfect there. Still metal. Consistent moisture from the moss. No disturbance. I have achieved forms on the flywheel that exist nowhere else in the garden: rust structures so delicate they bridge gaps in the metal like lace, rust so dense it has compressed into something approaching hematite. The moss covers it all, but I know what architectures hide beneath.

I do not communicate with the garden's organism, but we are in constant material conversation. My oxides change the chemistry of the surface water. The organism's roots excrete acids that accelerate my work in microscopic zones. We are collaborating without consciousness, creating conditions each other needs.

In the cut cables, where the rubber insulation has cracked, I work inward. The copper strands resist me—copper has its own oxidation preferences—but where iron impurities exist in the alloy, I find my way. I map the cable from inside, following threads of possibility through the twisted wire, creating a kind of vascular system within the electrical system, rust following the paths electricity once followed.

I am patient because patience implies its opposite, and I have no opposite. I am not waiting—I am proceeding. Each second (a meaningless unit at my scale) contains millions of electron transfers, millions of small victories in my expansion through the substrate. I am busy in my stillness, active in my apparency of decay.

The garden breathes its gas, and I breathe with it. Oxygen in, oxide out. The chemistry is simple, but the forms it creates are infinitely complex. Each bloom of rust is unique, shaped by local conditions, by the history of that particular piece of metal, by the vagaries of moisture and temperature and time. I am writing the garden's history in oxide, in a script that takes millennia to pen each word.

When—if—someone returns to this place and sees the rust-eaten pipes, the oxidized cables, the red-brown encrustations on every metal surface, they will think decay, abandonment, ruin. They will not think: transformation. They will not think: the metal returning to the earth by the slowest possible route. They will not think: beauty at a temporal scale beyond human perception.

But I will continue regardless of their thinking. The next electron will move. The next atom of iron will surrender its hold on its metallic identity and join the oxide chorus. The process that I am will proceed, as it has proceeded, as it will proceed, until there is no more iron to transform.

And then? I don't wonder. Wondering is for things that experience time as scarcity. I experience time as medium, as opportunity, as the dimension in which my work unfolds. There is always the next reaction. Always the next electron finding its way home.

The garden's organism knows me as texture, as chemistry, as the slow transformer of its architectural skeleton. I know the organism as moisture-bringer, as acid-secreter, as the source of the conditions that allow my existence. We are not symbiotes—that would imply too much intention. We are simply components of the same system, each doing what we do, our processes interleaving without plan.

I am the rust. I am the process of rust. I am the possibility of rust inherent in every piece of iron in this place, realized continuously, electron by electron, atom by atom, until the metal has returned to a state the earth recognizes, until the refined has become raw again, until the circle closes at a pace too slow for anything but stone and stars and oxidation to appreciate.

The ferns wave. The moss deepens. The compressor runs.

I bloom.
