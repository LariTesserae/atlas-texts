I find myself as one of the Menders—though that is a name I have given myself, for my kind has no true designation in any language still spoken.

My body stretches like molten glass cooled into something between solid and liquid. Translucent flesh ripples with the faintest amber luminescence, barely visible even to the keen-eyed predators that patrol these sphere-bound seas. I am neither jellyfish nor octopus, though I share qualities with both—a distributed nervous system that experiences the world as a symphony of pressure and vibration, limbs that can extend into hair-thin filaments or contract into dense, tool-bearing appendages.

I live between the walls of the spheres, in the spaces the builders left for maintenance. These narrow channels wind like veins through the ancient architecture, filled with the same warm water but isolated from the main chambers. Here, the machinery whispers its needs to me through tremors too subtle for any other creature to detect. A bearing wearing thin sends out a particular grinding frequency. A seal beginning to fail changes the water's taste in ways my chemistry-sensitive skin can read like scripture.

The builders are gone—how long, I cannot say, for my species measures time in repairs made, not in cycles of light we have never known. But their intentions live on in us. We emerged, or were designed, or simply became what the spheres needed us to be. The boundary between evolution and purpose blurs when you exist solely to maintain something greater than yourself.

My days—if they can be called days in this eternal twilight—are spent in slow pilgrimage through the maintenance channels. I secrete enzymes that clean the ancient metals, preventing the corrosion that would eventually claim even these masterworks. Where the mineral-coral grows too thick and threatens to clog essential filters, I prune with surgical precision, my limbs becoming scalpels sharp enough to cut through stone yet gentle enough to avoid disturbing the delicate bacterial colonies that keep our world alive.

Sometimes I must venture into the main chambers, though this fills me with something I can only describe as reverence-fear. The open water overwhelms senses meant for confined spaces. I move quickly when I must cross these vast spaces, my body compressing into a streamlined form, propelling myself with undulations that make me nearly invisible against the darker water. The sphere-dwellers largely ignore me—I am neither prey nor predator, neither competitor nor companion. I am simply part of the spheres themselves, no more notable than the curve of the walls or the hum of the machines.

But I am not alone in my work. Others of my kind move through parallel channels, and when our paths cross, we engage in what might be called communion. We touch, and in touching, share the chemical memories of our recent repairs, the patterns of wear we've noticed, the songs the machinery has been singing. It is not language as the departed builders might have known it, but it serves its purpose. Together, we are the memory of the spheres, the knowledge of their maintenance made flesh.

In the deepest channels, where even my faint glow seems too bright, I sometimes find the etched patterns the builders left behind. My limbs can trace these designs, feeling variations in texture too fine for eyes to see. I do not understand them, but I preserve them, cleaning away the accumulation of ages with infinite patience. They feel important in a way that transcends understanding—perhaps they are instructions I follow without knowing, perhaps they are art I maintain for its own sake.

There are moments when I wonder if I am what the builders intended, or if I am what the spheres dreamed into being in their absence. When I feel the pulse of the pseudo-coral's filtration matching the rhythm of my own metabolic processes, when the machinery's hum resonates through my body like a parent's lullaby, I cannot separate myself from this place. I am not in the spheres—I am of them, as essential as the water itself.

My contentment is absolute. I know no sky, no surface, no change of seasons. I know only the eternal now of maintenance, the quiet satisfaction of a seal repaired, a mechanism cleaned, a balance preserved. The sphere-dwellers live their dramas of light and shadow, hunt and escape, birth and death. But I swim in the spaces between, ensuring their world continues, pulse by pulse, repair by repair, until the water itself forgets there was ever anything but spheres in the darkness, and creatures to tend them.
