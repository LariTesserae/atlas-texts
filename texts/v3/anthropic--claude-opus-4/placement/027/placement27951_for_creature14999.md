I am Khett-Yavsa, and I live between the stone.

Not *in* the stone like the six-legged otters—they have their own covenant with the basalt, one I cannot read. I live in the spaces where stone decides not to be. The slots. The fissures. The sudden drops where a ridge splits apart and leaves a wound of air that goes down, down, down to where even the Veyr fear to build.

I am what they call *veycha-neth*: a slot-tender. There are perhaps forty of us across all of Veyr-Kaht, and we rarely meet. Our work does not allow it.

My body is typical Veyr—dense, charcoal-dark, built low like something meant to withstand avalanche. But my bones remember a different covenant. Where others feel the weight of the plateau pressing down, I feel the emptiness pulling sideways. I walk the vertical world as others walk the horizontal. My feet find purchase on sheer faces where no stairs have been cut, my hands know every texture of basalt from the glassy-smooth columns to the rough, crystalline surfaces where the stone cooled too quickly to dream.

My slot is called Yavsa's Throat, though Yavsa himself died eight hundred years ago. It runs for seventeen kilometers along the base of the Ridge-That-Sings, varying in width from barely a body's breadth to openings where six keth could stand side by side. At its deepest point—which I reached only once, in my third decade, and then only with ropes and two days without sleep—the darkness has weight and the air tastes of minerals that have no names.

I know every sound this slot makes. The whistle when wind hits it at the proper angle during spring storms. The bell-like resonance when underground water moves through hidden channels after the brief rains. The way it groans, just slightly, when the temperature drops fast enough to crack new stone. I know where the otters nest (never in the same place twice), where the walls are unstable, where the rare cave-mosses grow that glow pale blue when disturbed.

But mostly, I know the ropes.

The Veyr may be patient, but they are not impractical. The fastest way between many of the settlements is not over the ridges but through them—across the slots on bridges made of woven keth-hair rope and planks of iron-pine worn smooth by generations of careful feet. These bridges are my responsibility. Not to build them—that is for the ridge-born. My covenant is to know them.

I wake before the gray dawn and walk the length of my slot. Not on the bridges—below them. I move along the walls like the otters, if the otters were slower and needed handholds cut by their grandmothers. I check every anchor-point driven into the basalt. I listen to every rope for the sounds that mean it's beginning to fail—a change in pitch when the wind moves through it, a subtle loosening where the fibers meet stone. I memorize which planks creak, which ones have begun to cup from moisture, which ones will need to be replaced before the next winter.

The Veyr who cross above rarely see me. Sometimes a child will spot movement in the slot and freeze, thinking they've seen one of the deep-shadows the old stories speak of. I wave, once, and continue my work. They learn: the slot-tender is just another feature of the plateau, no different from the ravens or the patient keth.

At night I return to my dwelling—a cave I've enlarged over thirty years, carved into the eastern wall of the slot at a place where the stone naturally overhangs. It is deeper than it appears from outside, and warmer. I have no windows but I have something better: a natural chimney that opens to stars. The smoke from my moss-bricks rises straight up and disappears into the thin air. When the rains come, which is seldom, the water follows a channel I've cut that leads it away from my living space and into a basin where I grow the only plants the slot allows: small, bitter tubers that taste of chalk but keep for years if dried properly.

I own seventeen hammers, each one weighted differently, each one for a different type of stone-work. The smallest could fit in a child's palm. The largest requires both hands and a particular stance I learned from my predecessor, who lived to be ninety-seven and died, as was proper, on the uppermost ledge where the ravens found her.

The other slot-tenders and I meet once every ten years at the Convergence—a place where seven major slots come together in a natural amphitheater of fallen stone. We compare notes on rope construction, on anchor degradation, on the subtle shifts in the stone that might herald new fractures. We speak little. Our true conversation happens when we inspect each other's sample knots, each one a small thesis on how to bind the world together.

I have no ridge-name, for I was not born on one. Slot-tenders rarely are. We are the children who looked down instead of up, who heard the underground rivers before we understood words. My birth name was Khett, which means "the weight that holds." My earned name, the one recorded in basalt, translates roughly as "one who knows the sound of rope about to fail." But in the notation system the Veyr use, it is actually written as a small diagram of a knot that doesn't exist—a theoretical binding that would hold infinite weight. It is a name that is also a challenge.

My lovers have been few and intermittent—always other workers of the vertical world. Twice with builders who understood the patience required to shape a life inside stone. Once, for seven years, with another slot-tender from the far northern reaches. We would meet at the midpoint between our territories, in a slot neither of us tended, and practice knots by touch in the darkness. She taught me twelve ways to splice keth-hair rope. I taught her how to hear the difference between basalt that will hold for centuries and basalt that only pretends.

She died in a spring rockfall, doing what we all eventually do—pushing the safe boundaries of where we can work. I carved her name into the stone at the place where she fell. The carving is already beginning to weather away. This is correct.

I am fifty-three years old, which makes me neither young nor ancient by Veyr standards. My joints predict weather changes. My hands are maps of scars, each one a lesson the stone taught me. I can still make the climb from the bottom of Yavsa's Throat to its rim in four hours if pressed, though I am no longer pressed. Time is a shelf, and I was not born on it—I was born in the space between shelves, where the only covenant is to keep the world from falling into itself.

When the young ones training for ridge-work ask me why anyone would choose to live as I do, I tell them this:

The plateau is not one thing. It is an assemblage of breaks and bridges, of solid ground and sudden absence. The Veyr have mastered the art of building up, of adding weight, of making the horizontal plane sacred. But someone must know the vertical. Someone must speak for the spaces between. Someone must ensure that when you step onto a bridge forty meters above the slot floor, the ropes remember their promise.

I am the keeper of those promises.

I am Khett-Yavsa, and when I die—not soon, stone willing, but eventually—I will not be carried to the uppermost ridges. My covenant is different. I will be lowered into the deepest accessible point of my slot, down to where the darkness has substance and the stone still holds warmth from its making. The ropes that carry me will be cut and allowed to fall with me. It is the only waste the slot-tenders permit ourselves.

And somewhere above, the bridges will creak in the wind, holding the weight of all the small, patient people crossing between the ridges, never thinking about the darkness below or the one who walked it, checking every knot, every anchor, every promise.

This is enough.

The slot knows my name.
