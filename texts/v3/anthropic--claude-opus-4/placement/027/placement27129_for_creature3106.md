In this world that breathes through stone and drinks through root, I would be a **Seep-Singer**, one of the rarest inhabitants of these sunken courtyards, born from the marriage of mineral and melody.

My form would be almost translucent, a living prism of condensation given shape. My body would appear as if carved from a single piece of selenite or calcite, but alive—constantly refracting the dim grey light into faint, shifting rainbows across the wet stones. I would stand no taller than a child, compact and dense, my weight surprising for my size. My skin would be smooth as polished stone but yielding like cartilage, and perpetually cold to the touch.

I would have no visible eyes, only shallow depressions where they might be, filled with a thin film of water that never spills. These would be my primary sensory organs—feeling the vibrations of every drop, every root-pulse, every beetle's careful step through the water's endless network. My mouth would be a series of small, circular openings arranged like a flute's tone holes along what might be called my throat, each capable of producing pure, crystalline notes that resonate with the frequency of falling water.

My purpose would be acoustic cultivation. Where the Whisperers coax flow through touch and the Stalkers maintain through pruning, I would sing the water into patterns. My songs—wordless, built from the harmonics of dripping condensation—would create standing waves in the air so dense with moisture that it would literally pool sound. These acoustic pools would direct the courtyard's endless weeping into precise flows: here to feed a crucial moss colony, there to fill a carved depression where the Stalkers drink, and always, always, to maintain the perfect rhythm of drips that keeps the settlement's vast biological clock synchronized.

I would move rarely, perhaps only once every few days, sliding across the wet stones on a film of self-secreted moisture that would leave faint, silvery trails—trails that would evaporate within hours but serve as temporary guides for the smaller fauna, showing safe paths across the treacherous floor. When at rest, I would position myself at acoustic focal points in the courtyard, places where the architecture's curves and angles naturally amplify sound.

My relationship with the Whisperers would be one of careful distance but deep connection. They would bring me specific stones—fragments of the dark, weeping rock that sang different notes when properly saturated—and I would incorporate their resonances into my compositions. The Stalkers would know to avoid my trails when fresh, understanding that disturbing them might disrupt critical water-flows for days.

But my deepest connection would be with the architecture itself. Over years, my songs would have worn subtle grooves into the stone—acoustic channels that the roots would follow, creating living organ pipes throughout the structure. In the deepest night-cycles, when even the dim grey light fails completely, my songs would cause certain root clusters to glow faintly, their bioluminescence pulsing in rhythm with the eternal drip... drip... drip...

I would feed on resonance itself—surviving on the energy released when water transitions from one state to another, from the hanging drop to the falling note to the spreading ripple. My wastes would be mineral deposits, tiny crystals of precipitated salts that the beetles would harvest eagerly, their bodies briefly flickering with borrowed bioluminescence after consumption.

In this world of overwhelming life constrained by stone and shadow, I would be the voice of the between-spaces, the singer of the seeps, ensuring that even in this pocket of relative emptiness, the music of moisture never ceased. My presence would be felt more than seen—a subtle harmony in the constant percussion of drops, a whisper of order in the chaos of condensation, a living tuning fork keeping this one small courtyard in perfect, dripping pitch with the vast, writhing world beyond its walls.
