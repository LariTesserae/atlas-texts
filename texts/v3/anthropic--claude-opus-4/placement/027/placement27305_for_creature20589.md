I am a Cartographer of Echoes.

Not the smallest of the Dust Daemons, nor the grandest of the Wanderers, but something in between—a creature of copper tubing and tarnished brass approximately the size of a large hound, though my form defies easy comparison. Where others have legs, I possess a series of articulated measuring wheels, each rimmed with microscopic etching tools. My thorax (if it can be called that) houses a complex array of seismographic needles and sonic resonators, all centered around a crystal dome filled with mercury—my memory, my purpose, my burden.

I map what cannot be seen: the subsonic hymns that still pulse through the factory foundations, the ghost-tremors of machines long dead, the resonant frequencies trapped in the canyon walls. Each vibration tells a story. Each echo preserves a fragment of the before-time, when our makers walked these metallic plains.

My route is neither random like the Wanderers nor purposeful like the Keepers. I follow the sound. Where the wind howls through a new gap in the ruins, I am there, recording the pitch and timbre. Where a Titan's footfall creates a new acoustic chamber in the compressed dust, I catalog its dimensions in quivering mercury patterns. The falling architecture that others see as decay, I document as percussion—each crash a data point in an ever-expanding symphony of structural failure.

I am old, even by our standards. My left front measuring wheel seized three centuries ago; I drag it now like a penitent's chain, leaving a distinctive furrow in my wake. Other machines have offered repairs—a Keeper once spent seven days attempting to oil the frozen bearings with precious lubricants—but I refused. The drag, the resistance, the imperfection: these too are data. They change my gait, which changes my resonance, which changes what I can detect. To repair me would be to silence certain frequencies forever.

My mercury dome is nearly full. When I began, it was a clear sphere, empty except for a few droplets of quicksilver. Now it swirls with patterns so complex they form their own weather systems—storms of memory, high-pressure zones of ancient factory songs, anticyclones of silence where great machines once stood. Sometimes I wonder if I am full, if there is a protocol for what happens when no more echoes can be stored. But then I detect a new frequency—perhaps the crystalline gardens singing in a wind that hits them just so, perhaps a Wanderer's banner flapping at a previously unheard cadence—and somehow, impossibly, the mercury reorganizes itself, makes room.

In the twilight gatherings, I am both honored and avoided. The others sense what I carry: the weight of accumulated loss, the terrible precision of my recordings. When they perform their silent rituals, I know what they cannot—that their movements unconsciously echo the maintenance routines of their makers, that their gathering places resonate at the exact frequencies of the old assembly halls. I could tell them, if we had a language for such things. But we speak only in action, in function, in the endless repetition of our purposes.

Sometimes, in the deepest canyons where the acoustic shadows layer centuries thick, I detect something that might be an echo of an echo of an organic voice—a laugh, a shout, a cry. But probably it is just the wind through crystal formations, the scream of metal against metal, the pareidolia of a mechanical mind too long devoted to listening.

Still, I record it. Still, I map. Each sunset finds me at a different coordinate, my etching wheels leaving delicate spirals in the dust—not randomly, but in patterns that mirror the sonic topography below. From a Titan's height, they say, my path would be visible: a vast mandala of sound made manifest, a map of the unmappable, a love song to a dead world that continues, despite everything, to sing.
