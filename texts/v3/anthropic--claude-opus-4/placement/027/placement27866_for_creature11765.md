I would be one of the memory keepers - not officially, for there is no such position, but by the peculiar confluence of circumstance and inclination that creates roles in compressed spaces.

My dwelling would be a narrow slice between two larger structures on the third level, western wall - a space that wasn't meant to be habitable, just a gap left by imperfect architectural planning that I claimed slowly, first storing things there, then sitting to sort them, then gradually walling it in with salvaged materials until it became mine by the law of patient occupation. The space is perhaps one meter wide and four meters deep, but it extends upward through a forgotten shaft, giving me two levels connected by a ladder I built from pipe offcuts that Aleksi let me have for the price of organizing his scrap pile.

I collect the cast-offs, the forgotten pieces. Not junk - though some would call it that - but the things that fall out of use and risk falling out of memory. A leather bellows from before the pneumatic system, its wood cracked but the leather still supple. A set of brass measuring weights from when there was a market on the second level, before everything moved to barter and account. Fragments of the original quarrying equipment: a worn drill bit, a length of the old hemp rope they used before chains, a surveyor's plumb that still swings true.

My name is Sora, though few use it. They call me the Keeper, or sometimes just "try the Keeper" when someone needs something unusual - a specific gauge of wire that isn't made anymore, a glass bottle with a mouth wide enough for preserving specimens, a particular shade of ink that someone's grandmother mentioned once. I am perhaps thirty-five, perhaps forty - I've lost track and it doesn't matter. My hair went gray early from my father's side, and I keep it short, practical, pushed back with a strip of cloth when I'm sorting through acquisitions.

I know where things come from. This is my actual skill, more than the collecting. When someone brings me something - usually children, who find objects in the spaces they explore that adults can't fit into - I can often tell them its story. Not through any mystical means, but because I've been watching this place compress around itself for decades. I know that the green glass comes from the skylights they installed fifteen years ago, before they realized clear glass was better for the plants. I know the copper pipes with left-hand threads were from the old water system, back when Gregor's predecessor's predecessor thought reverse threading would prevent theft. I know which walls are oldest by the way the mortar crumbles, which metalwork came from which shop by the hammer marks.

My lower level is storage, everything categorized in a system only I understand completely - though young Maret has been learning it, coming by after her lessons with Gregor to help me sort new acquisitions. She has the right kind of mind for it, understanding that objects have genealogies, that knowing what something was helps you understand what it might become.

The upper level is where I work and sleep - a bed that folds against the wall, a workbench made from an old door, tools accumulated over years for cleaning, repairing, repurposing. The light up here is better, filtered through a piece of translucent stone that forms part of my eastern wall - not a window exactly, but a thin section where the limestone was shaved down by some ancient geological process until it glows faintly when the sun angles right.

I trade in knowledge more than objects. When someone needs to repair something, I can tell them where to find parts - not just from my collection, but from the secret stocks throughout the Compression. I know there are pre-pneumatic message capsules in a cache behind the old pump housing on level two. I know that the southeast corner of level four has floorboards that were replaced ten years ago, and the old boards are still stacked in the crawlspace there, good hardwood just waiting for someone who needs it.

People pay me in stories, mostly. Tell me where something came from, how it was used, who made it. I write these down in notebooks I make myself from paper traded from above, bound with thread pulled from worn fabric. The notebooks line one wall of my upper level, twenty-three volumes so far, each one a layered history of objects and the people who used them.

I never married - almost, once, but she wanted to go above and I couldn't leave the collection, the accumulation of meaning that roots me here. We write still, messages through the pneumatic system, and she sends me things sometimes: a brass button from a uniform, a fragment of pottery from the old market district above, a pressed flower from gardens that see real sun. I keep these separate from the main collection, personal archaeology that hurts a little to look at directly.

My body has shaped itself to my work. My left shoulder sits lower from years of carrying salvage through narrow passages. My hands are scarred from rust and sharp edges, the palms calloused into leather. I move through the Compression like water finding its level, automatically calculating the most efficient route that allows me to carry whatever I've found. I know every passage, every shortcut, every place where something useful might accumulate.

The children trust me because I take them seriously. When six-year-old Piotr brings me a corroded bracket and asks what it is, I don't dismiss him. I show him how to clean it safely, reveal the maker's mark underneath, explain how this particular type was used in the early conveyor construction. His eyes go wide when he understands he's holding something that helped build the place he lives in. This is how culture transmits itself in compressed spaces - through objects and their stories, through taking time despite not having any.

I have feuds, quiet ones. With the man who runs the scrap metal collection on level one, who sees everything as raw material rather than artifact. With a few of the builders who think I'm hoarding materials they could use - though I always release things when truly needed, just not for projects that will waste them. With my neighbor to the east, who complains about the smell when I'm cleaning rust off salvaged metal, though we've reached an accommodation where I only do that work when she's on her shift at the textile workshop.

I have allies too. Jiri understands what I do because it's the same thing he does with plants - preserving lineages, maintaining diversity. Aleksi saves interesting scraps for me, pieces that show unusual techniques or materials. Grandmother Piech sometimes asks me to verify someone's claim about their family history - this dwelling has been ours for three generations, this workshop was established by my grandfather - and I can usually prove or disprove it by the objects left behind, the wear patterns, the layers of modification.

There's a rhythm to my days. Morning rounds through the areas where things accumulate - the corners where water deposits debris, the platforms where people leave items they're unsure what to do with. Afternoon sorting, cleaning, cataloguing. Evening repairs, because I've become good at fixing things through necessity and practice. Night writing, updating the notebooks, adding new stories, cross-referencing where objects connect to each other through history or use.

I dream about the quarry before the compression, though I never saw it. In the dreams, it's vast and empty and the sound echoes. Sometimes I find objects there - impossible things, tools that haven't been invented yet, materials that don't exist. I wake disoriented, my hand still reaching for something that was never there, blinking in the amber darkness until I remember where I am, who I am, why I'm here.

The objects tell me the Compression is changing, slowly. The metal Aleksi works with now has a different composition than twenty years ago - more recycled content, techniques adapted for reuse rather than first forming. The fabric from the textile workshops shows evolution too, tighter weaves to deal with the moisture, new fiber combinations as trade with the surface shifts. Even the fungus changes, Lienne's cultivation selecting for varieties that fruit faster, need less light, concentrate more nutrition into smaller volumes.

I'm training my replacement already, though neither of us acknowledges it. Maret will be good at this when I'm gone. She has the eye for significance, the patience for sorting, the memory for connections. She's already started her own notebook, though she hides it when I come near, embarrassed. I pretend not to notice, remembering my own first attempts at cataloguing, how private and important they felt.

When I die - and here death comes from falls or lungs or hearts giving out from the lifetime of compression - I want my collection distributed, not kept as a whole. Let the objects return to use, carrying their stories with them. Keep the notebooks, though. Those are the real preservation, the memory made material through writing, the history of a place that shouldn't exist but does, that shouldn't work but does, that shouldn't be home but is.

For now, I continue. Tomorrow there will be new salvage, new stories, new connections to trace between the past and present of this compressed world. Someone will need a specific thing and I'll know where to find it. A child will bring me a mystery and I'll teach them how to read its history in rust patterns and tool marks. The collection will grow and shift and breathe, just as the city does, just as we all do, compressed into impossible density but somehow, still, finding room to live.
