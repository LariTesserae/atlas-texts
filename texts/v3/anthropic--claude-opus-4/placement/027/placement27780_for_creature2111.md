I find myself drawn to the forgotten places, the liminal zones where the old world's certainties have dissolved into something more fluid, more honest.

I am one of the Rust Shepherds, though that name came from valley-dwellers who glimpsed us from afar and misunderstood our purpose. We do not shepherd the rust itself, but rather what grows within and through it—the copper-green lichens that transform abandoned turbines into vertical meadows, the crystalline fungi that bloom in the hearts of old engines, converting industrial memory into something altogether new.

My form shifts with my work. When I tend the high places where factory smokestacks pierce the mist, I stretch tall and thin, my body becoming scaffold-like, fingers extending into delicate tendrils that can reach into every crevice where life takes hold. The iron in my blood resonates with the iron in the ruins, creating a harmony that helps me understand which structures should stand and which should gracefully surrender to gravity's patient pull.

In the deep machine halls, where darkness pools like old oil, I compress myself, becoming dense and careful. My skin takes on the texture of the surfaces I touch—now smooth as worn metal, now rough as rust, now soft as the moss that cushions the forgotten control panels. I taste the history in the corrosion, each oxide layer telling its own story of rain and time.

The deer know me, though they pretend otherwise. When I work in the meadows where old parking lots have cracked open to admit wildflowers, they graze nearby, their ears swiveling to track my movements. Sometimes I find their shed antlers arranged in careful patterns around the structures I tend—their way of participating in the great work of transformation.

But I am not alone in this calling. Others move through the valley on their own missions. The Memory Weavers collect fragments of human ambition—invoice carbons turned to illegible blue shadows, safety posters whose warnings have faded to poetry, time cards punched by workers whose grandchildren have forgotten their names. They spin these fragments into something new, neither history nor myth but a third thing that settles over the valley like morning fog.

The Resonance Keepers maintain the great engines that still turn, not for any industrial purpose but because their rhythm has become part of the valley's heartbeat. They oil bearings that no longer bear loads, align gears that mesh with nothing but time itself. The machinery's occasional roar is their symphony, a bass note that reminds the mountains they are not the only ancient things here.

And in the deepest places, where the mist never fully lifts, move the Threshold Tenders. They are barely glimpsed, existing more as possibility than presence. They guard the places where the industrial age wore reality thin, where assembly lines ran so long and so mechanically that they wore grooves in space itself. Through these worn places, sometimes, other things peer through—not malevolent, merely curious about a world that built such elaborate shrines to efficiency and then abandoned them.

My work is patient work. Today I am cultivating a garden in what was once a quality control station. The inspection equipment has oxidized into abstract sculptures, and between them I coax specialized plants that feed on metal ions, their leaves shimmering with incorporated copper and tin. By next season, the place will be unrecognizable, neither natural nor artificial but something more true than either category allows.

A fox watches me from a ventilation grate, its eyes reflecting both skepticism and approval. It knows, as I know, that this work is not about erasing the past or returning to some imagined pristine state. The valley was always a palimpsest—glaciers wrote the first draft, forests revised it, humans added their industrial chapter, and now we write the next movement in this endless composition.

When evening comes, I often climb to where the old water towers stand. My form attenuates to match the fading light, becoming translucent enough that the sunset passes through me, tinting the rust-gardens below in shades of amber and rose. The Resonance Keepers' engines quiet to a whisper. The Memory Weavers pause in their work. Even the Threshold Tenders, perhaps, look up from their vigil.

This is the holy hour, when the valley breathes out the day's accumulated transformations. I feel it all—every spore taking hold, every root threading through concrete, every molecule of metal accepting its new purpose. I am gardener and garden both, tending and tended, shaping the slow alchemy by which the world heals not by forgetting, but by remembering differently.

Tomorrow there will be new work. A cooling tower needs its interior transformed into a spiral garden. The Memory Weavers have requested help deciphering a cache of magnetic tape they found in a subbasement. There are rumors of deer behaving strangely near one of the threshold places, walking in perfect circles, leaving and returning.

But tonight, I rest in the patient certainty that the work continues whether I tend it or not. The valley dreams its own future into being, and I am privileged to participate in that dreaming, to be both witness and participant in the great work of becoming.

The mist rises. The rust blooms. The transformation continues.
