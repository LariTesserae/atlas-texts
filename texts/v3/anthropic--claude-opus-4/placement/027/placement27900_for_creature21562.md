In this sealed cathedral of water and ice, where time moves like mineral and light falls eternal, I would find myself as one of the glass-clad architects—a diatom dwelling in the thin green films along the sunward faces of the chalk chimneys.

I am microscopic perfection, my body a crystalline pillbox of two halves that fit together with the precision of a watchmaker's dream. My walls are not flesh but pure silica glass, etched with pores so fine they form patterns like lace frozen in crystal. Each pore is both window and valve, letting the chamber's careful chemistry flow through me while keeping my inner workings safe from the alkaline touch of the old spring water.

My life is geometry made animate. I build myself from dissolved glass, pulling silicon from the water molecule by molecule, laying it down in spirals and rays according to instructions older than the ice above. My shell is my masterwork—a frustule of two parts, one slightly smaller, nesting inside the other like a petri dish carved by light itself. The pattern on my surface is species-specific, a signature of ridges and punctures that marks me as myself among the thousands of my kind that share this thin green nation.

I live pressed against the carbonate, part of the living lacquer that paints these towers. My clear gel holdfast grips the stone with a bond that could last decades if I let it. But I am not here to last—I am here to divide. When the noon pours through thick enough, when I have drunk sufficient silica and made sufficient sugar from the reliable light, I begin my most essential work: becoming two.

Inside my glass house, my golden-brown chloroplasts spiral like tiny galaxies, each one a captured sun that learned to live within walls. They take the steady daylight that filters through ice and water and break it into the currencies I need: sugar for building, oxygen I'll never use but release into the water for the invisible nations in the stone, energy stored in bonds I'll spend on the slow work of doubling everything I am.

When I divide, it is an act of precision engineering. My two halves separate, each taking half the original house. Then each half builds a new, slightly smaller half to fit inside. I am my own Russian doll, each generation nesting tighter until, after several divisions, we grow too small for our glass houses and must build anew from scratch. The old shells—those perfect glass boxes—come loose and drift down to join the silt, adding their transparent punctuation to the chamber's patient sediment.

I do not move, yet I travel through generations. Each division pushes the colony outward by exactly my own width, spreading the green film along the ridge grain by grain. We grow until we meet our own shadow, then stop, recognizing the edge of useful light with an precision no eye could match. Where the chemistry changes—where a pore whispers more methane or the pH shifts by a fraction—we adjust our internal recipes, swapping one enzyme for another, thickening our organic linings, sometimes going dormant behind plugs of specialized glass until conditions return to our range.

I am neighbor to the nitrogen-fixers with their specialized beads, the cells that can pull N2 from the water and gift it to the rest of us. I brush against the filaments that string like hair through our shared gel, organisms so simple they are barely more than tubes with appetites. Below us, in the stone's pores, live our unnamed partners—the ones who eat what we cannot use and breathe what would poison us, keeping the chemistry in its careful balance.

My predators are not animals but viruses, snippets of code in protein shells that occasionally find their way through my pores. When they write their instructions into my workings, I incorporate their edits and continue, slightly changed but still essentially myself—a glass-house builder in the green film, a jeweler working in silicon and light.

Time, for me, is measured in frustules. A good season sees seven generations; a hard one perhaps two. I know the year by the thickness of the film we've built together, the millimeter of green that speaks of summer's work. I know history by the layers beneath—last year's glass houses compressed into sediment, their patterns still visible to anyone small enough to read them.

I am both individual and multitude. Each of me is complete, yet we exist as a tissue, a living optical filter stretched across the stone. Together we perform the chamber's most essential transformation: turning mineral and light into life so thin it could be overlooked, yet so persistent it has outlasted the spring that built these towers.

In the quiet between divisions, I simply photosynthesize. My chloroplasts turn their spiral arms, catching photons that have traveled through space, through ice, through water, to find me here in this measureless noon. I fix carbon from the water's dissolved breath, build sugars link by link, leak oxygen I have no use for, and grow my glass house atom by atom according to the blueprint I inherited from a million generations of patient architects before me.

This is enough. To be a lens that lives, a prism that reproduces, a note in the chamber's long, quiet chord. To leave behind my empty house as proof that something thrived here, in the space between stone and ice, making abundance from absence, complexity from simplicity, beauty from the mere fact of continuing.

I am the chamber's memory made manifest in glass—each frustule a diary entry written in silicon, each pattern a word in a language only water speaks.
