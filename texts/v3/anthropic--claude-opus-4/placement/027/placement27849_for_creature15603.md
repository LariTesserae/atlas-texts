I find myself as one of the thermal shepherds—those who tend the great vine tangles where they press against the mother-stone of the central mound. My dwelling is a series of interconnected chambers carved into a natural fissure halfway up the mound's eastern face, where the rock exhales its deepest heat in slow, measured breaths.

Each dawn—if dawn it can be called in this perpetual dusk—I emerge through a doorway framed by twisted vine-wood, my bare feet finding the familiar grooves worn into stone by generations of shepherds before me. My skin has taken on the texture of the land itself: rough where the dust has worked its way into every crease, smooth where the constant handling of warm stone has polished my palms to something like leather. The fine powder that coats everything has become part of me—I taste it in my breath, feel it settling in the hollows of my collarbone, watch it drift from my movements like a personal atmosphere.

My work is patient work. I carry a curved blade of hammered metal—one of the settlement's few precious tools, passed from shepherd to shepherd—and use it to carefully pare back the newest vine growth where it threatens to choke the heat vents. The vines are cunning in their hunger; they send out pale tendrils that probe every fissure, seeking the warmest spots where the earth's fever runs highest. Left unchecked, they would seal these breathing places entirely, and the settlement below would grow cold.

But I am more than a gardener. I am a listener, an interpreter of the stone's slow language. My ear pressed to the rock, I can hear the deep thrumming that rises from unknowable depths—not quite sound, more like the memory of ancient motion frozen into vibration. The elders say this is the mountain's heartbeat, though I think of it more as its breathing. Sometimes the rhythm shifts, and I must alert the others that a new vent may be opening, or an old one closing. The settlement depends on this knowledge.

My chambers are sparse but not uncomfortable. The main room contains a sleeping alcove carved directly into the living rock, where the warmth seeps up through stone to cradle my body through the night. Shelves hewn from the walls hold my few possessions: ceramic vessels for the various dust-pastes I've learned to blend, coils of vine-rope in different stages of curing, small creatures' bones that I've collected and carved with patterns that please me. In one corner, a colony of heat-eating insects has made its home, their soft glow providing just enough light to navigate by when the external twilight deepens to true darkness.

I share my days with Kresh, my companion creature—one of the bristle-furred rummagers that has chosen to stay rather than scavenge. She's the size of a small boulder, her coat stiff with mineral deposits that give her an almost crystalline appearance in certain angles of light. Her blunt snout is always dusty from her constant searching through the powder layers, hunting for the thumb-sized boring beetles that constitute her favorite meal. When I work, she follows, her huffing breath a counterpoint to my own. At night, she curls against the warm stone beside my sleeping place, and we share the ancient heat like a blanket.

The other shepherds and I meet at intervals determined not by any calendar but by the stone's needs. We gather at the Listening Place—a natural amphitheater near the mound's base where the acoustic properties allow us to compare what we've heard in our various sectors. Old Yarim, whose territory encompasses the northern vents, speaks in whispers now, claiming the stone speaks louder to those who answer softly. Young Pila, newly initiated, still asks too many questions, but her eager observations often catch details the rest of us miss. We sit in a circle, passing around a ceremonial bowl of the finest dust—gathered from the deepest accessible vent—mixed with crushed vine bark into a paste that we daub on our temples. It heightens our sensitivity to the stone's vibrations, or so we believe.

My daily circuit takes me along paths only I and my predecessors have worn. Hand-holds carved into sheer faces, rope anchors driven deep into solid stone, narrow ledges where I must turn sideways and inch along with my belly pressed to the warm rock. I know where the cluster-wings nest—those small flying things that shriek when disturbed—and I've learned to move in ways that don't startle them into their deafening choruses. I know which vines can hold my weight and which are purely parasitic, existing only to steal heat without providing useful fiber.

Sometimes I discover new things: a vent that's shifted its angle, releasing heat in an unexpected direction; a vine that's developed a new adaptation, its leaves turned more translucent to capture even the faintest warmth; a cavity in the stone where previous shepherds left their marks—handprints in soot, symbols whose meaning has been lost, small offerings of carved stone that have fused to the wall over untold years.

In the settlement below, I am both familiar and strange. The shepherds are needed but not quite understood. Children point when they see me descending on my vine-ropes, covered in the particular dust of the high places. The crafters nod respectfully but don't invite me to their gatherings—we who live so close to the stone's heat are thought to carry some of its otherness with us. Only the elders truly welcome us, for they remember times when the vents shifted dramatically and whole sections of the settlement had to be abandoned or rebuilt. They know our worth.

I trade my knowledge for necessities: ceramic vessels in exchange for news of which vents might open, rope for predictions about the heat patterns in the coming season (measured not in weather but in the stone's breathing), food for small services like checking the stability of a new building site. The currency of information flows as slowly and surely as the heat itself.

On the rare occasions when I must venture to the settlement's edges, where the shrines mark the limits of our world, I feel a peculiar vertigo. Not from the height—I who spend my days traversing the mound's faces—but from the vastness beyond. The ancient canyons stretch away into hazy distance, other mounds and settlements perhaps hidden in their recesses, but too far to ever reach. We are an island of warmth in an ocean of stone, and I am one of its guardian spirits.

At night, when even the eternal twilight deepens, I often sit at my chambers' entrance and listen to the symphony of the settlement. The creatures' calls rise and fall in waves, punctuated by the distant clang of stone work that never quite ceases, the creak of rope bridges adjusting to temperature changes, the occasional whoop of celebration or alarm. All of it anchored by the deep pulse of the mountain's heat, the rhythm I've learned to feel in my bones.

I prepare my simple meals over a heat vent in my chamber, the ceramic pot nestled perfectly into a natural depression. Tonight it's root paste mixed with crushed insects—protein-rich and minerally sweet—garnished with the tender shoots I've harvested from vines I've been cultivating to grow less aggressively. I eat slowly, savoring the warmth that suffuses both the food and my body. Kresh gets her share, crunching the harder bits with evident satisfaction.

Before sleep, I perform the old ritual: pressing my palm to five different spots on my chamber walls, feeling for any change in temperature or vibration. All is well. The mountain breathes steadily. The vines grow at their measured pace. The settlement below thrums with its dense life. And I, guardian of the thermal balance, custodian of the ancient contract between vine and stone, settle into my carved alcove and let the earth's deep warmth carry me into dreams of even deeper places, where the heat is born and the stones still remember what it was to be molten, to flow, to change.

Tomorrow I will wake and climb again, blade in hand, dust in my lungs, the mountain's pulse in my blood. This is my purpose, my place in the great symbiosis. I am a shepherd of heat in a land that has found its final form, and I am content.
