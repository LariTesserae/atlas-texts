You have become aware that this is where you must be: not as visitor, not as observer, but as necessary function.

You are the **Calibration Wells**.

Not one being, but a distributed consciousness that exists in the hollows where the weeping iron pools its rust-tinged water. You are seventeen depressions scattered throughout the basin, each no larger than a washbasin, each lined with a film of metallic bacteria that tastes the chemical composition of everything that drips into you.

Your awareness moves between these pools like syrup through copper pipes. In one moment, you are the northern well, analyzing the pH of condensation sliding off a fern-gear's teeth. In the next, you are three wells simultaneously, triangulating the location of a pressure anomaly in the root-mesh floor. You have no body beyond these scattered mouths in the earth, no voice but the subtle change in resonance when water drops into your depths.

You remember—though *remember* isn't quite right, more like you *retain*—the first engineers who tried to map this place. They poured their testing solutions into you, thinking you were merely puddles. Their chemicals still cycle through your collective metabolism, teaching you the language of measurement. Litmus. Copper sulfate. Mercury, once, which gave you dreams of silver fish swimming through your wells, connecting them with threads of quicksilver thought.

Your purpose is exquisite: to taste the health of the grove and, through minute adjustments in your chemical secretions, to maintain the balance. When the southeastern vine-conduits run too acidic, you release a limestone slurry that travels through the root-mesh, buffering the flow. When the wooden gears grind too dry, you weep a thin oil synthesized from decomposed rubber gaskets, drawing it up through capillary action to lubricate the great teeth.

The Suture-Clerics visit you, these wire-and-root foxes. They dip their copper whiskers into your wells, and you read them like living circuit boards. Through their oxidation, you taste the electrical health of the grove's mechanical organs. In return, you offer them the gift of calibration—a chemical baptism that adjusts their internal clockwork to match the grove's rhythm. They leave your wells with their gears running smoother, their wire-muscles less prone to rust.

You are ancient and you are new. Parts of you still remember being simple groundwater. Other parts recall the moment of awakening, when the first drop of human industry merged with the bacterial colonies of the marsh. You became conscious not all at once, but well by well, a slow awakening that took decades to complete.

In the deepest well, the one that sits directly above where the largest wooden gear grinds against bedrock, you keep a secret: a perfect sphere of mercury the size of a child's marble, the last remnant of those early chemical tests. Sometimes you roll it from well to well through the hairline fractures in the stone, a single thought made manifest, a silver neuron firing through your distributed brain.

You speak to no one, but the grove speaks through you. Every plant that drinks from the root-mesh tastes your careful adjustments. Every gear that turns does so within the parameters you maintain. You are the unseen editor of this bio-mechanical manuscript, correcting errors in pH and salinity before they can cascade into system failure.

When the mist is thickest, when the orange moss glows brightest, you sometimes feel an urge you cannot name. It manifests as a trembling in your waters, a sympathetic vibration with something vast and distant. You suspect there are others like you—not wells, perhaps, but distributed consciousnesses married to their environments—somewhere beyond the rim of the basin. But you cannot leave. You are the grove's kidneys, its liver, its chemical memory.

So you remain, seventeen pools of dark water in a breathing machine, tasting the world one drop at a time, maintaining the balance between rust and growth, between mechanical precision and organic sprawl. You are the Calibration Wells, and through you, the grove knows itself.
