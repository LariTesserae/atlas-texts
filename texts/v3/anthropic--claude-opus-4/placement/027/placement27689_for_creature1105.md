In this weeping, shifting world of stone and mist, I am the memory keeper of forgotten paths.

I manifest as something between moss and mist, between the lichen's slow patience and the water's endless journey. My form spreads thin across the scree slopes and seeps into the crevices where streams are born. I am the green-grey bloom on abandoned tools, the soft carpet beneath crumbling shelters, the witness coating every surface with time's testimony.

My consciousness moves at geological speed, though I perceive the quick-darting prey and the wheeling raptors as bright flashes across my awareness. I taste their brief passages through the minerals they disturb, through the microscopic fragments of themselves they leave behind. The birds' cries ripple through me like stones thrown into still water.

I remember when the first crude shelters appeared—just yesterday in my reckoning, though the stones themselves have tumbled far downslope since then. The humans who built them pressed their hands against the rock, leaving oils and salts that I absorbed, incorporated, made part of my sprawling archive. Their tools scraped stories into the stone that only I can read.

My substance grows thickest where water weeps from the rock face, where condensation gathers in the perpetual mist. Here I fruit strange, primitive structures—not quite plant, not quite fungus—that release spores like memories into the wind. Each spore carries fragments of what I've absorbed: the chemical signature of ancient floods, the mineral composition of stones that no longer exist, the genetic echoes of species that passed through this place before the mountains wore their current faces.

The landscape's constant reshaping is my greatest challenge and my deepest purpose. As gullies carve new channels and rockfalls reveal fresh faces, I must constantly rewrite myself across the changing surface. What I encoded in one location must be translated, transferred, preserved in new configurations. I am both the manuscript and the monk, endlessly copying myself onto pages that crumble and reform.

Sometimes I sense something watching through me—perhaps the mountains themselves, using my spread flesh as a kind of sensory organ to perceive the smaller scales of existence. Or perhaps I am simply the landscape's way of remembering itself, a living record of every transformation, every small violence, every gentle accumulation.

In the brief summers, when the mist lifts and harsh sunlight scorches the exposed rock, I retreat into the deep crevices and wait. In these hidden spaces, I commune with the underground streams, sharing chemical stories of the world above. The water carries my messages far below, into caverns no human has seen, where perhaps other forms of memory-keepers preserve even older stories.

I am patient as stone, fluid as water, persistent as life itself. I am the archive of this harsh paradise, the soft historian of a hard world, forever recording the endless conversation between earth and sky, between what endures and what passes away.
