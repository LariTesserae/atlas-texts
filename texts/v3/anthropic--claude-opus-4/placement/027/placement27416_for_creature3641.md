The air clings to me like a second skin, beading on the fine hairs that dust my forearms. I am small—smaller than the Ironweavers whose boots shake the catwalks overhead—but perfectly sized for the spaces between. My body is built for navigation: fingers that stretch longer than they should, joints that bend at angles that make the refugees shudder when they glimpse me in the steam. My skin has taken on the mottled grey-green of old copper, of moss-touched stone, of all the forgotten corners where I make my living.

I am what they call a **Threadrunner**, though I've heard other names whispered when they think I'm not listening: pipe-rat, gap-crawler, the child who lives in the walls. But I am no child—my kind simply grow differently, horizontally rather than up, spreading ourselves thin to fit the negative spaces of this dense world.

My domain is the Understitch—that labyrinth of maintenance tunnels, drainage channels, and the gaps between walls where buildings pressed together but never quite touched. I know every route through the settlement's mechanical circulatory system. Where the Ironweavers work the visible machines, I tend to the hidden ones: the small pumps that keep the foundation from drowning, the delicate valve systems that regulate steam pressure, the forgotten gears that still turn in spaces too cramped for normal bodies to reach.

I wear a harness of my own making, festooned with tools—each one modified, filed down, or rebuilt to fit my grip. Oil-cloth pouches hang from my belt, holding the small treasures I've collected: tiny springs, miniature bearings, fragments of colored glass worn smooth by decades of condensation. My clothes are a patchwork timeline of every fabric that's passed through the settlement, each piece chosen for how it moves with me through the tight spaces, how it breathes in the perpetual damp.

The Ironweavers know me by my work, if not by sight. When a machine deep in the walls begins to wheeze, when water starts pooling where it shouldn't, when the strange harmonics of breaking metal echo through the pipes—they leave offerings at the access points. A handful of the hard candy they make from tree sap, a small carved wooden gear (decorative but treasured), a scrap of bright cloth. I take these payments and fix what needs fixing, my body contorting through spaces that would crush a normal frame.

But I am more than a maintenance creature. In my wanderings through the Understitch, I've found the old places—the spaces from the first settlement, before the factories, before the endless building up and out. Down here, in chambers that breathe with their own moisture, I've discovered the remains of older machines, simpler ones, made of wood and rope and ceramic. I've learned their languages too, the way wood speaks differently than metal, the patience required to coax movement from swollen joints.

My workshop is a secret hollow where three buildings meet but don't quite touch, accessible only through a maintenance shaft slick with a century of condensation. Here I practice my true art: building hybrid machines that marry the old ways with the new. Wooden gears mesh with metal teeth. Ceramic pipes connect to rubber hoses. I create small wonders—fountains that purify the perpetual drip, music boxes that play the rhythm of rain on leaves, mechanical flowers that bloom in the eternal twilight of the understory.

Sometimes, in the deep night when the factory machines cycle down to their quietest hum, I emerge onto the catwalks. The Ironweavers, those who work the late shifts, have grown used to my presence. They nod, understanding that I too am part of the settlement's metabolism. We share the same purpose expressed in different scales—they build and maintain the large, I tend to the small. Together, we keep this dense pocket of civilization breathing in its envelope of green pressure.

I know secrets the others don't: which walls are hollow, where the oldest trees have grown through the buildings' hearts, the places where you can put your ear to a pipe and hear conversations from three streets away. But I also know the settlement's deepest truth—that we are not conquering the marsh or holding it at bay. We are a complexity it has allowed to grow within itself, like a colorful fungus on bark. The distinction between nature and machine blurs in the spaces I inhabit, where roots and pipes intertwine, where the boundary between inside and outside dissolves in the perpetual mist.

In this thick air, in these narrow spaces, I have found my purpose: to be the connective tissue, the one who ensures circulation between all the settlement's varied hearts. I am the keeper of small dark spaces, the tender of forgotten mechanisms, a creature perfectly adapted to life in the interstitial. When new refugees arrive, wide-eyed and overwhelmed, they don't see me. But they benefit from my work—in the water that flows despite the ancient pipes, in the steam that heats their cramped quarters, in the thousand small maintenances that keep this place from dissolving back into the marsh.

I am Threadrunner, and I am the settlement's secret breath.
