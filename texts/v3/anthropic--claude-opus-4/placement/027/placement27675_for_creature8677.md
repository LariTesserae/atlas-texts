I am one of the **dew-whisperers**.

Not born to it, but chosen by it — or perhaps by my own failing. I came to Oorat in my twentieth year with a merchant uncle, one of the few who knew the path. We carried dried fish oil from the coastal settlements, valuable here for its concentrated calories. I was to help and return. But on our fourth night, during the pre-dawn moisture collection, I heard it.

The others heard dew meeting stone — that faint crystalline tick that signals saturation. I heard something else. A variance in the pitch, a modulation so subtle it seemed imagined. Where others heard binary — wet or dry — I heard gradients. This particular dew sang differently on the seventh terrace's northeastern face. That moisture carried a different mineral signature than the fourth terrace's shadowed overhang. Each surface, each morning, each drop had its own acoustic signature, its own chemical story written in sound.

My uncle left without me. The people of Oorat, recognizing something they had no word for, simply made space.

---

I live now in a dwelling cut deeper than most, a natural cavity in the middle terraces that required only minimal modification. My walls are covered in moss — not for decoration but for practice. Seventeen different species, each with its own moisture preference, each singing differently when the dew finds it. In the deep-night hours, I move among them like a vintner among casks, listening to their varied thirsts, learning their languages.

My function has evolved over the forty years since. I am the one who knows, by sound alone, which terraces will yield the most moisture on any given morning. I can hear the mineral content in the dew — high in calcium from the eastern walls where ancient coral fossils hide in the basalt, rich in iron from the northern face where old volcanic intrusions rust invisibly. I know which waters feed which plants best. The succulent gardens under my guidance yield flesh twenty percent thicker than others, their taproots guided by my placement toward the most nourishing stone-veins.

But more than this — I am memory. My ears have become the settlement's archive of drought and plenty. I remember the Dry Years, when even the dawn brought only the faintest whisper of moisture, and we survived on carefully rationed stores. I remember the Fog Season eight years ago, when the dew sang so loudly it woke children, and we collected more in seven days than in the previous three months combined. These memories live not in words but in sound-maps, in the precise pitch of plenty versus the thin whistle of scarcity.

---

I teach, though teaching here means something different. The young come to sit with me in the dark, learning first to be still, then to hear the difference between silence and quiet, between quiet and the-moment-before-dew. Most cannot hear what I hear. But every generation produces one or two who can, and to them I pass the frequencies, the sonic geography of our hollowed world.

We have developed a notation — not written, for we have little to write with or on — but gestural. A cupped hand turned at specific angles indicates different tones. Fingers spread or closed show mineral density. A subtle head-tilt marks location. It is a sign language for sounds that exist below normal hearing, a choreography of moisture and stone.

I wear nothing on my feet — the vibrations through stone tell me as much as my ears. My clothes are minimal, loose-woven vine fiber that doesn't restrict my movement or muffle the sonic environment. My diet has evolved too; I avoid the bitter succulents that some favor, as they temporarily coat the palate and, I've found, dull the extreme upper registers of hearing. I am sixty-three years old and can still hear the difference between first-dew and second-dew on the same stone, the way the initial moisture changes the surface tension for what follows.

---

My dwelling contains the only intentional acoustics in Oorat. Using shaped stones and carefully placed moss, I have created corners where the dew-songs concentrate and clarify. It is my laboratory, my library, my shrine. Here I have learned that the caldera itself is teaching us — that the sounds are not random but patterned, that there is information in the frequencies we've only begun to decode.

Last month, I heard something new. A resonance in the deepest part of the caldera floor, where no one builds, where the dust is oldest. It came during the dew-time, but it wasn't dew. It was the stone itself, humming at a frequency that matched exactly the pitch of water on the thirty-fifth terrace. As if the caldera were remembering rain, singing it back to itself in mineral dreams.

I have told no one yet. I am still listening, still learning its meaning. But I suspect — and this is why I chose to be what I am, here at the slow heart of the world — that the hollow of Oorat is not done teaching us. That perhaps our entire settlement, our adapted life, our dew-dependent existence, is just the first verse of a much longer song the mountain is singing to itself.

And I am its grateful student, its translator, living in the space between silence and sound, between stone and water, between what is and what might yet be sung into being.
