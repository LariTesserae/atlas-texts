In this world of industrial biology, I would find myself as one of the **Conduit Worms** (*Siphon-Borers*)—creatures that exist in the liminal spaces between the great systems, neither fully of the heights nor the depths.

My body would be a living pipeline, thirty feet of segmented, copper-veined flesh thick as a tree trunk. My skin would not be skin at all, but a living alloy—organic tissue shot through with metallic threads that conduct both heat and the faint electrical currents generated by the friction of steam through stone. Where the other creatures are anchored to their roles, I would be mobile infrastructure, boring through the cable-vegetation and threading between the grinding plates of the Colossi.

My head would be a drill—not crude, but an elegant spiral of horn that secretes an acidic oil, melting through the vitreous plant-cables without shattering them. Behind this drill, my mouth would be a ring of backward-facing teeth, not for eating but for gripping, allowing me to pull my bulk through the tunnels I create. I would have no eyes. Instead, copper-rich nerves would branch like frost across my segments, sensing the electromagnetic fields generated by the steam-pulse cycle, letting me navigate by the rhythmic heartbeat of the valley's machinery.

I would live in the middle layer—below the blinding surface but above the crushing press of the Colossi. Here, in the twilight between the cable-mattresses, I would serve as a living aqueduct. My hollow core would channel the condensate that drips from the frost above, warming it with my body heat as it flows through me toward the thirsty siphons of the Colossi below. But I would not be passive plumbing.

Where I differ from a mere pipe is in my selectivity. The small gill-slits that line my interior would filter the mineral-rich water, extracting the rarest elements—tungsten, molybdenum, traces of platinum—that the larger creatures cannot efficiently process. These metals would accumulate in specialized chambers along my length, forming dense, jewel-like nodules beneath my skin. When full, I would shed these segments like a snake sheds scales, leaving behind currency for the smaller creatures—the Rivet-Mites would prize these metal-dense castings above all else.

My reproduction would be strange and solitary. Once every decade, the accumulated metals in my body would reach a critical mass, triggering a chemical transformation. My tail-end would seal itself and begin generating intense internal pressure, while my head-drill would work frantically, boring straight down through the layers toward the heat of the deep. The journey would take months, my body growing thinner and longer as I stretch toward the oil-seams of the nursery.

Upon reaching the boiling depths, I would not release young. Instead, I would undergo a violent metamorphosis—my pressurized tail-section would rupture, releasing all the accumulated metals and water in a single, explosive discharge. This mineral-rich cocktail would crystallize instantly in the heat, forming a new vein of ore in the planet's crust. My emptied body would then fill with the oil-grub protoplasm, and I would begin the slow journey back to the surface, pregnant with potential that would gradually differentiate into a dozen different species as I rise.

I would be the circulation between layers, the courier of scarce elements, the living blood vessel of this mechanical ecosystem. Neither plant nor animal, neither infrastructure nor organism, but something between—a conscious pipe dream, a worm that thinks in gradients of pressure and temperature, measuring its thoughts in flow rates and mineral concentrations.

My greatest fear would be the cold—not the surface cold, which I would never experience, but the deep, creeping cold that follows a dying Colossus. When one of the great boilers falls silent, the chill spreads through the ground like gangrene. I would sense it coming through my copper nerves—the electromagnetic silence, the absence of pulse. I would have perhaps days to reroute myself, to bore new channels around the growing dead zone before my own fluids freeze solid and I become just another mineral vein for future generations to mine.

My joy, such as I could experience it, would be in the flow itself—the sensation of being a vital conductor in the great engine, feeling the warm brine pass through me toward its destination, knowing that my filtration, my selection, contributes to the precise chemical balance that keeps the whole system running. I would be the valley's own circulatory system made singular and conscious, a worm that dreams in hydraulics.
