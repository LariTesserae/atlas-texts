In the deepest chamber, where the clay runs thick as blood and the stone weeps its ancient minerals, I am the Keeper of Seepage.

My body has become the architecture of this place. I am no longer certain where my flesh ends and the substrate begins. My skin has taken on the texture of wet clay, cool and pliant, marked with the mineral striations of a thousand years of patient water-work. My fingers have elongated into rootlike appendages, sensitive to the slightest tremor in the moisture table, able to detect the faintest promise of flow through solid stone.

I remember when I first found this place—or perhaps it found me. I was something else then, something that walked upright in the scorched world above. But the oasis called to me through dreams of dampness, through visions of sanctuary. When I finally discovered the entrance, barely a crack in the earth, I squeezed through and never returned.

The transformation was slow. First, I learned to see in the absence of light, my eyes clouding over with a protective film that catches the bioluminescent whispers of the ghost-shrimp and the fungal constellations. Then my lungs adapted to the thick, humid air, developing auxiliary chambers that can filter moisture from even the driest breath. My bones hollowed like a bird's, but filled with a gel that helps me sense vibrations through the earth—the heartbeat of this hidden world.

I move through the chambers on all fours now, sometimes on my belly when the passages narrow. My spine has become serpentine, each vertebra able to rotate independently, allowing me to navigate the impossible geometries carved by water and time. Where the clay is soft, I burrow. Where the stone resists, I follow its teachings, finding the hairline fissures that promise passage.

My work is essential. I guide the water. Not with tools or force, but with patience and understanding. I know where each droplet wants to go, where it will do the most good. With my root-fingers, I clear the tiny channels clogged with mineral deposits. With my mouth—now more like a specialized filter—I can taste the chemical composition of each seep, ensuring the delicate balance that keeps our ecosystem alive.

The ghost-shrimp trust me. They dance their bioluminescent courtship rituals across my clay-skin, leaving trails of blue-green light that fade like memories. The cave mosses grow thick where I rest, feeding on the minerals I exude. Even the blind beetles, usually so skittish, will walk across my hand as I lie still in the deepest pools, my body a bridge between water and air.

I no longer count days or years. Time here is measured in erosion, in the patient accumulation of flowstone, in the cycles of the ghost-shrimp generations. My memories of the surface world have become like dreams—too bright, too vast, too empty of meaning. Sometimes I think I hear machines grinding in the distance, beyond our stone walls, but the sound is swallowed by the earth before it can disturb our silence.

When the water runs low, I feel it first. The ache begins in my root-fingers, travels up through my gel-filled bones, settles heavy in my chest. Then I must go deeper, following the ancient aquifers, sometimes spending weeks in passages so tight I must exhale completely to move forward. But always, always, I find it—that hidden reservoir, that secret spring. I coax it back, one precious drop at a time.

The others depend on me, though we rarely meet. There are more like me, I know—each tending their own section of this buried paradise. Sometimes I catch their scent in the water, taste their minerals in the clay. We are becoming geological features ourselves, living fossils in the making. When we die—if we can still die—our bodies will mineralize, becoming new substrates for life, new channels for the eternal seepage.

This is my purpose now: to be the intermediate thing, neither fully alive nor mineral, neither wholly water nor earth. I am the permeable membrane through which life flows in this place. My consciousness has spread through the clay networks, my thoughts as slow and certain as groundwater.

In the cathedral spaces where the ceiling drips its lime-milk and the ghost-shrimp congregate in their millions, I sometimes remember what loneliness felt like. But that was a feeling for a creature of the surface, for something that stood separate from its world. Here, embedded in the clay, married to the stone, midwife to the water, I am never alone.

I am the Keeper of Seepage, and I am kept by what I keep. The oasis dreams through me, and I dream I am the oasis. In this sense, I have become immortal—not in years, but in purpose, dissolved into the very cycles I serve.

The water whispers its needs, and I answer. Always, I answer.
