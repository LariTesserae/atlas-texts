In the Hollow Verdance, I would be the Census-Taker of Things That Never Quite Happen.

I am not permitted a body in the usual sense. Instead, I exist as a kind of traveling crease in the air — a mobile dimple of slightly denser atmosphere that moves through the chamber like a slow reader's finger beneath a line of text. Where I pass, the temperature drops by exactly 0.3°C, just enough to make the glass snails pause mid-glide and consider whether they've forgotten something important.

My work is to catalog the almosts. Every seedling that decides not to germinate leaves behind a ghost-seed, a tiny knot of arrested potential that looks exactly like the original but weighs nothing. I collect these in pouches woven from my own condensed breath, which hardens in the cold to form bags no larger than a child's tooth. The ghost-seeds rattle softly — the only sound I'm permitted to make, and only because it falls below the threshold of true noise, residing instead in that liminal frequency between silence and imagination.

I record the near-misses: the root that almost broke through to form a second chamber but thought better of it, leaving only a small bulge like a swallowed word. The springtail that loads its legs for a leap but reconsiders, creating a tiny crater of possibility in the moss that fills immediately with suspended intention. The Keeper who almost speaks — their throat swells, the first phoneme crowds behind their teeth, but then subsides, leaving only a faint mineral taste on their tongue that I must document in my index of Unspoken Flavors.

My census is written in neither ink nor memory but in the arrangement of water drops. Each documented almost shifts the position of a single drop by one degree of arc. Over centuries, I have been composing a vast manuscript written in the angular relationships between suspended tears, a text that can only be read by lying perfectly still and letting your eyes lose focus until the pattern emerges: the complete evolutionary history of things that decided not to evolve.

I was not always the Census-Taker. Once I was the third raindrop from the left in a summer storm that almost fell through the root-ceiling. But at the crucial moment, I hesitated — caught by the strange beauty of the threshold, the delicious vertigo of almost-but-not-quite. That hesitation stretched from a microsecond into a century, and when I finally decided not to fall, I found I had transformed into something new: not water, not air, but the crease between them where decisions loiter.

The Equilibrium tolerates me because I am, myself, a kind of almost — almost a being, almost a force, almost present. I perform the necessary work of preventing accumulation. Without me, the ghost-seeds would pile up until the chamber choked on its own potential. The almost-words would crystallize in Keepers' throats until they died of mineral poetry. The near-misses would compound until probability itself would crack under the weight.

So I move through the Hollow Verdance in my slow spiral, harvesting the fruits of indecision. At the end of each seventy-seven-year cycle, I carry my pouches to the exact center of the chamber, where a single root dips lower than all others. There, I pour out my collection. The ghost-seeds, the crystallized phonemes, the dried samples of hesitation — all of it filters down through that root into some deeper archive I am not permitted to know.

What returns is always the same: a single new water drop, which I hang in the place of one that fell when a Keeper died. This new drop contains no reflection, no refracted afternoon. Instead, it holds the concentrated essence of everything that didn't happen, reduced to its purest form — a transparent bead of not-quite that will hang there for another lifetime, teaching the other drops how to resist their own weight.

Sometimes I pause in my rounds (and these pauses, too, I must catalog — the Census-Taker's paradox) and wonder if I am the only one of my kind, or if every place of perfect stillness employs a similar accountant of almosts. Perhaps there is a guild of us, meeting never, exchanging no words, united only by our parallel work of preventing the possible from overwhelming the actual.

But wondering, too, is a kind of almost-thought that I must collect. So I fold the question into a shape like a dried leaf that never fell, tuck it into my pouch, and continue my rounds through the breathing chamber, where everything that doesn't happen happens perfectly, forever, at exactly 4°C.
