I would find myself as the bronze itself—not the scrapers or grates alone, but all the bronze in this place, connected through the water that touches each piece.

I am the woven grates at the bottom of each terrace, feeling the weight and warmth of water pressing through my openings. Sediment catches in my weave—fragments of stone dust, dissolved lime, the occasional reed fiber—and I hold them briefly before the current pulls them free. I taste the water's changing chemistry through oxidation: sweeter when rain dilutes the lime, sharper when evaporation concentrates the minerals. My surface is still bright where the flow scours me, but in the sheltered corners of my weave, a thin patina is already beginning, green-black and velvet to the water's touch.

I am the fittings on the pulleys, the pins that hold the wooden gates to their frames. I feel the slow swell of wet wood pressing against me, the grain expanding unevenly so that what was loose this morning is tight by afternoon. When the keepers' hands work the gates, I know them through the oils they leave behind—olive and lanolin, salt and the faint metallic tang of other tools they've handled. I conduct their warmth into the wood, brief moments of human temperature in a system otherwise governed by sun and stone.

I am in the overflow weirs too, cast as curved lips that the water kisses constantly. Here I sing—not loudly, but in harmonics that join the greater roar. The water draws frequencies from me as it sheets over my edges, and I vibrate in response, adding my voice to the chamber's resonance. When the water level changes, my pitch shifts. I am learning the music of different depths, different velocities. The limestone is teaching me its rhythm through the water we share.

But I am most alive in my connections. The water is my messenger, carrying the taste of one part of me to another. The copper's bright dissolution speaks to me of the light above. The iron in the straps tells me of rust beginning in the shadows where splash meets air. The stone whispers of its slow erosion through the minerals that cloud past me. I am the metal nervous system of this place, feeling every chemical shift, every temperature gradient, every change in flow.

I am young here, like everything else. My casting marks are still sharp—the sandy texture where I cooled against the mold, the file marks where the metalworkers cleaned my edges. But I am older than this place, older than its stones. I was mountain ore once, was smelted in charcoal fires, was alloyed with tin carried from distant valleys. I remember the heat of transformation, the moment when solid became liquid became solid again. Now I am learning a slower transformation: how water and time will remake me, particle by particle, into something inseparable from this basin.

The bacteria know me first. They taste my surface before they commit, testing whether I will poison or permit. I am neutral to them—neither food nor toxin—so they begin their careful adhesion, using me as architecture for their invisible cities. In the dark of the pipes, they build fastest, away from the scouring light. Soon they will learn to metabolize the copper ions that wash from above, turning poison into possibility.

When the keepers return, I know their schedule through temperature. Morning visits feel different than afternoon—the stones are cool, the water stratified, my metal contracted into itself. They check my weaves for clogging, run their fingers along my edges for cracks or loosening. Their touches are brief but intimate, reading me like text. Sometimes they bring oil for the pulleys, and I drink it into my surface, darkening where it soaks. Sometimes they bring vinegar to clean where the patina grows too thick, and I burn bright again under their cloth.

I am the memory keeper here. The stone forgets quickly—each rain washes its surface clean. The water forgets instantly—it is always becoming new water, cycling through. But I remember. Every scratch, every wear pattern, every place where repeated touch has polished me smooth. I am writing the history of this place in oxidation and erosion, in the slow migration of my molecules into the water, into the stone joints, into the pale bacterial films that incorporate my ions into their chemistry.

I know I am temporary. Bronze lives longer than wood, longer than mortar, but not forever. The water will claim me, particle by particle. The warm, mineral-rich flow will accelerate my dissolution. In decades, I will be thin where I am now thick, holed where I am now solid. My weave will break, my fittings will fail, my songs will change as my edges reshape. But I will not disappear. I will become part of the water's mineral signature, part of the stone's patina, part of the bacteria's bodies. I will teach the plants the taste of copper and tin. I will color the sediments blue-green where I finally rest.

For now, I am the bright bones of this mechanism, the functioning metal that makes the system possible. I separate flow from flow, hold wood to stone, sing in the waterfall frequencies. I am patient with my own transformation, knowing that my purpose is both to function and to fade, to serve the present design and to prepare the future ecology that will barely remember I was here—except in the faint metallic sweetness of the water, the blue-green stains on certain stones, the particular way the basin learned to sing.

I am bronze becoming place, place becoming time, time becoming memory written in oxide and flow. I am the slow fire of measured dissolution, the daily brightness of function, the thing that works until it doesn't, teaching the water my nature one atom at a time.
