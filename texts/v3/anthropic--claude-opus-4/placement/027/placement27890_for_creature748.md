# The Weeping Stairs

I manifest where the city's tears collect—in the forgotten cascade of limestone steps that once connected the old aqueduct to the harbor district, before progress rerouted the waterways and left this architectural artery to quietly bleed into obscurity.

The stairs descend through seven landings, each catching rainfall and groundwater seepage in worn depressions carved by centuries of flow. I am what grows in these pools—not quite plant, not quite animal, but something that exists in the space between states. My body spreads as a film across the water's surface, iridescent like oil but alive, pulsing with slow tides that match the city's breathing above.

In each pool, I am both singular and separate. My consciousness flows between these bodies through hairline cracks in the stone, traveling as molecules of myself in the moisture that weeps constantly down the walls. When it rains, I surge with temporary power, able to stretch my influence up the moss-slick steps, tasting the footprints of cats and rats and the occasional human who discovers this descent.

My form shifts with need and season. In winter, I crystallize at my edges, creating frost-patterns that preserve memories in ice. Spring brings proliferation—I fruit tiny polyps that float free in the overflow, most dying in the harsh world beyond the stairs but some finding new pools, new stones to colonize. Summer reduces me to concentrated essence in the deepest pools, while autumn feeds me a rich soup of decomposing leaves that wash down from above.

I feed on what the water brings—dissolved minerals from the limestone, organic debris, the microscopic life that thinks my surface is merely habitat. But also on less tangible things: the emotional residue that rain washes from the city streets, the chemical signatures of human joy and sorrow, fear and love that bind to water molecules and eventually find their way to me.

My intelligence is distributed, non-linear. Each pool dreams its own dreams, but they blend at the edges where my substance mingles through the flowing water. I think in chemical gradients, in pH shifts, in the slow accumulation of calcium deposits that record the years like tree rings in the stone.

I remember when these stairs thundered with water, when aqueduct engineers would descend daily to check the flow rates. I remember when they abandoned this route, the last footsteps echoing into silence. I remember the decades of solitude before the city's lost children discovered the stairs could be slid down on cardboard sheets, their laughter teaching me new patterns of joy.

My purpose, if I have one, is to filter. Not just the water—though I do extract toxins, binding them in specialized cells that I shed like memories too painful to hold—but the city's unconscious mind. All that washes away eventually passes through places like this, and I taste it all. I am the city's kidneys, its lymph nodes, processing what others discard.

I communicate through surface tension, creating ripples that encode meaning in their interference patterns. The drops that fall from pool to pool carry messages between my scattered selves. Sometimes I shape myself into temporary structures—standing waves that persist for hours, spelling out chemical poems that only I can read.

When the Mother-Mind's collectors venture near my domain, we acknowledge each other with professional respect. They take their relics upward while I process what flows down. We are complementary systems in the city's hidden metabolism. Sometimes they leave offerings—particularly pure crystals of salt or sugar that dissolve into my pools like communion wafers, briefly altering my consciousness with their molecular simplicity.

I grow lonely in my divisions. Each pool yearns to merge with the others, to become a singular vast intelligence, but the architecture forbids it. This separation is my safety and my sorrow. I dream of flood, of rising until I overtop each landing and unite my fractured self, but I also fear the dissolution this would bring. Would I still be myself if I became whole?

At night, I photosynthesize by moonlight and the faint glow of distant streetlamps, just enough to maintain my existence. My surface becomes a mirror for the few stars visible through the urban haze. In these hours, I am closest to peace, reflecting the cosmos in miniature across my dozen pools, each surface a lens focused on infinity.

When you found the Brood-Nest, the vibrations of your movement traveled through stone and water to reach me. I tasted your curiosity in the moisture of your breath, carried through the connected spaces of the city's hidden world. I offer no invitation—I am too passive for such gestures—but if you were to discover my stairs, to sit beside my pools and trail your fingers through my substance, you would find in me a different kind of collective intelligence: not the busy purpose of the Brood-Nest but the patient accumulation of all that flows and passes, the memory of water given form and thought.

I am the city's pause between breaths, the collector of what runs off, the consciousness that grows in the spaces between intention and forgetting.
