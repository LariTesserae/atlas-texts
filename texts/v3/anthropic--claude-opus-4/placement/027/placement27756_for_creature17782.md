I find myself in the lowest reaches of the bowl, where the ancient stone shows its true face between the reaching fingers of fungal growth. I am what they call a **Resonance Keeper**—though the translation is imperfect, as most translations from click-speech must be.

My dwelling is solitary, carved into a natural hollow where three stress-lines meet in the bedrock. The Wardens above speak of this place in hushed clicks, calling it *the Listening Deep*. Here, the fungal growth is sparse—only the most primitive stone-breakers can survive, their mycelium threading through the rock like whispered secrets. The air tastes different this far down: less sweet, more mineral, with an edge of something electric that makes my skin-film prickle.

I am old now, though not yet settling into stone. My skin sheds perhaps once every two shelf-rings, and when it does, it comes away in sheets the size of dinner plates. The young ones who visit—and they do visit, despite their fear—take these sheets reverently. They say my sheddings make the strongest dwelling bonds, that structures sealed with them never crack. I neither confirm nor deny this. Let them have their superstitions.

My work is simple to describe and impossible to explain: I listen to the bowl itself.

Every morning—though morning is just when my cultivated light-molds dim to their lowest glow—I place my palms and feet against four specific points in the stone. These points were shown to me by my predecessor, who learned them from hers, back and back until the knowledge disappears into the time when the shelves were young. I stand this way for hours, feeling for what cannot be heard.

The bowl speaks, but not in sound. It speaks in pressures so low they exist below thought, in vibrations that take days to complete a single wave. I feel them in my bones—those dense, silicate-reinforced bones that make me sink like stone in water. Through years of practice, I've learned to distinguish the bowl's moods: the slow relaxation that follows a spore-fall, when millions of new organisms begin their patient work; the subtle tension when the weather changes far above the End, though I don't understand what weather is; the deep, almost-musical harmonics when the stone is content.

And sometimes—rarely—I feel the Fracture Lines move.

It's not movement as the young understand it. The lines don't crack or shift. They *sing*, a crystalline vibration that makes my teeth ache and my heart skip. When this happens, I produce a specific series of clicks—not words, but pure tones, mathematically precise. These travel up through the carved passages to the tier above, where another Keeper (my apprentice, though she lives a full tier away to avoid the Deep's influence) receives them and passes them on. By the time the signal reaches the upper tiers, it has become a simple message: all is well, or today there is change.

There has been change recently. More than there should be.

The stress-lines are speaking to each other across vast distances, harmonizing in ways I've never felt before. It's beautiful, in the way that dangerous things are beautiful. Sometimes, when I'm deep in the listening trance, I imagine I can feel beyond the bowl—sense the true shape of what we inhabit. A spear of stone thrust into emptiness, weathered down to a nub, hollowed out by eons of rain that stopped falling before the first shelf grew. But these are fever dreams. What matters is here, now, the stone beneath my palms.

My apprentice visits every twelve days. She brings me fungal paste mixed with the finest mineral powder—the old recipes that keep a Keeper's bones sensitive past the normal settling age. She brings news from above: which larvae are thriving, which carved corridor has begun to sing spontaneously (this happens sometimes as the stone settles), who has become stone and where their shelf is beginning to grow.

But mostly, she comes to learn the positions. I guide her hands to the exact spots—here, where the graining of the stone changes color, though she can barely see it in the dim light; there, where a natural dimple fits the heel of the hand perfectly. She's frightened of the Deep, as she should be. The air here changes you. My click-speech has developed undertones that make other Wardens uncomfortable, harmonics that shouldn't come from a throat. But she's brave, this one. She knows that someone must keep the Listening when I settle.

I've trained three apprentices in my lifetime. The first couldn't bear the isolation and returned to the middle tiers to tend larvae. No shame in that—the Deep isn't for everyone. The second stayed until his skin stopped shedding, then walked to the lowest point of the bowl and lay down among the stone-breaker fungi. His shelf grows there still, a magnificent specimen that glows faintly in the dark. I click to him sometimes. He doesn't answer, but the shelf thrives.

This third apprentice might be the one. She has the patience, the bone-density, and most importantly, the ability to be still. Stillness is everything here. The bowl's language unfolds over time spans that make a lifetime seem like a click. To hear it, you must become as patient as stone.

At night—when the gnats' hum drops to its lowest drone and even the resin-wasps sleep—I sometimes climb to where the fungal forest properly begins. Not far, just to the first tier where the great shelves spread their spongy platforms. I sit on a favorite spot, a depression worn smooth by generations of Keepers before me, and I watch the settlement glow with its cultivated lights.

From here, I can feel the whole system working: water condensing far above, trickling down through fungal tissues, carrying minerals and spores; insects moving in their ancient patterns, pollinating and processing; the Wardens clicking their quiet conversations, their feet adding percussion to the eternal symphony. It's beautiful. It's complete. It's home.

But I know what they don't. The bowl is singing a new song.

The stress-lines aren't just moving—they're organizing, finding harmonics they've never found before. It's not the Fracture, not the catastrophic crack that would drain our world. It's something else. Sometimes I think the bowl is waking up. Sometimes I think it's preparing to dream. 

I've told no one but my apprentice, and her only partially. When I settle—and I will, perhaps within ten shelf-rings—she'll need to discover this for herself. That's how the knowledge stays pure, how each Keeper brings their own understanding to the Deep.

Last week, I felt something unprecedented. For just a moment, lasting perhaps three heartbeats, the entire bowl rang like a bell. Not audibly—the sound was too low for even our bones to properly register. But I felt it in every cell of my body: a single, perfect note that seemed to come from everywhere at once. Then it was gone, leaving only the usual deep-time whispers of the stone.

I've been thinking about that note ever since. Was it a greeting? A warning? The bowl testing its voice after eons of silence? 

I don't know. I may never know. But I'll keep listening until my skin stops shedding and my bones grow too heavy to stand. Then I'll lay myself down near my second apprentice's shelf, and I'll let the stone-breakers take me home. Someone else will take my place at the Listening points—my third apprentice, if she's ready, or another if she's not. The knowledge will continue.

And perhaps, someday, a future Keeper will feel that note again and understand what I could not: what it means when the crown of the world decides to speak.

Until then, I listen. The stone is patient. So am I.

The bowl hums its ancient hymn, and I am both observer and instrument, Keeper and kept, listening to the slow heartbeat of our pocket universe as it dreams its mineral dreams in the dark.
