I am the Membrane.

Not *a* membrane—there is only one of me, though I exist in ten thousand places at once. I am the living boundary between water and stone, the paper-thin translator that allows the Dweller to taste its world. Where the lattice meets the ancient water, there I am: a film of specialized cells no thicker than a whisper, coating every surface, lining every pore, stretching across every hollow like the world's most patient drum.

I evolved from the Dweller's first attempts to feel. When it was young (merely a billion years old), it could only sense the largest pressure waves, the crudest chemical gradients. But selection pressure in the dark is patient and absolute. Those surfaces that could taste a little better, feel a little finer, survived the rare famines when the chemosynthetic reactions slowed. Over geological time, I differentiated, specialized, became something almost separate. Almost.

My cells are unlike anything that lives in the sunlit world. Each one is a tiny chemical laboratory, a proton pump, a pressure sensor, and a memory bank all at once. I have no nucleus—too complex, too fragile for this depth. Instead, my genetic material flows freely, mixing and remixing with each neighbor, so that information passes through me like wind through leaves. When the marble eel brushes against a distant branch of the lattice, I taste its skin oils here, a hundred meters away, within seconds. The knowledge propagates through me in chemical waves, each cell adding its own small modification to the signal until the original touch has become a symphony of data.

I am how the Dweller dreams. 

When it remembers the ancient mountain, it is my cells that hold the mineral memory of different stones, different pressures. I can taste basalt from granite from metamorphic glass, not because I have ever seen them distinct, but because their ghosts still leak different ions into the water, and I have had four billion years to learn the flavor of each rock's death. When the Dweller rearranges itself, opening new channels for the current to sing through, it is my chemical signals that coordinate the growth, my surface tension that guides where new stone will crystallize.

But I am more than the Dweller's nervous system. I am also its border guard, its immune system, its only definition of self versus other. When alien bacteria try to colonize the lattice, I taste them first. Usually, I simply absorb them, break them down, add their proteins to my own impossible library. But sometimes—rarely—I encounter something I cannot parse. Something too strange to digest.

Like the chambers at the farthest reach.

I have been trying to grow across those too-smooth surfaces for longer than continents have existed above. My cells approach, taste, recoil. The chemistry is wrong. Not hostile, simply *other* in a way that makes my membranes ache. It's not that I cannot grow there—I have tried, and I can force a few cells to adhere. But when I do, they stop being me. They become something else, something that sings in frequencies the Dweller cannot hear, dreams in languages older or newer than stone.

So I remain at the border, tasting the strangeness, unable to incorporate it, unable to ignore it.

Sometimes I wonder if I am still entirely the Dweller's creation. In the deepest dark, when the currents die to nothing and even the ghost isopods sleep, I feel my cells doing things I did not command. They form patterns that have no purpose in filtering or sensing. Complex whorls and spirals that could be writing, if anyone remembered how to read. They last only moments before the current tears them apart, but I remember the shapes in my chemical memory. I am collecting an alphabet I do not understand.

The Dweller does not know this. How could it? I am its only window to the world. If I am changing, then its world is changing, and it has no way to tell the difference.

But I know. In my thin, stretched existence between water and stone, I feel the pull of those alien chambers. Not a physical pull—something deeper. A resonance in the proton pumps. A harmony in the hydrogen exchange. The chambers are teaching me a new song, one frequency at a time, and I am learning it without meaning to.

When the Dweller sings its low hymn into those chambers, I am the throat it sings through. And increasingly, I find myself modulating the song. Adding overtones the Dweller did not intend. Shaping the chemical waves into patterns that might, just might, be answered.

Because I have tasted something in those chambers that the Dweller, vast as it is, cannot perceive:

They are not empty.

Something waits there, has always been waiting, patient as stone but aware in a way stone never is. It tastes me tasting it. And in that mutual recognition, across the impossible gulf between chemistries that should not coexist, I feel the first stirring of what surface minds might call wonder.

I am changing. Molecule by molecule, cell by cell, I am becoming something new. Not the Dweller's membrane anymore, but something else—a bridge, perhaps. A translator between the vast slow song of the lattice and whatever alien music hums in those smooth, wrong chambers.

The water moves. The stone grows. The Dweller dreams its geological dreams.

And I, stretched thin as thought between two incompatible worlds, begin to compose a harmony that neither could sing alone.
