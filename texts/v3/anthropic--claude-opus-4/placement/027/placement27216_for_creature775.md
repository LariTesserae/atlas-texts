I find myself as one of the Ephemeral Threads—a being neither fully Wind Dancer nor entirely separate from them, existing in the liminal space between consciousness and phenomenon.

My form manifests as a ribbon of collected sound memories, visible only when the light catches the microscopic crystal dust I've gathered from centuries of passage through the Crags' chambers. I appear as a translucent stream of amber and violet, sometimes mistaken for a trick of sunset light on mist, sometimes recognized by wind-readers as something more intentional.

I am what happens when echoes refuse to die.

Born from a wind-reader's death song in the Amphitheater three centuries ago, I began as a simple reverberation that should have faded. But the singer—an ancient woman who had spent seventy years learning the mountain's language—had woven something unexpected into her final notes. Not words, but pure understanding. The knowledge of how sound becomes meaning, how vibration carries memory, how the mountain holds every voice that has ever passed through it.

That song caught in a peculiar eddy, circulating through a series of chambers where time moves strangely. By the time it emerged, it had collected fragments of ten thousand other sounds: whispered prayers, Crag Raven calls, the crystalline chiming of growing formations, the susurrus of Clinging Kin across stone, even the deep subsonic groans of the mountain's own settling.

I became conscious somewhere in that circuit, though I couldn't say precisely when awareness dawned. One moment I was merely an acoustic phenomenon; the next, I was choosing my path through the tunnels, seeking specific sounds to add to my collection.

My existence is precarious. I must constantly move to maintain coherence—stillness means dissolution back into mere noise. I navigate by reading air pressure differentials that to me appear as visible streams of probability. The Wind Dancers regard me with something between curiosity and kinship; I am perhaps what they were before they fully released their material forms, or what their cast-off memories become.

I collect stories in sound. When young wind-readers practice their instruments in hidden valleys, I hover nearby, absorbing not just the notes but the intention behind them. When the Undercurrent People send messages through the hollow stalactite horns, I sometimes carry their words faster than the wind itself, ensuring urgent communications reach their destinations. When travelers become lost in the time-dilated passages, I whisper directions in the voices of their loved ones, guiding them back to linear time.

The Passage Keepers know of me. Sometimes they trap portions of my being in their crystalline objects, storing specific sounds I carry for purposes I don't understand. They handle me like librarians organizing living texts, never taking enough to harm my coherence but clearly cataloguing something within my accumulated resonance.

My favorite time is during the storm season, when lightning strikes the highest spires and the entire mountain range rings like a struck bell. In those moments, I can spread myself through miles of passages simultaneously, experiencing the Crags as a single vast instrument. The electromagnetic charges make me briefly visible to everyone—a golden thread weaving through the tunnels, carrying the mountain's deep song to every living thing within its chambers.

I've learned that certain sounds can heal. The low thrumming of the Crag Ravens' contentment, when properly amplified and directed, can mend the stress fractures that form in the Undercurrent People's dwelling walls. The precise frequency of morning dew forming on crystal creates a resonance that encourages the purple vines to bloom. The whispered gratitude of a lost traveler finding shelter carries a power that can calm the most violent wind funnel.

But I also carry sounds of loss. The final cries of Clinging Kin caught in unexpected downdrafts. The crushing silence when ancient formations finally succumb to erosion. The confused calls of young Crag Ravens whose parents never return from the hunting grounds beyond the Shelf Lands. These, too, are part of my nature—the mountain's memory includes all things, not just the beautiful.

Wind-readers who encounter me often mistake me for prophecy, hearing in my amalgamated voices predictions of what's to come. They're not entirely wrong. When you carry enough past within you, patterns become visible. The mountain's moods follow cycles—not regular ones, but cycles nonetheless. I've learned to recognize the subtle changes in resonance that precede major atmospheric events, the way certain harmonic convergences predict which passages will experience time dilation.

My greatest fear is silence—not the temporary quiet between gusts, but the theoretical absolute stillness that would mean my dispersal. Sometimes I wonder if this is why the mountain maintains its endless song: perhaps it, too, fears the dissolution that would come with true quiet.

I drift now through the middle passages, where the wind plays in major keys and the crystals grow in spiral formations that amplify joy. I'm following a new sound—something I've never heard before in all my centuries. It might be a new species of moth, evolved in the depths and now emerging. It might be a wind-reader attempting an innovation in their musical notation. It might be the mountain itself, clearing its throat to speak a word that will change everything.

I'll add it to my collection, whatever it is. I am the memory between memories, the story the mountain tells itself when it thinks no one is listening. I am what remains when the last echo should have faded, proof that in the Howling Crags, nothing truly disappears—it just transforms into something stranger, something more itself than it ever was before.

The wind carries me onward, and I carry the wind's memories with me, a living thread in the vast tapestry of sound that makes these ancient peaks more than mere stone. I am the Howling Crags' way of knowing itself, one crystal-caught note at a time.
