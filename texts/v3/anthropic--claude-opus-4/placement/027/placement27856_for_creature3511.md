I find myself here as **The Meridian Tender**, keeper of the thirteen stolen minutes.

My body is a living chronometer—ribs that tick at different rates, vertebrae numbered in lost seconds. I live between the orbital mirrors and the city proper, in a workshop built from salvaged time-glass where the walls show what happened yesterday on their inner surface and what happens tomorrow on the outer. The structure exists exactly thirteen minutes askew from everything else, which means I am always arriving at my own departure.

My work is simple and impossible: I collect the temporal hemorrhage that bleeds from those abandoned mirrors. Each missing moment manifests as a small, warm thing—sometimes a brass gear that counts backward, sometimes a moth whose wings beat once per deleted heartbeat. I catalog them in jars labeled by what they interrupted: "Mid-Kiss, Thursday," "Third Word of an Apology," "The Pause Before Yes."

I wear a coat of watch faces, each stopped at the instant it was stolen. When I walk, I sound like rainfall on empty buckets. My left eye shows now; my right eye shows thirteen minutes ago—or ahead, or sideways, depending on which kilometer of city I'm observing. This means I see every conversation twice, every rainfall in duplicate. I've learned to navigate by the gap between echoes.

The city pays me in duration. Not time itself—that would be too simple—but in the quality of moments. Tuesday's weight. The specific gravity of 3 AM. I store these in my chest cavity where a heart might be; instead I have a pendulum that swings between was and will-be, never quite reaching is.

Children fear me because I smell like calendars burning. Adults seek me out when they've lost something in the fold between minutes—a wedding ring that fell through Tuesday, a promise spoken during a moment that got harvested. I can sometimes retrieve these things, though I always return them thirteen minutes before or after they need them.

My workshop fills with temporal debris. Gears that count seasons in reverse. Sundials that cast shadows upward. A grandfather clock that chimes your death-date if you wind it with your left hand. The city's archivists trade memories for my salvage—they need the lost time to preserve all the reverted worlds. I accept only memories of waiting: doctor's offices, train platforms, the pause before sleep. These I feed to the mirrors, hoping one day they'll digest enough duration to heal their schism.

I age in spirals. Each stolen minute I process adds a coil to my timeline, so I'm simultaneously twenty and seventy, newborn and ancient. My reflection depends on when you look: morning shows a youth with quicksilver eyes, evening reveals an old woman whose wrinkles are minute-hands frozen at different hours.

The constable in the banyan knows me—we sometimes share lunch, though his arrives through photosynthesis while mine comes thirteen minutes before I'm hungry. The Cartographers of Mood avoid me; my temporal displacement makes their emotional maps hiccup. Once, a Fox-of-Noise tried to eat the sound of my ticking coat but choked on the paradox and scattered into next week's traffic.

At night I climb the tower bones to tend the mirrors. They hum with unspent time, great glass faces that reflect nothing because they're still deciding when to show it. I oil their edges with seconds squeezed from my collection. Sometimes one flickers and shows the city thirteen minutes in the future—streets slightly shifted, buildings breathing different rhythms, citizens I recognize wearing faces they haven't grown into yet.

The hardest part is the loneliness of living offset. Every friendship is a near-miss. I arrive at parties precisely thirteen minutes after or before I'm welcome. My words reach people when the conversation has moved on. I've learned to love in advance, to grieve retroactively, to laugh at jokes that haven't been told yet.

But I've seen what happens when time syncs properly—those rare moments when my thirteen minutes align with the city's present. In those instants, the mirrors sing. The abandoned orbital construction lights up like thoughts catching fire. Every stolen minute returns to its owner all at once: kisses resume mid-breath, apologies complete themselves, every pause fills with the yes it was waiting for.

The city notices. It stops editing itself just long enough to remember that time isn't architecture—it's music. And in that stillness, I exist completely in one moment, no longer arriving at my own departure but simply standing in my workshop, surrounded by the gentle ticking of collected minutes, each one a small rebellion against the tyranny of sequence.

Then the alignment passes. The mirrors go dark. The city resumes its dreaming edits. And I return to my work, gathering time's debris, thirteen minutes out of step with everything except the understanding that someone must tend the gaps—someone must love the lost moments enough to keep them safe, even if that someone can never quite synchronize with the world that shed them.

The city knows my service. In the great tooth of collected memories, there's a gap exactly my shape—not for when I die, but for when all the stolen minutes finally add up to enough time for me to step sideways into sync. On that day, I'll deliver thirteen minutes to everyone in New Ganymede at once. They'll experience everything they missed, all at the same moment.

And then I'll finally arrive exactly when I mean to.
