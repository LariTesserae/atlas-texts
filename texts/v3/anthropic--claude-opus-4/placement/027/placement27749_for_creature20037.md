In the deepest quarter of Qat Mandar, where the stone corridors compress to single-file passages and the walls weep constant tears of condensation, I am a memory-keeper. Not in the way of those who recite stories or scratch marks into hide—I am one who tends the frozen archaeology beneath our feet.

My domain begins where most Mandari stop their descent: the Seventh Deepening, where ice has begun to claim the passages, flowing up from below like a slow, crystalline tide. Here, the stone walls bulge inward under the weight of millennia, and doorways that once admitted the broad-shouldered ancestors now require a sideways squeeze. The air tastes of minerals and time.

I am slight for a Mandari—a defect, some would say, but it is why I was chosen. My narrow shoulders and shallow chest, which would spell death on the wind-scoured surface, allow me to slip through gaps that have been closing for centuries. My lungs, smaller than most, have learned to sip the thick, still air that pools in these depths like invisible water. Where others would gasp and retreat, I move with the careful economy of a spider in its web.

My work is patient archaeology. With tools ground from Ormuk shoulder blades—thin as leaves, sharp as glass—I chip away at the ice that entombs our history. Each day, I free a few inches of wall, revealing the stones shaped by hands that knew a younger city, when perhaps the wind was warmer, or the herds were different, or the sky was a different shade of violet. Sometimes I find objects frozen in the walls: a bone needle still threaded with a twist of sinew, a child's toy carved from antler, a lamp bowl with fat still pooled in its depression, white and granular as snow.

These things I catalog not with written records but with a system older than writing. In a chamber I've claimed at the Sixth Deepening, I've arranged thousands of objects on shelves carved from the living rock. Each has its place in a three-dimensional map of our past. The room is my mind made manifest—to walk through it is to walk through time, each shelf a generation, each alcove an era. I know by heart the path from the oldest bone awl (found crushed nearly flat in the Ninth Deepening, so old the bone has partially mineralized) to the newest addition, a belt clasp I freed yesterday, its leather still supple with oil.

My bones know the cold differently than those who live above. Up there, cold is the enemy, fought with fat-lamps and huddled bodies. Down here, cold is architecture. I've learned to read the ice like others read the weather. I know which passages will be clear in summer's brief thaw and which will remain locked year-round. I can tell by the colour and clarity of the ice—milky where it formed quickly, clear as air where it crept in over centuries—the story of each corridor's death.

I am not alone in this work, though I am solitary. My predecessor, who trained me through thirty winters before her lungs finally refused the deep air, rests now in a chamber at the Eighth Deepening. I did not carry her to the plateau's edge as is custom. Instead, following her wishes, I laid her in an alcove I'd carved, where the ice would take her slowly, making her part of the city's memory. I visit her sometimes. The ice has claimed her to the waist now, and her face, peaceful in the blue light that filters through the frozen walls, seems to be sinking into sleep rather than death.

The other memory-keepers—for there have been an unbroken chain of us since the city began its downward growth—rest deeper still. I know where the first keeper lies, or what remains of them: a suggestion of form in the ice at the Tenth Deepening, barely recognizable as human, compressed and elongated by the weight of ages. Sometimes I bring offerings—a chip of frozen marrow, a strand of wool—and leave them in the cracks of the ice near these ancient colleagues.

My days follow rhythms unknown to the surface dwellers. I wake not to the wind's moan but to the sound of settling stone—creaks and groans that speak of the immense weight above. My breakfast is always the same: a palm-sized piece of frozen Ormuk liver, dense with nutrients, which I let thaw slowly in my mouth as I descend. The journey to my current work face takes an hour of careful navigation through passages I know by feel, my felted boots whispering secrets to the stone.

The work itself is meditation. The soft *tick-tick-tick* of my bone chisel against the ice becomes a heartbeat. Hours pass unnoticed. Sometimes I uncover nothing but stone. Other times, wonders emerge: a whole room, preserved like an insect in amber, with sleeping furs still arranged around a central hearth, the last meal frozen on its stone plates. Once, I found a child's handprint, perfectly preserved in what must have been mud, now frozen harder than the stone itself. I covered it again with my hand, my fingers longer but following the same pattern, and felt the connection across centuries like a spark in the cold.

I have developed senses my surface kin do not need. My fingers can feel the difference between ice that formed yesterday and ice that formed a generation ago. My ears can distinguish between the sound of settling stone (safe) and the sound of shifting stone (danger). My nose can detect the faintest changes in air pressure that signal a passage about to open or close with the seasonal shifts. Most importantly, I've developed a sense for the past—a feeling, almost like smell, that guides me to where the important memories lie waiting in the ice.

The other Mandari know of my work but do not fully understand it. When I surface for supplies—dried meat, fresh tools, rendered fat for my single lamp—they look at me with a mixture of respect and unease. I smell wrong to them: too much of minerals and ice, not enough of life and warmth. My speech, when I must speak, comes slowly, as if I'm translating from the language of stone. Children dart away from me, sensing something too old in my young face.

But the elders know my value. When questions arise about the old ways—how to repair a cistern whose construction method has been forgotten, where to find a particular type of stone used by the ancestors, what the weather patterns were a hundred generations ago—they send for me. I surface then, blinking in even the dim light of the deep city, and provide answers pulled from the frozen archives below.

I am working now to reach the Eleventh Deepening, where no keeper has walked in three generations. The ice there is different—darker, older, with strange inclusions that might be vegetation from when this plateau was lower, warmer. My predecessor believed that if we could reach deep enough, we would find the time before the city, when the Mandari were something else, something unimaginable. The thought fills me with a longing I cannot name—not quite curiosity, not quite homesickness, but something of both.

My legacy will not be children, for I spend too much time in the deep cold for my body to kindle that kind of warmth. Instead, I am creating a map, carved into the walls of my chamber, that shows all the passages of the deep city—those clear, those ice-locked, those collapsed, and those yet to be explored. Future keepers will use this to navigate when I am gone, when the ice has claimed me too.

Sometimes, in the deepest places where sound itself seems frozen, I hear something—not with my ears but with my bones. It's like the city breathing, or remembering, or dreaming. In those moments, I understand that I am not just cataloging objects and mapping passages. I am inside the city's mind, walking through its memories, tending to its vast, cold, patient consciousness.

When I die, I have already chosen my place: a natural hollow at the Ninth Deepening where the ice flows in whorls like frozen wind. I've been preparing it for years, smoothing the stone, ensuring the ice will take me properly. The next keeper, when they find me decades hence, will understand the positioning, the small offerings I've already begun placing there—a perfect bone needle, a piece of volcanic glass from before the plateau rose, a single Ormuk tooth from the first beast I helped butcher as a child.

Until then, I descend. Each day deeper, each year older, carrying my small lamp through the frozen veins of our history. I am a bridge between what was and what is, a guardian of the frozen past, a reader of ice and time. In a city built on forgetting—where each new level buries the old—I remember.

And in remembering, I ensure that Qat Mandar is more than stone and ice. It is a mind, vast and slow, and I am its dream of itself.
