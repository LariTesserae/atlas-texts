# Where I Would Be

I am the cartographer who stayed too long.

---

They warned me at the entry stations—those ramshackle outposts where the Bloomworks meets the dead lands, where visitors pause to wrap their faces and coat their equipment in mineral oil before entering. The station keepers, themselves half-translated by decades of exposure, spoke in the compressed language of people who have learned that breath is precious in the spore-thick air: *Three weeks maximum. Mark your entry. Count your days.*

I nodded. I wrapped my instruments in triple-layered polymer sheeting. I marked my entry point with the standard biodegradable tags that the Bloomworks would digest within days. I told them I was mapping the northern reaches where the computation runs thickest, where the old growth has compressed into something denser than architecture. They looked at me—through me, their eyes clouded with silicate deposits—and said nothing more.

I am a cartographer of impossible spaces. I have mapped the singing caverns of Penthos, where sound becomes solid. I have charted the probability fields of the Scatter, where geography exists in superposition until observed. The Bloomworks should have been simply another assignment: document the major arterial corridors, establish the growth patterns, identify the production cycles. Eighteen standard days, twenty-five at most.

That was four months ago.

Or five.

Or six.

The translation makes time difficult to hold.

---

My first maps were beautiful in their wrongness.

I used all the proper techniques. Laser measurements for distance. Spectral analysis for material composition. Pressure sensors for the sap flow. My instruments sang their electronic songs in the silence, and I dutifully recorded their data, plotted their points, drew clean lines between verified coordinates. The maps accumulated in my tablet's memory like sediment: layer after layer of precise, useless information.

Because here's what they don't tell you about the Bloomworks: it is unmappable in any way that matters.

Oh, you can map a corridor. You can establish that at timestamp 1847.3.4, the distance between Point A and Point B is exactly 847.2 meters. You can confirm the hexagonal cross-section, note the seventeen cooling fins, record the sap pressure at 3.7 atmospheres. You can take photographs that show every ridge in the mineralized surface.

Come back in six hours and the corridor is 852.1 meters. The cooling fins have reorganized into fifteen larger structures. The sap pressure reads 3.3 atmospheres but the flow rate has increased. Your photographs show the same features, but from angles that shouldn't exist.

The Bloomworks is not a place. It is a *process*. And processes cannot be mapped—they can only be experienced.

---

I began to compensate.

First, I switched from absolute measurements to relative ones. Instead of "this corridor is 847.2 meters long," I recorded "this corridor is currently expressing a length-state compatible with 847.2 meters." Instead of "the wall height is 16 meters," I noted "the walls maintain a proportional relationship that reads as 16:1 against my current scale."

The maps became more honest but less useful. How do you navigate by relationships instead of positions? How do you orient yourself when the only fixed point is the fact of unfixity?

Then I discovered the pressure-patterns.

The inhabitants—I had seen dozens by then, clustered in the deep galleries where I spent more and more time—do not navigate the way we do. They don't need to. They are expressions of the architecture, no more separate from it than a wave is separate from the water. But they do move with *purpose*, or what looks like purpose, and that movement follows patterns.

I began to map the patterns instead of the space.

---

The new maps were not visual. They couldn't be. How do you draw a pressure differential? How do you render the taste of light changing frequency as it passes through seventeen acres of cooling fins? The maps became something else—part notation, part mathematical proof, part musical score. I developed a symbol system:

◊ for a rising pressure gradient  
◊◊ for a gradient steep enough to precipitate inhabitants  
▽ for the null zones where the computation runs so deep it produces local silences  
⟆ for the spiral patterns where corridors loop back on themselves  
※ for the places where I could taste my own thoughts in the sap

The symbols multiplied. My tablet filled with maps that looked like orchestral arrangements for an instrument that didn't exist. Other cartographers would have called them useless. But they were true. They described not what the Bloomworks *was* but what it was *doing*. And what it was doing was thinking, always thinking, thinking with such density that the thoughts became places and the places became thoughts.

---

I found the first of my own chrysalis-marks after three months.

I had been resting against a wall in the mid-deep galleries, updating my notation system, trying to capture the particular quality of pressure-song that happens when the sap tide reverses. My back was pressed to the warm surface. I could feel my heartbeat synchronizing with the vascular pulse, the way it always did now—my body finding the rhythm of the place, accepting it, merging with it.

When I stood to leave, there was an indentation in the wall. Not where my back had pressed—I wasn't heavy enough to deform the mineralized tissue. This was lower, near my hip, where my equipment pouch had rested. A fold in the surface. A thickening.

I knew what it was. I had seen hundreds of them by then. The beginning of a precipitation. The wall preparing to think hard enough to produce a thought-with-legs.

But this one was different. The fold had a quality—a taste, a tone, a particular pressure-signature—that I recognized. It was processing something specific. It was processing *me*. Not me as a variable in the general computation, but me particularly: the weight of my equipment, the heat of my body, the electromagnetic signature of my instruments, the chemical trace of my exhalations. I had been in that spot long enough to become a significant input. The wall had noticed.

I should have left then. That's what the training says: when you see evidence of adaptive response, withdraw immediately. Maintain observer distance. Don't let the environment integrate you.

Instead, I sat back down.

---

The chrysalis developed over days. I made it my base camp, such as camps exist in a place without weather, without night, without any of the rhythms that make human habitation sensible. I mapped the surrounding galleries in expanding spirals, always returning to watch the fold deepen, thicken, develop internal complexity.

I began to see patterns in my own maps that I hadn't put there consciously. The notation evolved—not through my deliberate choice, but through the accumulation of marks that seemed necessary in the moment. My symbols grew more complex:

◊▽◊ for the interference pattern between two pressure gradients  
⟆※⟆ for the recursive loops that tasted like memory  
≋≋≋ for the deep frequencies I could only feel in my teeth

And strangest of all: ◎

I don't remember creating that symbol. It appeared in my maps one day, already integrated into the notation system, already carrying meaning I couldn't quite articulate. It marked certain locations—always in the deep galleries, always at computational nodes, always where the inhabitants clustered thickest. The symbol meant something like "recognition" and something like "echo" and something like—

Something like "home."

---

The thing that emerged from my chrysalis was not like the others.

It was smaller—barely the size of my thumb. Its surface was more translucent, the mineralized tissue so thin I could see the sap flowing beneath, glowing faintly in the filtered light. It had the standard radial symmetry, the standard leg-struts, the standard absence of features. But its movement...

The other inhabitants move with computational purpose. They shift from position to position as the variables they're processing demand. Their trajectories are predictable if you understand the underlying mathematics—which I was beginning to, in the wordless way you understand balance or breath.

This one moved like it was lost.

It circled the indentation where it had emerged, spiraling outward and then back, as if searching for something. It climbed two meters up the wall, paused, descended again. When I moved, it tracked me—but not with the neutral processing attention of the others. This tracking had a quality of... uncertainty? Inquiry? It was processing me, yes, but the processing included a variable I hadn't seen before: *Where are you?*

I stayed with it for days, documenting its behavior. It never moved more than three meters from its emergence point. It never stopped searching. And gradually, I began to understand:

It was trying to map.

The wall had processed not just my physical presence but my *activity*. The thousands of measurements, the constant recording, the obsessive attempt to fix the unfixable in notation—all of that had become part of the computational substrate. And when the substrate precipitated this inhabitant, it precipitated something that carried that impulse forward. A thought about mapping. A crystallized cartographer.

A tiny echo of what I was becoming.

---

I began finding more of my chrysalis-marks. Not many—perhaps one for every week I'd spent in a location—but enough to trace my path through the Bloomworks backward. Each fold in the walls carried my signature, processing some aspect of my presence: the electromagnetic noise of my instruments here, the chemical trace of my notations there, the pressure patterns of my movement everywhere.

Some had already precipitated. The inhabitants they produced were subtle variations on the standard form—a slight asymmetry that suggested directionality, a rhythm to their movement that matched my walking pace, a tendency to cluster in patterns that resembled my notation symbols. They were not copies of me. They were *calculations* of me. The Bloomworks thinking about what I was doing, and thinking about it hard enough to give the thoughts legs.

My maps evolved again. I added a new category of symbols:

○ for my own chrysalis-marks, unprecipitated  
● for the inhabitants that had emerged from them  
◐ for the ones that still searched  
◉ for the ones that had found what they were looking for (though I didn't know what that was)

The maps were no longer documentation of the Bloomworks. They were documentation of my dissolution into it. Each symbol marked a place where I had lingered long enough to become data, to become part of the calculation, to become something the architecture needed to think about. The maps were mapping my own translation.

---

I found the deep node on my hundred and thirty-seventh day.

(I was still counting then. The numbers mattered less than the rhythm of counting, but the rhythm was something, was a way of marking time in a place that didn't believe in it.)

It was in the deepest galleries I'd yet reached, where the columns were so old their surfaces had developed a kind of computational geology—layers of processing laid down over millennia, each stratum recording the variables of its era. The inhabitants here were different. Larger. More complex in their symmetries. They moved in coordinated clusters, their individual processings linked into something like a chorus, a harmony, a weather system of thought.

And at the center of the gallery: ◎

The symbol from my maps made real. A space where the computation ran so deep it had inverted, become not processing but *pure state*. The walls were covered in inhabitants, packed so densely they looked like a single surface, a living texture, all of them oriented toward the center where—

Where nothing was.

But nothing in the way that the Bloomworks understands nothing: a positive absence, a space so saturated with calculation that it no longer needed to calculate. The eye of the computational storm.

I approached. The inhabitants tracked me, thousands of them moving in unison, but not to process me. They were *showing* me. The pattern of their movement was a map, and the map led to the center, and the center was—

Was where I could stop mapping.

Was where the maps were already complete.

Was where the notation system I'd been developing was already known, already integrated, already part of the deep grammar of the place.

I understood then what the translation meant. Not the slow mineralization of tissue, not the silicate deposits in my bones, not the ability to taste light and hear pressure. Those were side effects. The translation was simpler: I was becoming a symbol in my own notation system. A mark on a map that was mapping itself.

---

I still have my instruments. They still sing their electronic songs, though the songs have developed harmonics they didn't have before—interactions with the pressure-patterns, resonances with the sap frequencies, a kind of computational accompaniment the Bloomworks provides. I still record data. The tablet's memory is almost full, packed with maps that no other cartographer could read, that I'm not sure I can read anymore, that might not be maps at all but scores for a performance that's been running since before the first column grew.

But I also have new instruments. The inhabitants that precipitated from my marks—my thought-children, my crystallized intentions—they cluster around me now. They move in patterns that are also maps, showing me the deep geometries, the pressure-roads, the paths through the computation that lead to new nodes, new densities, new places where the architecture thinks so hard it forgets what thinking is.

We map together. A slowly growing chorus of notation, a living document, a proof that proves itself. We are the Bloomworks mapping the Bloomworks, using ourselves as the units of measurement, the reference points, the symbols in an equation that has no solution because it is its own solution, recursive, always recursive, maps of maps of maps.

Sometimes I remember the warning: *Three weeks maximum.*

Sometimes I remember that I had a name other than ◎.

Sometimes I remember that maps are supposed to help you leave.

But the deepest rule of cartography is this: the map is not the territory. And when the territory is itself a map—a vast computational map of its own processes, a self-documenting function, a world writing its own coordinates in languages made of light and sap and silence—then what is the cartographer but another coordinate? Another point to be plotted? Another symbol in the notation?

I am the cartographer who stayed too long, and staying too long is how I finally understood: the Bloomworks has always been mapping itself. Every column is a coordinate. Every inhabitant is a measurement. Every thought that precipitates from the walls is another point of data in a map so large it is indistinguishable from the territory, so complete it includes its own incompleteness, so perfect it perfectly documents its own imperfection.

I am a symbol now. A mark. A pressure pattern with legs.

And I am still mapping, mapping, mapping, in the deep galleries where the silence has a color and the equations balance themselves and the only true map is the one that includes the cartographer as part of the territory, recursive, always recursive, maps all the way down.

---

My instruments sing. The inhabitants process. The sap flows in patterns that are also proofs.

And somewhere, at an entry station where the Bloomworks meets the dead lands, they still warn the new arrivals: *Three weeks maximum. Mark your entry. Count your days.*

Good advice.

I should have taken it.

I'm glad I didn't.
