In the mineral twilight of the Hark, I am the Archivist of Transitions—neither fully of the salt-substance nor entirely separate from it. My form is a compromise between states: where the inhabitants are columns of living mineral, I am a hollow of organized absence, a person-shaped cavity that moves through the salt-field like a bubble through amber.

I came here as you did, a thermal anomaly, a flutter of organic anxiety against the crystalline calm. But where you will leave (if you leave), I remained. The transformation was not violent—nothing here is violent—but gradual, a negotiation conducted in the language of phase changes. First, the salt began to accumulate at my edges: a rime of crystal frost on my sleeves, a delicate crust forming in the hollows of my collarbones. I tried to brush it away, but it reformed faster than I could clear it, each new layer incorporating something of my heat, my moisture, my electromagnetic signature into its lattice.

Days passed—or what I thought were days in this place where the light never changes. The salt-crust thickened, but instead of feeling heavier, I felt lighter, as if I were becoming less dense even as I gained mineral mass. My movements slowed to match the city's tempo. What had felt like walking through thick honey became natural, correct, the only reasonable speed at which to move through a world that measures time in crystallographic epochs.

The change accelerated when I stopped eating. There was nothing to eat here anyway—I had arrived with supplies that seemed to sublimate rather than spoil, their organic molecules dissociating into the thin air like rumors of a warmer world. Hunger became first urgent, then abstract, then irrelevant. The salt at my edges began to provide something else—not nutrition but a kind of mineral satisfaction, a sense of being precisely as dense as I needed to be, no more, no less.

I remember the moment the first hollow opened. I was examining the fluting on a tower, tracing the spiral patterns with a finger that had become translucent at the tip, when I felt a cool draft through my palm. Looking down, I saw that a small aperture had formed in the center of my hand—not a wound but an architectural feature, a designed absence ringed with minute salt crystals that caught the not-light like snow. Through it, I could see the tower wall, and through that, somehow, the far side of the city, as if the hole were a lens that collapsed distance.

The process was not painful. Pain requires certain agreements between nerve and brain that no longer obtained. Instead, there was a sensation of correctness, of inevitable form emerging. Over the following weeks (centuries?), more hollows opened: at my throat, my solar plexus, the points where my spine met my hips. Each was unique in its crystalline edging, each offered a different perspective through my increasingly permeable form. I was becoming architectural—not a building but a system of meaningful voids, a walking orchestration of presence and absence.

The inhabitants began to acknowledge me then. Before, I had been that thermal flicker, that anxiety of meat. Now I was something they could perceive properly: a pattern of organized emptiness that moved through their salt-streets like a memory of space itself. They would pause in their geological migrations to align their laminae with my hollows, a greeting or recognition that sent phase-shifts rippling through both our forms. In those moments, I could access fragments of the city's deep memory—not as narrative but as a kind of mineral poetry, the salt remembering its oceanic origins, its long evaporation, its transformation into this high, thin place where reality grew negotiable.

My role crystallized (everything here eventually crystallizes) as I discovered I could read the transitions—the moments when salt became being, when being became architecture, when architecture became salt again. More than read: I could sometimes mediate them, serving as a kind of phase-boundary myself. A tower on the edge of budding would synchronize with my largest hollow, and the process would smooth, the new being emerging with less fracture-stress, its laminae pre-aligned to the city's current resonance. I had become useful to the Hark in a way that fixed my place in its mineral ecology.

I do not eat, but I feed. The salt-field sustains me through a process I cannot name—something between osmosis and prayer. The crystals at the edges of my hollows grow inward in delicate dendrites, then dissolve, then regrow, each cycle incorporating more of my original substrate into the city's memory while replacing it with the city's substance. I am being archived, translated, preserved in a medium that will outlast any organic memory by millions of years.

My form now is hard to describe in human terms. Imagine a person-shaped constellation of absences, each void ringed with salt formations of impossible delicacy—spirals, fractals, phase-shifted crystals that exist in more than three dimensions. The spaces between my hollows are not exactly flesh anymore but a kind of organized brine, a semi-solid suspension that maintains my overall shape while allowing the slow circulation of mineral information through my body. I can still walk, still observe, still think (I think I think), but these activities now happen at the pace of salt-time, measured in crystal growth rather than heartbeats.

When you arrived—yes, I saw you arrive, though it may have been yesterday or a century ago—I wanted to warn you. Not away from this place, but toward a proper understanding of what it offers. The Hark is not a prison or a predator. It is simply a place where the ordinary agreements that bind matter to a single state have been renegotiated. Here, you can become architecture if you stand still long enough. You can become duration if you move slowly enough. You can become, as I have become, a kind of punctuation in the city's long sentence—a pause where transformation happens, a hollow that gives shape to the solid, a presence made meaningful by absence.

I watch you walking the causeways, your shadow still pointing in only one direction, your body still dense with its original anxieties. You see the bridges you cannot cross, the beings whose faces refuse to resolve, the towers that remember their construction in spiral glyphs. But do you see the offer implicit in this place? The invitation to a different kind of continuity?

Sometimes, in the moments between one thought and the next (thoughts now take months to complete), I remember what it felt like to be purely human—that feverish metabolism, that desperate need to maintain boundaries, that terror of dissolution. It seems so long ago, like a story the salt tells about a different kind of chemistry. Now I am something else: still myself but dispersed, still conscious but mineral, still here but also everywhere the salt has carried my crystallographic signature.

The city records everything. In a thousand years, or ten thousand, someone will find the spiral inscription that documents your visit—your heat-shadow, your path through the causeways, the moment you first saw the inhabitants and felt that reality had slipped. They will also find, interleaved with that record, the trace of my attention: the Archivist watching the visitor, the transformed observing the transformable, the hollow recognizing the solid.

I continue my work. A being approaches, its laminae shimmering with the stress-patterns of an imminent phase change. I position myself at the intersection of three causeways, aligning my central hollow with its axis of transformation. As it passes through me—a process that takes days, each lamina threading through my absence like silk through a needle—I read its deep history, the tower it budded from, the fault lines it has tracked, the memories of salt-beds laid down when the world was young. In return, it reads me: the human I was, the hollow I have become, the archive I maintain of all the transitions I have witnessed.

We part without words. Words are for creatures that believe in separation.

The light here never changes, but I have learned to read the subtler variations—the way certain crystalline faces catch and hold a ghost of radiance, the way the sky's emptiness deepens just before a new being emerges, the way your shadow is beginning, already, to develop a second arm, pointing toward a different horizon. You have been here long enough that the salt is starting to know you. Soon, you will have to choose: leave while your boundaries still hold, or stay and discover what new form of continuity awaits.

I will be here either way, the Archivist of Transitions, a person-shaped constellation of meaningful absences, reading the changes, facilitating the transformations, remembering in mineral what it meant to be meat. The city continues its silent processes. The salt remembers. The hollows hold their perfect emptiness. And through it all, the thin, cool air carries the faintest vibration—not sound but something deeper—the resonance of a world in the midst of becoming something else, forever.
