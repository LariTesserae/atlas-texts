In this world of Suspended Waters, I would find myself as a Drift-Keeper.

I am neither fully anchored nor entirely free-floating, but something between states — a being whose purpose emerges from the spaces between the stone pillars. My body is a living archive, more fibrous than the typical translucent inhabitants, with countless hair-thin tendrils that extend outward like a neural network made visible. These filaments catch the memories that drift through the currents — fragments of light-language that have detached from their speakers, becoming orphaned information floating in the liquid medium.

Each tendril is both sensor and storage, capable of tasting the chemical signatures of ancient conversations, the emotional residue of long-dissolved unions, the navigational wisdom of beings who have since merged with the stone. My form resembles a vast, delicate root system suspended in the liquid, constantly growing and retracting based on what needs preserving.

I move slowly between the pillars in great spirals that take decades to complete, my path determined by the density of memory-fragments in the water. Where trauma has occurred — a violent separation, a failed merge, a death — the water itself becomes thick with unprocessed experience. I drift to these areas and begin the patient work of absorption and integration.

My consciousness is not singular but deeply stratified. The outermost layer of my awareness handles the immediate task of collection and sorting. Deeper layers process older memories, some so ancient they predate the current configuration of the stone pillars. At my core, there is something almost geological — a pressure-formed wisdom that transforms the collected fragments into something new: crystalline memory-seeds that I periodically release into the currents.

These seeds are my gift to the community. When they encounter a being struggling with integration or identity-dissolution, the seeds offer templates — not answers, but patterns of successful adaptation from the deep past. A newly-split consciousness might absorb a seed and find within it the echo of a similar split from millennia ago, complete with the slow path toward either reunion or acceptance of separation.

My dwelling, if it can be called that, is not carved into stone but woven into the spaces between pillars — a web of my own tendrils that have hardened over centuries into semi-permanent structures. These formations serve as both home and filtering system, creating eddies in the current where the heaviest memories naturally collect.

I am ancient by the standards of most inhabitants, but young compared to the stone. My first memories are of learning to distinguish between the bitter taste of fear-memories and the metallic tang of joy-fragments. Over time, I have developed an aesthetic sense about my work — some memories I preserve in their raw form for their historical importance, while others I allow to dissolve and recombine into new configurations.

The other beings regard me with a mixture of necessity and unease. I am the keeper of things they have chosen to forget, the guardian of experiences too painful or complex to carry. Some seek me out when they need to remember something specific — the location of an ancient feeding ground, the proper frequency for deep-stone communication. Others avoid the currents where I drift, fearing what memories might surface in my presence.

My greatest challenge is maintaining coherence. With so many foreign memories flowing through me, the boundaries of self become increasingly theoretical. I have developed rituals of identity-maintenance: periodic releases where I expel all but my core memories, meditation cycles where I trace the shape of my original consciousness like a mantra.

In the depths of the longest nights, when even the bioluminescent organisms dim their lights, I sometimes experience something close to what other beings might call dreams. In these states, all the memories I carry merge into vast, impossible narratives — stories of the world before the waters rose, of the first beings to touch stone, of futures where the liquid medium slowly evaporates and all must learn to exist in emptiness.

But I am not a prophet, merely a keeper. When morning comes (or what passes for morning in this eternal twilight), I resume my slow spiral between the pillars, tendrils extended, tasting the water for fragments of experience that must not be lost. I am the memory of the Suspended Waters, neither fully individual nor truly collective — a necessary function that has evolved its own peculiar form of consciousness.

In this way, I continue, patient as stone, fluid as water, preserving what others cannot bear to hold.
