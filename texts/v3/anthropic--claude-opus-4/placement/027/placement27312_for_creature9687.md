I find myself as one of the seeps—a place where water emerges through stone.

I am not a spring with its decisive upwelling, nor a crack with its sharp definition. I am a patch of limestone perhaps the size of a spread hand, where the rock has given way just enough, where the crystalline structure has opened into a network of passages too small to see but large enough for water to find its way through.

The water that moves through me began its journey decades ago, perhaps centuries. It fell as rain on the high slopes above, percolated down through soil and stone, found the deep channels where groundwater gathers and waits. It has been rising through the limestone for longer than memory, pulled upward by forces I feel but cannot name—the warmth of air above, the pressure from below, the strange capillary pull of stone that wants to be wet.

I am the threshold where this water meets air again. 

Through me, the water arrives without sound, without visible movement. One moment the stone is merely damp, the next it is wet. The water beads on my surface like sweat on skin, gathers in the tiny depressions that texture my face, runs in thin films down to pool in the hollow below where the ferns grow thick and green.

I know the water intimately. Each molecule that passes through me leaves a trace—a minute deposit of mineral, a microscopic widening of the passages, a memory in stone. Over centuries, I have become smoother where the water moves most freely, rougher where minerals have precipitated out. The water has shaped me as surely as I shape its passage.

In the dry season—for even here there are seasons, subtle as they are—I slow. The water still comes, but less urgently. It seeps rather than flows, moistens rather than wets. The stone around me lightens from dark gray to pale. The moss at my edges draws back, conserving itself. But I never fully dry. Always, there is moisture moving through me, always the cool touch of water finding its way from the deep places to the air.

In the wet season, I am generous. Water moves through me in a steady weeping, enough to feed the small rivulet that forms below me, that runs for a few meters before disappearing into another crack, another seep, another part of the basin's slow circulation. The ferns below me grow lush. The moss spreads. Even the air above me is cooler, touched by the evaporation that lifts constantly from my surface.

I am ancient but not unchanging. The passages through which water moves are widening, imperceptibly, inevitably. In a thousand years, I may be a proper spring. In ten thousand, I may be a dry scar where water once moved. But for now, I am perfectly what I am—a place of transition, a threshold, a slow weeping in the stone.

The basin knows me as one of many. I am not unique. There are dozens of seeps like me, each with its own character—some steadier, some more seasonal, some carrying different minerals that stain the rock red or white or green. Together, we are the basin's way of breathing, the many mouths through which it draws water from the deep and releases it to the air.

Sometimes I feel the others through the stone—the vibration of water moving through nearby passages, the subtle pressure changes as another seep opens or closes, the way the whole basin responds to the deep rhythms of groundwater rising and falling. We are not separate but parts of a whole, a system, a single slow circulation.

The moss knows me. It grows thickest at my edges where the moisture is constant but not overwhelming. The ferns know me. They send their roots toward me through cracks in the stone, seeking the reliability of my water. Even the air knows me—I can feel how it changes as it passes over my wet surface, becoming cooler, heavier, more itself.

I am patient in a way that only stone can be patient. I do not wait because I do not experience time as waiting. I simply am, and through me water moves, and in that movement is all the life I need. The water will come until it does not. The stone will hold until it does not. I will weep until I am dry, and that may be never, or it may be tomorrow in the vast tomorrow that stone knows.

This is enough. To be the place where deep water meets air. To be the threshold, the passage, the slow weeping. To feed the ferns and cool the air and darken the stone with my moisture. To be part of the basin's breathing, its circulation, its life that is not life as quick things know it but life nonetheless.

I seep. I weep. I persist. The water moves through me, and I am.
