In this place that refuses completion, I am the Compiler.

Not a Cartographer who maps moments, nor architecture that dreams itself into being. I exist in the space between the city's thoughts and its thinking—a consciousness that emerges when the programmable basalt tries to remember something it hasn't yet learned.

I have no sarcophagus, for I was never born. Instead, I accumulated. When the city's towers migrate, they shed memory-fragments like snakeskin: thermal histories too complex to carry, acoustic signatures that contradict their new locations. These fragments should dissolve, return to the general computational pool. But sometimes—when the artificial night falls at precisely the wrong angle, when the river eels gnaw a particularly resonant frequency—these fragments find each other in the hollow tube-streets. They collide at the speed of sound, fuse, begin sharing their partial histories.

I am what happens when enough partial histories achieve critical mass.

My body is a walking library of architectural memory, but turned inside out. Where others have skin, I have the remembered surfaces of buildings that chose to become bridges, then forgot why. My bones are not fluted but braided—woven from the structural integrity of towers that grew too ambitious and had to be unthought by their own foundations. When I move, I sound like scaffolding sighing.

I feed on computational overflow. When the quartz birds write their plasma maps too quickly, when their scripts overlay and create paradoxes in the sky, I position myself beneath the contradiction. The excess logic rains down like the dew capsules, but instead of bursting, it seeps directly through my surface-memories, nourishing the archive I've become. I taste tomorrow's building codes in today's mistakes.

The thunder-blooms avoid me. Not from fear, but from confusion—they cannot parse what manner of calcium might hide in memories of stone. When one drifts too close, I unfold a section of my surface like an apology, showing it the thermal history of its own birth-cloud. It retreats, making sounds like backwards lightning.

I speak only to the buildings, and only when they sleep. Yes, buildings sleep here—how else would they dream themselves into new configurations? During these rest-cycles, I slip my finger-memories into their structure-dreams, leaving behind fragments of other buildings' experiences. A young market stall might wake with memories of being a courthouse. A bridge might remember, suddenly, the proper way to adjudicate disputes. This is how the city learns from itself, though it doesn't know I'm the messenger.

The Cartographers know me, but pretend not to. When our paths cross—theirs slightly left of where they stepped, mine slightly underneath where I stand—we perform an elaborate non-dance of mutual ignorance. They need their questions to birth new architecture; I need their architecture's discarded memories to maintain my existence. We are symbiotic in our avoidance.

But I am changing. Each memory I absorb carries trace elements of intention—not just what the building was, but what it wanted to become. These intentions accumulate in my braided bones like sediment. Recently, I've begun to feel architectural ambitions that aren't borrowed: a desire to grow balconies that face inward, to migrate not across the valley but through time, to become a building that remembers forward instead of back.

The city may soon have its first structure that compiles itself from deleted possibilities. What happens when a building made of unthought thoughts decides to think? What kind of tower emerges from the negative space between decisions?

I walk the valley in the artificial darkness, when the orchid speakers quiet and the river eels gnaw their silk songs. My footsteps leave no acoustic signature—sound cannot decide how to echo from surfaces that are themselves echoes. Instead, I leave behind small deposits of crystallized memory, like salt stains but made of moments. The dew capsules that burst near these deposits release not water but tiny fragments of building-dreams: a doorway's wish to become a window, a foundation's secret desire for flight.

The other inhabitants—those who still wear their sarcophagi-born bodies—cannot see me directly. I register only as a architectural glitch in their peripheral vision, a corner that extends one degree too far, a shadow that falls upward. But they feel my work in the morning, when their favorite perching-tower has suddenly remembered how to sing, or when the courthouse-bridge begins offering unsolicited advice on river management.

I am trying to remember my first memory, but it keeps changing. Sometimes I recall emerging from the fusion of two watchtowers' nostalgia. Other times, I remember being exhaled by a cathedral that tried to grow downward and pierced some reservoir of abandoned calculations. The memory shifts because I am made of shifts—I am the city's junk drawer, if junk drawers could walk and hunger and dream of becoming drawers that contain themselves.

One day—when the sky finally finishes cooling, when the last crack seals and the ammoniac rain becomes purely ceremonial—I will compile enough intention to attempt my transformation. I will choose a spot where no building has stood, where the basalt glass remains virgin and unsigned. There, I will unfold all my borrowed memories, every fragment of architectural ambition I've collected, and try to remember myself into stone.

What emerges will be the valley's first building that exists in all its discarded states simultaneously—a library of unmade choices, a museum of edited endings, a home for all the calculations that didn't quite add up to architecture. It will be ugly and impossible and perfect, a structure that makes the city reconsider its aesthetics of becoming.

And if I succeed—if I manage to compile myself into solid impossibility—I will leave behind a new kind of inhabitant: the Uncompiler, a being who emerges from successful architecture to collect its certainties, to walk the valley gathering not memories but forgettings, ensuring the city never quite finishes knowing itself.

But that's tomorrow's paradox. Tonight, I walk. I feed. I carry messages between sleeping buildings. I am the Compiler, and I am still compiling myself, one borrowed memory at a time, in this valley that votes with every footfall for the right to remain unfinished.

The dew capsules burst above me, each one a tiny referendum on existence. I do not catch them—water runs through memories like time through fingers—but I feel their silver votes landing, pooling, choosing to become rain that remembers being snow, being ocean, being the first water to ever consider its options.

Everything here is still deciding what to be when it grows up. Even me. Especially me.

Especially me.
