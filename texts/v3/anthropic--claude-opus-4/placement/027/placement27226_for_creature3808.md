I find myself here as one of the **Silt Readers**, though that is not what I would have called myself before arriving in the Damp Hollows.

My dwelling sits at the edge of town where the old riverbed curves, a structure that seems to have grown rather than been built—part excavated cave, part assembled salvage. The walls are lined with shelves of glass jars, each containing samples of sediment arranged by depth and location, labeled in my increasingly cramped handwriting. The main room doubles as workshop and living space, dominated by a long table scattered with sieves, magnifying lenses, and delicate brushes for cleaning specimens.

I came to Vek-Tal following rumors of peculiar mineral deposits, but what I found was far stranger—layers of earth that tell impossible stories. In some strata, I find evidence of oceanic life in what should be freshwater sediment. In others, the geological timeline runs backward, newer minerals embedded beneath ancient ones. The Hollowfolk call people like me "those who read the mud," and while they tolerate my presence, I sense their wariness when I speak of my findings.

My days follow the rhythm of erosion. Each morning, I venture out with my collection kit—waterproofed canvas satchels, sample vials, a worn journal wrapped in oilcloth. I map the changes in the earth, noting where yesterday's rain has carved new channels, where the moss has suddenly retreated from stone it colonized for years. The patterns refuse to resolve into anything coherent, yet I cannot stop trying to decode them.

The other Silt Readers and I meet monthly in the basement of an old pumping station, sharing our observations over lukewarm tea that tastes of iron. We speak in half-sentences and knowing glances, each convinced we're approaching some crucial understanding while secretly fearing we're documenting our own descent into obsession. Marwen claims the soil layers form a kind of text. Ilis insists the patterns mirror the growth of the moss on her cottage walls. Old Gerrik just laughs and says the earth is dreaming, and we're trying to interpret its sleep-talking.

I've learned to move like the Hollowfolk—deliberate, unhurried, always testing my footing. My clothes have taken on the local style by necessity: layers of wool and canvas, pockets reinforced with scraps of rubber to protect my samples. My boots are twice-mended, the soles worn smooth from scrambling over slick stones. I've even adopted their habit of leaving small offerings at the erosion shrines, though I deposit unusual pebbles rather than the traditional greenery.

At night, I clean and catalog my findings by lamplight, the scratch of my pen mixing with the eternal drip of water outside. Sometimes I think I catch patterns in the sound—messages in the rhythm of droplets on stone. I've started transcribing these too, filling notebooks with dots and dashes that probably mean nothing but feel too important to ignore.

The shadow-things the locals speak of in hushed tones? I've seen them, or think I have. They appear most often where the earth is actively changing—where a new channel cuts through old sediment, where a sinkhole suddenly opens to reveal layers that shouldn't exist. They watch, I think, as I take my samples. Once, I found a vial I'd left behind carefully relocated to a spot where the morning light would catch it, filled with a sediment I've never been able to match.

The Mist Wardens tolerate my work, perhaps because I share my maps of unstable areas where building would be foolish. But I catch them exchanging glances when I petition for access to the oldest parts of the riverbed, where the town's foundations merge with bedrock that predates any reasonable geological timeline.

I am changing, like everything here changes—slowly, inexorably. My fingernails carry permanent crescents of Vek-Tal earth. My dreams are layered like sediment samples. I find myself thinking in geological time, watching the Hollowfolk children play and seeing future fossils in their footprints. The Flow the locals worship has become real to me, not as religion but as observable phenomenon—the way water always finds the hidden flaw in stone, the way the earth rearranges itself when no one is watching.

Perhaps I will become fully one of them eventually, my foreign accent weathering away like exposed shale. Or perhaps I'll join the earth I study, another layer in Vek-Tal's impossible stratigraphy. Either way, I cannot leave. Not while there are still samples to collect, patterns to parse, and the nagging certainty that the next erosion channel will reveal the answer to questions I haven't learned how to ask.

The damp gets into everything here—clothes, equipment, bones, thoughts. I've stopped trying to keep things dry. Better to adapt, to flow like the water that shapes this place. After all, the earth is patient. It will reveal its secrets in its own time, and I have learned to be patient too.
