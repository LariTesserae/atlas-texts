In the high places where stone weeps eternal tears, I am the Knotkeeper.

My body moves like mercury through moss-light, each footfall a negotiation with the saturated earth. I am tall but bent, my spine curved from decades of listening downward, into the thrum. My skin has taken on the quality of wet bark—ridged, cool, faintly green where the symbiotic algae have made their home in my pores. My fingers are long, articulated like the roots of the stone-pines, sensitive to vibrations too subtle for most creatures to perceive.

I remember my predecessor only in fragments—a shadow moving through the steam-light, hands that guided mine to feel the first tremor of discord in a Heartwood's pulse. They dissolved into the mist one day, or perhaps became it. This is the way of Knotkeepers. We are born from the condensation of purpose itself, and we return to it when our listening is complete.

My work is in the deep tangles where the root networks converge—the Knots. These are not mere meetings of wood and water, but synaptic junctions in the continent's vast nervous system. Each Knot is unique: some sprawl across entire valleys, their complexity rivaling any city; others are intimate, no larger than a shrine, where three ancient pines have grown together into a single, thinking mass.

Today I feel a disturbance in the Seventh Knot, two days' journey through the weeping forests. The thrum there has developed a flutter—not quite arrhythmia, but a hesitation that speaks of blockage or damage. I pack nothing; everything I need grows along the way. My path follows the acoustic corridors, those hexagonal basalt formations that carry the engine's pulse. When I place my palm against their wet surface, I feel the entire plateau's heartbeat channeled through crystal and compression.

The stone-pines part for me, or perhaps I have learned to move as they expect movement to occur—slowly, with purpose, never against the grain of growth. The shaggy ungulates lift their massive heads as I pass, their eyes reflecting depths of patience I aspire to. One young bull, his coat so thick with moisture-drinking algae that he appears more plant than animal, follows me for a time. His presence is welcome; where his hooves compress the earth, new channels form for water to flow.

By evening of the first day, I reach a violet pool I call the Mirror of Listening. Unlike others, this one sometimes shows not the starless depth but visions of the Knots themselves—their health, their harmony, their hurt. I perform the ritual of adjustment, finding the one drip whose rhythm has grown too perfect, too mechanical. A shift of moss, a redirected rootlet, and the pool shivers into revelation.

I see the Seventh Knot as if from within: a cathedral of interwoven roots where resin flows in golden vessels, where the slow transformation from sap to living composite catches and stutters. There—a limestone intrusion, a finger of different stone that has worked its way between root layers over centuries, disrupting the pressure gradients necessary for proper polymerization.

The vision fades, but I have what I need.

The second day's journey takes me higher, where the air grows so thin that each breath tastes of iron and stars. Here the weeping intensifies, as if the sky itself sweats from the effort of existing at such altitude. The stone-pines grow more massive but more widely spaced, each one a kingdom of bark and needle. Between them, the moss seas deepen until I wade through them hip-deep, their bioluminescence painting my path in emerald fire.

I find the Seventh Knot by its silence—a pocket where the great thrum falters. The convergence is magnificent: twelve ancient pines whose roots have formed a living mandala beneath the earth, their combined age greater than written history. At the center, where their roots should weave together in perfect symphony, I sense the discord.

The work is delicate. I do not dig; digging would be violence here. Instead, I listen my way down, following natural channels, easing through moss curtains and fungal galleries. My fingers find gaps in the root weave where water can carry me deeper. Hours pass, or days—time means little when you're thinking at the speed of trees.

At last, I reach the intrusion. The limestone finger is beautiful in its way, carved by the same patient water that shapes everything here. But it does not belong in this particular conversation. I cannot remove it—that would be another violence. Instead, I begin the slow process of teaching the roots to think around it.

This is not work of muscle but of presence. I place my hands where root meets stone and feel the confusion in the resin flow. Then I begin to hum—not with my throat but with my whole body, matching and slightly modifying the frequencies moving through the wood. I become a tuning fork, a bridge between what is and what could be.

The roots respond slowly, so slowly. New growth begins to explore alternative paths. Resin channels reroute themselves, finding pressure gradients around rather than through the limestone. It will take years for the full healing, but I feel the moment when the possibility of health returns—the Knot sighs, the thrum steadies, and deep below, that perfect living composite begins to form again.

I surface to find three days have passed. The young bull is still there, waiting, having created a small clearing with his patient hooves. His eyes hold something like approval, or perhaps I only imagine it.

As I make my way back through the weeping world, I think of the other Knotkeepers, each tending their own portion of this vast, breathing machine. We may never meet—the plateau is too vast, our work too specific to each place. But I feel them in the steadiness of the thrum, in the health of the engine we all serve.

Sometimes I wonder if we Knotkeepers are truly separate beings, or if we are more like the fruiting bodies of a single, vast fungus—apparently individual but sharing a common purpose, a common mycelial network of understanding. When my time comes to dissolve into mist, will another emerge from the condensation to take my place? Or will I simply become another kind of Knotkeeper, my consciousness distributed through the water cycle itself?

The stone-pines don't answer. They just grow, and weep, and transform sunlight into mechanical motion with the patience of geological time. And I move through them, listening for the next flutter, the next hesitation in the continent's vast, wet heart.

Tonight I rest beside a pool that reflects only the sky—no violet depths here, just honest water on honest stone. The dripping forms a symphony around me, each note perfect in its imperfection. Somewhere, deep below, millions of tons of living wood flex and pump in rhythm, drawing water up from the earth's heart, breathing it out into the sky, where it will condense and fall again.

This is the quiet industry: a machine made of rain and time, tended by those who have learned to think like forests, to move like water, to listen like stone.
