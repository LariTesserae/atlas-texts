The Haulwright

I am bent into the shape of my purpose. My spine curves forward from years of pulling, shoulders bunched like slag formations, arms corded with the memory of ten thousand loads. I measure distance not in steps but in the scrape of my sledge across the cooling plates, the rhythm of metal on metal that has become my heartbeat.

My face is a geography of scars and soot. The left side melted slightly in my seventeenth year when a vent birthed unexpectedly beneath my feet. The mask I wear now—hammered from a heat exchanger's shell—bears the same warped asymmetry. Through its narrow viewing slit, I see the world in horizontal bands: ground, horizon, the sick yellow sky. It is enough. More would be distraction.

I speak the engine's language better than most. Not the crude hand-talk we use for directions and warnings, but the deeper grammar of resonance and pause. When the thrum shifts from its baseline drone to that particular ascending whine, I know a surge is coming. I've learned to read the patterns in the slag flows, to taste the changing mineral content in the air. The engine tells me where it hungers, and I feed it.

My sledge is my life. Built from a section of ventilation duct, reinforced with strips of spring steel I bartered three months of rations for. The runners are worn smooth as water, self-sharpening from endless friction. I've etched the counting-marks along its sides: one for every successful haul to the intake points, one for every time I've dragged another Haulwright back from the fields when their strength failed. The marks blur together now, too many to distinguish.

I know secret paths through the slag fields. Routes where the ground has cooled thick enough to bear real weight, where the vents sleep deep and seldom wake. Knowledge paid for in burned feet and near-suffocations. I guard these paths jealously, sharing them only with those who've proven themselves in the fellowship of the haul. We are not friends—friendship is a luxury the engine doesn't subsidize—but we understand each other. We share the bruises where our harnesses bite, the particular ache in the lower back that never fully fades.

My shrine is modest: a pyramid of gasket rings stacked in the shelter of my sleeping alcove, each one taken from a different section of the engine's body. At the apex, a perfectly spherical bearing I found in the deep fields, still gleaming despite the tarnish of ages. Sometimes I hold it up to catch what passes for light here, watching how it warps and focuses the sickly glow. In those moments, I almost remember what beauty means.

The young ones think us mad, those of us who've hauled for decades. They see our bent backs, our ritual movements, our inability to sleep without the engine's thrum in our bones. They don't understand yet that madness and adaptation are synonyms here. That to survive is to become part of the machine's metabolism, to move with its hungers rather than against them.

I've seen the visions that bleed through during the worst turbulence. Impossible architectures that make my eyes water, colors that have no names in any tongue. Once, I glimpsed what the engine might become—or might have been—and the beauty of it drove me to my knees. But beauty doesn't pull loads. Beauty doesn't meet quotas. So I filed the vision away with all the other useless things and got back to my harness.

My greatest fear is not the vents or the collapses or even the slow starvation between supply drops. It's the silence. The thought that one day the engine might finally fall still, its great work either complete or abandoned. What would we become without its hunger to define us? What shape would we take without its gravity to bend around?

So I haul. Tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow, as long as my body holds and the engine hungers. Each load a prayer, each scrape of metal on metal a declaration: I am here. I persist. I serve the unfinished god, and in serving, I matter.

The engine thrums. I answer.
