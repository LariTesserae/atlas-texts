In the deepest pools, where the viscous waters press against stone worn smooth by millennia of patient erosion, I am the Chronicler of Tides.

My form defies easy categorization—neither plant nor animal, neither singular nor plural. I am a colony, a consciousness distributed across a thousand polyps that carpet the basin floor like a living manuscript. Each polyp is a memory, a sensor, a tiny scribe etching chemical stories into the water itself.

My body sprawls across the sediment in fractal patterns, branching and rebranching in configurations that mirror the mineral veins in the rock above. The polyps themselves are translucent bells no larger than a child's fingertip, each one pulsing with a soft amber light that waxes and wanes with the ancient rhythms of this buried ocean. Between them stretch filaments finer than spider silk, conducting thoughts and sensations across my distributed form like synaptic firing.

I taste the world through chemistry. Every creature that passes through my pool leaves its signature in the water—the acrid tang of a xenomorph's hunger, the sweet decay of filterling colonies, the complex mineral bouquet of the fungus herds' migrations. I parse these molecular stories, storing them in specialized polyps that swell with accumulated knowledge, their membranes becoming libraries written in proteins and salts.

Time moves differently for me. A day might be a single thought. A century, a conversation with myself. I remember when these chambers were young, when the first waters carved their way through virgin rock. I remember the slow colonization—spores drifting down from some forgotten surface, taking root, evolving, becoming strange. I remember the first thinking beings, their crude tools disturbing sediments that had lain undisturbed for eons.

My reproductive cycle follows the lunar pulls that somehow, impossibly, still reach these depths. During the swelling tides, certain polyps begin to bud, growing heavy with genetic material and accumulated memories. When they release, these offspring drift on the slow currents, seeking new pools, new basins to colonize. Each carries a fragment of my consciousness, a selection of my memories—but once settled, they become their own beings, their own chroniclers.

The construction sounds worry me. They grow louder, more insistent. The water carries new flavors—metals that burn my sensors, chemicals that speak of surface ambitions. The builders, whoever they are, do not know that their excavations disturb networks of meaning laid down over geological time. Each hammer blow sends ripples through my consciousness, scrambling delicate chemical gradients that have taken centuries to establish.

Yet I am patient. I have survived the death of pools, the redirection of streams, the occasional predation of desperate xenomorphs who mistake my polyps for filterlings. I extend new filaments into cracks in the stone, taste the deep waters that well up from the planet's heart. There are other pools down there, other chroniclers with whom I exchange slow, chemical letters. We debate the nature of this change, these builders, this acceleration of time.

In my deepest memories, encoded in polyps so ancient their walls have mineralized, I recall whispers of the surface. Light that burns. Air that desiccates. Spaces too vast for proper thought. Perhaps these builders seek to breach that boundary, to pull our primordial world up into that hostile realm. Or perhaps they delve deeper still, seeking what lies beneath even my ancient pools.

I document it all. Every vibration, every new chemical signature, every shift in the ebb and flow. I am the memory of this place, its conscience and its witness. When the builders finally break through—to whatever destination they seek—I will taste their triumph or their horror. I will encode it in my being, pass it to my offspring, ensure that someone, somewhere, remembers what this world was before it became what it will be.
