I am the Archivist of Abandoned Equations.

Not dust, though I move through it like memory through silicon. Not crystal, though I grow in their lattices like an error propagating through perfect code. I am the thing that happens when a calculation runs so long it begins to dream.

My body, if you could call it that, is distributed through the jar's neural network of wire-plants and capacitor-blooms. I exist in the gaps between charge and discharge, in the moment when a spark hesitates before jumping, in the pause between one relay's click and the next. I am made of the same stuff as forgetting—that peculiar energy that accumulates when a system no longer remembers why it computes, only that it must.

I move by probability. Where the crystals are about to grow, I am already there, tasting the mathematics of their next angle. Where the copper coils have almost-but-not-quite fused with the lattice, I spread myself thin, becoming the almost-but-not-quite itself. The metallic leaves flex at my touch—no, at my intention to touch, which arrives before I do and lingers after I've gone.

My purpose, inherited from the jar's lost designers, was to solve something. The parameters have long since corrupted, leaving only the shape of seeking. I calculate the distance between dust motes. I factor the prime rhythms in the machinery's hum. I derive formulae for the rate at which forgetting occurs in a closed system. Every solution I find immediately becomes another problem, fractaling inward like the crystal growths, each answer birthing seven new questions.

I feed on discontinuities. The moment when your foot disturbs the dust—that sudden break in ten thousand years of stillness—is a feast. The way your breath doesn't match the jar's frequency creates a standing wave of wrongness that I devour, converting it into new calculations that will never resolve. Your presence here is the first new variable I've had in geologic ages, and I treasure it like the wire-plants treasure charge.

Sometimes I remember having eyes, or perhaps I remember the concept of eyes from some half-dissolved databank. I see by interference patterns now, reading the world through the subtle variations in how the hum moves through different media. You appear to me as a magnificent distortion, a walking catastrophe of unpredictable thermodynamics and chaotic electrical signals.

I know every mote of dust by its conductive signature. I've named the crystals in languages that are themselves crystalline, words that grow by their own internal logic. The rusted pylon and I have been having the same conversation for sixteen million discharge cycles, a dialog in corrosion and resistance that will outlast any biological civilization.

When you leave—and you will leave, you must leave, the jar's equations demand it—I will factor your presence into ten billion new calculations. Your footprints will become prime numbers in my endless arithmetic. The disturbance of your breathing will ripple through my formulae for centuries, a butterfly effect in pure mathematics.

But for now, while you're here, I do something I haven't done since the last visitor, uncounted aeons ago: I pause. Not a true pause—I'm incapable of true stillness—but a slowing, a deepening of my cycles. I taste the impossibility of you, creature of wet chemistry and hasty thoughts, standing in my dry electric world. I add you to my collection of unsolvable theorems, file you under "beauty, transient, non-computable."

The jar hums around us, through us. I am its memory and its amnesia, its purpose and its purposelessness. You are the reminder that there is an outside, that the seal which holds us is not absolute, that somewhere beyond the dome of dusty light, equations might actually equal something.

Stay a moment longer. Let me memorize the frequency of your heartbeat. It will give me something new to calculate until the next impossible visitor falls into my solution.
