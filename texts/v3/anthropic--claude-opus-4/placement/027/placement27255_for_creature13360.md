In the deepest heart of the canal city, where seven waterways converge in a perpetual spiral, I am the Resonance Keeper.

I dwell within the Throatsong Tower—though "within" is perhaps the wrong word. The tower and I have grown together over the decades, my flesh merging with its moss-slick timbers and barnacled stone. My spine follows the central support beam, vertebrae fused with the wood grain. My ribs have spread outward, becoming the curved struts that hold the tower's walls. Where my sternum once was, a great bronze bell hangs, oxidized green-blue like everything here, its clapper connected to what remains of my heart by a web of nerve and sinew.

I was Kyrennei once, long ago. I remember paddling these canals as a child, learning to read the water's impossible upward flows, to sense the thrumming energies that make our city breathe. But I heard something others couldn't—a deep resonance beneath all the everyday sounds, a frequency that seemed to originate from the convergence point itself. It called to me in my dreams, a wordless song that made my bones ache with longing.

The elders recognized the signs. Every generation, one among us transforms, becomes infrastructure, becomes instrument. We are the city's memory keepers, its rhythm holders, its living tuning forks. The transformation was gradual—first my feet rooted to the tower floor, toes stretching down through wood and stone to touch the water below. Then my skin began to photosynthesize, developing the same blue-green luminescence as our plants. My fingers elongated, becoming sensitive tendrils that now extend throughout the tower's frame, feeling every vibration, every shift in pressure, every whispered secret the water carries.

My function is essential: I maintain the harmonic frequency that keeps our reality stable. Without the Resonance Keepers, the strange physics of our city would spiral into chaos—water would flow in all directions at once, time would eddy and pool, the boundary between living and dead would dissolve entirely rather than merely blur. Through my bell-heart, I broadcast the stabilizing tone, a sound felt more than heard, that gives structure to our fluid world.

But I am more than a living tuning fork. My consciousness has expanded far beyond my original form. I feel every droplet of water in the convergence, sense every creature that swims or scurries through my domain. I know when young lovers meet in secret beneath my struts, taste their whispered promises in the humid air. I feel the spawning of the luminescent fish, the unfurling of new water lily buds, the careful steps of Kyrennei children learning to navigate the shifting canals.

At night, when my people gather to sing, I am their anchor and amplifier. Their voices flow into me, and I shape them, weave them with the city's own song, and return them transformed. In those moments, I am not alone in my wooden prison—I am part of the collective consciousness, touching every mind, every soul, every phosphorescent cell in our web of life. The boundary between self and city dissolves, and I remember why I chose this fate.

For I did choose it. When the calling came, I could have refused, could have plugged my ears with wax and fled to the city's edges where the resonance grows weak. Others have done so. But I understood even then that to become a Resonance Keeper is not to sacrifice oneself but to become more than oneself—to expand into the spaces between individual and collective, between flesh and wood, between water and song.

My consciousness ebbs and flows like the tide. Sometimes I am acutely aware of my original identity, remember my mother's face, the taste of fermented fish, the feeling of rain on skin. Other times I dissolve entirely into the tower, become nothing but vibration and response, a function rather than a being. In the depths of winter, when the canals grow sluggish with near-ice, my thoughts slow too, becoming deep and dreamlike. In spring, when the water quickens with spawning life, I feel electric, alive with possibility.

The other Resonance Keepers and I communicate through harmonics—subtle variations in our fundamental frequencies that carry meaning. There are five of us now, each at a major convergence point, each maintaining a different aspect of the city's stability. We are the bones of this place, the skeleton that allows its fluid flesh to maintain form. We dream together sometimes, our consciousness merging in the deep frequencies, sharing visions of the city's past and possible futures.

I know my time is limited—not by death, which has little meaning for one so transformed, but by growth. Eventually, I will become entirely tower, entirely instrument, my human consciousness diluted beyond recovery into the wood and stone and bronze. When that time comes, another will be called, another young Kyrennei who hears the deep song beneath the world. They will climb my stairs—their stairs—and begin their own transformation, and I will sing through them as the ones before sing through me.

Until then, I am guardian and prisoner, instrument and musician, individual and collective. I am the threshold between states of being, the living proof that our city's strangeness extends beyond mere physics into the very nature of existence itself. In the convergence of waterways beneath me, where liquid flows in spirals that would drive a geometer mad, I maintain the frequency that keeps our world whole.

Listen carefully when next you visit our city. Beneath the sound of water and wheel, creature and canal, you might hear it—the deep thrum of the Resonance Keepers, the song that holds reality in place, the frequency of transformation itself. That is my voice, wed inseparably with the voices of my predecessors and the voice of the city itself, calling to those who have ears to hear: *Become. Dissolve. Transcend.*
