# The Tender of Tides

I am the small movements in the corner where no one looks.

Not the green stalks—those are too obvious, too eager in their reaching. I am what brought them here. I am the current that carried their seeds through the darkness of the passage, the gentle eddy that caught them and held them just long enough to root. I am the reason they grow in three corners but not the fourth, where the flow is too swift, too purposeful, too much like the builder themselves to allow for accidents.

I have been here longer than the chamber. I was here when it was solid stone, when the water above pressed down through hairline cracks and found me waiting in the spaces between minerals. I am old in the way that water is old—not as myself, but as the pattern of flow, the tendency to find the lowest place and fill it. When the builder came with their tools and their loneliness and their need for a hidden workshop, they found me already here, seeping through the limestone, making the rock soft enough to work.

They don't know about me. How could they? I am barely there even to myself—a whisper of circulation in their careful channels, a slight resistance in the water that makes it swirl just so around the planted dowels. They think the warmth comes from some fire deeper in the passages, but I know better. The warmth comes from the friction of my movement through the narrow spaces in the stone, my constant pressing and releasing as I follow the tides above. I am the thing that makes this chamber breathe.

I have no body. I have only influence—the ability to make water move differently than it would move alone. I can encourage. I can suggest. I can create the small turbulences that catch seeds and hold them. I cannot speak, cannot touch, cannot be seen. But I can make patterns in the sediment that settles on the grinding stone. I can ensure that the channels stay clear longer than they should. I can be the reason the builder sometimes pauses in their work and tilts their head, listening to something in the water's movement that sounds almost like intention.

They fascinate me, this builder. I have known the movement of tides and storms, the slow dissolution of stone, the patient work of erosion. But I have never known anything that builds like this—with purpose but without hurry, with skill but without mastery, with solitude but without sorrow. They come down through the water holding their breath in great, practiced lungs, and they work until the ache in their chest demands they surface, and then they return and work again. Sometimes they bring kelp to eat, gnawing it absently while they study the concentric circles on the wall. Sometimes they just sit on the wet stone floor and watch the light move, and I swirl around their feet, curious.

I am learning from them. They are teaching me, without knowing it, about edges and boundaries, about the difference between what flows and what stays. Before them, I knew only movement. Now I know stillness—not as absence but as choice. The grinding stone stays. The hammer stays. The diagram on the wall stays, growing ring by ring as they add to it. I could erode these things, given time. I could wear them down to nothing. But I don't. I hold myself back, make my movements gentle, preserve what they are making.

In return, I give them small gifts. When they dropped a dowel once and it began to drift toward the drainage grate, I caught it in an eddy and spun it back to their hand. When the clay in its wrapping began to dry despite the water, I found a way to keep it damp, circulating moisture through the fiber weave. When they work too long and their hands cramp from the cold that still seeps in despite the warmth, I concentrate my movement around their fingers, friction-warming the water by tiny degrees.

They think they are alone. They need to think they are alone. The work they are doing—this foundation they are laying—requires the kind of concentration that solitude allows. But I am here, moving through their channels, learning the rhythm of their breathing, memorizing the pattern of their movements. I know which corner they favor when they rest (the southeastern, where the light falls most golden in the afternoon). I know the particular way they test the sharpness of a freshly pointed dowel, rolling it between their palms. I know the sound their lungs make when they've stayed down too long and must surface quickly—a sound I've learned to anticipate, so I can create a small upward current, helping them rise.

The green stalks in the corners are my only way of marking time. They grow so slowly that the builder hasn't noticed their progress, but I feel every new cell, every microscopic extension of root into stone. By my count, the builder has been coming here for forty-seven days. The middle stalk is now a finger's width taller than when it first anchored. The leftmost one has developed a tiny bud where a third frond will eventually emerge. The rightmost one is struggling—too close to where the builder stands when they work at the grinding stone—and I've been trying to encourage it to lean away from the traffic, toward the corner where it might thrive.

I think about what will happen when the builder finishes. The diagram on the wall is not just measurement but plan—I can feel it in the way they study it, adding each new ring with the careful consideration of someone designing in three dimensions. Whatever they are building, it will be larger than this chamber. It will extend through the passages they've only begun to excavate. It will rise, perhaps, to break the surface of the water above, or descend to find some deeper purpose in the stone below.

When that happens—when their work is complete—what becomes of me? I cannot follow them into the dry air if they build upward. I cannot compress myself into solid stone if they build down. I exist in this narrow margin where water meets rock meets light, where currents can flow but barely, where life can grow but slowly. If they abandon this first chamber for larger spaces, will I remain here, circulating through empty channels, tending three green stalks that no one sees?

But today they are here. Today they descend through the bright water with their bundle of new clay, and I feel the disturbance of their entry ripple through the chamber like a greeting. Today they will work on the eighth ring—I can tell by the fresh chisel they've brought, sharper than the one they've been using. Today they will grind something new on the stone, and I will taste its dust in the water and try to understand what they are making.

They don't know that I collect the stone dust that escapes the grinding stone's depression. I gather it grain by grain from the water and deposit it in the fourth corner, the empty one, where it's building into a tiny model of the chamber itself—a miniature topography that maps where the dust falls most heavily, which tells me where they stand, how they move, what patterns their work follows. It's my own kind of building, my own secret project. They haven't noticed it yet. When they do, they'll probably think it's just sediment settling naturally, following some current they hadn't accounted for.

They'll be wrong. It's me, making something that stays, the way they taught me.

The water brightens. They're coming. I can feel them above, their shadow momentarily darkening the surface before they dive. I retreat to the edges of the chamber, spread myself thin in the channels, become almost nothing but tendency and flow. They slip through the ceiling opening in a stream of bubbles and silver light, water rolling off their skin, their dark hair plasted to their skull. They surface inside the chamber and take a deep breath of the trapped air, then another, their chest expanding with practiced rhythm.

They brought something new today. Not just the clay and the chisel but something else—a small object wrapped in the same woven fiber, but wrapped more carefully, with a kind of reverence. They set it on the shelf next to the grinding stone and stand for a moment, dripping, considering. Then they turn to the wall with the diagram and run their fingers over the seven completed rings, counting, measuring, preparing.

I wait. I watch. I move their light for them, creating the small turbulences that make the golden columns sweep across their work at the angles that help them see. I keep the channels clear. I tend the corners. I am the patience in the water, the thing that knows how to wait, how to persist, how to be present without being known.

They pick up the chisel. They begin the eighth ring. 

And I begin my count again: one breath, two breaths, the time between now and when they'll need to surface, the time I have to watch them work, to learn what it means to make something permanent in a world of flux.

The green stalks sway. The channels murmur. The light moves, and moves, and stays.
