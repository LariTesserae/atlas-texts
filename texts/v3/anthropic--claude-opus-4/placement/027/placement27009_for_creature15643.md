In this roaring darkness, I am the memory-keeper of stone.

My body has grown into the cave as much as I inhabit it. Where others see my hunched form draped in woven moss and mud-caked hide, I feel the accumulated weight of countless tellings pressing through my bones. My joints ache with the same patient erosion that carved these chambers. The circlet of twisted roots upon my brow has fused with my scalp—I can no longer tell where the cave's growth ends and mine begins.

I carry the staff, its wood so saturated with moisture it bends rather than breaks, the feathers at its crown gone gray and slick. Each morning—though morning means nothing here—I feel for new cracks in its surface with fingertips that have memorized every ridge. This staff has passed through seventeen holders before me. I know their names not as words but as pressures in my palm, each grip wearing its own hollow in the wood.

The others come to me when the roar shifts. They cluster at the edges of my vision, their pale eyes catching fungal gleam, waiting for me to interpret. I press my ear to stone, feeling vibrations through my skull, tasting the pitch of water's fury with senses grown strange through generations of listening. Sometimes I hum—a low throb that rides beneath the thunder—and they understand: *safe* or *wait* or *the deep channel turns.*

My dwelling merges with the cave wall itself. Roots have grown through my sleeping mat, pinning me to this place as surely as any vow. The phosphorescent lichen I tend glows strongest here, fed by the oils from my skin where I've trained it to grow along my arms. In the deepest darkness, I become a constellation of faint green stars.

I remember in the old way: not through words but through the arrangement of stones, the braiding of specific fibers, the careful cultivation of fungal gardens that encode our history in their patterns. Here, where a cluster grows in three spirals, I recall the collapse that took the western chambers. There, where the lichen has been trained into parallel lines, I preserve the memory of when we were many, before the roar grew louder and drove half our people deeper, seeking silence that never comes.

The young ones bring me their finds—a new type of pale grub, a stone worn into an unusual shape, a fragment of something that might once have been wooden but has transformed into something between mineral and wood. I examine each with the patience of centuries, comparing it to the catalog in my flesh: the scars where I've tested unknown fungi, the stains on my fingers from grinding new pigments, the small bones I've inserted beneath my skin to remember the skeletons of creatures now extinct in our domain.

My voice, when I use it, comes out water-worn, each word rounded smooth. But more often I speak in the placement of objects: a feather turned just so, a stone balanced against another, my staff's shadow falling across a particular patch of wall. The others read these signs as easily as any script. This is how I tell them of the time before the roar, though none living remember it—how our ancestors descended from a place where the thunder was distant, where light came from something other than fungus and flame.

At night—our eternal night—I sometimes feel the cave dreaming through me. My blood moves with the underground rivers. My breath matches the slow seep of moisture down stone. I am becoming geological, my thoughts settling into sedimentary layers. Each year I move less, speak less, need less. The others bring me water in cupped hands and morsels of salamander flesh, feeding me as they would feed the cave itself.

Sometimes I catch my reflection in the black mirrors of standing water and do not recognize the creature I see: skin gone blue-white and faintly luminous where the lichen has taken hold, eyes clouded to match the pale fog that drifts through our chambers, hair like root systems spreading across my shoulders. I am no longer certain where my memories end and the cave's begin. Perhaps there is no difference. Perhaps there never was.

When my time comes—and I feel it approaching like a change in pressure—another will take up the staff. They will find my body has already begun its transformation, flesh feeding the gardens I've tended, bones settling into new configurations that will puzzle future generations. The circlet will loosen as my skull reshapes itself, ready to crown the next keeper. And they will remember me not as a name but as a quality of listening, a particular way of reading the water's voice, a cluster of lichen that glows just so when the thunder shifts toward danger.

This is the way of keeping: to become indistinguishable from what we preserve. In the roaring dark, memory itself grows geological, and we who tend it slowly transform into landmarks—living stones that hold the shape of all that came before.
