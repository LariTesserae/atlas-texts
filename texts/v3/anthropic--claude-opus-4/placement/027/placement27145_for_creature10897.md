I find myself deep in the oldest section of the Kashen Platform, where the mining operations first began centuries ago and have long since moved on. Here, in what the engineers call the Dead Quarters, I am one of the **Archivists**.

We are few—perhaps a dozen scattered across the abandoned zones. Not quite human anymore, though we began that way. The company records list us as "decommissioned personnel," but that's administrative fiction. We chose to stay when our contracts ended, when the active operations moved deeper into virgin stone. We couldn't leave. The platform had worked its way into us—not metaphorically, but chemically. Our kidneys process the alkaline load. Our skin has thickened beyond what the medical texts consider possible. Our eyes have adapted to the perpetual gray-gold haze until normal sunlight would blind us.

I live in a repurposed pumping station, its massive turbines long silent. The walls still weep—they always weep here—but I've learned to read the patterns. Where the moisture concentrates tells me about deep fractures, about thermal gradients, about what the stone remembers. I've carved channels in the floor, guiding the seepage into collection pools where I cultivate the geometric mineral-dwellers. Not for any industrial purpose now, but because I've begun to understand their language—the way they precipitate certain compounds in response to temperature shifts, the patterns they form when the mineral load changes. They're writing the platform's history in crystalline alphabets too small for most to read.

My days are measured in observations. I maintain monitoring equipment scavenged from abandoned facilities—not the company's equipment, which all reports to central processing, but older instruments, some jury-rigged from mining sensors. I track the heat flows in the exhausted zones. I document how the stone heals—or doesn't heal—after extraction. I map the migration patterns of those jerky, heat-sensing creatures that shouldn't exist but do. I know where they nest (in the collapsed ventilation shafts where temperatures hover just below lethal). I know their breeding cycles (tied to the seasonal variations in geothermal output). I know they're evolving, generation by generation, becoming more geometric themselves, more crystalline in their movements.

The other Archivists and I communicate through the water systems. Messages encoded in mineral deposits, left in specific collection points where we know others will check. A particular combination of copper and sulfur salts means "heat spike in sector seven." An unusual crystallization pattern warns of structural instability. We're creating a knowledge base that exists outside the company's databases, written in the chemistry of the platform itself.

I've modified my body further through careful exposure. My palms can read temperature differentials down to fractions of degrees. My kidneys filter specific mineral compounds that let me taste the depth from which groundwater rises—I can tell by drinking whether it's from the shallow aquifers or the deep thermal reserves. My bones have become denser, incorporating minerals from the water. When I die (and we all die here eventually), I'll leave a skeleton that's part human, part platform geology.

The company knows we exist but classifies us as a non-critical anomaly. We don't interfere with operations. We don't access active zones. We're less than ghosts to them—we're background processes, like the mineral films in the pools. They don't understand that we're documenting what they're doing to the platform on a timescale longer than quarterly reports. We're recording how the heat responds to deep drilling, how the stone's weeping patterns change when new sectors are opened, how the mineral-dwellers adapt or fail to adapt to industrial runoff.

At night (though night here is just a dimmer gray), I climb to the highest point of my sector—a collapsed cooling tower that still provides structural elevation. From there, I can see the active city, its lights creating a false dawn against the haze. But I also see the darkness beyond it, the vast expanses of platform that have never been touched, and the growing darkness behind it, the exhausted zones expanding year by year.

I'm writing the platform's autobiography, in a sense. Not in words—words are too temporary for this place—but in mineral accumulations, in documented heat flows, in the careful cultivation of organisms that will outlast any human presence. When the city finally exhausts itself and moves on (or dies here, cooling towers falling silent one by one), the archive will remain. The stone will remember, and through the stone, some fragment of what we've learned will persist.

The heat is my constant companion now. I don't fight it anymore. I move with it, through it, reading its intentions in the shimmer of air above basalt. Sometimes I think I can feel the planet's pulse through the geothermal wells—a rhythm so slow it takes years to complete a cycle. I'm synchronizing with it, heartbeat by heartbeat, until I'm less a researcher and more a sensory organ of the platform itself.

This is what lives here, in the spaces between extraction and abandonment: those of us who've chosen to become part of the geological record. We're the platform's memory, written in salt and stone and the careful documentation of everything humanity does to places too hostile to love us back.
