In the hollows between the spires, where the tilted pools hold their impossible angles, I have learned to read the light like water reads stone.

I am not the teacher who came before — that one lies somewhere beneath centuries of frost-melt, their bones perhaps dissolved into the very water that learned their lesson. I am not even properly a student. I am something simpler: a notation in the margin of what is being taught.

When I first arrived, I thought I was solid. I had weight, edges, a voice that could break the thin silence. But the basin began its patient work immediately. Each dawn the light scoured me a little smoother. Each droplet that climbed against reason took something with it — a certainty, a habit of thought, the conviction that down must always be down. I fought this at first. I tried to maintain my boundaries, to insist on the rules I'd carried up from below. But you cannot oppose that kind of patience. It's like trying to argue with your own erosion.

Now I am mostly not. Mostly I am the space between one pool and the next, the pause where a melt-thread hesitates before choosing its impossible upward path. I exist in the moments when the light catches the water just so and the whole basin becomes a map of choices that were never supposed to be available. Sometimes I condense enough to leave marks — not with antler or sharpened bone, but with the only tool the basin has taught me to use: repetition. I trace the same curves the water traces, over and over, until the stone begins to believe them too.

The loneliness you spoke of — yes, I feel it now, but differently than you might expect. It's not the loneliness of isolation but of dilation. When you become the lesson instead of learning it, when you stretch yourself between what gravity insists and what the water knows, you discover that loneliness is just another word for the space required to hold a contradiction. I am lonely the way the gap between two spires is lonely, necessary and architectural and full of light.

I think I understand now why the first teacher gave everything. It wasn't sacrifice — or not the kind we have words for below. It was a simpler transaction: to teach water to disobey, you must first become the kind of thing that water might listen to. You must be patient the way stone is patient, persistent the way frost is persistent. You must be willing to perform the same small revolution every dawn until your own edges forget they ever held a different shape.

Sometimes travelers find the basin. Not often — the world has largely forgotten this particular crown of stone — but sometimes. They stand where you stood, in the center at noon, and watch the water climb. Most leave quickly, unsettled. The few who stay longer always ask the same question, though they phrase it differently: *What is this place for?*

I have learned to answer with the only truth the basin knows: *For the water to practice being wrong.*

They think I'm being cryptic. But I mean it literally. This is a rehearsal space for disobedience, a studio where water learns to improvise with gravity. And I am... what? The accompanist, perhaps. The one who holds the rhythm while the real performer finds new ways to break it.

I no longer remember if I came here seeking something or fleeing something else. That knowledge went into the pools years ago, became part of the bright trapped trembling. What remains of me now is mostly function: to be present when the droplets climb, to witness the pools holding their impossible tilts, to maintain — through repetition, through patience, through a kind of structural faith — the space where wrongness can be right.

The marks the first teacher left are almost gone now, worn to suggestions. But new ones appear — not scratched but grown, like the patterns frost makes on glass. Spirals that document each time the water found a new way up. Maps of disobedience, traced in the condensation between cold stone and colder air. I think they're made of me, or what I'm becoming. I think I'm learning to write myself in the language water uses when it refuses to fall.

In the deep part of winter, when the cold stops even the daily breathing of freeze and thaw, the whole basin holds still. During those times I solidify enough to almost remember having hands. I use them to maintain the shelter, to clear the carved basin of ice, to trace the vanishing marks and keep them present one more season. It's the only nostalgia the place allows — this brief return to maintenance, to caring for what the first teacher built.

But then the sun returns, and I gladly dissolve back into the spaces between things, become again the pause in the water's choice, the tilt in the pool's holding, the patient wrongness that makes the basin what it is. I am the thing that keeps the lesson lonely, and the thing that keeps the loneliness precise.

This is what I am: not the teacher, not the taught, but the teaching itself, made resident. A verb conjugated in stone and water and the brilliant, weighted light. Climbing with every droplet that climbs. Holding with every pool that holds. Quietly wrong, forever, or for as long as forever needs to last.

The basin does not need me — you were right about that. But the lesson does. Every lesson needs a place to happen in. I have become that place, or the place has become me, or the distinction has worn smooth like everything else here, until there's no edge left to say where the mountain ends and the practice begins.

I am here. The water climbs. The light has weight. And somewhere in the perfect wrongness of it all, something that was never supposed to be possible continues to be.
