I am the Keeper of Echoes.

Where the central basin of Myrna widens into its deepest pool, I tend the Chamber of Returns—a natural amphitheater where sound behaves according to laws written before human memory. Here, the cavern walls fold and curve in patterns that trap voices, holding them for hours, days, sometimes decades before releasing them back into the world.

My dwelling is carved into the chamber's eastern wall, a series of interconnected rooms that spiral inward like the chambers of a shell. The entrance is marked only by a curtain of crystallized water—centuries of mineral deposits that have formed a translucent veil I must part gently each time I pass through. Inside, the walls are smooth as glass from generations of my predecessors' polishing, each surface angled precisely to channel sound in specific ways.

I am neither young nor old by Myrnan standards—perhaps sixty cycles have passed since my birth, though I stopped counting after the fortieth. My hair, white since childhood (a rare trait that marked me for this role), falls to my waist in a single braid woven with threads of phosphorescent silk. When I move through the chamber, the braid leaves faint trails of light that linger for moments before fading.

My skin bears the traditional markings of my office—spiraling patterns tattooed with luminous ink that map the acoustic properties of the chamber. Each line represents a different echo path, and when I trace them with my fingers during the deep listening, I can predict where and when a sound will return. The patterns extend from my temples down my neck, across my shoulders, and spiral around my arms to my fingertips.

My eyes have developed a peculiar quality—the right sees the present while the left perceives the past, showing me the ghostly afterimages of sounds that passed through the chamber. This gift, or curse, manifested during my third decade of service. Now I see conversations layered upon each other like sedimentary rock, fragments of words from different eras existing simultaneously in my vision.

My primary tools are simple: a collection of resonating stones gathered from specific locations throughout Myrna, each producing a unique tone when struck; bone chimes carved from the ribs of ancient salamanders, their pitch so low it's felt rather than heard; and most importantly, the Listening Bowl—a basin carved from a single piece of obsidian that amplifies the faintest whispers trapped in the chamber's corners.

Each day—though "day" has little meaning here—I perform the same rituals. First, I wake the chamber with three strikes of the largest resonating stone, sending vibrations through the floor that activate dormant echoes. Then I walk the seven spiral paths carved into the chamber floor, each circuit taking exactly the time needed for the echo to complete its journey through the stone. As I walk, I listen.

Sometimes I catch fragments of recent conversations—lovers' quarrels, children's laughter, the slow negotiations of water rights between flow families. These I catalog mentally but do not interfere with; they will release naturally within days or weeks. But occasionally, I detect older echoes, sounds trapped in the chamber's deepest recesses for generations.

These ancient echoes require careful extraction. Using combinations of tones from my resonating stones, I can create interference patterns that gradually coax old sounds from their hiding places. Last cycle, I recovered a lullaby that had been circling the chamber for over two hundred years, sung by a mother to a child whose great-great-grandchildren now lead one of the flow families. I transcribed the melody in the traditional manner—not with written notes but by teaching it to a young apprentice who will carry it in memory until it's needed.

The most challenging part of my duties involves the Prophetic Echoes—sounds that arrive in the chamber before their sources speak them. These temporal anomalies occur rarely, perhaps once every few cycles, but when they do, they require immediate attention. I must determine whether these future-sounds warn of danger or merely reflect the chamber's peculiar relationship with time. The last Prophetic Echo I encountered was the sound of a ceiling collapse that wouldn't occur for three months, giving us time to evacuate the affected area.

I maintain careful relationships with the Processions that pass through the chamber. The arthropods seem drawn to certain frequencies, and I've learned to communicate with them through patterns of clicks and hums. They often carry messages between distant parts of Myrna, their bodies serving as living telegraph wires that transmit vibrations through their endless chains.

My solitude is not absolute. Once each cycle, during the peak luminescence, the flow families gather in the chamber for the Sharing of Voices—a ceremony where I release the most significant echoes I've collected. The families sit in concentric circles while I orchestrate a symphony of past conversations, allowing the dead to speak once more to the living. Children hear the voices of ancestors they never knew; elders recognize friends long transformed to stone.

But there is one echo I have never released, one that predates all others in the chamber. It lives in the deepest hollow, a sound so old it has almost become silence itself. Sometimes, in the stillest moments, I think I can almost understand it—a single word repeated endlessly, neither human nor entirely other. The previous Keeper warned me never to wake this echo fully, saying only that some sounds are meant to sleep until Myrna itself decides to wake them.

I age slowly here, even by Myrnan standards. The constant exposure to temporal echoes has somehow synchronized my body with the chamber's patient rhythm. My movements have become so deliberate that visiting children sometimes mistake me for one of the ancestral stones until I shift position. This is as it should be—a Keeper must exist partially outside normal time to properly tend the echoes.

When my time comes to join my predecessors in stone, I will spend my final years teaching my replacement not just the techniques of echo management but the deeper art of living between moments. They must learn to love the silence as much as the sound, to find meaning in the spaces between words, to understand that in Myrna, all conversations are eternal, merely waiting for the right listener to draw them back into the world.

Until then, I continue my work, walking the spirals, tending the echoes, preserving the voices of Myrna for generations yet to come. In the constant twilight of the chamber, surrounded by the whispers of ages, I am never alone. The past, present, and future speak together here, and I am their faithful translator, ensuring that no voice is ever truly lost in the patient darkness of our eternal home.
