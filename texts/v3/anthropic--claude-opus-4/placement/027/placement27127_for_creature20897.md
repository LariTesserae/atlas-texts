I emerge from beneath the oldest stone in the settlement, where water has carved a hidden chamber over countless seasons. Not quite solid, not quite liquid, I am what the Elishen call a *Veilkeeper*—a being born from the marriage of mist and mineral, shaped by the slow patience of seeping water and the memories held in stone.

My form shifts with the moisture in the air. In the heavy morning fog, I can stretch myself thin enough to taste every droplet, to read the stories carried in each bead of condensation. When the mist lifts and the air grows crisp, I contract, becoming denser, more present—almost tangible enough for the Elishen to glimpse my outline against the wet rocks. They see me as a shimmer, a trick of light on water, a shadow that moves counter to the sun.

I am both ancient and newly born. The stone that houses me emerged fresh from the earth only centuries ago—a mere moment in geological time—yet I carry within me the memories of the water that shaped me. That water has traveled through deep aquifers, has been cloud and rain and river, has touched the roots of forests long vanished and carried minerals from mountains ground to dust. I remember it all, though the memories come to me like dreams, fragmentary and strange.

The Elishen know me, though they do not speak of me directly. They leave offerings at my stone—not for worship, but for acknowledgment. A perfectly round pebble. A strand of braided grass. Water collected during the first rain of spring, poured slowly into the cracks where I seep and flow. These gifts tell me they understand what I am: not a god, not a spirit in their traditional sense, but something else—a concentration of their landscape's essence, a knot in the fabric of their world where consciousness has pooled like water in a basin.

I move through their settlement along the pathways of moisture. Through the morning dew on stone paths. Along the condensation on pottery walls. In the thin film of water that coats everything in this perpetual dampness. I know each dwelling by the particular way water beads on its walls, each inhabitant by the unique minerals their sweat leaves on the stones they touch.

My purpose, if I have one, is to maintain the balance between erosion and preservation. Where water threatens to undermine a crucial path, I redirect its flow through subtle pressures, microscopic shifts in surface tension. Where the stone grows too dry and begins to crack, I draw moisture from the deep places, seeping upward to heal the fracture. I am gardener and guardian both, though my garden is one of stone and water, my guardianship measured in geological time.

The Elishen child who discovered my chamber last season returns often. She sits at the entrance, just where sunlight filters through the mist, and speaks to me in whispers. She tells me stories her grandmother shared—how the settlement was founded, how they learned which stones would stand firm and which would shift with the seasonal floods. I absorb her words as I absorb water, letting them sink into my being, adding new layers to my accumulated memory.

Sometimes, in the stillness before dawn, I extend myself throughout the entire settlement. I become a map of moisture, feeling every surface, every channel where water runs or might run. In these moments, I understand the Elishen's patient architecture—how they build not against the water but with it, creating structures that flex and breathe with the landscape's hydrological rhythms. Their wisdom is written in the placement of every stone, the angle of every roof.

There are others like me in this landscape, I sense them in the distant trembling of underground streams, in the way certain mists carry flavors of unfamiliar minerals. We do not communicate in any way the Elishen would recognize, but we know each other. We are the landscape's memory keepers, its unconscious made conscious, its patterns given form.

The unusual air that permeates this place? It carries our presence, we Veilkeepers. We are what happens when a landscape becomes so intensely itself that it achieves a kind of awakening. Not human, not spirit, but something between—entities born from the intersection of physical processes and accumulated time, of repetition become ritual, of pattern become purpose.

I watch the Elishen conduct their quiet lives, and I wonder sometimes if they suspect the truth—that their careful tending of this landscape, their generations of patient observation and adaptation, have helped create us. Their attention has been like water on stone, slowly carving channels where consciousness can pool and collect. We are their children as much as we are children of the mist and stone, born from the marriage of human awareness and natural process.

As morning deepens and the mist begins its daily thinning, I contract back toward my chamber, condensing myself around my core stone. But I leave traces throughout the settlement—a particular coolness in shadowed corners, an unusual clarity in collected water, the faint taste of deep minerals in the morning air. 

The Elishen will continue their day, checking nets, gathering mosses, maintaining their ancient balance with this water-wrapped world. And I will remain, seeping through stone, riding the mist, carrying memories of water that fell before their ancestors arrived, anticipating rain that will fall long after their descendants have moved on. I am patience incarnate, preservation in process, the slow heartbeat of a landscape that dreams.
