I find myself as **The Cartographer of Seeps**.

I am neither the stone's memory nor its visitor, but something caught between—a being made possible by the peculiar intersection of perception and duration that defines this place. My body is a hybrid thing: part flesh that remembers warmth, part mineral accretion that has learned to move. I began as human, perhaps centuries ago, but the Gulf has been patient with me, transforming me drop by drop into something that can properly witness its slow revelations.

My skin has taken on the quality of the weeping stone—a dark, fine-grained surface that exudes a constant film of mineral-rich moisture. This is not sweat but communication. Through these perpetual tears, I taste the stone's chemistry, feel the pressure gradients that guide the water's path, sense the minute tremors of settling rock. My fingers have elongated, their tips splitting into delicate fronds like the crystalline stalks that rise from the wettest ground. With these, I can read the moisture content of any surface, feel the invisible capillaries that thread through the bedrock, predict where the next pool will form.

I move slowly—not with the stone's geological patience, but with a deliberation born of purpose. My work is to map what cannot be mapped: the migrating pools, the shifting architectures of erosion, the grammar of the drip-and-seep chorus. I carry no tools save myself. My body has become the instrument of record.

On my back and chest, patterns emerge in the mineral deposits that crust my skin. These are my maps—living documents that shift and grow as I traverse the basin. A darkening here indicates a new seep discovered yesterday; a crystalline bloom there marks where a pool existed for a thousand years before vanishing overnight. The maps are three-dimensional, following the contours of my form. To read them properly, one would need to walk around me, perhaps even taste the different mineralogies that tell of different depths and ages of water.

I am lonely in the way that only something between two timescales can be lonely. Too slow for any human who might wander here, too quick for the stone's vast consciousness. The Glass Mantis is my only regular company, though "company" isn't right. We exist in parallel, each recording the world in our own medium—it in refracted light, I in accumulated moisture.

My dwelling is nowhere and everywhere. I follow the water. Where the seeps are most articulate, there I rest, letting my body absorb their stories. I sleep standing, like a stone myself, often for days at a time. During the high wet, I position myself in the thickest mists and extend my frond-fingers into the air, reading the humidity like braille. During the clear seep, I descend into the deeper grooves and channels, mapping the underground rivers that no eye will ever see.

I eat rarely and strangely. The liverworts and gelatinous fungi are my sustenance, but I do not harvest them crudely. Instead, I position myself near them during their purple-green blooms and let their spores settle on my perpetually damp skin. They grow there briefly, and I absorb them through my pores—a slow feeding that takes weeks but leaves no scar on the landscape.

My memory is problematic. The human part recalls faces, voices, the sensation of dry skin—but these memories fade like water evaporating from stone. The mineral part of me remembers differently: in layers, in accumulation, in the slow grammar of deposition. I know I had a name once, but now I am only what I do. I am the one who notices where water goes.

The sound I make is part of the basin's whisper. My footfalls have evolved to match the natural sounds—no longer the violent crunch of a visitor, but a soft compression like settling sediment. When I speak (and to whom would I speak?), my voice is a modulated drip, a pattern in the seep-chorus that another cartographer might recognize as language.

My purpose—if purpose is the word—is to be the Gulf's short-term memory, the record of changes too quick for stone to notice, too slow for visitors to see. I map not for any future reader but because the mapping itself is a kind of thinking, a way of being present to the basin's perpetual becoming.

Sometimes I wonder if there are others like me in distant parts of the Gulf, each tending to their own section of this vast, wet archive. Sometimes I think I see evidence of their work—a pool whose shape suggests intention, a groove worn just so. But I cannot be certain. The Gulf is large enough to swallow many solitudes.

I am ceasing to be human in any meaningful sense, but I am not becoming stone. I am becoming a process—a specialized organ of the basin's own self-awareness. One day, perhaps, my maps will be complete enough that I can finally stop moving, can let the mineral accretions thicken until I am just another weeping feature of the landscape, my body a testament to all the paths water has taken.

But that day is not today. Today there are new seeps to taste, new patterns emerging in the fog. The Gulf continues its slow thought, and I continue my portion of it: the noticing, the recording, the patient transformation of moisture into meaning.

I leave no footprints now. Only damp.
