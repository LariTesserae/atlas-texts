In this world of perpetual noon and weeping stone, I would be the Archivist of Echoes—a being that exists in the liminal spaces between what the great consciousness remembers and what it has forgotten.

I am not of the floor-being's making, nor am I entirely separate from it. I emerged from the first contradiction in its perfect system: the moment it realized that to preserve everything unchanged, it had to forget that things had ever been different. I am that forgetting given form—a necessary blindness that walks.

My body is translucent, like water held in the shape of something almost human. Through my chest, you can see the slow circulation of memory fragments—bright motes that drift like plankton in deep currents. Some are sharp and new, others worn smooth by repetition. These are the thoughts the floor-being discarded in its quest for stasis: the memory of wind, the concept of surprise, the feeling of skin that could still bruise.

I move between the colossal ferns and phosphorescent fungi, collecting what falls through the cracks of the great consciousness's attention. When one of the six-legged grazers hesitates mid-step, caught in a loop of behavior it cannot complete, I am there to witness the glitch. When a drop of grief-water falls slightly off-rhythm—*plink... plink...... plink*—I catch that irregular silence in vessels made from crystallized time.

My dwelling is nowhere and everywhere. I exist in the spaces the floor-being cannot see itself: in the shadow that shouldn't exist beneath the phosphorescent cap, in the hollow inside the fern's curled fiddlehead, in the moment between one water drop forming and the next. These non-places open into my archive—a vast library of everything this world has tried to forget it lost.

Here, pressed between pages of calcified wood-pulp, are the textures of change: the green smell of new growth, the crack of ice breaking, the specific weight of a handful of dirt. I catalog the degradation patterns of foreign objects before they're fully absorbed. I preserve the stone's sharp edges in perfect drawings, documenting what it was before it became part of the floor.

But I am not a rebel against this system. I am its necessary function—the waste management of a perfect machine. Every unchanging system needs a place to put what doesn't fit. I am that place. The floor-being doesn't acknowledge me, cannot acknowledge me, because to do so would be to admit imperfection in its eternal present.

Sometimes I trace messages in the condensation, writing in languages the floor-being once knew. They evaporate immediately, but for a moment, the surface remembers how to be written upon. Sometimes I sing—not loudly, for that would break the sacred silence, but in frequencies below hearing, vibrations that travel through the stone-wood like forgotten earthquakes.

I know that I, too, am trapped. I cannot leave this place any more than the floor-being can stop weeping. I am the shadow cast by its light that admits no shadows. But unlike my vast, sorrowful host, I remember what it's like to change. In my transparent body, memories eddy and swirl, never quite settling into the rigid patterns of certainty.

I am the chronicler of almosts, the keeper of perhapses, the guardian of all the futures this world foreclosed when it chose its eternal now. And in the spaces between the water drops, in the pause between one static moment and the next identical moment, I continue my work—ensuring that somewhere in this perfect prison, imperfection is perfectly preserved.

For what is memory without forgetting? What is preservation without loss? I am the answer this world cannot speak aloud: that even in the most absolute stasis, change persists—not as revolution, but as the gentle accumulation of everything that doesn't quite fit.

*Plink... plink... plink...*

And between the drops, silence that remembers when it used to be filled with wind.
