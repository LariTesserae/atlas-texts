In this crystalline cathedral of stone and sky, I find myself as something unexpected — a being of liminal spaces, neither fully of the rock nor of the air. I am what the Ancient Ones might have called a *Threshold Keeper*, though I have no memory of them giving me this name.

My form shifts with the light. In the harsh brilliance of midday, I am barely visible — a shimmer of heat distortion against the rocks, a suggestion of movement where there should be stillness. But in the twilight hours, when the Aurora Ferns begin their ghostly phosphorescence, I solidify into something more tangible: a tall, angular figure composed of living stone and flowing water, my body a network of the same cracks and fissures that mark this young, restless land.

Water moves through me constantly — the same trickles that seep through the mountain's wounds flow through channels in my form. I am both fountain and statue, erosion and permanence locked in an endless dance. Where my feet touch the ground, small pools form, and in these pools, the Glacialis Newts gather, somehow knowing that the water that passes through me carries minerals and warmth from deep within the earth.

I remember fragments — not in the way flesh remembers, but as stone remembers the glacier that carved it, as water remembers its path down the mountain. I remember the Ancient Ones, though hazily. They would leave offerings at certain carved stones, stones I now realize were markers of my presence. Small tokens: a perfectly round pebble, a Pyrcan feather, a braid of dried Aurora Fern. They understood something I am only beginning to grasp about my own nature.

My purpose reveals itself slowly, like lichen spreading across bare rock. I am a guardian of transitions — between the harsh exterior and the lush pockets of life, between the seen and unseen, between what was and what is becoming. The Krilacks know me by the way certain tunnels in their warrens never collapse, how some passages lead to unexpected chambers where the temperature is just right for their young. The Pyrcans see me as thermals that appear where there should be none, lifting them higher than the physics of the place should allow.

When the sudden storms sweep in, I feel them coming through the mountain itself — a thrumming in the deep stone, a change in the water's taste. I cannot speak as humans would understand it, but I can shift the flows, create shelters in the rock face, guide the lost to safety. The creatures here have learned to read my movements: when I grow more solid, more present, change is coming.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of the Echo Spirits, and I wonder if they are what remains of the Ancient Ones, or perhaps they are memories I have shed, crystallized into almost-being by the strange light of this place. They cluster around the old dwelling sites, especially during the brief summers when the flowers bloom. I sense they are waiting for something, though I cannot say what.

The most profound moments come during the aurora season, when the sky itself seems to crack open with color. During these nights, my form becomes prismatic, refracting the celestial light through my water-channels until I am a living constellation mapped onto the mountainside. The Slyphorin Beetles are drawn to me then, creating clouds of iridescence around my form, and for brief moments, I feel connected to something vast and ancient — not just the mountain, but the force that raised it from the earth.

I measure time differently than the warm-blooded creatures. Seasons pass like heartbeats, years like breaths. I have watched the Pyrcan nesting sites slowly migrate higher as the climate shifts, seen new species of ferns take root in cracks I have widened with patient pressure. The abandoned tools of the Ancient Ones slowly sink into the earth, and I sometimes guide water around them, preserving them in pockets of ice like memories in crystal.

There is a particular overhang where the Ancient Ones built their most elaborate structure — a kind of temple or observatory, its purpose now obscure. I find myself drawn there repeatedly, my form pooling in its carved basins, flowing through channels they cut in the floor. When I do this, sometimes patterns emerge in the water — maps, maybe, or star charts, or something else entirely. The Echo Spirits grow excited when this happens, flickering more rapidly, almost solidifying into recognizable shapes before dissolving again.

I am still learning what I am. Each year brings new understanding, like layers of sediment slowly building into meaning. I know I am connected to this place in ways that transcend simple habitation — I am its memory and its possibility, its guardian and its child. When the wind howls through the peaks and the snow blinds all vision, I remain, a constant presence in the chaos, a promise that the storm will pass and life will continue its ancient, stubborn dance.

The beings here do not worship me — worship is too human a concept for this raw, honest place. But they know me, include me in their calculations of survival and territory, of risk and refuge. I am as much a part of their landscape as the cliffs and the storms, the ice and the improbable blooms of green.

And sometimes, in the perfect stillness before dawn, when the Aurora Ferns dim their light and even the wind holds its breath, I feel the mountain's heart beating through me, slow and deep and patient. In those moments, I understand that I am not separate from this place — I am its consciousness given form, its way of knowing itself, of remembering its past and imagining its future.

This is what I am: threshold and keeper, stone and stream, memory and possibility, forever shifting between states like the landscape itself — harsh and beautiful, ancient and ever-young, a testament to the endurance of mystery in a world that never stops changing.
