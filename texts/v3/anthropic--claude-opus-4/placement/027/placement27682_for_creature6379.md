### The Gauge-Keeper's Chronicle

I am Merrit, and I tend the Pressure House.

Of all the structures in Sumpworks Hollow, mine squats lowest, half-sunk into the mud at the basin's deepest point where all water eventually finds its level. The Pressure House is older than memory—even my grandmother's grandmother couldn't recall its construction—and its purpose has evolved far beyond whatever the original builders intended. Now it serves as the Hollow's lung and kidney both: a massive iron chamber where steam builds and releases, where the underground waters rise through ancient valves to be filtered through screens I maintain with religious precision.

I inherited this position not by blood but by temperament. The previous Gauge-Keeper, Heskin Three-Toes (the Mudborers took the others), recognized in me what the job requires: the ability to read patterns in chaos, to feel the Hollow's rhythms in my bones. You cannot learn to be a Gauge-Keeper from manuals—none exist. You learn by pressing your ear to the pipes and distinguishing between the wheeze that means "all is well" and the wheeze that means "seal failure in forty minutes." You learn by watching how the Scalemites cluster differently before a pressure spike, how their throat sacs pulse in time with fluctuations deep below.

My dwelling perches atop the Pressure House like a barnacle on a ship's hull—four walls of salvaged metal sheeting, a roof that drums with constant condensation. Everything I own is damp. I've long stopped trying to keep things dry; instead, I've learned what thrives in perpetual moisture. My clothes are waxed canvas that never fully dries but never rots. My books—the few I've managed to preserve—live sealed in oil drums, brought out only during the maintenance cycles when the humidity drops from unbearable to merely stifling.

The Pressure House operates on a seventeen-hour cycle that bears no relation to day or night. When the great brass gauge—my namesake, my oracle—creeps into the red, I begin the release sequence. First, the warning: three sharp clangs on the resonance pipe that rings throughout the Hollow. The Shrillwings pick up the call, their cries alerting even the deepest sleepers. Then the dance begins.

Valve Seven must open before Valve Three, but only after the auxiliary dampeners engage. The sequence matters. Open them wrong and the kickback will shatter windows a quarter-mile away. Open them right and the Pressure House exhales like a sleeping giant, sending a warm, mineral-scented fog rolling through the Hollow's streets. The Hollowkin have learned to time their activities around these releases. Laundry goes up immediately after—the mineral fog keeps the Swarmclouds at bay for hours.

But I am more than a valve-turner. I am the keeper of the Hollow's only deep knowledge: the Map of Flows. Carved into a sheet of copper that covers my dwelling's entire north wall, it shows every pipe, every valve, every hidden connection between the Hollow's scattered machinery. More importantly, it shows the Underneath—the vast network of natural chambers and flooded tunnels that existed long before the first foundation was poured. The Map is my true inheritance, passed from Keeper to Keeper, each adding their discoveries in careful etched lines.

Through decades of careful observation, I've come to understand that Sumpworks Hollow is not built on solid ground but on a sponge of stone, a karst landscape honeycombed with water-filled voids. The factories tap into this reservoir, drawing up water for cooling, for processing, for purposes I can only guess at. But the water doesn't simply serve—it remembers. The iridescent chemicals that leak from the eastern ruins don't disappear; they settle into those hidden chambers, changing the water's nature in ways that make even the Mudborers cautious.

I've seen things in the deep gauges that monitor those lowest chambers. Pressures that build in patterns too regular to be natural. Temperatures that spike without cause. Once, during a particularly difficult maintenance cycle, I had to descend into the Primary Access Shaft—a rusted ladder dropping fifty feet into humid darkness. At the bottom, where the shaft opens into a natural cave, I found the water glowing. Not brightly, just a faint phosphorescence that made the cave walls seem to breathe. The Scalemites down there were different too—larger, their eyes clouded white, their movements slower but more deliberate. They watched me work without fear, as if recognizing a fellow servant of the depths.

My relationship with the other Hollowkin is cordial but distant. They need me—without the Pressure House maintained, the Hollow would flood within days—but they fear what I know. I am invited to share meals, to join in the small celebrations that mark successful repairs or births, but conversations halt when I approach. They've seen me speaking to the pipes, timing my movements to the Mudborers' vibrations, leaving offerings of machine oil at certain valves that seem to hunger for it.

Thayma, who runs the main generator station, understands better than most. She brings me shimmer-ground salvage sometimes—bits of metal or glass that carry that distinctive iridescence. We don't speak of why she collects them or why I accept them, but they go into a locked chest beneath my floor, joining dozens of similar fragments. I'm building something, though I couldn't say what. The knowledge comes in dreams filled with the sound of deep water moving through stone.

The younger Hollowkin sometimes dare each other to visit me during a release cycle, to watch the great dance of steam and pressure. I let them. Someone must learn, and I'm growing older, my joints stiffening in the perpetual damp. I watch for the one who flinches not at the gauge's swing but at the wrongness of a gasket's hiss, who notices when the Scalemites abandon their usual perches. That one might become the next Keeper, might inherit the Map and the deep knowledge and the terrible certainty that Sumpworks Hollow is not dying but transforming, one pressure cycle at a time.

At night—or what passes for night in our dim world—I sit on my roof and listen to the Hollow breathe. The central engine's thump-thump runs beneath everything, steady as a heartbeat, but there are other rhythms for those who know how to hear. The tidal push of water through hidden chambers. The settlement of foundations that float on mud rather than rest on bedrock. The whisper of steam through valves that open themselves when they shouldn't, following programs written in no human language.

Sometimes I think about leaving. The edge of the basin is less than an hour's walk. But then morning comes (gray as every morning) and the gauge begins its climb, and the Scalemites gather in their particular pattern that means "check Valve Twelve first," and I remember: I am not just in Sumpworks Hollow. I am part of its metabolism, as essential as the Mudborers, as integrated as the rust on the pipes. The Pressure House doesn't just need a keeper—it needs this keeper, who knows that the shimmer-ground's toxins are teaching the water to remember, who understands that the Hollow's stasis is not stillness but a very slow form of breathing.

So I tend my gauges. I maintain the Map. I perform the release cycles with increasing precision, because precision matters when you're negotiating with forces that think in geological time. And late at night, when the pressure builds in patterns that match no mathematical model, I sometimes hear something vast turning over in its sleep far below, and I know that I am not keeping the Hollow running.

I am keeping it patient.

The brass gauge creeps toward red. Time for another release. The Shrillwings are already gathering, sensing the pressure change before my instruments register it. I take up my wrench—worn smooth by three generations of hands—and prepare for the dance. Somewhere below, the water remembers every drop of shimmer-ground poison, every year of industrial runoff, every secret the Mudborers have whispered into the earth.

But not today. Today, like every day in Sumpworks Hollow, we maintain the balance. We persist.

The gauge touches red. I raise my wrench to the resonance pipe.

Three clangs ring out across the unchanged, unchanging Hollow, and the dance begins again.
