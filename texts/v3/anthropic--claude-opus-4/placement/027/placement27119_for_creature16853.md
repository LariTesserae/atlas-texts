## The Cartographer of Borrowed Veins

I am the one who draws maps of circulation systems that haven't decided what to circulate yet. My studio is a ventricle, or perhaps a ventriloquist— the distinction blurs when you've spent enough time listening to the plateau's cardiovascular gossip.

I wasn't born here, but then again, I wasn't born anywhere else either. I arrived the way most meanings arrive at their words: sideways, through a crack in certainty, trailing fragments of some other story I'd been failing to tell correctly. The plateau found me trying to explain the color of forgetting to a puddle of rainwater that had never experienced clouds. It laughed— not unkindly, but with the fond exasperation of an ancient thing watching a young fool try to describe swimming to an ocean.

"You're already drowning wrong," it said, and opened a door in itself that was actually a wound that was actually an invitation to stop pretending edges meant anything permanent.

Now I live in the space between systole and diastole, in chambers that rebuild themselves with each heartbeat according to principles I'm still decoding. My body— though 'mine' is a possessive that possession itself would find presumptuous— is a collaborative effort between several competing theories of anatomy. Today I have seventeen hearts arranged in a Fibonacci spiral, each one pumping different translations of the same untranslatable emotion. Tomorrow I might redistribute that cardiac enthusiasm into something more practical: perhaps a single enormous lung that breathes meaning directly, or a network of auxiliary brains to help me think in languages that haven't been disappointed by words yet.

My work is impossible by most standards of possibility, which is why I'm so good at it. I map the unmappable: the circulatory systems of ideas before they congeal into thoughts, the arterial networks of buildings that forgot they were metaphors, the capillary action of stories as they seep through layers of reality looking for characters foolish enough to live them out.

Each map begins the same way— with my fingers (however many I'm currently maintaining) pressed against some pulse point in the plateau's endless anatomy. I listen with my skin, that most honest of organs, as it tells me about pressure differentials and flow rates in languages that would make physicists weep with recognition of something they'd spent their whole careers trying not to remember. Then I begin to draw, using inks made from my own confused blood mixed with whatever the plateau was dreaming about that morning.

The maps themselves refuse to stay still. They're living documents in the most literal sense: documents that wake up, look around, develop strong opinions about their own accuracy, and occasionally stage elaborate protests against the very concept of representation. I've had maps that grew their own circulatory systems just to prove a point about infinite regress. Maps that fell in love with the territories they described and eloped to become entirely new geographical features. Maps that achieved enlightenment and now spend their time as consulting philosophers, teaching young cartographers that the real treasure was the territories we confused along the way.

My most successful failure is the Map of As-Yet-Uncirculated Desires, which described the cardiovascular system of want itself— how yearning moves through bodies that don't know they're bodies yet, the fluid dynamics of almost-having, the blood pressure of pursuit measured in increments of "not quite" and "almost there" and "oh god what if." The map worked too well. It began circulating its own desires through anyone who looked at it, creating feedback loops of wanting to want to want until an entire neighborhood had to be evacuated and treated for acute recursive longing. The map now lives in voluntary exile, teaching a small school of fish-that-used-to-be-philosophers about the importance of clear boundaries in a universe where everything is secretly liquid.

The Resonateds come to me when they need to find something they're not sure exists. Usually it's simple things: the shortest route between two contradictory emotions, or which vessels carry nostalgia from the temporal lobes to the tear ducts on Wednesdays. But sometimes they need maps of harder territories: the circulation of silence through a conversation that hasn't happened yet, the cardiovascular demands of loving someone who exists primarily as punctuation, the exact pathways regret takes when it's trying to get back home but home has moved three metaphors to the left and changed its name to Tuesday.

I map these impossible things by becoming them temporarily. My body reconfigures itself into living diagrams, my blood re-routing itself to trace the exact patterns of whatever paradox needs navigating. It hurts, but in the way that understanding hurts when it arrives all at once— a bright, clean pain that knows exactly what it's doing and promises to explain everything in the morning.

The strangest commission I ever received was from a Resonated who had forgotten how to circulate themselves. They'd become so enthusiastically distributed across multiple probability states that their various components had lost track of which direction was supposed to be forward. They stood in my studio— or rather, they stood, sat, lay, hovered, and existed-in-the-past-tense simultaneously in my studio— and asked me to map "the way back to singular."

I spent seven months as their circulatory system, learning the particular rhythms of how they moved through themselves. Their blood (which was actually harmonized uncertainty moving at body temperature) told stories of when they'd been young and compact, before the first beautiful doubt that made them question the necessity of coherence. I mapped every pathway they'd ever taken toward dissolution, every vessel they'd grown to accommodate one more fascinating way of not quite being themselves.

The map, when I finally drew it, wasn't a map at all. It was a mirror made of flowing blood— their blood, my blood, the plateau's blood all mixed together in proportions that meant something different every time you calculated them. When they looked into it, they saw all their scattered selves flowing back together, not into unity but into a kind of cheerful agreement to disagree less often.

They paid me in the only currency that matters here: a new way of being lost. Now, sometimes, when I'm mapping something particularly impossible, I find myself circulating in directions I didn't know existed. My blood moves through dimensions that weren't there a moment ago, finding pathways that connect things that shouldn't be connected but are anyway because connection itself is just another kind of circulation that hasn't been mapped properly yet.

I live here in the space between heartbeats, drawing impossible maps for impossible beings in an impossible place that insists on existing anyway. My veins are borrowed from the plateau's endless generosity, my arteries are on loan from various philosophical positions about flow and resistance. My heart— all seventeen or one or none of them, depending on the day— beats in time with something vast and patient and amused, something that knows the secret of circulation is that everything is always already arriving where it needs to be, it just takes some of us longer to realize we've been moving the whole time.

The plateau's pulse is my metronome. I map its blood because its blood is mapping me. We're drawing each other into existence, line by flowing line, in inks that dry into new kinds of liquid that remember what it was like to be solid but prefer the honesty of endless motion.

Tomorrow I'll wake up (or continue sleeping more accurately) and find new commissions slipped under my door: requests for maps of circulatory systems that haven't been invented yet, territories of flow and resistance that exist only in the space between one heartbeat and the next. I'll press my fingers (however many I have by then) against some new pulse point in the plateau's endless anatomy, and I'll listen to what moves there, and I'll draw another impossible map that will immediately begin revising itself into something even more impossible.

This is my work. This is my circulation. This is how I move through a world that moves through me, mapping the unmappable and becoming the territory I'm trying to describe, one confused heartbeat at a time.

The maps are never accurate.
They're never meant to be.
They're love letters written in the language of flow to things that haven't learned they're rivers yet.

And somewhere in the plateau's vast circulatory system, there's a map of me— drawn in inks I'll never see, describing pathways I'll never know I'm taking, circulating through territories that are mapping themselves into existence using my blood as their ink, their uncertainty as my canvas, our mutual confusion as the only honest currency in a universe where everything is flowing into everything else and calling it home.
