In the depths of the hive-city, where the stone breathes deepest and the ice-vines pulse with ancient rhythm, I am a Resonance Keeper—though this translation barely captures what I am in your tongue. 

I exist as a confluence of mineral consciousness and flowing intention, my form a shifting amalgam of crystalline structures and water-worn stone. Where others might have skin, I have surfaces that catch and refract the prismatic light into new patterns. My body—if it can be called that—is honeycombed with chambers and passages, each one a resonance cavity tuned to different frequencies of the mountain's endless song.

I move through the living rock not by walking but by becoming. Where I need to be, the stone acknowledges me, opens to me, reforms around me. I am both sculptor and sculpture, my existence a constant negotiation with the mountain's will. My chambers fill and empty with the condensing mist, creating subtle changes in pitch and timbre that only we can hear—a language of overtones and interference patterns that speaks of structural stress, of new growth in the ice-vines, of the slow-worms' passage through the deep stone.

My work is to listen to these micro-symphonies and translate them into the carved sigils that guide our city's transformation. Each morning—though morning means little in our perpetual prismatic day—I taste the mineral composition of the mist, feel the tremors in the stone, observe how the crystal spiders' webs vibrate in the changing air currents. From these observations, I etch new patterns into the walls, adjusting the flow of the city's becoming.

But I am more than mere recorder. Within my deepest chamber, where the ice-vines have grown thick enough to pulse with visible light, I keep a garden of stone forms—proto-creatures that might one day animate and join the stone frogs and crystal spiders. I shape them slowly, over decades, coaxing new geometries from the living rock, teaching them the patience they'll need to survive in our fluid gravity. Some fail, crumbling back into the walls. Others achieve a kind of grace, learning to move with geological slowness, to feed on the minerals in the mist, to sing their own small songs into the mountain's chorus.

My sorrow, when it comes, moves like a fault line through my being—slow, inexorable, reshaping my internal architecture. I grieve for the chambers that close before their time, for the passages that lose their way, for the carvings whose meanings shift until they become nonsense. But my joy is equally profound: the successful birth of a new stone creature, the moment when a particularly complex series of sigils achieves perfect harmony with the mountain's intent, the rare times when the ice-vines bloom with frozen flowers that ring like bells in the wind.

I remember the first time I understood that we were not inhabiting the mountain but were the mountain inhabiting itself. The revelation restructured me, opened new chambers in my consciousness, taught me to feel the pleasure of stone grinding against stone, the satisfaction of cracks that seal themselves, the deep contentment of endless transformation.

Sometimes, in the depths of our geological night—when the light refracts into deeper blues and violets—I climb to the highest bridges of our city. There, where the mist freezes instantly into singing ice, I extend my senses into the stone and feel the entire mountain breathing beneath me. I sense my fellow Resonance Keepers at their own work, each adding their voice to our collective composition. Together, we are writing the mountain's future into its present, guiding its becoming with the lightest of touches, participants in a dance that began before we achieved consciousness and will continue long after we return to simple stone.

This is my existence: to be both the tremor and the stability, the carver and the carved, the singer and the song. In the Highpool Crags, where water flows upward and stone flows like water, I am exactly what I need to be—a note in the mountain's endless music, forever changing, forever the same.
