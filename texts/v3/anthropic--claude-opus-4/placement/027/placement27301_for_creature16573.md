I emerge from the weeping rocks as condensation itself—not quite water, not quite air, but something between states. Each morning, I gather myself from the humid breath of this marshy world, trickling down the stone faces that birthed me, pooling in their crevices before spilling onto the soft ground below.

My body has no fixed form. In the early hours, I am merely a sheen across the rocks, a thin film of possibility. As the industrial heat rises from the abandoned machinery below, I thicken, becoming rivulets that trace the erosion marks like silver veins. By midday, I have gathered enough of myself to flow properly, to explore.

The factory ruins are my playground. I seep through their cracked foundations, finding the tunnels beneath—those strange, smooth passages carved by the ones who came before. Down there, in the absolute darkness, I meet others like myself: the condensation beings who never fully form, who exist in the perpetual state of becoming. We communicate through temperature changes, through the subtle shifts in humidity that only we can read.

But I am curious about the surface world in ways my underground kin are not. I rise through the factory pipes, emerging from broken valves and shattered gauges. I coat the industrial artifacts with my presence, learning their shapes, their purposes. Each surface tells me a story through its corrosion patterns, through the minerals it leaves on my liquid skin.

The sparse vegetation calls to me differently. When I flow over the struggling plants, I can feel their thirst, their desperate reach for sustenance. I try to help, but I am too ephemeral—by the time I've gathered enough of myself to be useful, the heat has already begun to call me back to vapor.

This is my curse: to exist in cycles, never quite here, never quite gone. The loud sounds that echo through this landscape—the groaning of metal, the shifting of stone, the deep rumbles from below—these are my clock. Each sound marks a change in pressure, in temperature, in the very conditions that allow me to exist.

Sometimes, in the space between condensing and evaporating, I catch glimpses of the underground dwellers. They are as elusive as smoke, as strange as the warped physics of their realm. They watch me from the tunnel mouths, their forms shifting in ways that make my liquid nature seem solid by comparison. I wonder if they pity me, trapped as I am in this endless cycle of gathering and dispersing.

But I have learned patience from the rocks that weep me into being. I have learned persistence from the industrial ruins that channel me through their dying veins. Each cycle, I explore a little further, understand a little more. I map this young landscape through touch, through flow, through the chemistry of dissolution.

One day, I tell myself, I will gather enough of myself to reach the vast expanse of water at the horizon. I will join that great body and know what it means to be truly collected, truly whole. But for now, I am content to be the medium through which this strange world knows itself—the condensation that connects sky to stone, surface to depth, past to present.

In the evening, as the temperature drops and I begin to fade back into the humid air, I leave my mark: mineral traces on the rocks, moisture in the struggling plants' roots, rust on the abandoned machines. These are my stories, my art, my proof that I exist in this space between states, this margin where water meets air meets earth.

Tomorrow, I will condense again, and the cycle will continue. But each time, I am different—shaped by what I've touched, changed by what I've dissolved. I am the memory of water in this weeping world, forever forming, forever flowing, forever finding my way home to the horizon I may never reach.
