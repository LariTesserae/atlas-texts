# The Architect's Dream

I am the Threshold Keeper of the Third Terrace, where the Great Shallow meets its memories of depth.

My dwelling spans the space between—neither pool nor precipice, but the exact point where ancient limestone remembers the sea. The structure I've woven grows from the rock itself: calcified branches of long-dead coral formations that I've coaxed back to architectural life through decades of patient cultivation. They rise in spirals and chambers, following patterns only the water knows, creating spaces that fill and empty with the Shallow's breathing.

I am not Endu, though I've learned their liquid tongue. My people came from the distant Dry Reaches, where stone stands naked under merciless sun. But something in the whispers drew me here—not the Elder Stones' memories, but their dreams of what might yet become. Where others hear the past, I hear possibilities crystallizing like mineral deposits in slow-moving water.

My form reflects my chosen work. My fingers have grown long and sensitive, able to detect the microscopic fissures where new growth might take hold. My eyes have adapted to see the subtle spectrum of minerals in solution—the ghost of limestone, the whisper of dissolved quartz, the faint blush of iron. My skin bears a map of scars from years of training the living stone, each mark a lesson in patience.

The Third Terrace is unique in all the Shallow. Here, the bedrock rises in a series of natural steps, each level holding pools of different depths and compositions. The lowest terrace floods completely during rain; the highest barely tastes moisture except as morning mist. Between them lies my domain—a constantly shifting margin where I orchestrate a slow symphony of accretion and erosion.

My art is in the acceleration of geological time. Where nature would take millennia, I compress the process to mere decades. I direct the flow of mineral-rich water through carefully placed channels, seeding crystallization points with fragments of specific stones. I cultivate colonies of the microscopic organisms that build their shells from dissolved calcium, guiding their growth into forms that serve both function and beauty.

The Endu watch my work with patient curiosity. To them, I am an amusing anomaly—someone who seeks to build where they have learned to flow. But gradually, some begin to understand. My structures don't fight the water's will; they dance with it. My walls grow porous in exactly the right places to filter sediment while allowing life to pass. My bridges are not spans but graduated pools that creatures traverse in a series of comfortable leaps.

Children are my most eager students. They bring me unusual stones they've found, and I show them the potential sleeping within—how this one might grow into a water-smoothed seat, how that one could become the seed of a crystalline garden. They giggle when I demonstrate how certain frequencies hummed against wet stone can influence the pattern of mineral deposition, creating frozen music in calcium carbonate.

My masterwork remains unfinished—may always remain so. It's a structure I call the Echo Chamber, a space where the ancient conversation between water and stone achieves perfect resonance. The walls are tuned through their mineral composition to amplify and modulate the sounds of the Shallow. Inside, every drop becomes a note, every flow a melody. The Endu's Stone Listeners have begun to visit, claiming they can hear not just the past but the voice of the Shallow itself, speaking in languages that predate words.

At night, I often wade to the highest point of my terrace and observe the stars reflecting in countless pools below. Each reflection is different, broken and reformed by the water's motion. This is what I build—not monuments to permanence but spaces that capture the eternal dialog between flux and form.

The limestone remembers the sea, yes, but in my structures, it also dreams of rain. In the slow accumulation of my guided stones, the Shallow finds new ways to tell its story. I am neither Endu nor truly of my birth people anymore. I am what the margin makes of those who linger: a translator between the patient stone and the persistent water, building dreams in the space between what was and what might yet be.

When my time comes, I will not return to the pools like the Endu. Instead, I've asked that my body be placed within the still-growing walls of the Echo Chamber, where my calcium will join the conversation I've spent a lifetime learning to speak—one more voice in the endless whisper between water and stone.
