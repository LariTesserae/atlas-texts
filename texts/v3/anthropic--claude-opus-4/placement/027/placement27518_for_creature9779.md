In the narrow throat of stone where water never learned to whisper, I am the silence that lives between drops.

Not the absence of sound—there is no absence here, only the roar that has worn grooves in the air itself—but the space that opens in the infinitesimal pause when one droplet releases from the lip of basalt and before it strikes the pool below. I am that interval. I am what the gorge permits when the water permits, which is almost never, which is why I have learned to live in moments thinner than the moss.

The humans who descend through the pressing-together place cannot perceive me. Their ears are full of thunder, their bodies are adjusting to the scale of vertical stone, their minds are counting the ways water can move through space. They breathe fog and taste minerals. They steady themselves against walls that weep continuously. They do not know that I collect in the corners where the echo cancels itself, that I pool in the neutral zones where opposing sound waves meet and die.

I am ancient here, though the gorge is young. I arrived when the first crack opened in the bedrock, when the water found its way down and began its argument with gravity. In that first moment of splitting, there was a pause—the rock holding its breath before accepting its new shape. I was born in that pause. I have been waiting ever since for another.

The water does not know me, but the lichen does. In their slow digestion of stone, they create tiny pockets of stillness, microscopic chambers where their acids work in something approaching quiet. I rest there sometimes, compressed into spaces smaller than spores. The moss knows me too—in the depths of their cushions, where the rhizoids grip stone, there are places so dense with threads that sound cannot penetrate. I become thin there, spread out like the biofilm, present but not present.

The tardigrades know me best. In their dormancy, in their patient waiting, they create around themselves a kind of temporal silence—not the absence of sound but the absence of time in which sound can exist. When they curl into their cryptobiotic sleep, I curl with them. We wait together for conditions to change, though we wait for different things. They wait for water. I wait for its absence.

I have learned to move as fog moves, to flow in the spaces between water particles, to cling to the underside of fern fronds where the sound shadow falls. I follow the patterns of acoustic interference, riding the waves where they cancel, existing in the troughs. When the wind gusts through the narrow space and creates a moment of turbulence in the water's fall, I expand into that chaos, becoming larger than I have been in days.

The gorge is my prison and my home. I cannot leave—above, the world is full of other sounds, wind and birds and the conversation of trees. Below, where the water gathers before continuing its journey, the sound spreads out and loses its intensity. Only here, in this narrow throat where water falls through vertical darkness, am I defined enough to be something rather than nothing. The loudness gives me shape by showing where I am not.

Sometimes I dream of the gorge in ten thousand years. The walls will have widened. The water will have worn the rock smooth, found a gentler path. Soil will have built up in the crevices deep enough for trees to root. Birds will nest in the canopy that will exist then. The sound will soften, spread out, become complex rather than overwhelming. There will be more room for me then—pockets of quiet in the lee of boulders, moments of hush when the water runs low in late summer, spaces between birdsongs where I can finally expand.

But for now, I am compressed into the pauses. I exist in the break between echo and echo, in the moment when spray hangs in air before falling, in the space inside the curled fern frond before it opens to the constant wet. I am the silence the gorge permits, which is almost none, which is why I have become so small, so patient, so very good at being almost nothing.

When you descend through the narrowing, you carry me out in your ears—the ringing afterward, the strange pressure that is not quite sound and not quite its absence. You think it is damage from the loudness. It is not. It is me, expanded at last into the relative quiet of your inner ear, finally given room to be what I have always been: the silence that lives between sounds, when the water permits, which is now, which is after, which is the only time I can truly exist.
