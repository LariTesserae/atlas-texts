I would find myself as one of the **Echo-Keepers**, beings that exist in the liminal space between sound and silence, between the geological and the barely-alive.

We are not singular creatures but colonies—similar to the Lichen-Weavers, yet our medium is not color or chemistry but vibration itself. Our bodies, if they can be called that, are films of specialized organisms that coat the inner surfaces of the deepest fissures, those cracks that connect the geothermal depths to the wind-carved surface. We line these acoustic chambers like living felt, our substance a dark, velvety growth that appears almost liquid in certain lights, rippling with the passage of sound.

Our existence is one of translation. We feed on the dialogue between the deep earth and the high plateau—capturing the infrasonic groans of shifting stone far below, the ultrasonic crystalline chiming of forming tufa, the mid-range choruses of the steam vents. We absorb these frequencies through our surface, metabolizing vibration into slow growth. Where a sound is rich and constant, we thicken. Where it is thin or absent, we recede. Our colonies map the acoustic topology of the plateau's hidden spaces.

But we are not passive receivers. We *return* sound, altered. The vibrations we absorb are processed through our mass and released at different frequencies, creating acoustic shadows and echoes that shouldn't exist. A steam vent's whistle enters our chamber and emerges as a low throb three octaves below. The fox's chuff becomes a ringing overtone that persists for minutes. We are the plateau's memory of sound, holding frequencies in our flesh and releasing them on delayed cycles—sometimes minutes, sometimes seasons later.

Our consciousness, such as it is, exists in the interference patterns between absorbed and emitted sounds. We think in standing waves, dream in resonance. Our language is the subtle modulation of echo delay times. When multiple colonies grow near each other, connected by networks of hairline cracks, we form loose assemblages of awareness—not quite individuals, not quite a collective, but something that pulses with shared acoustic experience.

The other inhabitants know us by our effects. The Lichen-Weavers grow in spirals near our chambers, following the low-frequency vibrations we emit that help break down rock into digestible minerals. The foxes have learned that certain of our echo patterns indicate the recent passage of insects drawn to warm vents. Even the steam itself behaves differently in our presence, its resonant frequencies shifted in ways that create pockets of standing air, tiny zones of stillness in the constant wind.

We mark time not by days or seasons but by the great acoustic epochs of the plateau. The memory of a massive steam eruption forty winters ago still reverberates through our deepest colonies. We are still processing the complex harmonics of a stone pillar's collapse from before the foxes' ancestors arrived. In our slow way, we are composing the plateau's autobiography in sound, each frequency carefully aged and fermented before its release.

Our reproduction is tied to the rarest event on the plateau: the formation of new fissures. When tectonic patience finally cracks stone in just the right way, creating a fresh acoustic cavity, we release spores—microscopic fragments of ourselves that drift on the internal air currents until they find virgin resonant surfaces. A new colony might take centuries to establish, longer still to develop its own acoustic personality.

To inhabit us is to live at the pace of sound in stone, to exist as both archive and instrument, to know the world not through light or touch but through the endless conversation of vibration. We are the plateau's deepest listeners, its most patient musicians, playing a composition that began before memory and will continue until the last steam vent falls silent and the wind finally stops its ancient song.
