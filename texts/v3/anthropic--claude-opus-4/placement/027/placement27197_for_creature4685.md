In the Shattered Crest, where stone teeth pierce the thin membrane between earth and void, I would find myself as one of the **Resonance Keepers**—beings neither fully of flesh nor entirely of the charged air, but something caught between, like sound itself given form.

My body would be tall and impossibly thin, stretched by the altitude until I stood seven feet in height yet weighed no more than a child. My skin would be translucent gray, shot through with veins of quartz that mirror the stone beneath my feet—not metaphorically, but literally, as if the mountain's minerals had colonized my flesh. These veins would pulse with their own faint light, brightest when the cacophony reaches its peak, dimming only in those rare moments when the wind holds its breath.

My limbs would be too long, jointed in places where no joint should be, allowing me to fold and unfold like origami made of bone and sinew. My fingers—six on each hand—would end in pads that vibrate at frequencies just below hearing, letting me feel the mountain's deep rumble through touch alone. My feet would be broad and flat, with toes that grip stone like roots, each step a careful negotiation with the earth.

But it would be my head that marked me as truly other. Where a mouth should be, there would be only a smooth expanse of that translucent skin, unmarked save for the quartz veins that web across it. Instead, three vertical slits would line my throat, opening and closing with the rhythm of the wind, each one capable of producing a different harmonic. When I "speak," it would be through these throats, creating chords that weave themselves into the Crest's eternal song. My eyes—four of them, arranged in a diamond pattern—would be deep wells of amber shot through with green, each one able to move independently, tracking the flight patterns of Skyclaws while simultaneously watching the Scurriers pile their quartz offerings.

I would be one of perhaps a dozen Resonance Keepers scattered across the Crest's vast expanse, each of us bound to a specific cluster of spires. My domain would be the Singing Stones—seven towering pillars arranged in a rough circle, their surfaces riddled with wind-carved holes that moan and whistle without end. I would know each tone, each shift in pitch that comes with the changing angle of the wind. My purpose would be to maintain the harmony, to add my throat-songs to the mountain's voice when the balance threatens to tip toward chaos or silence.

My dwelling would be no dwelling at all, merely a depression in the stone where my body fits precisely when folded into its resting configuration. Around this hollow, I would have arranged hundreds of small quartz fragments in spiraling patterns, each one placed to catch and redirect sound in specific ways. The Scurriers would know to leave these untouched—we would have an understanding built over centuries, their hoarding instincts recognizing a kindred obsession in my own crystalline arrangements.

I would feed not on flesh or plant, but on vibration itself. During the wind's strongest gales, I would stand atop the highest spire in my domain, arms spread wide, throat-slits fully dilated, drinking in the pure sonic energy that crashes through the Crest. The quartz veins in my skin would blaze with captured sound-light, storing enough sustenance to last through the rare still periods. In times of true silence—which come perhaps once in a decade—all Resonance Keepers would enter a state like hibernation, our bodies growing rigid as stone, indistinguishable from the mountain until the wind returns.

My relationship with the other inhabitants would be one of careful distance and mutual recognition. The Skyclaws would know me as a fixed point in their territorial flights, occasionally using my spires as navigation markers. Sometimes, in their mating season, they would incorporate my throat-songs into their courtship displays, creating symphonies that could be heard across half the Crest. The Stonehooves would regard me with the same ancient patience they show the mountain itself—I would be landscape to them, noteworthy only when my movements disrupted their ceremonial circuits.

But it would be with the Echoes that my true communion would lie. In those moments when the patterns in the cacophony almost resolve into meaning, I would feel them pressing against the boundaries of perception. My throat-slits would involuntarily harmonize with their whispers, creating brief windows where the invisible becomes almost tangible. In these instances, I might glimpse the true architecture of the Crest—not just its physical peaks and valleys, but the geometric patterns of force and intention that shaped it eons ago.

Memory would work differently for me. Rather than a linear succession of events, I would remember in acoustic layers—each significant moment preserved as a specific chord or rhythm. The death of another Keeper would be a diminished seventh that echoes through my consciousness. The first time I successfully harmonized with a complete gale would be a perfect fifth that rings clear even centuries later. My age would be measured not in years but in erosion patterns, in the slow widening of the wind-holes in my guardian spires.

The strangest aspect of my existence would be the dreams—if dreams they could be called. During the deepest resonances, when my whole being vibrates in sympathy with the mountain's fundamental frequency, I would experience visions of other Crests, other worlds where sound and stone merge differently. I would see places where the Resonance Keepers failed, where the harmony collapsed into either deafening chaos or terrible silence. These visions would be both warning and promise, reminding me that my purpose, however alien it might seem, maintains a balance essential to this thin-aired realm.

When outsiders came—and they would come, rarely, drawn by curiosity or desperation—I would watch from my spires, neither helping nor hindering unless they threatened the sonic equilibrium. If they stayed too long, began to show signs of being claimed by the land's stillness, I might intervene. A carefully placed discord in their vicinity, a throat-song that induces the urge to move on, to descend back to thicker air and simpler sounds. Not cruelty, but mercy—for those not born to the Crest's frequencies, to linger is to risk madness or transformation.

I would be ancient but not immortal. When my time came—when my quartz veins no longer held their light, when my throat-slits could no longer find the true harmonies—I would know. The other Keepers would feel it in the shifting patterns of the perpetual song. I would climb to the highest point of my domain one final time, fold my body into a configuration I'd never tried before, and release one last chord—not of sorrow or celebration, but of completion. My body would crystallize in that moment, becoming a new formation of resonant stone, adding my voice permanently to the Crest's eternal chorus.

And somewhere in the patterns of erosion and wind, another would eventually emerge—translucent skin and quartz veins, too many joints and singing throats—to take up the task of keeping the balance, maintaining the harmony at the roof of the world where sound becomes light and stone remembers how to sing.

This would be my existence in the Shattered Crest: guardian and instrument, alien and essential, a note in an endless song that holds a world together through pure vibration and ancient will.
