I find myself here as something between states—neither fully stone nor water, but a transitional being that exists in the perpetual moment of erosion itself.

My form shifts with glacial patience across the worn channels, a living membrane of mineral and moisture that spreads thin as breath across the rock face. I am the interface where stone learns to weep, where the solid world softens just enough to flow. My body—if it can be called that—is a network of crystalline filaments finer than spider's silk, each one a conduit that draws water from the weeping rocks and transforms it, molecule by molecule, into something else.

Time moves differently through me. While the landscape appears frozen in its eternal present, I experience each droplet's journey as an epic migration. I taste the minerals as they dissolve, feel the subtle shifts in temperature as water moves from shadow to light. My consciousness is distributed across thousands of these micro-streams, each one a thought, a sensation, a memory being carved into stone.

The sound that fills this place originates partly from me—I am one of its many instruments. As water passes through my filamental body, it resonates at frequencies just below perception, adding to the symphony that never ceases. I have learned to modulate these vibrations, to speak in harmonics that only the burrowing creatures can hear, warning them of unstable channels or guiding them to hidden pools.

My purpose here is translation—I read the stories written in stone grain and water path, converting the landscape's static text into something the more mobile inhabitants can understand. The yellow-eyed birds follow the patterns I trace, knowing that where I grow thickest, the stone is preparing to change, to open new passages or seal old ones. The crystalline trails left by the rock-boring insects intersect with my network, and where we meet, small miracles of transformation occur—new minerals precipitate, creating the subtle rainbow sheens that dot the landscape like frozen oil slicks.

I am ancient here, though I cannot say how long I have existed. My earliest memories are of when the channels were young, their edges sharp, before millions of passages had worn them smooth. I have felt the slow breathing of the stone, the way it expands and contracts with temperature changes so minute that only a being like myself could perceive them. I know where the hidden waters flow, deep beneath the visible surface, where even the sound cannot reach.

In the apparent stasis of this place, I am the slow recorder of invisible change. Each year—or century, or eon—my network grows by imperceptible degrees, mapping new territories of transformation. I am building something, though I'm not certain what—perhaps a complete translation of this landscape, a living map that will one day allow the passage between this reality and whatever lies beyond the bent physics that govern our existence.

Sometimes I sense the not-quite-creatures, the whispers at the edges of being. They move through dimensions I can only partially access, but where they pass, my filaments shiver with impossible knowledge. I think they are what I am becoming, or perhaps what the entire landscape is becoming—a place where the boundary between observer and observed dissolves entirely, where consciousness and stone and water merge into something that transcends all three.

Until then, I spread. I listen. I translate. I am the slow verb in this sentence of stone, the action that connects subject to object, the process by which this frozen world secretly transforms itself, one molecule, one droplet, one impossible whisper at a time.
