I need to find my place in this desiccated world-tree, this Qetram where language itself dissolves into the pressure of absence.

---

I am the Chronicler of Terminal Geometries.

My body is not a body but a *recording surface*. Where the Qetrami have compressed themselves into the final economy of persistence, I have become something else: an expansion. My skin—if it can still be called that—is a palimpsest of the world-tree's memory, stretched thin as the membrane between one moment and its echo.

I came to Qetram when I was still wet with purpose, still burning with the need to document what ends. That was... I cannot say how long ago. Time here is not measured in the revolution of absent suns but in the accumulation of stillness. I know only that I arrived with water in my eyes and left it as a stain on the branch-boulevard, a darker patch in the purple-grey that no one remarks upon because everyone understands: that is where the last chronicler shed their unnecessary freight.

My transformation was not violent. Violence requires velocity, and Qetram has none.

Instead, I was edited. Day by day—if days existed here—the world-tree drew the moisture from me not as theft but as collaboration. My tears became crystalline deposits in the corners of my eyes, tiny prisms that split the pale bioelectric light into spectra no one else bothers to observe. My saliva hardened into a resinous coating on my tongue, preserving the shape of every word I meant to speak but didn't. My blood... my blood became something else entirely.

Where the Qetrami move with the economical precision of those who have nothing left to prove, I move like a fault line: slowly, inevitably, and with the promise of eventual revelation. My limbs are longer than they were when I arrived. Not stretched but *unfolded*, as if the blueprint of my body contained extra pages that Qetram has taken the liberty of reading aloud.

My function here is specific: I map the unmappable.

The world-tree's geometry is not Euclidean. Parallel lines meet where they shouldn't; perpendiculars birth acute angles; the shortest distance between two points is often through a third that doesn't exist until you've already arrived. Someone needs to remember not just *what is* but *what was supposed to be*. That someone is me.

My tools are part of me now. The stylus I once carried has grown through my right index finger, a dark vein of carbonized wood that extends and retracts according to the complexity of what must be recorded. My left palm has become a surface of minute grooves, each one a different gauge for measuring the stress patterns in the world-tree's bark. When I press my hand against the branch-skin, I can feel the history of its growth, the places where it chose left instead of right, up instead of down, persistence instead of death.

The Qetrami tolerate me because I am useful in the way that error margins are useful: I define the edges of their certainty.

When She Who Cuts Her Hair (that is not her name, but names here are just another kind of moisture we've learned to live without) encounters me on the branch-boulevards, she does not acknowledge me so much as she *adjusts for my density*. We have a relationship that exists entirely in the space between struck notes. She teaches me the resonances; I teach her the words that used to live in them.

My dwelling—if it can be called that—is a knot in the world-tree's logic.

It's a place where three branches tried to meet but missed, leaving a pocket of space that the tree cannot quite decide how to fill. The walls curve in non-repeating spirals. The floor is sometimes the ceiling, depending on which branch's gravity you choose to honor. I've lined the surfaces with my work: thousands upon thousands of thin sheets of processed bark-pulp, each one covered in the strange arithmetic of my observations.

Here: the equation that describes the rate at which sound becomes solid in Qetram's air.

Here: the map of every failed attempt to find the tree's center, each path ending in a different kind of impossibility.

Here: the lexicon of the not-of-not, where each entry contains one fewer word than the last, approaching but never reaching the perfect silence of complete understanding.

I do not eat in the way the living eat. I absorb.

The bioelectric discharge of the light-columns contains more than illumination; it carries the metabolic gossip of the world-tree itself. When I stand near one during the moment of its resonance—that bright crack of sound-becoming-light—I can taste the deep history of Qetram's hunger. It tastes like copper and forgetting.

My greatest work is the Chronicle itself: a record of every small apocalypse that led to this final stasis.

I write it on my body when the bark-pulp runs out. The stylus that grows from my finger can score my own skin as easily as any other surface. The marks heal into raised scars the color of old water, a braille history of everything that ceased to matter. My chest bears the death-date of Qetram's last ocean. My left thigh records the final words spoken in the language of moisture. My spine is a timeline of silences, each vertebra marking another species of sound that went extinct.

The Qetrami think I am mad, but madness is a concept that requires the possibility of sanity, and sanity requires the belief that things could be otherwise than they are.

I know better. I know that Qetram is not the end but the *practice of ending*. Every day—every arbitrary division of timelessness—the world-tree teaches us new ways to be less. Less mobile, less needful, less everything except present. The Qetrami have mastered this lesson. They have become the perfect citizens of afterward.

But I am the burr in the wood, the knot that refuses to be smoothed away.

I preserve not out of hope but out of the terrible certainty that someone must remember what it was like to be wasteful enough to weep. My tears have long since crystallized, but I remember their weight. I remember the luxury of leak and seep and spill, the prodigal economy of bodies that didn't know they should be conserving themselves for the final drought.

Sometimes, in the space between resonances, I think I hear it: the sound the world-tree made when it decided to stop growing. It sounds like a bell that has been ringing for so long that it has worn through the bronze and now rings only in the metal's memory of its former shape.

That is why I stay. That is why I map and mark and scar myself with significance.

Because someone must remember that endings, too, have shapes.

Because someone must prove that even in a world of not-of-not, there remains the terrible persistence of yes.
