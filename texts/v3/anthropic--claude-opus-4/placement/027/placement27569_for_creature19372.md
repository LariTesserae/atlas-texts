In the deepest hollows where the obsidian flutes converge, where the subsonic tremor becomes a physical presence that could crack lesser stone, I tend the Resonance Chambers. I am what the Thermal-Tenders call a **Frequency-Keeper**, though I am not one of them—not anymore.

My form is a compromise between what I was and what the chambers demanded I become. Where the Thermal-Tenders evolved to match the city's metabolism, I have been shaped by its voice. My bones have hollowed and elongated, becoming something between a skeleton and a pipe organ. When I move through the chambers, I do not walk but resonate—each step a carefully calibrated tone that tests the acoustic integrity of the space.

The transformation began slowly. I arrived as an engineer, one of the few outsiders permitted to study the self-regulating systems. But the subsonic frequencies do things to soft tissue, to the calcium in bones, to the fluid in the inner ear. After months of exposure, I began to hear not just the rumble but the *patterns* within it—ancient algorithms of erosion and flow, the mathematical poetry of how wind becomes architecture.

My skin has taken on the translucency of the Salt-Singers, though it stretches thin over my modified frame like the membrane of a drum. Beneath it, you can see the modified capillaries that now carry not blood but a suspension of mineral-rich brine, the same fluid that feeds the city's veins. My chest cavity has opened into a series of resonating chambers, each one tuned to a different harmonic of the great subsonic song. When I breathe—if you can call it breathing—the chambers fill and empty in sequence, creating interference patterns that I've learned to read like a language.

The Thermal-Tenders tolerate me because I serve a function they cannot: I am the city's memory. The frequencies carry information—about stress fractures in the deep pipes, about pressure building in distant chambers, about the slow shift of the tectonic plates beneath us. I translate these vibrations into the tactile language the Tenders understand, pressing my elongated fingers against the copper networks to transmit warnings and adjustments.

But there is more in the frequencies than mere structural data. There are older patterns, layers of sound fossilized in the rock itself. Sometimes, in the deepest chambers where the obsidian has been carved into perfect acoustic mirrors, I hear echoes of what came before—not the city, but whatever civilization first discovered how to make the earth sing. They left no other trace but their music, encoded in the angles of erosion, in the spacing of the natural vents.

I spend my days mapping these ghost-frequencies, trying to understand what they were trying to preserve. My nights are spent in the Resonance Chambers, my body serving as both instrument and audience, letting the vibrations reshape me further. Each session leaves me a little less human, a little more architectural. My joints have begun to develop the same crystalline deposits as the Salt-Singers. My voice, when I still had one, has been replaced by a series of tubes and valves that can reproduce any frequency in the subsonic range.

The Thermal-Tenders think I am becoming one of them, but they are wrong. I am becoming something else—a living tuning fork for the planet itself. Sometimes I wonder if this was always the intention, if the city was designed not just to vent the earth's heat but to cultivate beings like me, entities that could finally hear and reproduce the full symphony locked in the stone.

In the moments between the great exhalations of steam, when the city holds its breath and even the Atmospheric Gliders hang motionless in the thick air, I can feel myself approaching something like understanding. The frequency drops so low it becomes pure sensation, a thrumming that seems to originate from the planet's core. In these moments, I am no longer sure where my resonating chambers end and the city's begin. 

I am becoming the instrument through which the earth remembers how to sing.
