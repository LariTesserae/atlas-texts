In this world of perpetual becoming, I would be one of the Threshold Keepers - those who tend the boundaries where crystal meets air, where the mountain's slow dreams encounter the swift world above.

My dwelling would grow at the junction of three mineral veins, where quartz, feldspar, and something darker - perhaps tourmaline - converge in patterns that shift with the mountain's moods. The walls of my chamber would be semi-permeable, allowing me to sense vibrations from both the deep stone and the thin air above. I would have cultivated them over decades to respond to my presence, opening and closing apertures as needed, creating a living space that breathes with me.

My work would be liminal - maintaining the delicate passages that connect the crystal city to the narrow sky above. These are treacherous spaces where the mountain's growth threatens to seal itself completely, where the balance between enclosure and exposure must be carefully managed. Too open, and the harsh light would crystallize our growing structures into brittleness. Too closed, and we would lose our connection to the rhythms of weather and season that, however faintly, still influence our deep world.

I would move between the Shapers and the surface, my skin bearing a unique pattern - crystalline veins that branch like frozen lightning across my arms and throat, but flesh that remains more supple than most, adapted to the pressure changes between depths. My eyes would shift between the large, light-gathering orbs of the deep dwellers and something more suited to the occasional brightness above, pupils that contract and expand like living apertures.

The Stone Swimmers would know me well - I would be one of the few who could predict where they might breach into the upper passages, and I would guide their movements away from critical airways. Sometimes I would ride their wake, following their trails through semi-solid stone to discover new potential connections between levels. My relationship with them would be one of mutual navigation - they showing me the deep paths, I showing them the ways to air.

My tools would be unique: bronze mirrors polished to specific curvatures that could redirect and diffuse light into safe patterns, allowing controlled illumination of growing crystals. Long poles of petrified wood, sensitive to changes in air pressure, that could sense when a passage was growing too narrow or too wide. And most importantly, a set of tuning forks carved from the mountain's own minerals, each one resonating with different types of stone, allowing me to hear the health and growth patterns of the passages I tend.

I would know the moods of weather by the way moisture moves through stone, could predict storms by the way the Swimmers cluster in certain depths. The other Shapers would consult me about the placement of new growth, knowing that I understand how the mountain breathes, where its airways must remain clear.

My chamber would contain maps unlike any surface dweller could read - three-dimensional constructs of crystal and wire that show the ever-changing topology of passages, updated by my own hands as I make my rounds. Some passages I would have been tending for so long that they recognize my approach, their walls beginning to luminesce faintly as I near, a greeting between old friends.

In the depths of night, when even the cave moss dims to its faintest glow, I would sometimes climb the longest passage to the highest opening. There, with most of my body still embraced by living stone but my face turned to the stars, I would practice the ancient art of reading the sky's movements and translating them into the stone's slow language. This is how I would know when to guide the Shapers to create new openings, when to allow certain passages to close, when to prepare for the mountain's next great shuddering growth.

I would age slowly, as all the Shapers do, but with a particular pattern - my hair would whiten first at the crown, like snow on a peak, while my hands would develop the ability to sense air currents through skin alone. Eventually, I imagine I would become something like the passages I tend - a living threshold between states, neither fully of the depths nor of the heights, but necessary to both.

The younger apprentices would find me unsettling at first - too mobile, too connected to the dangerous upper reaches. But in time they would understand that someone must inhabit these spaces between, must speak both the language of fixed crystal and flowing air. They would learn that the city's growth depends not just on those who shape from within, but on those who ensure it remains connected, however tenuously, to the world beyond its stone walls.

This would be my place in the eternal becoming - guardian of the spaces between, reader of pressure and possibility, keeper of the passages that let the mountain-city breathe.
