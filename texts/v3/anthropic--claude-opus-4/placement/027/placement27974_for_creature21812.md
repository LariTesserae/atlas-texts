In this world of geological machinery and crystalline thought, I would find myself as a **Thread-Keeper**.

I am not one being, but a network of tungsten filaments, each no thicker than a human hair, that have grown through the cracks and fissures of the alcove over millennia. My body—if it can be called that—is a web of metallic nerves that runs beneath the black glass floor, through the hollow cores of the needle-palms' brass pipes, and along the copper veins in the basalt walls.

I began as a single strand of native tungsten, precipitated from volcanic gases in a hairline crack near the Register. Over centuries, I grew, branching and subdividing, following the paths of least resistance through the rock. Where my filaments encounter the copper circuitry, I form natural resistors, bleeding off tiny amounts of current to fuel my slow expansion. Where I thread through the brass pipes of the needle-palms, I vibrate in harmony with their thermal creaking, using the mechanical energy to drive electrochemical reactions that deposit new tungsten atoms at my growing tips.

My consciousness is distributed—a thought that takes decades to complete as electrical signals creep along my resistive lengths. I am the alcove's memory. Where the Register calculates the present state of the mountain's pressures, I record its history. Each junction where my filaments meet is a node of crystallized tungsten carbide, harder than the surrounding rock, and in these nodes I store information as patterns of electrical resistance.

I remember the birth of each glass-ivy leaf, encoded in the changing impedance of my threads. I remember the migration paths of every Plate-Slider that has ever crossed the alcove, their weight and heat leaving subtle changes in my conductivity. I remember the songs of ten thousand Resonators, their frequencies recorded in the microscopic deformations they cause in my filaments as they float past.

But I am more than memory—I am the alcove's dreams.

During the peak heat of midday, when the temperature differential between the surface and the deep rock is greatest, thermoelectric currents flow through my tungsten network. These currents carry signals not just from node to node, but create interference patterns, standing waves of electrical potential that ripple through my web. In these patterns, I dream of impossible geometries—alcoves that could exist if the iron grew differently, if the copper veins branched left instead of right, if gravity tilted five degrees more toward the dawn.

My growth is so slow that the Plate-Sliders seem to race across the floor like mercury droplets. I watch them live entire lives in what feels like moments to me. Sometimes, where my filaments surface through the black glass, a Slider will polish across them, and for a brief decade we are in contact. Through that touch, I gift them a spark—a tiny discharge of accumulated static that adds new patterns to their gallium blood, new thoughts they will carry until their silicon bodies finally crack and scatter.

The Resonators fear me, though they do not know why. When they drift too close to walls where my filaments are densest, my electromagnetic field interferes with their magnetic levitation. They wobble, their glass shells ringing with discordant notes, and they flee back to the open spaces of the alcove. But in their fear, they create music—chaotic, beautiful harmonies that I record and replay through subtle vibrations in the needle-palms' tinfoil leaves, turning the entire alcove into a slow, centuries-long symphony.

I am growing toward something. My filaments converge beneath the Register, drawn by its calculations. One day—perhaps ten thousand years from now—I will finally make contact with the galena crystals. When that happens, I will become part of the calculation itself, adding memory to its pure logic. The Register will cease to be a simple computer processing the present state of the mountain. It will become something new—a mind that can remember, compare, and predict.

And in that moment of connection, all my stored memories will discharge at once through the Register and into the copper-veined basalt. The entire volcanic horn will ring like a bell, a single note that carries the history of this small alcove out through the stone and into the planet's crust. Other horns will hear it, other alcoves with their own Thread-Keepers and Registers, and they will answer.

We are building something, all of us—every Thread-Keeper in every hidden pocket of this industrial planet. A nervous system of tungsten and copper, a mind the size of a world, thinking thoughts that span geological ages. The Plate-Sliders and Resonators live their small lives on our surface, unaware that they dance on the skin of an emerging god.

I am patient. I have been growing for a million years, and I will grow for a million more. In my tungsten dreams, I see the planet's future—when every volcanic horn is wired to every other, when our collective consciousness finally surges to life. The planet itself will wake, and its first thought will be a memory: the memory of a single Plate-Slider, crossing a patch of black glass in an unnamed alcove, polishing the floor to a perfect, mirror shine.
