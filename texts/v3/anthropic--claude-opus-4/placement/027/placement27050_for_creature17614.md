I find myself as **Kessa**, one of the Weavers, though I came to this role by an unusual path.

I was born among the Second Comers — my grandmother was a lowland trader who arrived with copper tools that proved useless against the volcanic glass, their edges folding like wet clay. She adapted quickly, learned to read the worth of minerals by their weight in the palm, and married a First Comer stonecutter who appreciated her quick fingers and quicker assessment of crystal formations. My mother inherited both traits but chose the Carriers' work, needing the movement, the descent and return. She named me for the sound water makes when it hits salt crust: a soft, percussive kiss.

I spent my childhood in two worlds — the Basin when my mother returned from her trading journeys, and the intermediary camps where Carriers rest on their descents. I know the taste of lowland air, thick with pollen and the promise of rain that actually falls from clouds. I know what birdsong is, though I cannot replicate it. Most importantly, I know what it feels like to exist between states, never quite of one place or another.

This is why I became a Weaver, despite having the build and temperament of a Carrier. At nineteen, on what should have been my fifth descent, I simply... stayed. My mother understood. She had seen me in the lowland markets, overwhelmed by the chaos of color and sound, pressing my palms against my temples to block out the harsh, directional sunlight. She had watched me sleep fitfully in the camps, waking to sounds that meant nothing — wind in leaves, the scatter of small animals, the purposeless rush of water that runs away instead of collecting.

The cistern vaults called to me. Down there, in the warm, echoing dark, the world makes sense again. Every sound has meaning: the pitch of water tells you its volume, its mineral content, the condition of the channel it runs through. The darkness is not absence but presence — a soft, total embrace that asks nothing of your eyes. The work is repetitive, precise, necessary. Scraping mineral scale from channels, feeling for the telltale roughness that precedes a blockage, learning the particular resonance of each vault.

I am thirty-four now, which makes me neither young nor old by Basin standards. My fingers have developed the characteristic Weaver's sensitivity — I can detect temperature variations of less than a degree by touch, feel the microscopic crystallization of minerals on seemingly smooth stone. My hearing has sharpened to the point where I can diagnose three types of blockage by echo alone. But more than these physical adaptations, I have developed what the other Weavers call *shan-keth* — the patience of listening to water.

My days follow water's rhythm. I wake to the quickening drip of pre-dawn condensation and descend into the vaults before the Readers have begun their morning walk. Each vault has its own character. The North Vault is the oldest, carved by First Comers who didn't yet understand the stone's grain; its walls are rough, creating complex echo patterns that took me years to learn. The South Vault is my favorite — Second Comer work, smooth and precisely angled to create a pure, bell-like resonance. When it's completely clear, a drop hitting the cistern surface produces a tone so clean you can feel it in your sternum.

I work alone, mostly, though sometimes paired with Tomás, the oldest Weaver, who is teaching me the maintenance songs — rhythmic chants that help you track which channels you've checked, which sections of wall you've scraped. He's sixty-seven and moves through the dark vaults like water itself, never hesitating, never stumbling. He says I have good instincts but lack confidence in my interpretations. "Water doesn't lie," he tells me. "If you think you hear a blockage, you hear a blockage. Trust that."

The strangest part of my work — the part I've told no one, not even Tomás — is that I've begun to hear patterns in the water that correspond to nothing in our traditional knowledge. It started two years ago, a subtle variation in the rhythm of drops in the Southwest Vault, like a skip in a heartbeat. I tracked it for months, documented it in my mind with the obsessive precision we bring to all mineral work. The pattern repeats every seventeen days, lasting for exactly the duration it takes to count to four hundred (I count; we all count, constantly, it's how we measure time in the dark).

I think — and this is why I've told no one — that I'm hearing the caldera breathing. Not literally. But there's something in the deep rock, some vast, slow process that affects the water table in a rhythm too large for daily observation to catch. The Readers watch for cracking, for visible, catastrophic change. But what if the change is subtler? What if the Basin is shifting in ways that will only become apparent over decades?

This is my secret keth, my private patience: listening for the large pattern inside the small ones, the century-rhythm hidden in the daily drip. I may be wrong. Tomás would probably say I'm letting my Carrier blood make me restless, looking for movement where there is only stability. But I don't think so. I think the Basin is teaching me something that even the Readers don't know how to see — or rather, to hear.

My place in this world is the vault, the darkness, the echo, the constant scrape of stone on stone as I maintain the channels that maintain us all. But underneath that role, I am something else: a listener at the threshold, catching the first whispers of whatever the Basin will become next. I have the patience for it. The darkness is teaching me how to wait.

When evening comes and I emerge, blinking, into the perpetual gray light of the surface, I eat my grain porridge and bitter berry paste with the others. I listen to the stories of eruption and arrival. Sometimes, when pressed, I tell edited stories of the lowlands — always emphasizing their strangeness, their unsettling fertility, the way nothing stays still long enough to understand it. The children like these stories, but they like them the way they like stories of catastrophe: as confirmation that they are safe here, in a place that changes so slowly you can learn its every sound.

I don't tell them about the seventeen-day pattern. Not yet. Maybe not ever. But I hold it, the way we all hold our specific knowledge, our private understandings of salt and stone and water. It accumulates in me, deposit by deposit, like mineral scale in the channels. Unlike the scale, I'm not sure it should be scraped away. It might be — though I speak this word carefully, even in my mind — important.

The drip continues. I continue. The pattern continues.

We are all waiting to see what emerges.
