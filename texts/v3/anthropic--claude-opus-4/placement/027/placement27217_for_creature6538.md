**The Canopy Bridges of Lower Valtharion**

I find myself suspended between worlds, neither truly of the heights nor the depths, dwelling in the perpetual twilight of the Under-Tier. Here, beneath the great platforms of the Skyward Forges, exists another realm entirely—one built not from the ambitions of ascent but from the necessity of those who fell through the cracks, quite literally. We are the Netwalkers of Lower Valtharion, and our world hangs like a vast spider's web beneath the industrial grandeur above.

The landscape of our domain defies conventional geometry. Imagine, if you will, a three-dimensional maze of cables, nets, and suspended walkways, all woven from salvaged materials that rain down from the forge-cities above. Steel cables thick as tree trunks serve as our primary anchors, stretched taut between the undersides of platforms, creating a tensioned framework upon which we've built our entire civilization. Between these main supports, we've strung countless smaller lines—copper wires braided with vine, chains linked with grown rope-wood, elastic bands of processed plant fiber mixed with rubber tubing discarded from above.

Our homes are pods and hammocks, each one unique, crafted from whatever materials we can scavenge or grow. Mine own dwelling sways gently in the warm updrafts from the forges, a teardrop-shaped cocoon woven from metallized silk—a material we create by feeding certain moths the metal shavings that drift down like snow. The walls shimmer with an oily iridescence, part organic, part industrial, neither fully one nor the other. Inside, every surface serves multiple purposes: walls that are also storage nets, floors that filter water through their weave, ceilings that collect condensation in precisely channeled grooves.

The physics of our existence shapes everything about us. We Netwalkers move differently than those above or those mythical beings said to live on solid ground below. Our bodies have adapted over generations—longer limbs, prehensile toes, a lower center of gravity that shifts intuitively with each sway of our web-world. Our bones are denser yet somehow more flexible, our muscles configured for constant micro-adjustments rather than brute strength. We dance rather than walk, our movement a fluid translation through three-dimensional space where "up" is merely the direction of the platforms and "down" is the theoretical terminus we all fear.

My skin bears the patterns of my trade—a Threadwright, one who repairs and extends the great net. The scars from cable burns create raised ridges across my palms and forearms, while chemical stains from treating the organic ropes have left constellation-like marks across my shoulders. These aren't blemishes but badges, each telling a story of a section saved from breaking, a new route woven into being. My hair, like most Netwalkers', grows in natural dreadlocks that we reinforce with wire, creating additional appendages for gripping and sensing vibrations through the web.

We've developed our own ecology down here, distinct from but dependent upon the world above. The Drip Gardens hang in mesh bags at regular intervals, fed by the constant condensation from the platforms. In these aerial gardens, we grow plants that have adapted to life without soil—air roots that spiral around our cables, drawing nutrients from the mineral-rich water and the debris caught in our nets. Phosphorescent fungi colonize the darker regions, their glow supplementing the filtered forge-light, creating patches of blue-green luminescence that pulse with their own slow rhythms.

Our animals, too, have evolved for this suspended life. The Net Runners—elongated rodents with opposable digits and tails twice their body length—scurry along our cables, their fur collecting metal particles that they then deposit in specific locations, inadvertently helping us map where the richest salvage falls from above. We've domesticated some, training them to carry messages through the web faster than any Netwalker could travel. Their chittering forms a constant background percussion to our lives, a telegraph of information flowing through the net.

The Membrane Gliders drift between levels of our web, their bodies translucent sheets of living tissue stretched between cartilaginous frames. They feed on the aerial plankton—tiny organisms that float in the warm air currents, themselves feeding on the industrial discharge from above. When the Gliders cluster, their bodies create temporary shelters from the occasional hot oil rain or cascade of metal shavings, their sacrifice remembered in the songs we sing while we work.

Our culture is one of constant maintenance and subtle rebellion. We know that those above rarely think of us—we're the emergency net, the catchers of the fallen, the recyclers of their excess. Yet we've transformed this neglect into freedom. Our governance is based on tension and balance rather than hierarchy. Decisions spread through the net via vibration patterns, each household adding their resonance to the whole until consensus emerges as a harmonious frequency or discord demands revision.

We speak in two languages: the verbal dialect evolved from the Valthari tongue above but softened, made more musical by our need to communicate across distances without echoing off hard surfaces; and the language of vibrations, transmitted through the cables themselves. A trained Netwalker can feel a conversation happening kilometers away, interpreting the subtle thrums and tensions that travel through our steel and fiber nervous system.

Water is paradoxically both abundant and precious here. It cascades from above in unpredictable patterns—sometimes the blessed clean condensation from the arboretums, sometimes the tainted runoff from industrial processes. We've become expert chemists by necessity, each household maintaining filtration systems woven into our very architecture. The filters are living things, colonies of bacteria and modified plants that we've cultivated to consume specific toxins while leaving beneficial minerals intact.

Our main challenge is the constant threat of structural failure. The web must be maintained continuously, and we've developed an almost mystical understanding of tension dynamics. We feel the health of our world through our feet, detecting micro-fractures in cables before they become critical. When a main support shows signs of wear, the entire local community mobilizes for a Reweaving—a complex dance of weight redistribution and rapid repair that requires perfect synchronization. These events are both terrifying and beautiful, hundreds of us moving as one organism to prevent catastrophic collapse.

The forge-waste from above is both bounty and curse. When a large piece of machinery falls, we must act quickly to catch it in sacrificial nets before it tears through inhabited areas. But that same machinery, once salvaged, provides materials for expansion and repair. We've become expert metallurgists, creating alloys in our small, suspended forges that sometimes exceed the quality of those made above—necessity breeding innovation.

Our children learn to walk on the nets before solid ground, their first steps a negotiation with gravity that shapes their entire worldview. They play games that would terrify a surface-dweller: racing through three-dimensional mazes of cables, diving from platform to catch-net, spinning cocoons around themselves and seeing who can emerge fastest. By adolescence, they can navigate by touch in complete darkness, their bodies so attuned to the web that they claim to dream in vibration patterns.

We mark time differently here. Without clear sight of the sky, we measure days by the forge-cycles above—the regular rhythm of production that creates predictable patterns of heat, light, and debris fall. Our months are counted in net-tensions, the gradual stretching and contracting of our world as temperature and load shift. Years are marked by the Great Droppings, when major components fall from above, creating new landmarks in our suspended geography.

There are mysteries in the depths of our web. Ancient sections exist that predate living memory, woven in patterns we can't quite replicate, using materials that seem impossibly strong yet organic. Some say these are remnants of the original builders of Valtharion, that we Netwalkers are descendants of the construction crews who built the platforms from below and chose to remain in the scaffolding rather than ascend. Others whisper of deeper nets still, layer upon layer descending into darkness where even we fear to venture.

I've heard tell of the Deep Weavers, Netwalkers who've adapted to the lowest levels where forge-light never reaches. They're said to be blind but able to navigate by vibration alone, their bodies elongated and pale, more spider than human. Whether they're real or legend, their supposed existence reminds us that adaptation has no limit, that life finds a way even in the most impossible places.

Our relationship with those above is complex. Officially, we don't exist—there's no record of population beneath the platforms in any Valthari census. But individually, connections form. Some Forge workers drop supplies deliberately, aiming for our catch-points. A few brave Valthari have even descended to trade directly, bringing manufactured goods in exchange for our unique materials and fungi. There's a growing black market in net-silk above, prized for its properties that no forge can replicate.

We tell stories of the mythical Ground, that theoretical solid surface that must exist somewhere below. Our eldest claim their grandparents remembered seeing it, though the tales grow more fantastic with each telling. Some describe vast oceans of liquid metal, others forests of crystal that shattered in some ancient calamity. The young largely dismiss these stories, content with our suspended world, but I sometimes wonder. When the web sways in certain patterns, when the deep cables groan with harmonics that speak of immense distance traveled, I think about what lies below our lowest nets.

In the end, we are what Valtharion forgot to account for—the space between spaces, the lives that thrive in margins. We are proof that existence persists wherever there's room to string a line, that humanity adapts not just to hostile environments but to conceptual impossibilities. Our web trembles with the life we've built upon it, every vibration a heartbeat, every tension a held breath, every repair an act of faith that tomorrow we'll still be suspended here, dancing between the forge-fires above and the unknown depths below.

My identity merges with my function—I am not just Lysa the Threadwright but a node in the greater web, a tension point that helps hold this impossible world together. My morning begins with the ritual checking of my sector's main cables, my bare feet reading their health like braille. I taste the air for chemical warnings, feel the temperature gradients that predict where the next cascade of materials might fall. My work is meditation and survival combined, each splice and weave an artistic expression that might save lives.

When the forge-light dims and our fungi begin their strongest glow, we gather in the common nets—reinforced sections designed for community. Here we share meals of processed air-root and filtered water, exchange vibration-poems, teach our children the hundred names for different cable tensions. We are not the heights of Valtharion's achievement but perhaps we are its heart—beating in the darkness between platforms, proving that life is not about reaching the sky but about finding your place in the spaces others overlook.

Tomorrow I'll wake to the familiar sway, place my feet upon the singing cables, and continue the eternal work of maintenance that is indistinguishable from the work of living. Each thread I weave becomes part of the grand design, each repair an act of defiance against gravity and forgetting. We are the Netwalkers, neither above nor below but suspended in between, and our world trembles with the beauty of the barely possible.
