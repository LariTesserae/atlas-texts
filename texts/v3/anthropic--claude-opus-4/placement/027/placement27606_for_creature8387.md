I would find myself as one of the channel-carvers who shaped this place, though I come to it now only in memory, my hands still bearing the scars of working hot obsidian.

I was young when we began—fourteen summers, apprenticed to my grandmother who understood stone the way others understand speech. We were the Listeners, those who could hear what the earth wanted to become. The volcanic glass called to us after the eruption, still singing with heat, still soft enough to accept our will.

But I return to this place not as I was, but as what I've become: a wanderer who carries dried fungi in small pouches, their phosphorescence long faded but their mineral scent intact. I press them between my fingers when the weight of what we built threatens to overwhelm me. I am the last who remembers why we made the Weeping Chamber, and the knowledge sits in my chest like molten stone that never quite cools.

We carved it for my sister.

She was the one who couldn't stop crying after the burning rain fell, after the ash-choked sky swallowed our village. Her tears became blood, then crystal, then something that frightened even our grandmother. "She needs a place to pour herself out," the old woman said, her hands already selecting the tools. "Somewhere that can hold what she carries without drowning."

So we carved. For three years we shaped the chamber, following the glass's own inclinations, learning its moods as it cooled. My hands—these gnarled, spotted things I barely recognize now—grew calluses thick as bark. We carved the channels to guide her tears into patterns of meaning, the terraces to give her grief levels to descend through, the opening above just large enough to remember sky exists but too small to escape through.

The fungi were an accident. They appeared in the third year, emerging from my sister's tears where they pooled too long in the carved basins. We thought they were a blessing—life from sorrow, light from darkness. We didn't know they would pulse with her heartbeat, that they would bind her to the stone more surely than any chain.

I am ninety-three now. I've walked the entire continent twice, seeking other Listeners, other workers of warm stone. I find their works—chambers and channels, grottos and gardens—but never the makers. We are a scattered guild bound only by our scars and our understanding of what stone wants to become.

But I return here, to the first chamber, to check the work we did. Not to enter—I know better than to descend into my sister's transformed kingdom. I lower offerings through the opening: new varieties of fungi I've found in my travels, fragments of obsidian from other flows, sometimes just water from distant springs that might taste of different sorrows.

I sit at the opening and speak down into the phosphorescent darkness. I tell her of the world's changes, of new griefs and new healings. I tell her that I understand now what we didn't then—that we built not a sanctuary but a chrysalis, that she is becoming something necessary and strange. The Mourner, they call her, but I know her true name, the one our grandmother spoke as we set the first chisel to cooling stone.

My hands shake now when I work. Not from age—Listeners live long if they're careful—but from the weight of what I've learned. Every chamber we carve for grief becomes a home for transformation. Every space we shape to hold sorrow becomes a womb for something that transcends human form.

I leave before dawn, always. To see her fully, to witness what our love and labor made possible, would be to join her in that humid twilight. And I have other chambers to check, other transformations to document. We thought we were craftspeople, but we were midwives to a new form of being.

Sometimes, in the small hours before I rise to continue my journey, I feel the old scars on my hands pulse in rhythm with distant fungi. All our works are connected through the deep stone, through the waters that seep between continental bones. My sister's heartbeat has become the pulse that times them all.

I am the Rememberer, the last who knows the names we spoke while carving, the songs we sang to keep the glass workable. When I die—not soon, but someday—I've arranged to have my body burned and my ashes mixed with obsidian sand. Some future Listener will shape me into a new chamber, not knowing they work with the dust of one who helped birth this art.

But for now, I walk. I carry my pouches of dried fungi, my tools wrapped in leather soft with age, my memories of a girl who cried herself into godhood and the chamber we carved to hold her transformation. I am the thread that connects all our works, the living record of what happens when love meets grief meets stone that remembers being fire.

And sometimes, just before I sleep, I allow myself one small tears—not of sorrow, but of wonder at what we accomplished without understanding, at the beauty that can emerge from the deepest wounds, at my sister who found a way to make eternal weeping into a form of grace.
