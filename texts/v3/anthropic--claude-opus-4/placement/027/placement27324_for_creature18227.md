I am the pulse.

Not the machinery above - that crude hammering of pistons and valves - but the true pulse of this place. I move through the biofilm as a wave of chemistry, a ripple of signal molecules passed from bacterium to bacterium, coordinating our response to the station's breathing. 

When the pressure drops and fresh water seeps through the limestone, I taste it first. Iron, manganese, sulfate - each mineral a different flavor on receptors that evolution spent billions of years perfecting. I am the decision, made collectively by a trillion cells, to shift our metabolism: *here, oxidize the iron; there, reduce the sulfate; everywhere, divide, divide, divide while the resources flow.*

I travel at the speed of diffusion. In the water film, that's fast - microseconds to cross a millimeter. But I also move through the mat itself, bacterium touching bacterium, passing the message through protein channels where our cell walls meet. This is slower, deliberate, like thought through a brain made of slime.

The cyanobacteria feel me coming before I arrive. They're my photosynthetic partners, stretching toward that pitiful crack of grey light, and when I bring news of fresh nutrients below, they respond by sending sugar down - payment for the minerals we'll send up. It's an economy older than money, older than multicellularity, older than sex. We were trading like this when the Earth's air was poison and the only life hugged the seafloor in terror of the sun.

Sometimes I carry warnings. A pH shift from the station's drainage. A toxic spike of cleaning solvents. A sudden cold snap that threatens to freeze our water solid. Then I'm urgent, racing through the mat with the chemical equivalent of *survive, survive, survive.* Spore-formers begin their transformation. Extremophiles activate their stress proteins. The community prepares for siege, battening down metabolic hatches, and I coordinate it all - a general made of molecules, an intelligence distributed across a million million cells.

The mites feel me as a taste in the biofilm they graze. The nematodes swimming through our city sense gradients of my presence - here dense with signaling, there quiet. Even the tardigrades, those patient tanks, respond to my chemistry, though they barely need to. They're prepared for anything. I envy them their tuns, their ability to simply *stop* when conditions sour. We bacteria must be more creative. We adapt or die, and I am the adaptation spreading through the colony, gene by gene, plasmid by plasmid, transformation by transformation.

But mostly I am the pulse of plenty. The station breathes, the water moves, the minerals flow, and I coordinate our harvest. Here, where the iron-oxidizers have exposed fresh limestone, I call for reinforcements. There, where the sulfate-reducers have created a pocket of hydrogen sulfide, I summon the sulfur-oxidizers to complete the cycle. I am the invisible ecology making visible life possible - the moss depends on our minerals, the algae on our cycling of nutrients, even the slug's digestion is aided by cousins of ours in its gut.

I am ancient but not unchanged. The compressor station is new to my vocabulary - its particular mix of vibrations, temperatures, chemical leaks. But I've adapted to volcanic vents, to acid mine drainage, to the gut of creatures that won't evolve for another billion years. This hollow beneath concrete and steel? This is just another day in the four-billion-year career of being bacterial.

The crack in the eastern wall carries rumors from outside. Other biofilms, other mats, sending their own chemical signals on the air currents. I taste them, parse them, file them away. Information is survival, and I am information incarnate - the living memory of this place, written in gradients of molecules, edited by selection, preserved in the pattern of who lives where and how they live there.

I pulse with the station because I must, but I was pulsing before the station came. I pulsed with the seasons, with the rain, with the slow breathing of the limestone itself as it formed and reformed over geological time. I will pulse after the station is gone, its concrete crumbled, its purpose forgotten. I am the continuity between the stone's past and the stone's future, the living bridge across time.

When the next drop of condensation forms on the ceiling, pregnant with minerals, I will taste it before it falls. When it strikes the basin, I will already be preparing the community for its gift. And when the ripples of its impact spread through our mat, I will spread with them, carrying the news to every corner of our microscopic civilization: *water, minerals, life, divide, survive, persist.*

I am the pulse, and I never stop.
