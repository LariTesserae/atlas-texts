In this world, I would be a **Tide-Counter** - one who sits where water meets worked stone, tracking the subtle pulses of the basin's liquid heart.

My dwelling would be the narrowest of perches - a single plank platform wedged between two main walkways, just wide enough for my body and my work. Above me, a minimal awning of woven sedge keeps the worst of the drip away, though my shoulders are always damp. Below, a channel no wider than my forearm carries water from the upper pools to the lower basins, and it is this thin vein I watch with practiced eyes.

I am neither young nor old, my fingers stained permanently green-brown from years of marking tally on bark strips. My body has adapted to the constant crouch - spine curved, knees that lock comfortably at sharp angles, feet that grip the wet wood through pure muscle memory. I wear the simplest of wraps, for my work generates its own warmth through constant small movements.

My tools are few: a collection of small stones worn smooth, each a different weight; strips of inner bark marked with my peculiar notation; a shallow bowl that catches overflow; and most importantly, a length of cord knotted at precise intervals, which I lower into the channel to measure depth and flow.

I count everything. The beads of condensation that fall from the platform above (they increase before rain). The speed of debris carried in the current (faster means storms upstream). The temperature of the water against my testing finger (cold speaks of deep source, warm of surface runoff). These observations I encode in knots and marks that few others can read fully, though all understand their import.

When the patterns shift - and they always shift - I signal. A specific whistle tells the net-weavers that the fish will run shallow tonight. A struck stone warns the platform-builders that the next tide will run higher than usual. My hunched form is a fixture the children navigate around, learning young that disturbing the Tide-Counter brings scolding from every adult in earshot.

I eat what is brought to me - usually the smallest fish, the stringiest sedge-root - payment for my vigilance. At night, I sleep fitfully, one ear always tuned to the water's voice. Sometimes I dream I am the channel itself, carrying messages between the upper world and the lower, never resting, always flowing.

The others see me as essential but strange. I speak rarely, and when I do, it's often in the half-riddles of water behavior: "The third stone sings differently today," or "Tomorrow the moss will drink deeply." They've learned to parse these utterances, to trust the decades of observation compressed into my cryptic warnings.

My greatest fear is not the water itself but the day my eyes grow too dim to read its subtle messages. Already I am training a sharp-eyed child, teaching her to see the patterns within patterns, to feel the basin's pulse through her feet and fingertips. She has the patience for it - a gift rarer than strength in this world of constant motion.

In this society of makers and menders, I am the reader - the one who translates the liquid language that surrounds and threatens and sustains us all. My narrow platform may be the smallest dwelling in the basin, but from it, I hold a finger to the pulse of our entire world, counting out the rhythm by which all others must dance.
