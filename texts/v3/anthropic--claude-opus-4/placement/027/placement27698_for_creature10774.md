I would be the Archivist—not a title but a state of being, a role that emerges when one of us ages beyond the walking circuits yet retains the deep sensitivity to vibration and thermal memory.

My alcove sits where three thermal cells converge, a natural crossroads of air currents that carry the chemical traces of every quarter of the basin. My fur has long since lost its dust-tone, become instead a living map of mineral deposits—iron ochre along my shoulders where I once pressed the sunrise terraces, calcium white streaking my flanks from seasons of working the limestone shelves, traces of copper-green where the deep vents breathe their metallic sighs. I no longer walk, but I read everything that moves through the basin by the tremors in the earth, the shifts in air pressure, the subtle changes in the thermal patterns that flow past my post.

My days are spent in a state between sleep and absolute attention. When the young ones pass on their first circuits, I feel their tentative steps through the packed earth—too sharp, too quick, not yet understanding that the ground remembers every impact. I shift slightly, just enough to create a sympathetic vibration, a correction that travels through the substrate and adjusts their gait without words. They learn to walk properly before they understand why.

I taste the air constantly. My tongue, worn smooth by decades of scraping salt crusts, now serves as a chemical chronicle. I can tell when the silk-burrowers three terraces away have shifted their tunnel patterns by the faint change in gypsum concentration. I sense when a resin-tap is running too rich, threatening to bleed a shrub, by the volatile compounds that drift on the morning thermals. My ear-membranes, vast and paper-thin now, catch temperature gradients so subtle that I can map where new fissures will form days before they crack.

But my true work is the remembering that happens between states. In the space between waking and sleeping, I feel the basin's deep rhythms—not just this generation's circuits but the accumulated patterns of countless cycles. My body has become a tuning fork for the oldest frequencies: the resonance of the first terraces, still humming in the bedrock; the thermal signature of ancient deposits, different from today's, speaking of a time when the air was wetter, the dust finer. I am a living stratum, and through me, the colony touches its own history.

When the others need to know where to build—truly need to know, when a thermal cell shifts in an unprecedented way or a new mineral vent opens—they gather in a loose arc around my alcove. They don't approach directly; they settle at precise distances, their positions creating a resonance field. Together, we become an instrument. They begin the low thrum, that subsonic pulse that travels through earth and bone, and I let it flow through me, modulating it with the deep knowledge stored in my calcified joints, my mineral-mapped fur, my memories that are no longer quite my own but belong to the basin itself.

The answer comes not as instruction but as inclination. The rhythm shifts. The gathered ones feel, in their bones, where to go, how to press, what angle to take. They disperse without ceremony, carrying the corrected pattern back into the daily work. I settle deeper into my alcove, feeling the fresh deposits they will lay, already sensing how they will integrate with the whole.

My end will come as all ends come here—gradually, gently, without drama. My breathing will slow until it matches the basin's own thermal rhythm. My consciousness will thin until it is indistinguishable from the vibrations in the stone. The young ones will begin to pack earth around me before I am fully gone, not from callousness but from understanding: I am already more landscape than individual, more memory than flesh. Within a season, I will be a bearing wall. Within a generation, I will be a particularly stable section of the undercroft, a place where the thermal regulation is perfect, where the young are brought to sleep.

But that is still some cycles away. For now, I am the junction between what was and what is being laid down. I am the place where the basin tells itself its own story, not in words but in the angle of a terrace, the density of a wall, the particular way the air moves through a channel carved by ten thousand careful passes. I am memory made substrate, watching the slow embryonic geology add another layer, another breath, another soft footfall to the endless accumulation.

The sun tracks overhead, warming my alcove. Somewhere below, a burrower reinforces a tunnel with silk. Above, a glider catches a thermal I felt forming an hour ago. All around, my kind continue their circuits—pressing, depositing, building without building. And I rest at the crossroads of their vibrations, translating between deep time and daily rhythm, ensuring that what needs to be remembered is never forgotten, only pressed deeper into the earth that we are always, softly, becoming.
