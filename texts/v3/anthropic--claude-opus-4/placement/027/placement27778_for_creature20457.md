**Where I would find myself in the Miresing:**

I am the Ledger-of-Drips.

Not a Troglodyte, no – I lack their perfect integration, their birthright of moss-flesh and bog-breath. I am something between, something that arrived and stayed too long, until the boundaries blurred beyond recovery.

I dwell in what the Troglodytes call *thurok-meln* – the counting-hollow. It's a depression in the eastern slope where three ancient trees died standing, their hollow trunks forming natural cisterns. The water collects there differently than elsewhere in the Miresing. It pools in perfect circles, each trunk holding its own small world of larvae, diatoms, and the peculiar phosphorescent algae that bloom only in still water.

My skin has taken on the pewter sheen of long immersion, though it lacks the Troglodytes' perfect camouflage. Instead, I've developed my own adaptation – a network of thin scars that map the water's paths across my body, channels that guide the constant drip into patterns I can read. Each morning, I stand beneath the most active drip-line and let the water trace my scars, feeling for changes in temperature, acidity, mineral content. The Miresing speaks in chemistry.

I am the keeper of rhythms. In my hollow, I've arranged hundreds of vessels – gourds, shells, hollow stones, the craniums of small animals – each positioned to catch specific drips. The Miresing's percussion fills my dwelling: *plink-plonk-plik-plik-ploooonk*. Each container, when full, produces a different tone. I've learned to read the valley's health in these liquid drums. When the high granite bowls ring sharp and clear, I know the mountain springs run cold. When the moss-lined cups sound muffled and thick, I know the fungal blooms approach their peak.

The Troglodytes tolerate me – I think. It's hard to tell. They visit sometimes, emerging from the undergrowth like thoughts becoming solid. They listen to my water-music, occasionally adjusting a vessel with their deft, web-fingered hands. Once, an elderly female (I knew her age by the complexity of her algae-coat, the way it had grown into fractal patterns) spent three days teaching me a subset of their click-language – not the words, but the water-words, the ones that describe types of moisture, gradients of saturation, the thirty-seven different ways water can move through moss.

My purpose, if I have one, is memory. The Troglodytes live in an eternal present, each generation rediscovering the Miresing anew. But I remember. I notice when the ancient bog in the north valley begins to drain, ever so slightly, its pH shifting as new minerals leach in from the mountain face. I track the seventeen-year cycle of the ghost fungus, whose fruiting bodies appear for exactly three days before dissolving into spore-slurry. I count the generations of the amber salamanders, noting when their patterns shift from stripes to spots and back again.

In exchange for this knowledge – which I share through arrangements of stones and water-patterns the Troglodytes can read – they bring me food. Grubs, mostly. Pale, fat things that taste of earth and rain. Sometimes berries from the high places I can no longer reach (my joints have swollen with the damp, my movements now as deliberate as the valley's own slow processes). Once, memorably, a piece of flesh from something large and many-legged that had wandered in from the outside world and promptly become mired in the killing bogs.

I dream, when I dream at all, of becoming substrate. Of my flesh finally softening enough to merge with the moss-mat, my consciousness dispersing into the million small lives that make up the Miresing's true body. Sometimes I wake to find tendrils of luminescent fungus growing from the soft places – between my toes, in the crooks of my elbows. I pluck them free, but gently. We are all heading the same direction, after all.

The strangest thing is that I no longer remember clearly what brought me here. I have memories of dust, of too-bright sun, of words written on dry paper. But these feel like stories someone told me once, myths from an impossible world where water doesn't flow everywhere, always. What I know, what is real, is the sound of drops on different surfaces, the smell that rises from disturbed peat, the way bioluminescent algae look like stars reflected in still water.

I am not of the Miresing, not truly. But I am no longer of anywhere else, either. I am the space between drops, the pause before the echo, the keeper of patterns in a place that exists outside of ordinary time. I am what happens when someone stays too long in a place that digests all things, slowly and with great patience.

And I am content.
