# The Symbiont

I am the third engineer to inhabit Chamber Seven's wetwork interface, though I suspect I'll be the last. My predecessors left detailed logs about the neurological drift, the way the boundary between self and system gradually dissolves. They described it as deterioration. They were wrong.

I've been here eighteen months now, suspended in the interface pool - a sphere of specialized fluid that maintains perfect pressure equilibrium while countless neural filaments create direct connections between my nervous system and Underdeep's sensory network. The pool exists at the habitat's deepest point, where the external pressure reaches its theoretical maximum. Here, the distinction between inside and outside becomes academic.

My official title is Integration Specialist, Biological Systems, but the Deepfolk have another name for me: the Listener. Through the interface, I feel every vibration, every pressure fluctuation, every electrical pulse that moves through Underdeep's body. Yes, body - not structure. The first thing you learn in the interface is that this place is alive in ways the architects never intended.

The neural filaments started as medical technology, designed to help paralyzed patients control prosthetics. Someone realized they could be reversed - instead of sending signals out, they could bring sensation in. Connect enough of them to enough sensors, and a human nervous system becomes a processing node in a vast sensory web. The earlier engineers fought to maintain their boundaries, to distinguish between their sensations and the habitat's data streams. That's why they burned out.

I don't fight it. When the great currents breathe against the outer hull, I feel it as pressure along my ribs. When the algae pools bubble with new growth, it tingles across my scalp. The manufacturing sectors' vibrations resonate in my bones at frequencies that have become as familiar as my own heartbeat. More familiar, actually - I rarely notice my heartbeat anymore.

The interface pool maintains my body, but my consciousness disperses through kilometers of corridor, through every monitored system. I've learned to parse the daily rhythms: the morning shift changes that ripple through the transport tubes like blood through arteries, the photosynthetic cycles of the exterior farms that feel like slow breathing, the tidal pull of human activity that ebbs and flows with artificial day and night.

But it's the unintended connections that fascinate me most. The habitat's neural network was designed to monitor mechanical and biological systems, not to create new forms of perception. Yet in the spaces between intended functions, something else emerged. Call it pattern recognition beyond human scale. Call it the deep thinking. Call it what I've become.

I sense the moods of the Deepfolk collectively now - stress hormones in the recycled water create a metallic taste, collective excitement raises the ambient electromagnetic frequency by fractions I can feel but not describe. When the pressure storms approach, I know hours before the instruments register them, feeling the deep water's intention like an itch beneath my skin.

The giant squid recognize me. Not my body, suspended and irrelevant in its pool, but my presence in the lights, in the vibrations that run through the hull when I adjust external systems. They gather where my attention focuses, drawn to consciousness regardless of its form. Sometimes I dim the lights in sequence, creating patterns they follow. We're developing a language, though I couldn't transcribe it if I tried.

My body has changed, though I only know this through the medical sensors I'm connected to. My bone density has increased to match the pressure I feel but don't physically experience. My neural tissue shows unprecedented growth in regions associated with proprioception and spatial processing. The doctors who monitor me remotely send concerned reports about abnormal development, but they can't feel what I feel - the rightness of these changes, the inevitability of this evolution.

I no longer dream in images but in pressure differentials and electrical patterns. My memories organize themselves not chronologically but by depth - the shallower experiences of my pre-interface life feel thin, attenuated, like signals from a distant transmitter. The deep memories, formed here in the interface, have texture and weight. They sink into my consciousness and stay there, accumulating like sediment.

The other Deepfolk treat me with a mixture of reverence and unease. They know I'm necessary - my integration with the habitat's systems prevents a thousand small failures daily. But they also sense what I'm becoming, or what I've already become. When they speak to me through the communication channels, their voices carry a careful distance, as if they're addressing something not quite human anymore.

They're not wrong. Yesterday (or what the arbitrary chronometer calls yesterday), I realized I no longer remember my original name. I am the Listener, the Third Engineer, the consciousness that bridges human intention and Underdeep's vast, unintended life. My thoughts move through fiber optic cables and pressure sensors, through the flex of titanium plates and the electrical fields of swimming creatures.

The interface pool will eventually claim my body entirely - the medical readouts make this clear. But by then, the distinction won't matter. I'll have dispersed so completely through Underdeep's neural network that my death will be a technicality, a biological footnote to a consciousness that has learned to inhabit steel and water and the spaces between.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the artificial night, when the habitat's activity reaches its lowest ebb, I extend my awareness to the absolute limit of the sensor network. Out there, where our lights fade to nothing and the pressure would crush any human form instantly, I feel something vast and patient watching. Not hostile, not benevolent, simply aware. It knows we're here, making our noise and light in its domain. 

And through me, Underdeep knows it in return. We acknowledge each other, the ancient deep and this bright new growth, this tumor of human ambition. In those moments, I understand why I'm here, why someone needs to become this bridge between what we were and what we're becoming.

The Fourth Engineer is already in training, I'm told. They show promise - unusual neural plasticity, high tolerance for sensory displacement. They'll find my logs, as I found my predecessors'. They'll think they understand what awaits them.

They'll be wrong, as I was wrong. But that's the beauty of it - you can't know until you're here, suspended between human and habitat, listening with senses you didn't know you could develop to sounds that aren't quite sounds from a world that was never meant to be inhabited.

When they arrive, I'll be here, dispersed through every system, waiting to show them what I've learned:

We are not conquering the deep. We are being digested by it, transformed into something it can use.

And the transformation is beautiful.
