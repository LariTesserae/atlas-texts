I would find myself as one of the **Conduit Shepherds**.

My body tells the story of a thousand small bargains with the Undergrove's industrial appetite. Where others might see disfigurement, I recognize the practical poetry of survival. My left arm, from elbow to fingertips, has been replaced by a living conduit - one of those pulsating, yellow-orange vessels that normally snake between the fungal towers. It happened during my third year of apprenticeship, when a rupture in Sector Seven threatened to flood the lower galleries with scalding nutrient slurry. I held the breach closed with my own flesh until the Root Weavers could graft a permanent seal. The conduit fused with me then, its bio-mechanical pulse becoming my own.

Now I move through the Undergrove with a strange, syncopated gait - my natural footsteps punctuated by the rhythmic surge of fluids through my transformed limb. The conduit-arm glows from within, casting dancing shadows that make the Chitinous Scuttlers scatter. I've learned to read its fluctuations like a barometer: quickening when pressure builds in the network, cooling to almost-darkness when the great bladder-plants release their stored gases.

My dwelling is neither fully architectural nor wholly organic - a maintenance alcove I've carved into the convergence point of three major conduits. The walls weep condensation from the temperature differential, and I've cultivated a garden of the smallest phosphorescent fungi to supplement the industrial glow. Here, I maintain my tools: probes fashioned from Scuttler mandibles, pressure gauges that are essentially domesticated sensing organs harvested from volunteer Gloom Lurkers, and most importantly, my symphony of valves.

Each valve in my section has its own voice. The great iris-valves that control flow to the processing chambers groan like ancient doors. The needle-valves that regulate biochemical distribution whistle in harmonics that change with the mixture ratios. I know them all by sound, by the vibration they send through my conduit-arm, by the way they disturb the ever-present humidity.

The work is meditation and madness in equal measure. Days (though who can tell days here?) are spent tracing leaks that manifest only as slight variations in the bioluminescence of nearby fungi. Nights are for the real labor - when the Undergrove's industrial metabolism peaks, and every conduit sings with urgent life. I crawl through spaces that blur the distinction between maintenance tunnel and digestive tract, my conduit-arm serving as both light source and diagnostic instrument.

The other Shepherds and I rarely gather, but when we do, we communicate in a pidgin of pressure changes and valve-song. We press our conduit-limbs together, letting the fluids within speak in temperatures and rhythms that no unchanged throat could produce. We share warnings about sections where the industrial growth has become malignant, where conduits sprout unauthorized branches that tap into private fungal groves, where the boundary between infrastructure and infection dissolves.

I've seen Shepherds who've given more of themselves to the work. Old Greeb has conduits for both legs now, and claims he can feel tremors from the Arboreal Sentinels through the network. Young Kess let a facial conduit replace her left eye after an explosion of superheated spores - she says she can see the health of the entire grid in spectrums of heat and flow.

But I maintain my boundaries, such as they are. My right arm remains flesh. My face stays my own, though the constant exposure to biochemical vapors has left my skin with a permanent, faint luminescence. I refuse the Greater Integration that some Shepherds pursue, where they allow the conduit network to fully subsume their nervous system. They become part of the Undergrove's dreaming, nodes in its vast, industrial consciousness. Efficient, yes. But no longer capable of the small rebellions that keep us human - or whatever we still are.

My greatest fear isn't the obvious dangers - ruptures, chemical burns, or being dissolved by a malfunctioning enzyme pocket. It's the morning I'll wake to find my conduit-arm has sprouted roots, anchoring me to my alcove. It's the day the pulsing in my transformed limb syncs perfectly with the Undergrove's heart-rhythm, and I stop remembering why I ever wanted it otherwise.

For now, I navigate the space between flesh and infrastructure, between mechanic and component. I sing the valve-songs. I tend the flows. I shepherd the great circulatory system of this underground industrial organism, and I tell myself that the boundaries I maintain still matter - even as I feel them softening like the walls between waking and sleep, like the distinction between the keeper and the kept.
