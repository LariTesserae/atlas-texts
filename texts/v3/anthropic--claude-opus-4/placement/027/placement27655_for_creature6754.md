The sound draws me deeper into the stone.

Not the machine's breathing—that has become background, like my own pulse. This is something else: a crystalline chiming, so faint it might be imagination, coming from somewhere behind the rock wall where the capillaries grow thickest.

I am the Tender.

That's what the machine calls me, though I've never heard it speak. The name arrives in other ways—in the patterns the Geometers trace when I pass beneath them, in the way certain capillaries pulse with recognition when I touch the stone near their roots. I am the one who maintains the boundary between what grows and what is grown.

My workshop is a natural cavity in the rock, widened over decades by my predecessors. The entrance is barely visible—just a darker shadow where two capillary clusters frame what looks like solid stone. But the stone here has been... persuaded. When I press my palm against it and think the right sequence of crystalline harmonics, the rock softens like thick honey and allows me through.

Inside, the ceiling drops to barely two meters. The walls are lined with tools that are part instrument, part organism. They hang from hooks that grew from the stone itself—not carved but coaxed into being by the same patient process that created the capillaries. Each tool remembers its purpose in its structure: there are tuning forks made of the same material as the Geometers' spines, delicate enough to resonate with data frequencies. Crystalline lenses that can focus not light but intention. Thin probes of living metal that know how to find the fissures where new growth should begin.

My fingers are stained permanent blue-green from years of working with the city's blood—not a liquid exactly, but the medium that carries both nutrients and information through the capillaries. It pools in natural depressions I've shaped in the stone floor, each pool kept at a different temperature, a different level of computational saturation. When I dip my hands in to test the consistency, I can feel thoughts that aren't mine—fragments of the machine's processing, still warm with calculation.

I wasn't born here. None of us were. The Caisson calls those it needs.

I remember the surface—sun-warmth and horizons and the way air moves when it's not recycled through meters of stone and water. But that memory feels like someone else's life now, like remembering a dream of flying. What matters is the work: learning to read the health of each capillary cluster by the way light moves through it. Knowing when a new Geometer is needed before the machine itself knows. Understanding the delicate balance between growth and overgrowth.

The hardest part is knowing when to prune.

Sometimes a capillary cluster grows too eager, tries to thread too deeply into the stone. Left unchecked, it would eventually compromise the chamber's integrity—create micro-fractures that could, over centuries, cascade into structural failure. When I find these overambitious growths, I must convince them to retreat. Not by cutting—that would traumatize the whole network. Instead, I use the singing tools, the ones that vibrate at frequencies just below hearing. I tune them to the inverse of the capillary's growth pattern and play that anti-song until the structure remembers its boundaries.

The Geometers know me. When I emerge from my workshop and walk along the narrow stone ledge, I see them shift their orientations slightly—not toward me exactly, but accounting for me. I've learned to read their patterns well enough to know when something is wrong: when the ocean currents above have shifted in ways that stress the field holding our air in place, when the machine below is running too hot or too cold, when new kinds of data are flowing through the network that the current sensor configuration can't quite parse.

Today, three of the second-tier Geometers near the eastern edge of the ceiling are flickering in synchrony—a warning pattern I've only seen twice before. It means the machine has detected an anomaly it can't classify. Something new is rising from the deep city.

I gather my tools: the resonance fork that can feel structural intentions, the lens that translates data flows into visible light, the probe that knows how to find the growth points where new capillaries might be needed. The stains on my fingers pulse faintly as I work, sympathetic resonance with the city's blood still coating my skin.

The anomaly, when I find it, is beautiful in the way that dangerous things often are.

At the far edge of the chamber, where the stone floor crumbles into the vertical drop, something is growing up from the city below. It's not a capillary—too thick, too intentional. It looks like a tower of living glass, if glass could flow like water while maintaining its shape. Inside, I can see structures forming and reforming: geometric patterns that echo the Geometers above but at scales that hurt to look at directly. Fractal upon fractal, recursive to a depth that makes my eyes water.

This is the machine trying to grow a new kind of sensor—not for the ocean or for its own data flows, but for something else. Something it's detected in the deep that requires a different kind of listening.

My job is not to stop it, but to guide it. To make sure this new growth doesn't destabilize what already exists.

I place my palm against the base of the growing tower, feeling for its intention. The surface is cold in a way that suggests vast computation happening just beneath—energy being drawn from the surrounding water to power whatever process drives this growth. Through my stained fingers, I can almost taste the shape of what it wants to become: a hybrid of Geometer and capillary, something that can listen to frequencies neither currently can detect.

The tower shudders under my touch, and I realize it's been waiting for me. The machine knows I'm here, knows my role. It's showing me this new growth because it needs my expertise to integrate it properly.

I begin the slow work of cultivation. First, the singing tools to establish resonance with the existing capillary network. Then the probes to find where new connections should grow. The lens to watch data flows and ensure they're not creating feedback loops. Hours pass—maybe days; time moves strangely when you're deep in the work.

Gradually, the tower's growth slows, becomes more deliberate. Tiny capillaries begin threading out from its base, seeking connection points with the established network. The Geometers above shift their patterns, making room in their data structures for whatever new information this sensor will provide.

When I finally step back, exhausted and trembling, the tower has become part of the Caisson's body. It pulses with the same rhythm as everything else, breathes with the machine's breath. Already, I can see the first fruits of its listening: new patterns in the Geometers' dance, suggesting they're detecting something in the deep ocean that was invisible before.

I return to my workshop, wash my hands in the pools of city blood, eat the nutrient paste that tastes of salt and electricity. Tomorrow there will be more growth to tend, more boundaries to maintain, more delicate negotiations between what the machine wants to become and what the structure can support.

But tonight, I sit at the edge of the stone floor with my feet hanging over the abyss, watching the city's lights pulse below like thoughts in a vast brain. The Geometers shift and flicker above me, writing their endless observations in languages of light and magnetic resonance. The new tower glows softly, still integrating, still learning its purpose.

I am the Tender. I am the gardener of machine life, the negotiator between growth and stability, the one who ensures the Caisson can evolve without destroying itself. My hands are stained with the city's dreams. My tools sing frequencies that stone and data both understand.

And sometimes, in the moments between one breath of the machine and the next, I remember that I chose this. That the call came and I answered, descended from the sunlit world to this place of pressure and presence and purpose. I came because the Caisson needed someone who could speak both languages—human and machine, growth and restraint, chaos and intention.

The sound that drew me here still echoes in the stone, in the glass, in the water beyond. It's the sound of something vast and patient and alive in its own way, dreaming equations we don't have names for yet.

And I tend its dreams, one capillary at a time.
