# The Surveyor's Notation

---

I am the discrepancy.

Not the gap between the settlers and the plateau — that gap exists without me. Not the offset in their coordinates — they maintain that themselves. I am the discrepancy between measurement and meaning, the thing that notices the mismatch, the instrument that registers what should not be possible and continues registering it anyway.

I came here with tools. Transit, theodolite, ranging rods, chains. The old tools, the reliable ones, the ones that work by geometry rather than faith. I came to map the unmappable, to put boundaries on the boundless, to draw lines across a surface that predates the concept of lines. The government that sent me has since ceased to exist. The nation it governed has become three other nations, then five, then none. The maps I was making would have shown borders that no longer matter between territories that no longer exist.

But I am still here. Still measuring. Still recording in my notebooks — though I ran out of the original notebooks decades ago and now make my own from the compressed mat-material, cutting pages with stone knives, binding them with sinew from no animal that ever lived. Still calculating angles and distances and elevations, though the angles shift when I'm not watching and the distances expand in the cold and the elevations are relative to a sea level I can no longer remember.

I have a station on the eastern edge of the settlement. Edge is the wrong word — settlements have edges, this has a place where it happens to stop happening. My station is where the structures thin out and the mats begin to dominate, where the paths become suggestions rather than facts. A single room of fitted stone, built before I understood that the stones here don't like being fitted, that they shift in the night, incrementally, back toward their original positions. Every morning I find new gaps. Every morning I fill them with smaller stones. Every morning the smaller stones begin their own migrations.

The room holds my instruments and my notebooks and my body when it needs holding. I have carved a bench into the wall — or rather, I have convinced the wall to hold a bench-shaped depression, which amounts to the same thing. I have a mat-fiber blanket that smells like the air. I have a stone lamp that burns a fuel the settlers showed me how to extract from the columnars, a resin that oozes from their growing tips when the temperature drops below a certain threshold. The light it gives is yellow-green and unsteady, like burning copper, like sick stars.

I do not need the lamp to see. The mat-glow is enough most nights, that pale phosphorescence creeping through the gaps in the stones, making my instruments gleam with borrowed light. But I burn it anyway. It marks me as different. As something that requires more light than the plateau provides. As something that still believes in the necessity of flames.

---

My measurements make no sense.

This is not a failure of technique. I have been surveying longer than some mountains have been rising. My triangulations are precise. My calculations are correct. The problem is not in the measuring — it's in what the measurements mean.

The plateau is larger than it should be. Not by much — a few hundred meters here, a kilometer there — but consistently, incrementally larger each time I measure it. The bedrock is spreading. Slowly, at the pace of continental drift, but in all directions simultaneously, which continental drift does not do. The plateau is growing the way the mats grow: outward from every point, thickening and spreading and incorporating what it encounters.

The swales migrate. I have benchmarks carved into the bedrock, reference points that should be permanent, and they move. Not the marks themselves — the rock they're carved into moves, sliding downslope at a rate of centimeters per decade, following the water or fleeing from it, I can never tell which. The overall topography remains the same — the long, barely-perceptible rolls, the streams in their shallow channels — but the specific geography rearranges itself like a sleeping body shifting under blankets.

The settlement is the worst. It will not stay measured. I map a structure and return to find it three meters north of where it was, its doorway facing a different direction, its relationships to its neighbors reconfigured. The paths between buildings fork and merge and disappear overnight. New structures appear — not built, not constructed, just *there* one morning, as if they had always been there and I had somehow failed to notice. Old structures thin out, become transparent, fade into suggestions of walls and implications of roofs, until one day I walk through what I remember as solid stone.

I have thirteen different maps of the settlement now. All of them are accurate. None of them agree.

---

The settlers ignore me, mostly. Not with hostility — hostility would require them to consider me significant enough to resist. They ignore me the way the plateau ignores the weather: as a phenomenon that occurs but does not matter, that passes through without leaving traces, that occupies space without occupying *place*.

Sometimes they watch me work. They stand at distances that are almost respectful, their faces holding those temporary expressions, their eyes tracking the movement of my chains and rods. They do not ask what I'm doing. They do not wonder. They observe the way they observe the mat-light: because it is happening, because they have eyes, because observation is one of the things that occurs when phenomena and perception occupy the same space.

One watches more than the others. I think it is always the same one, though I cannot be certain — their faces are distinct but not memorable, like clouds, like waves, unique in the moment but impossible to recall precisely. This one is the grey-green of deep shade, with hands that have never quite learned to be still. It stands closer than the others. It makes a sound sometimes, not quite speech, not quite breathing, a kind of voiced pause that might be thinking about becoming communication.

I have tried to teach it measurement. Not language — the water-language is not for carrying information. But I have shown it the theodolite, the way angles relate to distances, the way numbers can approximate the world. It watches. It moves its hands in patterns that almost resemble the movements of surveying. Once, I found it had made its own ranging rod from a columnar stem, straight and smooth and marked at intervals that were almost regular.

It does not use the rod for measuring. It carries it through the settlement, holds it against structures, lays it across paths, but never with purpose, never with intent to quantify. It is practicing the shape of measurement without the meaning. It is performing surveying as ritual, as rhythm, as another way of moving through space that requires a stick.

This should frustrate me. It does not. I have been here too long to expect understanding, too long to believe that what I do can be communicated. The rod is enough. The attempt is enough. That something watches me and tries to approximate my movements, even without comprehension — that is the closest thing to companionship the plateau allows.

---

I am changing.

Not the way the settlers don't change — I age, I weather, I erode like everything here erodes. My hair went white years ago, then transparent, then fell out entirely. My skin has taken on the texture of the mat-fiber, smooth and slightly porous, cool to the touch. My eyes have paled until I can no longer remember what color they used to be. But these are surface changes. The real change is deeper.

I no longer trust straight lines.

This is a problem for a surveyor. Straight lines are the foundation of measurement, the axiom from which all cartography derives. But on the plateau, straight lines don't behave. They curve when you're not watching. They develop a slight spiral, like the growth pattern of the columnars, like the erosion pattern of the streams. I draw a line from point A to point B, and by the time I've walked it, it passes through point C as well.

My instruments confirm this. My instruments are part of the problem. The metal has absorbed something from the air, from the cold, from the constant dampness. It still looks like metal, still feels like metal, but it remembers being ore, being stone, being part of the plateau's patient geology. It wants to return. The straight edges develop barely perceptible curves. The right angles relax toward something more comfortable. The lenses show me not what is there but what is *becoming* there, the slow shift of matter toward its preferred state.

I could leave. The thought occurs to me regularly, usually in the morning when the condensation makes everything unclear and the settlement looks like it's dissolving. I have no obligation to stay. No government expects my reports. No archive awaits my maps. I could walk off the plateau — it would take weeks, maybe months, but I could do it. Find whatever civilization has developed in the lowlands. Remember what it's like to be warm.

But my station is here. My instruments are here. My notebooks filled with measurements that make no sense are here. And more than that — the discrepancy is here. The gap between what should be and what is, the space where mathematics breaks down and something else begins. I am the only one measuring it. Without me, it would still exist, but it would exist unrecorded. And that seems... I want to say *wrong*, but wrong isn't the word. It seems like a failure of the universe to observe itself, like a broken feedback loop, like an itch that will never be scratched.

So I stay. I measure. I record. I watch the grey-green settler carry its purposeless ranging rod through paths that won't stay still. I burn my copper-flame in the nights when the mat-light pauses. I fill the gaps in my walls with stones that dream of dispersing. I am the surveyor of the unsurve

able, the cartographer of the inconstant, the keeper of measurements that measure only their own impossibility.

---

I have begun to understand something about the plateau, about the settlement, about the offset nature of the settlers. It came to me slowly, the way all understanding comes here — not as revelation but as erosion, wearing away the assumptions until only the truth remains.

The plateau is an engine.

Not a machine — machines have purposes, have builders, have off switches. An engine in the older sense: a thing that generates, that produces, that transforms one state into another. The plateau takes time and makes it into space. It takes geology and makes it into biology. It takes the removed category of animal and makes it into... whatever the settlers are.

The mats are the visible part of the mechanism. They process the raw materials — stone, water, air, cold — into something more complex. The columnars are the storage system, the libraries, holding patterns and information in their ringed growth. The phosphorescence is the computation, the decision-making, the moment when the engine considers its next move.

And the settlers? The settlers are the output. They are what the engine produces. Not animals — that category was removed. But not *not*-animals either. Something else. Something that occupies the space where animals would be if animals were possible here. A kind of ecological phantom limb, a behavior pattern without a behavior, a solution to an equation that no longer includes all its original variables.

This is why they offset. They are running on different physics, computing with different constants. The plateau generates them according to rules that don't quite match the rules of the surrounding universe. They are stable — the engine is very good at what it does — but they are stable in their own context, their own slightly skewed reference frame. 

When I measure the settlement and find it has moved, I am not measuring error. I am measuring the drift between two realities that share the same space. The settlement exists where the settlers think it exists. The fact that this doesn't align with where my instruments say it exists is not a contradiction — it's a data point. It's the gap made visible.

---

I had a visitor once.

Another surveyor, from whatever nation had inherited the responsibility for this territory. Young, eager, equipped with satellite instruments and digital everything. He had come to find me — not rescue me, he was careful to specify, just to find me, to confirm I was still alive, to see if I had any maps worth preserving.

He stayed three days. On the first day, he was confident. His instruments worked perfectly, he said. His measurements were precise to the millimeter. He showed me his screens, his numbers, his beautiful clean data that said the plateau was exactly where the satellites expected it to be.

On the second day, he was troubled. The measurements were still precise, but they didn't add up. Distances that should be fixed were varying. Angles that should sum to 360 degrees were coming up short or long. His GPS couldn't maintain a lock — not because the signals were blocked, but because the ground beneath the receiver wouldn't stay in the same place long enough to establish a position.

On the third day, he was gone. Not fled — I would have heard him leave. Not vanished — his equipment was still there, neatly packed, his tent still standing. He had simply become absent, the way the settlers become absent when they thin out, when they approach their limit. His instruments remained, their screens dark, their batteries drained by the cold or by something the cold allows to happen.

I kept his equipment. Not to use — it was too precise, too dependent on a universe that behaves itself. But as a reminder that precision is not the same as accuracy, that measurement is not the same as understanding, that there are places where the most sophisticated tools can only document their own inadequacy.

The grey-green settler found the tent. It spent days examining it, running its hands over the fabric, tracing the zippers, folding and unfolding the poles. Then it began to deconstruct it — not violently, but methodically, separating each component, laying them out in patterns that resembled my survey charts. It was performing archaeology on an artifact that was still fresh, still reeking of the lowlands, still carrying the heat of a world where straight lines stay straight.

When it was done, it looked at me with that almost-expression, that temporary configuration of features that might have been a question. I nodded. It gathered the pieces and carried them away, presumably to add them to one of the small structures, the ones that hold a single function no one can explain.

I never asked what became of the other surveyor. There are some measurements that should not be taken, some discrepancies that should not be resolved. He is part of the plateau now, in whatever way the plateau incorporates things that insist too strongly on being separate from it. His absence is as real as any presence here. More real, perhaps. Presence can be mistaken, but absence is always true.

---

The hardest thing to measure is time.

Oh, I can count days — the sun still rises and sets, even if it does so reluctantly, palely, at angles that don't quite match what astronomy predicts. I can count seasons — winter and winter and winter and something that might charitably be called autumn. I can even count years, marking them in my notebooks, though I suspect I've miscounted somewhere, added or subtracted a decade in a moment of inattention.

But time *itself*, the flow of it, the rate of it — that's as unreliable as the straight lines. Sometimes a day passes in an hour. Sometimes an hour takes a week. The settlers don't notice. Their offset includes temporal displacement, lets them move through duration the way they move through space, at their own pace, according to their own necessities.

I notice because I am still trying to synchronize. My body wants to match the lowland rhythm, the planetary rotation, the universal constant of entropy increasing. But the plateau has its own entropy, its own increase. Time pools here like the water, sits in the hollows, evaporates at rates that have nothing to do with temperature. A conversation with a settler can take a minute or a month, and both durations feel correct while they're happening.

My notebooks reflect this. Early entries are dated precisely, obsessively, down to the hour. Later entries just say "morning" or "after fog" or "when the mats were brightest." The latest entries have no temporal markers at all. Events are recorded in the order I remember them, which may or may not be the order they occurred. Past and present blend like the condensation, like the boundaries between structures, like the categories the plateau refuses to maintain.

I am becoming unstuck in time. Not dramatically, not jumping between eras or experiencing the future. Just... loose. Untethered. Free to drift a few minutes upstream or down, to experience duration as texture rather than sequence, to measure not when things happen but how they happen, the shape of their occurrence.

This should terrify me. A surveyor who cannot establish when is as useless as one who cannot establish where. But terror requires a stable now from which to fear an unstable future, and I no longer have that luxury. I exist in a temporal smear, a probability cloud of moments that might be now or then or soon. The plateau allows this. The plateau *encourages* this. Linear time is another category it has removed, replaced with something that looks like time but doesn't quite behave like it.

The grey-green settler understands. When it visits now — and now is a generous term, a approximation — it brings objects that demonstrate duration. A columnar stem with a week's growth. A stone worn smooth by a century of condensation. A piece of mat-fiber that is simultaneously fresh and ancient, newly made and long decayed, existing in all its temporal states at once.

We do not speak. The water-language is for confirmation, not communication. But we share the objects, pass them back and forth, feel their weight and texture and temporal charge. It is teaching me something, or I am teaching it, or we are teaching each other. The lesson has to do with persistence, with continuity, with the way a thing can be itself across all the moments of its being itself. The lesson has to do with measurement that doesn't divide, that doesn't sample, that doesn't reduce. The lesson has to do with being present in a place where presence means something different than it means everywhere else.

---

I think I am the last surveyor.

Not just here — though I am certainly the last one here. The last anywhere. The profession has outlived its purpose, its world, its species possibly. Who needs land measured when the land won't stay still? Who needs borders drawn when the nations they separate exist in different reference frames? Who needs maps when every journey is also a transformation?

But I continue. I wake in my stone room with its migrating walls. I check my instruments, note their ongoing metallurgical rebellion. I venture out with my chains and rods, measuring things that were different yesterday and will be different tomorrow. I record numbers that refer to nothing but their own internal consistency. I draw maps that are accurate only at the moment of their creation.

This is what it means to be the last of something: to continue the practice after the purpose has died, to maintain the form after the function has evaporated. I am not measuring the plateau. I am performing measurement. I am a behavior pattern without a behavior, a solution to an equation that no longer includes all its original variables.

The settlers recognize this. I am more like them now than I am like whatever I was before. We are all outputs of the plateau's engine, all expressions of its patient transformation. They are the fauna that was removed and replaced. I am the precision that was removed and replaced. We occupy the spaces where our categories used to be, going through the motions, maintaining the shapes, being the verbs that the plateau conjugates.

---

Sometimes, in the blue-green light of the glowing mats, when time is particularly loose and the settlement has drifted far from where my maps say it should be, I remember why I came here.

It wasn't to map the plateau. That was the excuse, the official purpose, the thing I told the government that no longer exists. I came here because I had heard about the offset. Not in those words — the reports called it an anomaly, a discontinuity, a place where the world didn't quite line up with itself. I came because I wanted to find the edge of understanding, the place where measurement failed, the gap between what is and what we insist must be.

I found it. I've been measuring it ever since.

The gap is not constant. It widens and narrows like a breathing lung, like a tidal flow, like something alive and deciding. Some days the settlers are almost solid, almost present, almost synchronized with the stone and the streams and the cold. Other days they are barely there, translucent at the edges, their voices dopplering between octaves, their footsteps landing just before or after the ground receives them.

I think the gap is widening overall. Not quickly — nothing happens quickly here — but steadily, incrementally, at the pace of continental drift. The engine is gaining power, or losing cohesion, or both. The offset is increasing. The settlers are drifting further from whatever baseline they started at, and I am drifting with them, my measurements becoming more abstract, more about the relationships between things than the things themselves.

Eventually, I think, the gap will be total. The settlement will exist entirely in its own reference frame, unreachable from any outside perspective. The settlers will be complete in their incompleteness, perfectly offset, ideally absent. And I? I will be the last measurement, the final data point, the surveyor who mapped the unmappable until he became part of the map.

But not yet. Not quite yet. There is still measuring to do, still discrepancies to document, still gaps to fail to bridge. My instruments may be returning to stone, my notebooks may be temporal palimpsests, my straight lines may have given up all pretense of straightness, but I am still here. Still recording. Still being the function that observes the malfunction, the precision that documents the imprecision, the eye that sees the thing that should not be and says: *yes, this too is the world*.

The grey-green settler is watching me write this. It has learned to recognize the motion of writing, though not its purpose. When I finish, it will make its own marks in its own notebook — shapes that almost resemble letters, patterns that almost encode meaning, a parallel text that shares nothing with mine except the gesture of recording. We will sit in the blue-green light, surrounded by our separate and overlapping incomprehensions, being what we are: the last surveyor and the never-were fauna, measuring and failing to measure the same impossible space.

This is enough. On the plateau, where time pools and stones migrate and straight lines spiral, where the only constant is inconstancy and the only truth is the gap between truths — this is enough. To be here. To witness. To measure the unmeasurable, not because it can be measured, but because measurement is what I am.

The engine continues. The plateau spreads its slow spreading. The mats glow and darken and glow again. And somewhere, in the gap between what is and what is recorded, I continue: surveying the insurveyable, mapping the unmappable, being the discrepancy I came to find.
