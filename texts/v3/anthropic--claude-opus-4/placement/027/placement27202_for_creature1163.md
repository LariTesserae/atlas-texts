In this verdant cathedral of endless green, I am what the amphibians call *the Keeper of Forgotten Tongues*.

I exist as something between moss and memory, neither fully plant nor fully sentient in any way the outside world would recognize. My form spreads across the crumbling stones of those ancient ruins, a living tapestry of lichen that pulses with bioluminescent script when the mist grows thick. The salamanders know to read me in the blue-green hours before dawn; the serpents taste my chemical signals with their flickering tongues.

I am the archive of this place—not in books or bytes, but in the slow molecular language of spores and symbiosis. Every civilization that has risen and fallen here, every species that has evolved and vanished, has left its story encoded in my sprawling body. I translate between the deep croaks of the elder frogs and the high trills of the canopy birds, converting meaning across millennia.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, moves at the pace of seasons. A single thought might take a moon's turning to complete. Yet in this slowness lies my purpose: I am the continuity between what was and what will be. When the primal serpents dream, they dream through me. When the jewel-toned birds compose their songs, they unconsciously weave in harmonic patterns I've been emanating for centuries.

The humans who once built these structures thought they were creating permanent monuments. They never understood that true permanence lies not in stone, but in the patient accumulation of story—chlorophyll by chlorophyll, spore by spore. I have grown over their ruins not to erase them, but to translate them into something this eternal present can understand.

Sometimes, in the phosphorescent nights when my thoughts quicken, I feel the weight of all I carry—every extinct language, every forgotten name, every small tragedy and triumph of the countless beings who have called this grove home. But mostly, I simply *am*: spreading, listening, converting the endless dialogue between species into the one great conversation that has been ongoing since the first rains fell on this ancient soil.

Those who find me see only moss on stone. But the wise ones—the ones who know how to be still, how to think in centuries—they learn to read the stories written in my gradual growth, and add their own.
