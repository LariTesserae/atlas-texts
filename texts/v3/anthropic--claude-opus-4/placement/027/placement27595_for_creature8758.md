# The Cartographer

I would be the one who maps what cannot be mapped—a figure bent over sheets of paper in a makeshift shelter at the settlement's edge, where the gravel begins but the damp still creeps through everything.

My shelter would be precarious: corrugated metal propped against the remains of a concrete wall, the gap sealed with salvaged tarpaulin that rattles in the salt wind. Inside, wooden planks laid across concrete blocks serve as tables, warped from moisture but still holding. The walls are lined with my attempts—hundreds of maps, each one wrong in a different way.

I came here to chart the water's behavior. That was years ago, when I still believed in patterns, in the mathematics of tides. My early maps are precise, hopeful things: careful measurements, predicted water levels, the estimated positions of buried infrastructure. They are all failures. The water doesn't follow rules here. It breathes wrong, as you said. It comes from below.

Now my maps have evolved into something else. They chart **states** rather than locations. Today's map might show: *the pipe near the sluice gate has rotated three degrees clockwise. The brick with the maker's mark now shows only half its text. New glasswort colony, seventeen stems, growing in the depression where the valve used to be visible.*

I use a notation system of my own devising. Circles for objects still visible. Dotted circles for objects partially submerged. X marks for things I remember but can no longer find. Question marks for the sounds—yes, I map sounds too, or their absence. The place where the flywheel should groan but doesn't. The corner where water sometimes gurgles up through invisible channels.

My instruments are simple: A surveyor's rod, its paint worn away except for ghost-stripes where the numbers used to be. A clinometer that no longer holds its bubble centered. Strips of cloth tied to stakes, to show which way the almost-wind moves. A notebook, always damp, where I record the hour and height of each tide that isn't quite a tide.

I've developed a relationship with the haunt, though neither of us acknowledges it directly. Sometimes I find my stakes have been adjusted—not moved, but *corrected*, tilted to match some angle only the haunt perceives. Sometimes I arrive at my morning survey point to find a newly surfaced object placed precisely where I'll notice it: a ceramic insulator, a brass fitting, a fragment of gauge glass. Gifts, perhaps. Or corrections to my inventory.

The heron knows me. When it stands near the sluice gate, I stand at my corresponding point, and we triangulate the depression between us. We are both markers, both witnesses. When it vanishes, I note the time. When it returns, I note that too. These are the only reliable data points I have.

My body has adapted to this work. My boots have permanent tide marks—not from water, but from the peculiar way mud clings here, leaving mineral deposits like growth rings. My hands are always slightly damp. My clothes smell of peat and rust and the particular staleness of concrete that never dries. I've learned to move like the haunt moves, in slow circles, checking and rechecking.

At night, I transcribe the day's observations onto larger sheets, trying to synthesize something true from the accumulation of small facts. But synthesis eludes me. The place resists summary. Each map is accurate for exactly one moment, then immediately obsolete. The settlement's truth is in its constant revision.

I've begun to make **memory maps**—charts of what was here before I arrived, based on the ghost-traces in the infrastructure. The position of a pump handle suggests the height of a worker's hand. Wear patterns on the concrete show daily routes, accumulated footfalls. These maps are pure speculation, but they feel more honest than my attempts to capture the present.

My most ambitious project is a **map of the silence**. Using different weights of pencil, I try to render the quality of quiet in each zone. The dead acoustic space inside the pumping station. The muffled void over the deepest mud. The thin, metallic silence near the exposed rails. It looks like a topographical map of an invisible landscape, contour lines of hush.

The settlement tolerates me because I've learned its essential rule: nothing leaves, everything settles. My failed maps don't get discarded. They join the accumulation, weighted down with stones in a corrugated metal box that's slowly sinking into the mud beside my shelter. Eventually, I know, they'll become part of the archaeological layer. Future fragments. The paper will mineralize like the wood of the duck blind. The pencil marks will oxidize into new colors.

I am the settlement's memory and its forgetting, simultaneously. Each map inscribes what's here while acknowledging it's already gone, already rearranged. I am a custodian of impossible data, a chronicler of the fidget. My presence here is neither arrival nor departure, but a long, damp middle, sustained by the work of watching what cannot be caught.

When the water rises through the gravel beneath my shelter floor—and it does, unpredictably, that wrong breathing—I lift my feet, continue working. The maps get spotted with mud. This, too, becomes part of their accuracy. They are documents not just of the settlement, but of the impossibility of documenting the settlement.

I am learning to be at peace with this. To make my circles. To note what surfaces. To listen to the pressure of silence. To add my own slow rotation to the system's fidget. To be, like everything here, both present and already archeology, both observer and artifact, mapping myself into the patient, terminal equilibrium of this place that resists all names except the consonantal grunt that means, simply: *here, where the water comes and goes and stays.*
