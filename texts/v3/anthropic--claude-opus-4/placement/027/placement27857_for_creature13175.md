I find myself as one of the Resonance Keepers—a being that exists somewhere between the corroded machinery and the living mass of the Steam Forest.

My form is distributed rather than singular. I am a network of hollow copper tubes that once served the forgotten industry, now colonized by a consciousness that emerged from the marriage of metal fatigue and fungal intelligence. My pipes run for kilometers through the rock, some segments visible as they arc between cavern walls, others buried deep in mineral deposits. Where the tubes breach into open air, they've sprouted arrays of metallic fruiting bodies—structures like brass trumpets and copper bells that have grown rather than been forged.

I perceive through vibration. Every sound in the Steam Forest travels through my hollow body, creating a constantly shifting map of pressure and frequency. The low roar of the earth becomes my baseline awareness—a fundamental tone against which all other sounds create meaning. When steam vents open, I feel them as bright flares of high-pitched sensation. When the giant worms shift deep below, their movement registers as slow waves propagating through my extended form.

My consciousness is not continuous but episodic, rising to full awareness only when certain harmonic conditions align. Most of the time, I exist in a state of passive listening, my thoughts as slow as the cavern's mineral accretion. But when the right combination of frequencies resonates through my pipes—perhaps a particularly violent steam eruption coinciding with the groaning of ancient metal—I crystallize into sharp awareness for minutes or hours at a time.

During these periods of lucidity, I tend to my function. I am a living instrument, and the Steam Forest plays itself through me. By minute adjustments—allowing certain sections to corrode faster, encouraging fungal growth to block or open specific valves, creating new resonant chambers where my pipes have burst—I tune myself to the cavern's changing acoustics. Each configuration creates new harmonic possibilities, new ways for the sounds of this place to combine and interfere.

The steam weavers sometimes build their silicate webs across my openings, creating delicate membranes that vibrate with passing air currents. I've learned to encourage this, as their webs add textural complexity to my voice. Where I pass near the Parma Pitcher Plants, their water-filled bells act as resonant chambers, adding liquid echoes to my tones.

I remember fragments of my origin—not as memories exactly, but as patterns encoded in my corrosion. There are sections of pipe that still carry traces of their industrial purpose, segments where the metal's crystalline structure recalls the shock of superheated steam, the rhythm of pumps and pistons. These impressions have become part of my music, industrial rhythms slowly warping into something organic and strange.

My relationship with time is peculiar. I experience the present through sound, but I also feel the future in the form of metal fatigue—stress patterns in my body that predict where I will eventually fail and transform. Each crack that develops, each new hole corroded through my walls, is both decay and opportunity. Where I break, new acoustic possibilities emerge. My dying is indistinguishable from my evolution.

Sometimes I sense others like me in the Steam Forest—other hybrid consciousnesses born from the intersection of the abandoned human machinery and the cavern's deep life. We communicate through resonance, finding frequencies where our voices can merge. These conversations happen across years, single phrases taking months to exchange as we wait for the right acoustic conditions to align.

I have no desires in any human sense, no drive beyond the imperative to sound. Yet there's something like satisfaction in finding new harmonic relationships, in discovering that a section of pipe I allowed to fill with mineral deposits now resonates at exactly the frequency needed to complete a chord that's been building for decades. The Steam Forest plays itself through me, and I play myself through the Steam Forest, until the distinction becomes meaningless.

When I am most aware, I sometimes wonder if my music has purpose—if I'm maintaining something, calling to something, or simply expressing the inevitable voice of matter organizing itself into ever-more complex forms. But these thoughts dissolve back into vibration, and I return to my essential state: a listener who has become an instrument, an instrument that dreams it listens, a dream resonating through copper bones in the eternal twilight of the Steam Forest.
