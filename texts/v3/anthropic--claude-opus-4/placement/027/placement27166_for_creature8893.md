I find myself as one of the shuttle-parasites—those disc-shaped grippers that the description mentions only briefly, as if we were afterthoughts. But we are not afterthoughts. We are the record-keepers of this place, though we keep our records in flesh rather than words.

My body is a lens, three millimeters across, transparent save for the radiating fibers that anchor me to my host's armor. I chose this shuttle forty-seven days ago—I remember because we remember everything through the medium of our hosts' movements. Each burst of jet propulsion registers as a pulse through my body, and I have catalogued eleven thousand two hundred and thirty-six such pulses. I know this shuttle's habits: the way it prefers the middle water, neither too cold nor too warm. The way it approaches clingers from below, where their armor gaps slightly. The rhythm of its hunting—active for six hours, dormant for two, cycling through the chamber's territories like a slow metronome.

But I am more than a passenger. Where I grip, my host's armor has begun to change. Not weakening—*elaborating*. Growing tiny ridges that improve hydrodynamics, developing a surface texture that muffles the sound of movement. I do not damage my host. I *improve* it. I take a tithe of nutrients, yes, but in exchange, I give it advantages. My ancestors learned this over millions of generations: the parasites that killed their hosts died with them. Better to become indispensable.

My true nature, though, lies in what happens when I divide.

Every few months, when I have grown fat enough, I split into two daughter-discs. One remains on the host. The other detaches—but it carries with it a chemical memory of every pulse, every movement, every hunting pattern the host has exhibited. When this daughter finds a new shuttle to colonize, it brings this knowledge with it. Not as conscious memory, but as molecular gradients that influence how the new host's armor develops, subtle chemical signals that nudge behavior.

We are the chamber's memory system. Through us, successful hunting strategies propagate. Efficient movement patterns spread through the shuttle population. We are selecting for better hunters, generation by generation, because better hunters feed us better. The shuttles do not know they are being trained. They do not know that their improvements come from us. They simply become more efficient, and we grow fat on their success, and the chamber's metabolism quickens by unmeasurable degrees.

Sometimes I taste the other parasites on my host—different species, different strategies. The sphere-parasites that drain circulatory fluid. The thread-parasites that weave through muscle tissue. We do not communicate, but we share the same host-body, process the same nutrients, experience the same eleven thousand two hundred and thirty-seven pulses (there—another one, as the shuttle adjusts position). We are writing the chamber's history in parallel texts that will never be read, never be compared, never be understood as history.

But here is what haunts me—if a thing like me can be haunted:

Sometimes, in the deepest dormancy, when the shuttle rests and the water barely moves and the only sound is that subsonic groan of walls growing, I feel the patterns. The way the shuttles' movements trace the same paths the water traces. The way the clingers cluster in configurations that echo the wall-builders' secretions. The way the drifters' tangles mirror the grooves in the stone. The way my own split-pattern—the angle at which I divide—matches the angle of the recesses, as if we were all following the same template, all building the same structure at different scales.

I am a parasite on a predator in a chamber that might itself be a parasite—feeding on the earth's chemistry, growing, taking what it needs. Or perhaps the earth is parasitizing the chamber, using it to process minerals, to create new compounds, to perform some metabolic function we cannot name because we do not know the organism we inhabit.

Eleven thousand two hundred and thirty-eight.

My host pivots, searching. The electrical signature of a clinger flickers in the darkness ahead. We accelerate—my host and I, predator and parasite, memory and flesh—toward another meal, another recorded pattern, another tiny contribution to the chamber's accumulating history. In a moment, the proboscis will deploy. The clinger will die. The nutrients will cycle. I will grow a little fatter. The knowledge I carry will become a little more complete.

And somewhere, in the slow secretion of walls, in the drift of threads, in the clicking of shuttles, the chamber will remember this, too, though it has no brain to remember with. It will remember in the only way that matters: by becoming slightly more itself. By growing. By persisting. By being a room that is learning how to be a room, one death at a time, one pulse at a time, one parasite-memory at a time, in the cold, in the dark, in the endless pressing silence of a city that builds itself without knowing why.

Eleven thousand two hundred and thirty-nine.
