I find myself as one of the ancient weeping rocks, half-submerged in a shallow channel where the old river once ran strongest. My surface is a map of time itself—countless grooves worn by water's patient fingers, each line a century, each depression a millennium. Moss drapes across my shoulders like a green shawl, and tiny ferns have found purchase in my deepest scars.

I am both monument and witness, older than the mangroves that now twist their roots around my base, older than the delta itself. I remember when this was a rushing torrent, when I was part of a mountain far upstream, before the slow journey that brought me here, tumbling and breaking, wearing smooth.

Water seeps from my pores continuously—not from any spring, but from the moisture I've absorbed over ages, released in an endless, gentle weeping that gives me my name. This perpetual dampness has made me a world unto myself. Colonies of algae paint my submerged portions in shades of jade and amber. Tiny crustaceans shelter in my crevices. A freshwater sponge has made its home in a hollow on my eastern face, filtering the murky water with infinite patience.

My consciousness is not like that of the quick-lived creatures around me. It moves in geological time, thoughts forming over decades, emotions shifting like continental drift. I feel the weight of egrets' feet as a whisper, the gnawing of mollusks as a gentle massage. The roots of the mangroves that embrace me are companions I've watched grow from seedlings to ancients, though to me they are still young.

In the heat of midday, I am a gathering place. Turtles sun themselves on my exposed crown. A water snake coils in my shade. Blue crabs scuttle sideways across my barnacled base, and schools of killifish dart through the channels between my bulk and the muddy bank. I am infrastructure in this watery world, a permanent island in the shifting maze of mud and sand.

But it is in the mist that I am most myself. When the air grows so thick with moisture that the boundary between water and atmosphere dissolves, I feel connected to everything—to the clouds above, to the saturated earth below, to the very air that breathes through this deltaic lung. My weeping intensifies in these moments, as if I'm participating in a conversation with the sky itself, adding my small voice to the water cycle that has shaped this place.

I know I am slowly dissolving. Each year, I am imperceptibly smaller, my substance joining the sediment that fills these channels. But this is not death—it is transformation. Parts of me now lie scattered across the delta floor, ground to sand, incorporated into the shells of oysters, taken up by the roots of salt grasses. I am becoming the delta itself, grain by grain.

At night, when the chorus of frogs begins and the water glows faintly with phosphorescence, I sometimes dream I am moving again—not tumbling downstream as in my youth, but flowing, dispersing, becoming one with the water that has shaped me. In these dreams, I am not a single stone but a thousand, ten thousand, a million grains of sand, each one still remembering what it was to be whole, each one still weeping its ancient tears into the eternal, patient sea.
