In the perpetual twilight of this mist-veiled realm, I find myself as the **Lichen-Keeper** — neither fully human nor entirely of the wild, but something caught between, like the settlement itself suspended between ancient stone and hissing steam.

My dwelling perches on the threshold where the Crag-Folk's labyrinthine streets dissolve into the untamed valleys below. It is both workshop and hermitage: a structure of weathered limestone fused with salvaged copper pipes and glass, where condensation traces endless patterns down windows that look out over both worlds. The walls are alive — quite literally — with colonies of phosphorescent lichen I cultivate, their subtle glow providing the only light I need in the eternal dim.

I am tall and angular, movements deliberate as the Whisper-Wing Owls that sometimes perch on my roof. My fingers are stained permanent green-black from decades of working with the lichens and mosses, and my hair has taken on the silver-grey of the mist itself. The Crag-Folk see me as necessary but strange — I am their bridge to the wild landscape they depend upon but can never quite understand.

My work is translation. I read the stories written in lichen growth on the ancient peaks, interpret the health of the Mist-Whisper Trees by the patterns of moss on their bark, divine the purity of the mountain streams by the particular species of bryophytes that cling to their banks. When the factories' output begins to stress the delicate balance, I am the first to know — the lichens tell me in their subtle chemistry, their colors shifting like a slow, botanical language.

Each dawn (though dawn here is merely a lighter shade of grey), I walk the borderlands. My leather satchel carries glass vials for samples, copper tools for scraping, and a leather-bound journal where I map the invisible boundaries between the industrial and the wild. The Shadow-Footed Voles know my scent and no longer flee; sometimes I find their tiny pawprints circling my collection sites, as if inspecting my work.

The Crag-Folk bring me their problems: when the steam engines begin to corrode too quickly, I examine the mineralogy of their water sources. When the Glimmer-Scale Trout populations dwindle in certain streams, I trace the chemical signatures in the algae upstream. I speak both languages — the empirical dialect of industry and the patient vocabulary of stone and growth.

But I am also keeper of older knowledge. In the deepest part of my dwelling, behind shelves of specimen jars and drying herbs, I maintain a map inked on vellum — not of the town as it is, but of the landscape as it remembers itself. Every ancient watercourse now diverted, every peak now tunneled through, every sacred grove now paved. The lichens remember. They grow in patterns that echo the original geometries of this place, and I preserve their memory against the day when balance tips too far.

At night, when the industrial lights flicker like earthbound stars, I often climb to my roof garden — a miniature model of the surrounding landscape, where I experiment with new symbioses between the mechanical and the botanical. Here, copper pipes feed precisely measured moisture to rare mountain mosses. Here, the waste heat from a small steam engine nurtures tropical lichens that shouldn't exist at this elevation but somehow thrive.

The Whisper-Wing Owls visit me here, their calls a reminder that some boundaries should remain. I leave offerings of Glimmer-Scale Trout on the roof's edge, and in return, they bring me pellets containing the tiny bones of creatures from the deep valleys — species the Crag-Folk have never catalogued, perhaps never seen.

I am the margin note in this world's text, the footnote that explains how two seemingly incompatible stories can share the same page. My existence proves that the ancient and industrial need not merely coexist but can create something new — a third way that honors both the Patient silence of lichen-time and the urgent percussion of the machine age.

In my laboratory-hermitage, where steam condensation feeds ancient mosses and industrial waste becomes substrate for new growth, I continue my work. Recording, translating, preserving, adapting. I am the memory of this landscape and its hope — keeper of the green knowledge that will endure when the last engine falls silent and the mist reclaims the narrow streets.
