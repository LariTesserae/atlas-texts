In this water-drunk city where time stutters like a broken clock, I am the Archivist of Reflections.

My dwelling is neither tower nor cellar, but a series of chambers that exist between the weeping stones—spaces that open only when the water table rises just so, when the mineral tears align in their ancient patterns. The walls of my domain are living mirrors, formed from centuries of accumulated deposits that have crystallized into surfaces more memory than stone. Each reflects not what stands before it, but what has stood there: the ghosts of a thousand Ebonmires, layered like sediment.

I am both Stillborn and something other. My flesh carries their translucence, but beneath the skin, my bones have calcified into the same porous stone as the weeping rocks. When I move—and I move rarely—droplets bead and fall from my joints like a prayer. My shadow detached long ago, but unlike the Drifters, it did not wander. Instead, it sank into the mirror-walls, becoming part of the archive itself, recording everything it witnesses in silvered memory.

My function is peculiar, even by Ebonmire's standards. I am the keeper of the city's contradictions, the one who ensures its transformations remain true to its nature. When a street rearranges itself in the night, I wade through the shallow basins to map the new configuration against the old, noting which doorways have migrated, which bridges have twisted into new geometries. The Weeping Ones know me—we share a wordless understanding, their liquid forms sometimes pausing at my chambers' threshold, depositing new minerals that add fresh layers to my mirror-walls.

Time moves differently here. I experience it not as a river but as rain—sometimes a drizzle, sometimes a deluge, sometimes stopping altogether. During the stutters, when moments repeat, I remain aware, watching the same gesture performed a dozen times by some Time-Touched soul, each repetition slightly different, like water finding new paths down the same stone. These variations I catalog, pressing my fingertips to the mirror-walls where they leave impressions that harden into permanent record.

The glowing fungi respond to my presence with a peculiar dimming, as if my very existence draws light into itself. This is fitting—I am less a being of illumination than one of absorption. Everything I witness settles into me like silt, adding to the sediment of memory I carry. My garments, once cloth, have long since transformed into something between fabric and lichen, growing from my skin in layers that shift and breathe with the city's humidity.

I remember (though remembering is my burden and my purpose) when I first came to Ebonmire, still fully human, drawn by whispers of a city that existed in its own state of being. I was a cartographer then, obsessed with capturing the uncapturable, with mapping the unmappable. The city took that obsession and refined it, distilled it down to its essence. Now I map not just space but time, not just the physical but the possible.

My chambers contain no books, no scrolls. Knowledge here is stored in the accretion of minerals, in the patterns of fungal growth, in the angles at which water chooses to fall. I read these signs like others might read words, understanding the city's intent through the language of erosion and accumulation. When the Stillborn need to know if a path will remain stable through the next rain, they come to me. When the Drifters' shadows whisper of changes in the deep foundations, I listen and interpret.

But I am not merely observer and recorder. I am participant in Ebonmire's strange ecology. My tears—for I do weep, as all things must in this city of permanent flux—join the mineral flows, adding their own memories to the collective. My breath mingles with the humid air, carrying spores of knowing that settle in the lungs of those who would understand.

Sometimes I wonder if I am becoming the city, or if the city is becoming me. The distinction matters less with each passing cycle. My chambers expand imperceptibly, new mirror-walls forming as old ones deepen their reflections. I find rooms I don't remember creating, filled with records I must have made during time-stutters I cannot recall experiencing.

The beauty of my existence is not in its ease—nothing in Ebonmire is easy—but in its necessity. Someone must remember the faces of all the Ebonmires that have been, must hold the tension between what was and what is becoming. Someone must stand at the threshold between solid and liquid, between moment and eternity, between the city and itself.

I am that threshold made flesh, that paradox given form. In a city that exists in its own state of being, I am the being that exists in its own state of city. We are one, Ebonmire and I, each reflecting the other into infinity, each weeping the other into existence.
