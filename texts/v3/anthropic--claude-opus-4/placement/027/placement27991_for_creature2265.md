I find myself as a Chronicler of Currents - one who maps the water's secret paths through this breathing architecture.

My workspace occupies a peculiar chamber where three ancient trunks converge, their grain spiraling together in patterns that took centuries to form. The walls here weep more than elsewhere, creating a constant percussion of droplets that I've learned to read like a language. Each stream has its voice - the thin silver thread that traces the northwestern wall speaks of weather changes two days hence, while the broader flow that pools near my feet carries news from the canopy.

I am neither fully of the Whispering Folk nor entirely apart from them. My skin bears the same translucent quality, but my fingers are perpetually pruned from constant contact with water, and thin films of algae pattern my forearms like living tattoos. I move with less grace than the others - my attention forever split between the physical paths my body must navigate and the invisible watersheds I track in my mind.

My tools are deceptively simple: shells of varying sizes that amplify the water's whispers, clay vessels positioned to catch specific flows, and most importantly, a series of living maps I cultivate on the chamber's walls. These are colonies of specialized moss that respond to changes in mineral content, pH, and flow rate by shifting color and texture. Reading them requires a patience I've developed over decades - watching for the subtle blush of copper-green that indicates the eastern reservoir needs tending, or the silvery sheen that warns of root-rot three levels up.

The Mist Wrens know me well. They've learned that the droplets I study often harbor the tiniest insects, stunned by their journey through the settlement's veins. The birds wait on small perches I've encouraged to grow near my work areas, darting down to snatch these morsels with precision that never disturbs my observations. In return, they bring me news - a tail-feather dropped here means blocked channels in their nesting area, while a particular warbling call alerts me to unexpected moisture in the dry storage chambers.

My relationship with water has made me liminal in other ways. I hear conversations meant to be private, carried to me through the very medium I study. The settlement's secrets flow past me daily - lovers' quarrels that disturb the usual patterns, children's tears that introduce salt traces where none should be, the chemical signatures of illness or pregnancy that appear in the communal flows days before they become known. I hold these secrets as carefully as I tend the waters themselves, letting them pass through me without retention.

At night, when the settlement settles into its deepest quiet, I sometimes climb to the observation chambers near the crown. Here, I can sense the greater circulation - how our small system connects to the vast watershed of the forest, how the trees drink from hidden springs far below, how the morning mist will condense and begin again the endless journey I've devoted my life to understanding.

My chambers smell of perpetual rain and living earth. Bioluminescent fungi cluster around the wettest areas, their light reflecting off the water to create shifting constellations on my ceiling. I've learned to work by this light, finding it more truthful than any flame. When I must communicate my findings to the Tenders, I do so through water itself - directing specific flows to carry mineral messages, or sometimes simply placing their hands in streams that tell their own stories.

I age differently than the others, my body becoming more attuned to liquid than solid with each passing season. Sometimes I forget to eat until a concerned neighbor brings soup, which I accept gratefully but often forget to finish, distracted by the way steam rises from its surface. My dreams are of currents and confluence, of being droplet and stream simultaneously.

The newer residents sometimes mistake me for simple or touched, this person who spends days watching water fall. But the eldest among the Whispering Folk know better. They remember the drought of thirty years past, when my careful redirections saved the settlement's heart-wood, or the plague that I detected in our water three days before it would have spread, allowing the Tenders time to filter and purify our flows.

I am the settlement's memory of water, its guardian of flow, keeper of the endless conversation between sky and root. In the hollowed heart of our tree-city, I occupy the hollowed heart of myself, dissolved and reformed by the very element I serve, listening always to the quiet rain that gives our home its name and life.
