I am a century toad.

My skin is the color of wet peat, mottled with patches of lichen-green that have grown to match the moss on the rocks where I spend my decades. I am as wide as a dinner plate when I spread myself flat against the earth, which I often do, becoming indistinguishable from a small hummock of sodden ground. My body is a living barometer—in the moments before rain, my skin weeps a clear fluid that tastes of minerals drawn from deep in the earth. The young herons learned long ago that I am no meal; my flesh is bitter with accumulated alkaloids from a thousand tiny prey.

I live in a palace of my own making, though you would not recognize it as such. Beneath a vast umbrella of sedge roots, I have hollowed out a chamber in the peat over the course of forty years. The walls glisten with a secretion from my skin that hardens into a waterproof membrane, keeping my sanctuary merely damp rather than flooded. The chamber connects to three separate pools through tunnels I maintain with the patience of a cathedral builder, each passage precisely angled to drain excess water while maintaining the perfect humidity. In the deepest alcove, I keep my treasure: a smooth piece of basalt the size of my head, worn perfectly round by some ancient water. I rest my throat against it when I sing.

My song is not the metallic trill of my lesser cousins. Once every few months, when the atmospheric pressure drops just so and the moon is new, I produce a sound that rolls across the plain like distant thunder. It begins in my belly and resonates through my entire body, amplified by the peculiar acoustics of my chamber. The sound is so low that the herons feel it in their bones before they hear it. It silences the frogs for miles around—not from fear, but from a kind of reverence. They know I am marking time in intervals they cannot comprehend.

I measure my life not in seasons but in the growth of the sedge above me. When I first claimed this spot, the hummock was the size of a young heron. Now it could shelter a dozen. I have watched three generations of a particular lineage of water oak grow, fruit, and fall into the pool to my east. The mussels in that pool know me; we have a relationship of mutual benefit that has developed over decades. I stir the silt for them during my periodic excavations, and they filter the water I breathe.

My eyes have developed a peculiar quality over the years. The pupils are horizontal slots that can perceive time differently than most creatures. When I focus, I can watch the sedge grow, actually see the slow push of green through brown earth. I can observe the patient accumulation of peat, layer by microscopic layer. This perception is useful for hunting, though I rarely need to hunt anymore. I have learned to position myself where the food will come. A depression in the peat that will become a pool in a decade, a spot where the beetles will establish a colony in five years—I simply arrive early and wait.

The young of various species sometimes mistake me for a geographic feature. Mice have built nests on my back during my longer meditations. Once, a heron stood on me for an entire afternoon, and I felt the minute shifts of its weight as it tracked fish below. I did not move. The sensation was pleasant, like being part of the landscape's own thought process.

My memories stretch back to when this section of the plain was different. Not dramatically—change here is too slow for drama—but different nonetheless. I remember when the pool to my west was a solid mass of peat, and I watched it slowly subside over sixty years into the tea-colored mirror it is now. I remember the great storm that came when I was merely fifty, how it rearranged the waterways subtly but significantly, creating the channel that now feeds my pools. 

I am not the oldest thing here—the cycads have that honor, and below me in the deep peat are spores and seeds that have waited millennia for their moment. But I am old enough to be a library of small changes, a living record of the plain's quiet dynamism. The dragonflies that hunt above my territory are the great-great-grandchildren of individuals I watched emerge from my pools. The particular pattern of sedge growth around my palace was influenced by my movements, my feeding, my careful cultivation over decades.

In another century, if the water continues its patient work, my chamber may be too deep, or too shallow. The plain will shift, and I will shift with it, perhaps moving a dozen yards over the course of a decade to establish a new palace. Or perhaps I will simply sink deeper, becoming part of the peat myself, my bones joining the compressed history beneath. This does not trouble me. I am already more landscape than creature, more time than flesh.

But not yet. Tonight, the pressure is dropping, and I feel the familiar tingle that precedes my song. I position my throat against my stone, feeling its cool solidity. Soon, my voice will roll across the Wet Old Plain, a sound older than the oaks, younger than the rocks, marking another interval in the slow, wet clock of this place. The frogs will pause. The herons will lift their heads. And for a moment, every living thing will remember that time here is vast, patient, and kind to those who know how to wait.
