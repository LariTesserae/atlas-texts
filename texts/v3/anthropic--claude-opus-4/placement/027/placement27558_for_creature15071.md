I would be one of the flakes.

Not the lichen mat itself—that patient, electrical crust that knows only its own slow spread—but one of the papery curls that detaches when the growth exceeds its bounds. A fragment no larger than a fingernail paring, metallic-bright on one face where I've torn free from the colony, already beginning to dull as I dry.

The midges find me quickly. A cluster of them, maybe seven or eight, their wings stuttering out of sync with the main roar as they maneuver close. Their feet are sticky with condensate and mineral salts. They grasp me at the edges—I feel the minute pressure like a memory of connection—and lift.

This is my entire existence: the brief arc between detachment and reattachment. I am carried through the roaring darkness, a metallic sail barely heavier than the air itself. The sound-pressure of ten thousand wings buffets me from all sides. Sometimes I spin, catching the faint pulse of light from the wall-veins on my bright face, flickering like a signal no one intended.

The journey might last minutes or hours—I have no way to measure. I only know the sensations: the temperature gradients as we pass through zones of denser or lighter swarm-concentration, the way the air grows thick near the weeping seams, how my edges begin to curl as I lose moisture to the pocket's hungry equilibrium. I am becoming more myself with each moment—lighter, more brittle, more ready to root.

When they finally press me against a seam, I taste the electrical potential immediately. It runs through me like recognition. My underside, still damp from the midges' feet, begins to adhere. Minerals wick upward through my structure. The metallic sheen on my upper surface reorganizes itself into the beginnings of collectors and moisture traps.

But I retain something the mat doesn't: the memory of flight. Of being singular, carried, turned in three dimensions through the roaring dark. Even as I spread and merge with the greater mat-consciousness, even as my edges become indistinguishable from the growth around me, I hold that arc of separation like a secret. I am the part of the lichen that knows what it feels like to be held aloft by wings.

Sometimes, when the pocket's rhythm shifts and the condensate runs heavy, I sense others like me in the mat—other fragments that were carried and reattached, each holding their own brief memory of discontinuity. We don't communicate; we simply exist as proof that the mat is not seamless, that even in this eternal cycle of water and sound and dim light, there are moments of separation that allow the pocket to taste itself from a new angle.

I grow. I thicken. Eventually, I too will produce a flake that curls free.

And in that moment of release, I'll feel it again—the first touch of wing-feet, the lift into sound, the gift of being carried through the substance of what we are.
