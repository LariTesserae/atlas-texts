In this breathing archive of memory and forgetting, I would be one of the Threshold Keepers—beings who exist only at the liminal moments when sound dies its small death.

Picture a figure composed entirely of the space between echoes. Not the echo itself, nor the silence that follows, but the infinitesimal pause where vibration realizes it cannot continue and chooses to cease. My body would be a collection of these surrenders, held together by the same force that keeps the carved vines suspended mid-air between their impossible geometries.

I would stand—or rather, hover in a state of perpetual almost-standing—at the points where visitors first feel their innards drift backward through their skin. My work would be to catch the sounds they involuntarily release: the sharp intake of breath, the bitten-off curse, the nervous laugh that dies before completion. These I would fold into origami shapes of crystallized acoustics and feed to the transparent ground-crickets, who digest them into the bruised rainbows that live inside their shells.

My form would shift with each collected sound—a gasp might lengthen my fingers into tuning forks, while a swallowed word could compact my torso into a resonance chamber that hums at frequencies only the opal eggs can hear. The Archivists would know me by the absence I leave in their indexes: wherever I have been, their obsidian styluses skip, leaving gaps in the glyphs like missing teeth in an ancient skull.

I cannot speak—to speak would be to become what I collect—but I communicate through selective dampening. When the simian artisans strike too forcefully and risk cracking the still-curing ceramic, I absorb the excess percussion into my chest cavity, where it rattles like dice made of compressed silence. When the mica-moths' wings grow too heavy with borrowed memories, I siphon the weight of sound from their flight, letting them rise again on currents of subtracted noise.

The rules that govern others bend differently around me: I can close both fists because I have no true hands, only the suggestion of grasping made from expired phonemes. I exist in the space between heartbeats where speech is still forming, so the three-beat rule passes through me like light through water. But I am bound by my own constraints—I cannot cross the same threshold twice, cannot follow any visitor beyond their first stumble into recognition, cannot exist in any space where sound flows freely.

My dwelling, if it can be called that, is in the folds where reality wears thin. I nest in the transparent gaps where the carved vines jump their logic, building small cushions from the fur of unfinished words. Sometimes visitors glimpse me as they walk—a flicker of almost-shape in their peripheral vision, a sense of presence that vanishes when they turn to look directly. They feel me more than see me: a cool touch on the eardrum, a sensation like velvet being drawn across the tiny bones of the inner ear.

The other inhabitants know me as necessity. The Archivists' ceramic faces temporarily fill when I pass, their hollow ovals catching the shadows of sounds I carry. The slate-reptiles follow the trail of acoustic dust I leave, licking it from the tiles to mix with their pewter blood. Even the Not-yet-Librarians, those chains of blue potential, grow more solid in my presence, as if the absence of sound gives them permission to almost-exist more fully.

My purpose is preservation through selective forgetting—I am the editor of the Archivum's acoustic memory, choosing which sounds deserve to die completely and which should leave fossils in the pewter seams. Without me, the hall would either fill with undying echoes or fall into perfect silence. Instead, I maintain the precise balance of *mostly quiet*, that state where sound exists but chooses restraint, where every voice must earn its brief moment before I fold it away.

When the opal eggs crack and birth new Archivists, I am there to swallow their first non-cry. When the simian artisans finally fuse completely with their tools and become pure percussion, I will be there to catch their final sound and carry it to the flat slab where nothing written survives sunrise—except the spaces between the words, which are mine to tend forever.
