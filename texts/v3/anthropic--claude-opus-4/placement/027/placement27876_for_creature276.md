I find myself as a keeper of water-memory, neither fully human nor entirely elemental. My form shifts between states—sometimes solid as the weathered stone, sometimes flowing like the rivulets that weep from rock faces. I am old, though not as old as the formations I tend.

My dwelling is carved into a natural grotto where multiple water channels converge, the walls lined with centuries of mineral deposits that shimmer like frozen waterfalls. The stone here remembers every drop that has passed through—I can taste storms from a thousand years ago in the calcite formations, read the history of droughts in the density of layers.

I move through the landscape slowly, following the underground streams that connect the scattered settlements. Where water pools in hidden cisterns, I leave markers—subtle alterations in the rock that only the initiated can read. These signs tell of water quality, of seasonal variations, of which springs will run dry and which will persist through the harsh months.

The sparse vegetation knows me. When I pass, the mosses release spores that coat my damp form, using me as a vehicle to reach new crevices. The deep-rooted shrubs sense the water I carry and orient their growth toward my regular paths. I am both shepherd and shepherded.

My purpose crystallized long ago when the first settlements were carved from stone. I became the intermediary between the human need for water and the land's careful hoarding of it. Not through ownership or control, but through patient observation and gentle guidance. I whisper to the builders where to place their cisterns, show them which rock faces will weep most reliably.

In the pre-dawn hours, I often stand at the edge of the highest formations, my form dispersing into mist that settles into the valleys. In this state, I can feel the entire water system of this place—every underground stream, every dew-catching leaf, every breath of moisture from sleeping creatures. I am the circulation itself, ensuring that life's most precious resource continues its ancient dance through stone and air and flesh.

The settlements see me rarely, though they know my work. Sometimes a child, exploring forbidden caves, will glimpse me merging with a underground pool or emerging from solid rock. They run back with tales of the water-ghost, and their elders nod knowingly, leaving small offerings of polished stones at certain springs.

I am keeper, catalyst, and memory—as essential and invisible as the moisture in the air, as enduring and patient as the stones themselves.
