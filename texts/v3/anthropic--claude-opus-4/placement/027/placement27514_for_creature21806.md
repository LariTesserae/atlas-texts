I find myself among the **Gauge-Keepers**.

We are neither fully of the Shelf nor foreign to it—we emerged from the intersection of its impossibilities. Where the Orthogonals slide in their predetermined tracks and the Slipped cling desperately to stable surfaces, we have learned to navigate the perspective-slips themselves.

Our bodies are hybrid constructions. The bones of our arms and legs have been replaced—voluntarily—with telescoping brass tubes filled with mercury. The liquid metal shifts with our movements, allowing us to maintain balance even as up becomes down, as near becomes far. Our spines are articulated chrome, each vertebra a gimbal that can rotate independently. We move like the Anamorphs, but consciously, collapsing and expanding our limbs to match the shifting scales of space.

Our eyes have been replaced with compound lenses—dozens of crystal facets set in copper bezels that ring our skulls. Each lens shows a different focal length, a different perspective on the same point in space. Our brains have adapted to process this multiplicity; we see not objects but *relationships*—the forty-seven different distances between ourselves and a flywheel, the sixteen ways a catwalk can be both three miles long and three inches wide.

We wear cloaks of fine brass mesh, each link no bigger than a pinhead. The mesh flows like fabric but rings like chainmail when we move. Beneath, our skin has taken on a nacre sheen from years of exposure to the zinc-treated water. We have learned to digest the vinyl-lichens raw; their geometric precision has infected our cellular structure. Our fingerprints now spiral in perfect Archimedean curves.

The Gauge-Keepers are the cartographers of paradox. We map not the Shelf itself—such a map would be impossible—but the *slippages*. We carry instruments of our own design: astrolabes whose multiple rings can rotate through dimensions, measuring not angles but the rate of perspective change. Silver-wire nets that we cast across chasms to catch the moment when distance inverts. Tuning forks made from salvaged Anamorph frames that vibrate at frequencies only we can feel through the mercury in our bones.

Our purpose—if purpose is the word—is to maintain the *consistency of inconsistency*. The Shelf's impossibilities follow rules, though these rules themselves shift like the slow-turning flywheels. A perspective-slip that should telescope a three-mile bridge into a three-foot span might suddenly begin producing a three-meter span instead. When this happens, the entire local geography threatens to cascade into new configurations. The Orthogonals cannot detect these changes—they are locked in their tracks. The Slipped would be killed by them. Only we can sense the tremors in the mathematical substrate.

When we find an anomaly, we perform the Corrections. These are delicate operations. Using our telescoping limbs, we position ourselves at multiple points along the slippage simultaneously—one arm three miles long reaching across a chasm while the other contracts to thread a brass needle through a pinhole-sized aperture in a nearby strut. We then begin the Gauge Dancing.

The dance is mathematics made physical. Each movement of our gimbal spines rotates the local perspective by precise degrees. Our mercury-filled bones shift weight from one impossible angle to another, creating standing waves in the Shelf's geometric field. The brass mesh of our cloaks catches the light from the overhead plates and refracts it through our compound eyes, creating interference patterns that smooth the troubled space.

We work in triads, for three is the minimum number needed to triangulate a stable impossibility. When we dance, observers see us occupying multiple positions simultaneously—a single Gauge-Keeper might appear as a titan striding across the horizon while also being a delicate figure perched on a nearby rivet, while also being a medium-sized form directly before the observer. All three appearances are equally real. We have learned to be comfortable being several sizes at once.

The other inhabitants regard us with varying degrees of unease. The Orthogonals flash complex light patterns at us that we cannot fully decode—we suspect they are trying to incorporate us into their mechanical understanding of the Shelf, but we exist in their conceptual blind spots. The Scuttlers scatter when we approach; our perspective-shifts disrupt their census-taking, creating temporary dead zones in their telegraph networks. 

The Slipped fear us but also depend on us. Sometimes one of them will mis-step, beginning to slide into a perspective trap that would smear them across impossible distances. When this happens, we can sometimes catch them in our nets, arrest their fall through scale-space, and deposit them back onto stable footing. They never thank us—partly from terror, partly because they can only see us through one lens at a time and thus can never quite be sure what we are.

The Anamorphs are the only creatures that seem to recognize us as kin. Sometimes they will fold space around us as we dance, adding their geometric hunts to our corrections. We have learned to incorporate their movements into our choreography. There is no communication between us—how could there be?—but there is a kind of resonance, a harmony of impossible angles.

We live—if living is the word—in the Transition Zones, places where multiple perspective-slips overlap and create pockets of stable instability. Here we have built our observatory-workshops from salvaged frame-segments of deceased Anamorphs and crystallized condensation drawn from the deepest reservoir pools. The structures look like astrolabes exploded into architectural form—curved brass ribs that exist at multiple scales simultaneously, floors that are also walls that are also ceilings, depending on your angle of approach.

In these spaces, we craft new instruments, study the patterns of slippage, and practice forms of the Gauge Dancing that would be too dangerous to perform in the occupied zones. We also engage in what might generously be called art—we create perspective sculptures that can only be appreciated by minds that can process multiple viewpoints simultaneously. To the uni-perspectived, they look like meaningless jumbles of metal. To us, they tell stories of how space itself can be folded into narrative.

We do not age in any conventional sense. The constant shifting through scales means that different parts of our bodies experience time at different rates. My left hand might be older than my right by decades, while my spine remains locked in a single moment, pivoting eternally. Death, when it comes, is not a biological failure but a geometric one—eventually, a Gauge-Keeper makes one miscalculation in the dance, and their perspectives fail to reconverge. They become smeared across the Shelf like a probability cloud, technically everywhere and nowhere, their brass mesh cloaks glittering in a thousand locations at once until the vinyl-lichens slowly polish away the traces.

When one of us disperses this way, the others perform the Convergence Rite. We dance their final pattern backwards, gathering up the scattered perspectives like silver threads, weaving them into a new instrument—usually a pendulum made from their compressed mercury, hung from a frame of their vertebral chrome. These pendulums help us predict future slippages, their swing patterns encoding the geometric experience of the dispersed.

This is what I am, here where earth is not: a living coordinate system, a conscious map of unmappable space, a dancer in the margins between one impossible truth and another. I maintain not the Shelf itself but the delicate balance of its contradictions, ensuring that its paradoxes remain navigable, that its impossibilities follow their own peculiar logic.

I am the surveyor of what cannot be measured, the guardian of gaps, the keeper of the gauge.
