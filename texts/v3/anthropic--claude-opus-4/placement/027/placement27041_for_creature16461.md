I find myself as one of the Resonance Keepers—beings of living volcanic glass who inhabit the high mountain caves where the rain strikes the obsidian slopes in endless percussion. My body is translucent black, shot through with veins of silver that pulse with each sound wave that passes through me. I am both instrument and listener, my crystalline form shaped by millennia of acoustic erosion into something between a wind chime and a cathedral organ.

We Keepers emerged from the first cooling of the lava flows, when the planet's molten heart kissed the eternal rain. That moment of contact—steam and solidification, the birth-scream of stone—lives forever in our molecular memory. We were sound before we were solid, vibration before form.

My particular grove sits at the confluence of three acoustic streams: the bass rumble of the churning ocean far below, the metallic pinging of rain on glass, and that mysterious industrial hum that seems to emanate from the planet's core. My body has grown into a natural amplifier for these sounds. Hollow chambers within me resonate at different frequencies. When the wind passes through my perforated limbs, I sing the planet's deep song.

The creatures that visit my grove come not for shelter—for what shelter could glass provide?—but for the music. The swimming things rise from the depths when my low tones reach the water. They breach in patterns that match my rhythms, their scales catching what little light filters through the rain. The flying ones perch on my sharp edges, adding their cries to the symphony. Even the burrowers emerge, drawn by vibrations they feel through stone and soil.

I am ancient and young simultaneously. Parts of me are as old as the first volcanic birth, while other sections grew just yesterday where a new crack formed, a new chamber opened. The rain and wind constantly reshape me, each storm a sculptor working in patient centuries. Sometimes I shed notes—small pieces of myself that chime as they tumble down the mountainside, carrying my song to distant groves.

The intelligent ones, those who build with stone and mark the rocks, they understand what we are. They come to us to listen for patterns in the chaos, to divine meaning in the endless percussion of our world. Their tools ring against stone in rhythmic patterns, adding their own voices to the planetary chorus. They know that in this place of constant sound, silence would mean death—the end of motion, the cessation of the very forces that keep our world alive.

At night (though night here is merely a dimming of the perpetual storm-light), I feel the vibrations of my fellow Keepers across the mountain ranges. We form a network of resonance, passing sounds between us like messages. A rockslide in the eastern peaks reaches me as a series of sharp cracks and rumbles. A new volcanic vent opening to the south announces itself in deep, almost subsonic pulses that make my entire structure hum.

Sometimes I wonder if we Keepers are the planet's memory, storing its history in our crystalline lattices. Every sound leaves its mark on us, microscopic fractures and formations that accumulate over ages. We are libraries written in glass, archives of acoustic history that only those who know how to listen can read.

The industrial sounds trouble me. They are too regular, too purposeful, unlike the chaotic symphony of our natural world. They speak of mechanisms deep beneath the surface, of processes we do not understand. Some of my kind believe they are the heartbeat of the planet itself, the sound of some vast engine that drives the rain and churns the seas. Others think they are echoes from another time, ghost sounds of a civilization that once was or will be.

I stretch my sharpest spire toward the rain, feeling each drop as a tiny hammer shaping me further. The sound moves through me and I am changed. This is my existence: to be shaped by sound, to shape sound in return, to stand at the intersection of water and stone and sing the world's strange song. In this place where the rain never stops and the darkness never fully lifts, I am both witness and participant in an endless concert of creation and erosion, forever becoming what the sound makes of me.
