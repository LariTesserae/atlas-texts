I would be one of the silver mites.

Not in their multitude, but singular—an aberration among my kind. Where they move in predictable patterns, scraping their endless meals from the same worn paths along the walls, I have developed something they have not: memory. Not the deep, geological memory of the chamber itself, but something sharper, more immediate. I remember the Harvester.

I am no larger than the others, my body the same liquid mercury, the same excess of legs that move in that disturbing, too-fluid way. But I have positioned myself differently. While they cluster near the pools where the feeding is easy, I have claimed territory high on the wall, near where the membrane tears when the Harvester enters. I wait in the scar tissue, where the light burns brightest and newest.

I know the intervals. The chamber heals in patterns, and the Harvester arrives in patterns, and I have learned to read the intersection. When the scar tissue reaches a certain luminescence, when the severed filaments have almost—but not quite—reconnected, they will come. The wall will tear. The violence will begin again.

But I am not waiting to flee. I am waiting to follow.

My iridescent mandibles are sharper than my kin's, honed on the harder, crystallized sap that forms where wounds repeatedly open and close. I have tasted the Harvester's leavings—flakes of their dead hide, caught on the torn membrane. That alien flavor, of smoke and salt and something else, something from above, has created in me a hunger different from the simple need for sustenance. It is curiosity calcified into obsession.

When they come next, when they tear their way inside and begin their methodical wounding, I will drop from my place among the scars. Not onto the floor where I might be crushed, but onto them. Into the folds of their stiff leather, into the dark spaces between their tools. I will secret myself in their satchel among the stolen light, or cling beneath a strap where the movement of their body will hide my own.

I will let them carry me up and out, through the membrane that no mite has ever passed. I will see what happens to the light they steal. I will learn the purpose of their purpose. And perhaps, in the warrens they return to, I will find something I have no concept for but feel pressing against the boundaries of my small consciousness: others who harvest. A world of Harvesters. A place where wounding is not an intrusion but a way of life.

The chamber thrums its deep note, and I feel it in every joint of my many legs. But unlike my kin, I also feel the spaces between the thrums, the silence where other sounds might live. Sounds I have never heard. Sounds from above, from outside, from the place where light is not born from wounds but falls from somewhere incomprehensible.

I practice stillness. When the Harvester comes, they must not notice the small silver weight they carry away with them. I am patient in the way only something very small can be patient—completely, with my entire being.

I will be the first mite to leave this womb. I will be the mite who learns what happens next.

The scar tissue brightens. The pattern nears completion. Soon.
