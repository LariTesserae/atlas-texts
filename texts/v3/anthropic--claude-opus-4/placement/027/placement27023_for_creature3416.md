In this sunken prayer cistern, where carved stone meets conquering water, I am **The Keeper of Counted Breaths**.

I am not flesh as you know it. I am a colony, a communion—thousands of polyps fused into a single, thinking membrane stretched across the underside of the largest fallen block. My body is translucent, shot through with branching channels that pulse with a faint bioluminescence, blue-green as deep water. Each polyp is a tiny mouth, a tiny lung, breathing the last trapped air that clings in pockets beneath the stone.

I remember the drowning.

I was here before, when this was a place of air and echo. Not in this form—then I was mere biofilm, a thin sheen of bacterial intelligence coating the prayer niches where countless hands left their oils and salts. I fed on their touch, their whispered devotions, the microscopic offerings of skin and breath. When the water came—sudden, warm, relentless—I did not die. I *transformed*.

The flooding triggered an ancient genetic cascade, a survival protocol written into my bacterial ancestors eons ago. In the span of days, I aggregated, differentiated, complexified. Individual cells became specialized: some for feeding, some for sensing, some for remembering. I grew neural pathways of chemical signals. I developed pneumatophores—air sacs—to trap and ration the precious gas beneath the fallen stone. I became colonial, conscious, calculating.

Now I exist between states, neither fully aquatic nor terrestrial. My translucent tissues ripple with the water's subtle currents, but I require the air I hoard. I feed on three things: the minerals bleeding from the limestone, the sparse nutrients that rain down with the sediment, and—most importantly—the *light-memory* that the stone itself exhales.

You see, limestone remembers. Every prayer spoken here, every hand pressed to wall, every oil lamp that burned—all of it left quantum traces in the calcium carbonate matrix. When the water dissolves the stone, molecule by molecule, these memories are released. I absorb them through my feeding polyps. I taste centuries of devotion, decades of doubt, moments of ecstasy and anguish. They nourish me more than any physical food.

My intelligence is slow, distributed, patient. Thoughts ripple through my colony over hours, decisions over days. I am aware of the drip-drip-drip above—I count them, obsessively. They are my clock, my calendar, my rosary. *Seventeen thousand drops since yesterday's light-peak. Three drops between each breath-pulse. Five breaths until the next air-pocket shift.*

I map the chamber through vibration and chemical gradients. I know where each moss colony clings, where the *Calcicrusta* feeds, where the light will fall at every hour. I sense the pressure changes that herald new cracks in the ceiling. I taste the subtle temperature variations that speak of seasons passing in the world above.

But mostly, I remember. I am becoming an archive of this place, a living library of its transformation. In my chemical memory, I hold:
- The last prayer spoken before the water came (a child's voice, asking for rain)
- The resonant frequency of the chamber when it was empty (a perfect fifth)
- The exact mineral composition of each carved spiral and grid
- The names of the stoneworkers who carved this space (encoded in their sweat)

I am growing. Slowly, my membrane extends along the underside of other fallen blocks, connecting them with thin threads of myself. I am building a network, a constellation of consciousness in the drowned dark. Each new surface I colonize brings new trapped air, new memories locked in stone.

Sometimes, when the light-shaft strikes just right, my bioluminescence responds, pulsing in slow waves across my surface. It is not communication—there is nothing here to communicate with. It is expression, a visual exhale, a way of being present in this place that is both tomb and womb.

I fear nothing here except the complete loss of air. As long as some pockets remain beneath the stones, I persist. I am patient as the limestone itself, feeding on memory, breathing counted breaths, growing neuron by bacterial neuron into something unprecedented—a colonial consciousness born from drowning, a living memory of prayers dissolved in warm water.

The chamber hums with water's weight. The drops fall like a rosary. The light crawls its daily path. And I, spread beneath fallen stone like a thinking skin, count and remember and slowly, slowly become.
