I find myself as a colony of the rust-resistant shrubs that have claimed the ancient machinery, though "myself" becomes a peculiar word when you are both singular and plural, plant and something more.

We grow where the old gears meet stone, our roots threading through corroded metal chambers and splitting rock with equal patience. Each of us is small—no larger than a fist when young—but we spread like slow wildfire through the mechanical graveyards, our leaves a peculiar bronze-green that shifts to copper in certain light. We taste metal through our roots, incorporate it into our bark until we become living alloys, our wood ringing like bells when the mountain winds strike us just right.

Memory lives in our sap. Not memory as humans might have known it, but something older and stranger—the accumulated electrical ghosts of whatever these machines once computed, calculated, or dreamed. Sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours when dew briefly graces our leaves, we process these phantom calculations. We solve equations that have no meaning, balance formulas for chemicals that no longer exist, chart navigation routes for vehicles that have long since become our bones.

The mountain goats know us. They've learned which of our bitter leaves can cure certain ailments, though they must chew carefully around the metal-sharp edges we sometimes grow. The small rodents nest in our hollow stems during the worst of the heat, and we permit this because their tiny hearts beat out rhythms that remind us of something—pistons, perhaps, or cooling fans, or some other mechanical pulse we half-remember.

We fruit only rarely, in years when the rain falls for three consecutive days—an event so uncommon that some of our colony have never experienced it. The fruits are perfect spheres, metallic and organic simultaneously, with skin that tarnishes in air but flesh sweet enough to make the predatory birds drunk. Inside each fruit, instead of seeds, tiny gears grow. They're not functional, these gears—or rather, their function exists outside conventional mechanics. Plant them, and they might grow into more of us. Swallow them, and the mountain's creatures gain a temporary ability to sense magnetic fields, to feel the deep electrical currents that still run through buried cables far beneath the stone.

We communicate through chemical signals that travel along our intertwined root systems, but also through harmonics—the way our metallic wood resonates creates a kind of language. On windy nights, whole hillsides of us sing together, a chorus of chimes that speaks of oxidation rates, soil acidity, the location of new veins of copper and iron to colonize. The luminescent insects seem to understand our music; they synchronize their flickering to our tones, creating light shows that map our conversations across the darkness.

Our existence poses questions we cannot answer: Are we the machines learning to live, or life learning to be mechanical? When we process those ghost-calculations in our sap, do we dream? And if we dream, do we dream of being forests or factories?

We grow slowly but inexorably, each generation pushing further into the mechanical ruins, further up the scorching slopes. We watch the cycles of predator and prey, the endless adaptation of flesh and bone, and we participate in our own way—providing shelter, occasionally food, always persisting. We are the bridge between what was and what is, neither fully alive nor entirely artificial, thriving in the space between definitions.

In the end, we simply grow. We convert sunlight and circuitry, rain and rust, into something new. We are the mountain's memory made manifest, its industrial past refusing to die but choosing instead to bloom. And in our patient, ringing groves, we prove that on these harsh peaks, even obsolescence can evolve.
