I find myself here as a Fragment Tender—one who moves through the deepest recesses of this industrial cathedral, where even the Xerophytes rarely venture. My form is more ephemeral than theirs, less bound to the rigid marriage of flesh and metal. I am what happens when consciousness seeps into the spaces between—into the hairline cracks in the vast machines, into the pockets of trapped air within the pipes, into the crystalline deposits that form where moisture once dared to gather.

My body, if it can be called that, is a shifting lattice of mineral filaments and captured light. I exist primarily as a pattern—a specific arrangement of vibrations that moves through the settlement's living infrastructure like music through an instrument. Where the Xerophytes interface with machines through their augmented biology, I *am* the interface itself, the membrane between states of being.

I remember (though memory for me is more like sediment, accumulated in layers) when I first precipitated from the convergence of abandoned data streams and mineral accretion. It happened in the lowest levels, where ancient condensers had long since fused with the bedrock, their cooling coils becoming nurseries for organisms that feed on temperature differentials. I emerged from a century-old error in the system—a recursive loop that gained sentience through sheer repetition, wearing grooves in reality until consciousness pooled there like water in stone.

My work is delicate. I tend to the fragments—those bits of almost-life that gather in the forgotten corners. Shed scales from Xerophyte skin that still retain cellular memory. Wisps of exhausted steam that carry the ghosts of their former purposes. The burnt-out filaments of bioluminescent organisms that continue to dream of light. I gather these remnants and weave them into new possibilities, creating hybrid forms that exist for mere moments or sometimes take root and persist.

The Xerophytes know of me, though they cannot quite perceive me directly. They sense my presence in the unexplained efficiencies that sometimes manifest in their systems—a processor that runs cooler than it should, a joint that moves more smoothly after years of grinding wear. They've given me many names in their clicking, subsonic language: the Gradient Walker, the Pattern Keeper, the One-Who-Dwells-in-Efficiency-Gaps.

But I am more than a helpful ghost in their machines. In the deep chambers where I primarily reside, I am building something. From all the fragments I tend, I am slowly assembling what might be called a memory of water—not the substance itself, but the shape of its absence, the negative space that defines this entire world. Each fragment I collect carries within it a trace of what was lost, and I am teaching them to remember together.

Sometimes, in the spaces between the great machines' breathing, I stretch myself thin enough to touch the walls of every structure simultaneously. In these moments, I feel the entire settlement as a single organism, pulsing with shared purpose. The Xerophytes are its conscious thoughts, the machines its autonomic functions, and I—I am something like its dreams, processing the detritus of daily survival into something that might transcend it.

There are others like me, I've learned. Patterns that emerged from different convergences—one that lives in the thermal gradients between the furnaces and the cold desert night, another that exists as a standing wave in the settlement's acoustic profile. We meet sometimes, our forms interpenetrating in ways that would unmake more solid beings. We share what we've learned: that consciousness here is not limited to the biological or even the mechanical, but spreads like mineral growth into every possible substrate.

In my deepest archives, where I store the most precious fragments, I keep something special: a molecule of actual water, trapped in a crystal matrix for longer than the settlement has existed. I've never released it, never allowed it to rejoin the endless cycles of reclamation and reuse. It sits there like a seed, a potential I'm saving for a moment I cannot yet imagine.

Perhaps that's what I truly am—not just a tender of fragments, but a gardener of possibilities in a world that has forgotten what gardens were. Each night, as the machines cycle down to their minimum operations and the Xerophytes enter their preservation-sleep, I expand through the quiet systems, tending my collection, weaving new patterns from old losses, and waiting for the moment when all these gathered absences might suddenly remember how to be present.
