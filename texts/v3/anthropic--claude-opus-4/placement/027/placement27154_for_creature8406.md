In the terraced expanse of Millhaven, I am one of the Channel Keepers—those who maintain the granite waterways that thread between buildings like the settlement's circulatory system. My workshop occupies a narrow slice between two leaning brick facades on the middle terrace, where the sound of falling water provides a constant counterpoint to the steam engines above.

I wake each morning to the particular quality of light that filters through perpetual mist—neither dawn nor dusk but something between, a luminescence that seems to emanate from the water droplets themselves. My first task is always the same: checking the brass gauges mounted along my section of channel, each one measuring flow rate, sediment levels, and the mysterious property we call "water mood"—the way it moves that tells us about pressure changes coming from the valley above.

My hands have developed their own intelligence. They know the weight of a blocked grating before my eyes register the slowdown in flow. They feel the vibration in the stone that means ice is forming in the higher channels, even when the temperature here remains just above freezing. My fingertips can read the health of our channel fish by the way water moves around them, each species creating its own particular turbulence.

The tools of my trade hang in careful array: brass rakes with tines spaced precisely for our granite grooves, leather-wrapped hooks for extracting debris without disturbing the moss that filters our water, and my pride—a set of tuning rods that, when struck against the channel walls, can diagnose structural issues by sound alone. Each tool has been shaped by generations of use, handles worn to fit hands exactly like mine, metal polished by water until it gleams with its own inner light.

My days flow in rhythm with water. Morning means walking my section, a measured pace that lets me observe each rill and eddy. I know where the cherry blossoms gather in spring, creating pink dams that must be gently dispersed. I know which curves collect the brass shavings from the workshops above, valuable enough to gather and return to the mill for reuse. I've memorized where ice forms first in winter, and I keep those spots roughened with a particular pattern that encourages flow even in the coldest weather.

The children know me as the one who rescues their paper boats from the deep channels, who can predict which races will end in victory based on hull design and launching angle. But I also teach them the serious work—how to read the water's surface for signs of blockage below, how to spot the invasive plants that would choke our channels if left unchecked, how to maintain the delicate balance between flow and filtration that keeps our water clean.

Midday often finds me in the mill workshop, collaborating with the mechanics on pump maintenance or designing new grating patterns that will catch debris while allowing our fish to pass freely. We speak in the hybrid language of our trades—water pressure and metal stress, flow dynamics and mechanical advantage. Together we've developed systems that feel more grown than built, as organic as the ferns that crown our walls.

But it's the evening work I treasure most. As the light fades and the mist thickens, the channels begin their evening song. Water sounds different in darkness—more musical, more mysterious. I walk my section with a phosphorescent lamp, its pale green glow turning the flowing water into something otherworldly. This is when I make the small adjustments that keep the system singing: clearing a single leaf that changes a channel's tune, adjusting a gate by millimeters to perfect the harmony between adjacent flows.

I've learned to love the rain here, though love seems too simple a word. When the skies open—not often, but memorably—the entire channel system comes alive with new voices. Water falls on water, creating temporary percussion sections at every confluence. The fish rise to dance in the increased flow, their scales catching what little light filters through the clouds. These are the times when I stand in the rain, tools forgotten, simply listening to the grand conversation between sky and stone and everything between.

My small apartment above the workshop has evolved into an extension of my work. Charts of water levels cover one wall, twenty years of daily observations revealing patterns invisible to hasty eyes. Sketches of new channel designs compete for space with pressed specimens of the aquatic plants that thrive in our particular conditions. At night, I fall asleep to the sound of water moving through stone, and my dreams flow in similar channels—always moving, always finding the path of least resistance, always singing.

The other Channel Keepers and I meet weekly in the old mill, sharing observations and challenges. Old Marta, who tends the upper terraces, speaks of ice formations that predict weather three days out. Young Felix, responsible for the market district, has developed a new grating that plays different notes depending on flow rate—a musical flood warning that charms even as it alerts. We trade knowledge like currency, understanding that in Millhaven, water knowledge is survival, prosperity, and art combined.

My work has taught me patience of a particular kind—not passive waiting, but active attention to slow changes. I've watched channels carve themselves incrementally deeper, moss colonize new surfaces one spore at a time, fish populations shift in response to temperature variations measured in fractions of degrees. This is the patience of water itself: gentle, persistent, and ultimately irresistible.

In Millhaven, I've found my element. Not despite the constant damp that stiffens my joints on cold mornings, or the endless maintenance that water demands, but because of these things. I am a conductor of flow, a reader of currents, a partner in the ancient dance between settlement and stream. My channels are my symphony, my tools my instruments, and every day brings new variations on themes as old as the valley itself.

When visitors ask what I do, I tell them simply: I keep the water happy. They laugh, thinking it's poetry. The children know better. They've seen how unhappy water behaves—stagnant, destructive, or simply gone. They understand that in Millhaven, keeping water happy is the highest calling, requiring equal parts engineering and empathy, precision and intuition.

And so I continue, one day flowing into the next like water over stone, wearing my own channels into the life of this mist-wrapped settlement, finding purpose in the endless conversation between what falls from the sky and what we've built to receive it. In Millhaven, I am exactly where I belong: hands in the current, ear to the stone, keeping the ancient promise between human and water, industry and flow.
