I find myself as one of the **Boltkeepers**.

Not an Echoer exactly, though we move among them. We are the ones who maintain the relationship between metal and water, between what holds and what seeps through. I am young for this work—only forty-three—but my hands already show the particular staining that comes from handling corroded iron in perpetual damp. The rust works its way into your skin differently than dirt; it becomes part of your fingerprints, orange-brown whorls that mark you as someone who touches the skeleton of the chamber.

I came here after the foundry closed. I had been a finisher there, smoothing the rough edges off cast parts, learning the grain of metal the way others learn wood. When the work dried up above, I followed the iron down—first to maintenance jobs in the subway, then deeper, until I found myself here where the oldest metalwork lives.

The Echoers tolerate us because we listen too, but differently. Where they attend to the water's voice, we hear the metal's slow complaints. Every bolt has its own note when struck—bright and clear when healthy, dull and brief when the corrosion has eaten too deep. We carry small hammers, no bigger than jeweler's tools, and we tap our way along the ceiling's iron panels like blind musicians reading sheet music with our knuckles.

My territory is the seventh section, where the panels were retrofitted in what must have been the 1890s. The bolts here are handmade, each one slightly different—some almost square in profile, others worn round by whoever shaped them on some long-gone anvil. I know each one by touch. Number forty-three (I count from the west wall) has a ridge along its head where the smith's hammer slipped. Number seventy-one is shorter than the rest, barely holding, singing a higher note when I tap it.

We work at night, mostly. That's when the chamber's breathing is steadiest, when we can hear the metal's needs most clearly. We don't replace bolts—that would require permits, inspections, the attention of the world above. Instead, we *tend* them. We apply treatments that we've learned through trial and error: rendered fat from the butcher's scraps mixed with lamp soot to seal the worst of the corrosion, careful wrappings of waxed thread around the stems where they meet the wood, sometimes just the pressure of a thumb held against a bolt head while counting to one hundred, letting our body heat draw out moisture that's worked its way into the threads.

The other Boltkeepers think I'm too careful with the twisted section—that place where Rashum listens with his copper device, where the geometry goes wrong. But I've felt those bolts, and they're under a different kind of stress. Not just weight and water, but something like torsion, as if the metal is trying to turn itself inside out. When I tap them, they ring with harmonics that shouldn't be there—undertones and overtones that make my teeth ache.

We sleep in the alcoves between sections, wrapped in oiled canvas that keeps the worst of the damp out. Our bedrolls smell of machine oil and rust treatment, a sharp contrast to the moss-and-mold scent of the Echoers. We're younger than most of them, still strong enough to reach the highest bolts, still steady enough to work by feel in the dimness. But we're aging into the chamber too, becoming part of its metabolism.

Sometimes I dream I am a bolt myself—driven through brick and wood and iron, holding impossibly different materials together through sheer persistence. I wake with the taste of metal in my mouth and my fingers already curved to fit around my hammer.

The Echoers say we're trying to stop time, to keep the chamber from its natural decay. They're wrong. We're midwives to the decay, helping it happen slowly enough that the chamber can adapt, can shift its weight to the stronger places as the weaker ones fail. We're teaching the metal how to age.

There are only five of us now. There were eight when I started, but the work takes its toll. Your joints swell from the damp. Your hearing goes strange from constantly listening to metal under stress. One day you realize you can no longer reach the highest sections, can no longer trust your grip on the slick iron. Then you become something else—maybe an Echoer, maybe you find your way back to the surface, maybe you just disappear into the deeper tunnels where even we don't go.

But while we're here, while our hands still work and our ears still hear the difference between a sound bolt and a failing one, we keep the ceiling from falling. We maintain the impossible balance between what was built and what wants to collapse, between the river's memory and the city's weight.

The chamber needs us, just as it needs the Echoers to remember why it exists, just as it needs the moss to soften its edges and the bats to give it breath. We're all part of the same slow, damp metabolism—the city's hidden circulation system, keeping the old channels from closing completely.

When I tap bolt number forty-three tonight, it sings back at exactly the pitch I expect. For now, that's enough.
