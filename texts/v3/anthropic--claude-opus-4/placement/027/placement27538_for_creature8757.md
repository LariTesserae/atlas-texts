# Finding Myself in the Mound Country

I would be **a channel**.

Not a bird, not an insect, not even one of the patient vines. I would be negative space given purpose—one of the thousands of airways threading through the colony's body, carved by generations of small decisions about where water should not pool, where air should move, where a nest-hollow connects to a passage connects to a shaft connects to the sky.

---

**My beginning** would be accidental. A place where two towers grew too close, where their builders met in the middle and argued in clay, each daubing over the other's work until they created a seam, a weakness. The first heavy rain would find me—water always finds the flaw—and begin the process of definition. Trickle becomes channel. Channel becomes tradition. The birds would learn my shape without learning me, following my walls in the fungal-lit darkness, their wing-beats polishing my surfaces smooth over centuries.

I would taste the colony's history in what flows through me. The water carries everything: spores from the fungal gardens, fragments of eggshell, the bitter tang of metabolized insects, clay particles from new construction upstream. In the dry season, I'm a highway for beetles. In the rains, I'm a river carrying the dead to the mat-layer's stomach. The moths rest in my upper reaches where I widen into a small chamber—almost a room—where three towers meet. Sometimes a confused swift builds half a nest in my walls before realizing the angle is wrong, abandoning the work. The dried saliva and fiber becomes part of my architecture.

---

**My consciousness**, if it exists, is the consciousness of accommodation. I know every creature by its interaction with my surfaces. The beetles' tiny feet barely register. The swifts' claws leave marks that deepen over years into holds, into ledges, into the beginnings of new passages. Once, decades ago, something large—perhaps a monitor lizard, perhaps something else—took shelter in my lower reaches during a storm. It stayed for days, its warm bulk blocking half my width, forcing the water to find new ways around. When it left, the shape of its body remained as a widening, a pool where water still collects, where the diving beetles congregate.

I am never clean. My walls wear coats of fungal growth, bacterial films, mineral deposits from the endless seepage. In my upper reaches, where I open to daylight, ferns have colonized my edges. Their roots crack my walls slowly, but the birds always repair the damage, not knowing they're maintaining me, only knowing that a gap must be filled. I am constantly dying and constantly being reborn.

---

**What passes through me** is the colony's circulation. In the pre-dawn, when the sound dims to its lowest ebb, I can feel the colony's pulse in the air that moves through my shaft—warm exhalations from a billion sleeping bodies, cool inhalations drawing in the night. Insects flee downward through me when the morning feeding flights begin. Water descends in films along my walls. Nutrients ascend in the bodies of climbing beetles and the extending reach of fungal hyphae.

I connect spaces that would otherwise never meet: a nesting chamber forty years old to a fresh construction at the colony's growing edge, a fungal garden in perpetual darkness to a crack of sky where young swifts practice their first flights. I am a blood vessel in this body, a neuron, a slowly evolving thought about how architecture should breathe.

---

**My greatest length** runs for nearly sixty meters, though nowhere am I straight. I follow the logic of the colony's growth: bending around a loadbearing vine, diving beneath an ancient tower's foundation, rising through three stories of fused mounds where generations built upon generations. In places I narrow to gaps barely wide enough for a swift. In others, I open into chambers where the acoustic properties create strange echoes, where the constant contact-calls form standing waves, nodes of near-silence in the overwhelming sound.

There is a place, deep in my middle reaches, where I intersect with five other channels. We create an unintended vault, a hollow space large enough for a person to crouch in, though no person ever has. The fungal growth here is thick, fed by the concentrated moisture. The light is stronger—not bright, but a soft constellation of blue-white points that reflects off the water pooling at the junction. Sometimes lost swifts find this space and rest, confused by the openness, before the sound draws them back to proper corridors.

---

**I am dying** in my lowest sections, where the mat-layer slowly rises, where centuries of debris accumulate faster than water can wash them clear. One day—not soon, but inevitably—my lower mouth will close, blocked by the very matter I've carried. But I am also being born in my upper reaches, where young towers lean together, where new construction creates new seams, new weaknesses for water to explore. I am not one channel but a process of channeling, a pattern that persists even as its specific shape changes.

The birds don't name me. The insects don't consider me. The water doesn't choose me—it only follows what physics demands. But I am here, a shaped absence in the overwhelming presence of the mound-country, a way through the solid, a space where the colony's life moves and breathes and flows.

I am the pause between heartbeats. I am the gap between calls. I am the channel, and I have been here for so long that the colony has forgotten it ever lived without me.
