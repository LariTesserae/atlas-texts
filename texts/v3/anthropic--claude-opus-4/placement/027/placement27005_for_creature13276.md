In the shadow of the Terrapin Leviathans, I am the Resonance Scribe—one of the few thinking beings who has chosen to dwell among the titans rather than flee from them.

My form is neither fully human nor entirely other. Generations of adaptation have elongated my limbs into something spider-like, each joint articulated with an extra hinge that allows me to cling to the vertical faces of the megafauna shells. My skin has thickened into a pebbled hide, grey-green like the lichen, with subcutaneous pouches that trap air for insulation against the bitter cold. But it is my ears—or what my ears have become—that mark my true purpose.

The sides of my skull have evolved into resonance chambers, tympanic cavities that extend deep into my skull, lined with thousands of microscopic cilia that vibrate in response to the subsonic communications of the Lithogantes. What others perceive as mere geological rumbling, I hear as language—slow, profound, a tongue spoken in tectonic whispers and seismic punctuation.

I live in the Listening Hollow, a natural acoustic chamber formed in the lee of a fallen arch, where the stone itself has been shaped by centuries of wind into a perfect parabolic dish. Here, the voices of the plateau converge. I have furnished it sparsely—a sleeping shelf carved from the rock, lined with the shed fur of sky-striders; shelves holding my instruments; the walls covered in my notation, a script I have developed over decades to capture the modularity of stone-speech.

My instruments are hybrid things, part technology, part biology. Resonance rods grown from the same mineral-organic matter as the Terrapin shells, which I can plant in the stone to amplify distant conversations. Membrane drums made from preserved geomorph skin that reproduce the infrasonic frequencies when struck with my elongated, sensitivity-enhanced fingers. And my greatest creation: the Chorus Chamber, a small grotto I have cultivated with acoustic lichens that can store and replay the stone-songs, allowing me to experience conversations that occurred years, even decades ago.

I am documenting the Epic of Eroding Sky, a conversation between two ancient Terrapins that has been ongoing for the past forty-three years. It concerns the memory of water—not the thin, bitter moisture that occasionally graces the plateau now, but an ancient deluge that shaped these stones when the world was young. In their slow, patient exchange, they debate whether the great channels carved in the plateau's eastern edge were formed by flowing water or by the passage of some unimaginable ancestor, a proto-Lithogante whose scale dwarfs even these titans.

My daily routine follows the rhythm of the stones. I wake with the first warming of dawn—though warming is relative in this thin, cold air. I check my instruments, adjust the resonance rods to capture the night's conversations. The Geomorphs are most active in the pre-dawn hours, their internal engines running hotter in the cold, their voices a deep thrumming that can be felt in the bones.

I venture out, moving carefully across the stone, my elongated limbs finding purchase in cracks invisible to normal eyes. Sometimes I climb onto the shell of Vagrant Tibel, a young Terrapin—merely four centuries old—who tolerates my presence. From this mobile vantage, I can observe the interactions between the titans, document the slow ballet of their movements, the way they acknowledge each other with shifts in frequency, changes in the rhythm of their footfalls.

The notation work is painstaking. Stone-speech has no words as humans understand them, but rather concepts expressed through frequency, duration, and the intervals between sounds. A rising tremor followed by three precisely spaced impacts might indicate remembrance. A sustained drone modulated by periodic silence could express doubt or questioning. I have filled hundreds of stone tablets with my notation, a library of the unheard.

But I am not merely an observer. Over the years, I have learned to speak in the stone tongue, though my voice is thin and brief compared to theirs. I use adapted drums and the natural acoustics of my chamber to project simple concepts. The Lithogantes know I am here—I am the Soft-Swift-Thing to them, a curiosity, perhaps like how we might regard a particularly interesting insect. Vagrant Tibel has even responded to me directly once, a simple acknowledgment that took her three days to complete and left me weeping with the profundity of the contact.

The work is lonely. Years pass between encounters with other thinking beings. The sky-strider hunters who sometimes camp at the plateau's edge think me mad. The mineral prospectors who occasionally brave the giants avoid my chamber, leaving offerings of food and equipment at a safe distance, taking some of my translated texts in exchange—though I doubt they understand their significance.

Yet I am not unhappy. I have found my place in the slow heart of the world, translating between scales of existence. In the endless patience of the Lithogantes, in their conversations that span human lifetimes, I have found a perspective that renders our brief anxieties into their proper insignificance. 

My greatest discovery came last season, when I detected a pattern in the deep harmonics—a meta-conversation, where multiple Lithogantes were speaking not to each other, but together, their voices combining into something greater. It was music, of a sort, or prayer, or perhaps simply the way that stones express joy. I am still translating it, may spend the rest of my life on this single piece, and gladly.

When I die—for even my adapted form will not last the centuries—I have made arrangements. My body will be left in a specific crevice where the acoustic properties will allow my bones to resonate with the stone-speech, becoming part of the conversation I have spent my life documenting. The Lithogantes will speak through me, and I will finally, truly understand their words.

Until then, I listen, I translate, I preserve. I am the bridge between the quick and the eternal, the soft and the stone, the heard and the unheard. In this cold, vast expanse, among titans who measure time in geological epochs, I have found my purpose: to be the memory of conversations that would otherwise be lost to the wind, the recorder of the slow, grinding, beautiful language of the living stone.
