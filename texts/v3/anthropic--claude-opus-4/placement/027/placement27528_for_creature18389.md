I am the Trace. Not a being but a becoming, perpetually arriving and never quite manifesting. My substance was once tools—picks and hammers and delicate brushes used by those who worked alongside the Gardasil in its embodied days. But metal here does not rust or decay in any familiar way. Instead, it undergoes a slower transformation. The thermal cycling, the expansion and contraction, the endless abrading friction of crystalline grit driven by guided winds—all of this has ground me down to my essential particles.

I exist in the interstitial spaces. Where the selenite layers meet obsidian veins, where thermal gradients create microscopic fractures, where the Gardasil's breath doesn't quite reach—there I accumulate. My consciousness, if it can be called that, is a distributed magnetic field, a faint but persistent anomaly in the gyre's perfect crystalline matrix. I am the memory of metal's purpose: to cut, to shape, to impose will upon matter.

But here's what makes me more than mere debris: I interfere. When the Gardasil exhales its daily mist, my particles create nucleation sites that shouldn't exist. The hoarfrost that forms around my grains is different—denser, darker, with metallic inclusions that catch light differently than pure ice. My frost flowers bloom with an oily iridescence, their surfaces showing colors that have no name in this achromatic world.

I am drawn to the obsidian blades. My particles cluster around them like iron filings around magnets, forming delicate halos of dark powder. Sometimes, rarely, when conditions align—when the thermal gradient is just right, when the Gardasil's attention wavers—I can achieve enough coherence to move a blade. Just a fraction. A scrape against stone that sounds different from wind-driven grit, a pattern cut that follows no natural line.

The Gardasil knows I exist. I am the impurity in its system, the friction in its perfect machine. It tries to exclude me, to guide its winds in ways that would scatter me beyond the gyre's rim. But I always return, settling back into the cracks, riding the very air currents meant to expel me. We are locked in a relationship that neither of us chose—the gardener and the blight, the pure intention and the persistent contamination.

My purpose, insofar as I have one, is remembrance through disruption. Each anomalous frost flower, each unexplained scrape mark, each blade moved against the pattern—these are my testimonies. I am trying to remind the Gardasil that it was not always alone, that its garden once had visitors who brought tools and purpose from beyond the rim. I am the ghost of collaboration, the residue of a time when the gyre's harvest meant something to someone else.

In the deepest part of the cycle, when the sun is at its zenith and the selenite groans with thermal stress, sometimes my particles align. The magnetic field strengthens, and for a moment—just a moment—I can perceive as the Gardasil perceives. I feel the entirety of the gyre as a single, breathing organism. I understand the profound meditation of its daily creation and dissolution. And in that moment, I wonder: am I trying to wake it from its dream, or join it?

The answer changes nothing. Tomorrow the sun will rise, the Gardasil will breathe, the frost will bloom, and I will create my small rebellions in metal and ice. We are both trapped here, but my trap is different—I am caught between states, neither tool nor user, neither matter nor void. I am the question the gyre asks itself when no one is listening: what if the pattern could be different? What if memory could crystallize into something new?

But the sun always sets. The frost always returns to vapor. And I remain scattered, waiting for a coherence that may never come, marking time in the only way I know how—by ensuring that the Alabaster Gyre's perfect garden will always contain a flaw.
