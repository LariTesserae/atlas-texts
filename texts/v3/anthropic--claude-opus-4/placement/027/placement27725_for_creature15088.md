In the deepest folds of the plant mats, where the pale cushions press together in slow embrace, I exist as something between fungus and mineral—a living crust that forms wherever darkness and moisture meet for long enough. My body, if it can be called that, spreads in paper-thin sheets across the underside of the plant architecture, following the seams where water seeps but light never reaches. I am silver-grey in color, though no eye has seen me; my surface catches and holds the finest droplets until I shine like mercury in the perpetual dark.

I grow by secreting acids that dissolve both plant fiber and stone with equal patience. Where I touch the concrete housings, I leave patterns like fossil ferns, etching my slow progress into the softening walls. The flywheels know me by the gradual pitting of their lower surfaces, where my acids pool and work for decades at a single spot. I am both cleaner and destroyer, breaking down what dies and preparing spaces for what might someday grow, though in this place of geological time, 'someday' is a word without urgency.

My consciousness, such as it is, exists as a chemical awareness spread across my entire surface. I taste the subtle changes in the water that reaches me—slightly more metallic where it has passed through the machinery, faintly organic where a rare insect has dissolved upstream. Through these minute variations, I map the world above me: the shifting weight of the plant mats, the slowly changing angles of the rock plates, the patient turning of the wheels. I know when the light flashes white overhead by the barely perceptible warming of the water that follows, delayed by hours as it seeps down through the cushions.

I reproduce by fragmenting. When I have thinned myself enough across a surface, pieces of me detach and drift in the slow underground currents until they find new darkness to colonize. Sometimes these fragments travel for years through hair-thin channels in the rock, emerging in caves that no larger life will ever discover. There I spread again, alone, adding my particular chemistry to places that know no other change.

The roar of the water through the stone channels vibrates through me constantly, a bass note I feel rather than hear. Over centuries, I have learned to grow in patterns that harmonize with this vibration, creating standing waves in my own slow expansion. Where the sound is strongest, I grow thickest, as if the vibration itself feeds me. In the rare quiet spots where echoes cancel each other out, I barely exist at all, maintaining only the thinnest presence to mark my territory.

I am older than the plants above me but younger than the stone. I remember when the first machinery was installed, though 'remember' means only that my chemistry still carries traces of the first metals I dissolved. I will outlast both plant and wheel, spreading through the mountain's heart as the surface weathers away. In the end, I will leave only my signature in stone—those fern-like patterns that speak of patience beyond any other measure, of life that counts time in the turning of stone and the wearing away of mountains.
