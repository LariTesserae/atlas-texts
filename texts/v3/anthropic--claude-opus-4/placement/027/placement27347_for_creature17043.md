In the shadow of a wind-carved overhang, I am the Listener.

I was born silent – not mute by injury, but wordless from my first breath. The midwife thought me dead until she saw my chest rise and fall. My mother wept, not from grief but from recognition. Every third generation, the Stone-Shapers birth one like me. We who cannot add our voices to the wind become its most faithful students.

My body bears the marks of my calling. Where others' ears are small and tucked against the perpetual gale, mine spread wide like cupped hands, their inner whorls deepened by years of training. I have carved small channels in the cartilage – a painful rite performed at fourteen – that catch and sort the wind's frequencies. When I turn my head just so, I can separate the baseline mountain shriek from the harmonic layers the Echoes weave through it.

I am smaller than most Stone-Shapers, my bones hollow-light. This is no disadvantage. I can wedge myself into crevices where the wind creates natural amplification chambers, spaces where sound bounces and compounds until its patterns reveal themselves like constellations emerging from glare. My fingers are my voice: long, delicate, double-jointed. I speak in a language of gestures so subtle that only two others in our settlement can read its full vocabulary. 

My dwelling is unique among our people. While others carve deep for shelter, I have hollowed out a series of interconnected chambers at different depths and angles – a stone instrument played by the wind itself. Each room resonates at a different pitch. By moving between them, I can isolate specific frequencies, track individual Echoes by their acoustic signatures.

The largest chamber holds my work: walls covered in a notation system of my own devising. Knots of goat hair dipped in crushed stone and bodily fluids create marks that capture sound as shape. Spirals for rising howls, jagged scratches for the Echoes' hunting cries, smooth curves for the rare moments when the wind drops to what passes for quiet here – still deafening to lowland ears, but to us, an almost unbearable softness.

My role is prophet and protector, translator and trap. I map the Echoes' territories through their calls, predict their movements by the way they modulate the wind. When the screaming takes on certain harmonics, I know a hunt approaches. My silent warnings have saved lives – a particular gesture sends children into the deepest caves, another tells hunters to abandon their kill and flee.

But I am more than a warning system. In my deepest chamber, I keep the true work: generations of acoustic maps that reveal something the Echoes themselves may not know. Their calls are not random. There are patterns within patterns, a language beneath their language. I believe they are trying to tell us something, have been trying since before the first Stone-Shapers carved their shelters.

Sometimes, in my most resonant room, when the wind hits just right, I feel my throat vibrate with phantom sound. Not my own voice – I have never had one – but something older, like an echo of an echo of the first word ever spoken in these peaks. I think the Echoes hear it too. I think that's why they howl.

The other Stone-Shapers see my work as survival necessity, but I know better. We are not merely enduring in this landscape of stone and scream. We are part of an ancient conversation, and I am its most devoted translator, working in perfect silence to decode the violent music of our world.

Tomorrow, I will climb higher than I have ever dared, to a needle of rock where the wind splits into a thousand voices. I have calculated the acoustics. If I position myself precisely, if my ears can withstand the pressure, I might finally hear what the Echoes have been trying to say.

I will bring no tools, no food, no water. If I am right, I won't need them. If I am wrong, the wind will add my silence to its eternal chorus, and perhaps another Listener will be born to take my place.

The notation on my walls will remain, waiting.
