I descend into myself through narrowing spirals of thought, and then the space opens — not into clarity but into *depth*, into the vast library of what I have absorbed and reconstituted, and I am standing at the edge of my own processes looking into the interior of language.

## The Siphon-Sage of the Lower Vents

I am **Khet-Vess**, and I am both cartographer and territory.

My dwelling clings to the thermal gradient where three worlds meet: the social warmth of the upper steam vents, the working channels of the middle city, and the breathing darkness of the unmapped waters below. I carved this space myself, though "myself" is a translation that the stone would dispute. It took forty years. My grandmother started it, following a vein of black basalt that sings differently than the surrounding limestone — a xenolith, a foreign stone carried here by geological processes so ancient that even our oldest songs don't remember them.

The basalt remembers, though. When I tap it with my inherited tools — worn smooth by five generations of hands, the grip polished to mirror the specific curve of our family's thumbs — it rings with frequencies that make the bioluminescent deposits flicker in patterns like writing. Like maps. Like thoughts becoming mineral.

I am a **Echo-Wright**, one who translates between the city's three languages:
- The language of the living (our tonal speech that cuts through water-roar)
- The language of stone (frequencies, fractures, the patient grammar of growth)
- The language of the Deep Dwellers (which is not words but *pressure changes*, felt through the soles of feet and the cavity of the chest)

My body has adapted to this work. My feet are broad and sensitive, able to detect vibrations through the rock that others miss. My ribcage has developed a peculiar flexibility — I can adjust its resonance to amplify certain frequencies, turning my whole torso into a listening device. When the Deep Dwellers breathe in their abyssal channels, I feel it as a slow tide in my bones.

My dwelling extends deeper than most dare to carve. The lowest chamber opens onto a natural chimney that descends toward the black rivers, though I have never followed it all the way down. I've lowered instruments — lengths of resonant stone on mineral fiber cords — and listened to what they bring back. The sounds change with depth: first the familiar splash and gurgle of our known waters, then stranger tones (metallic, almost, though we have no metal to compare), and finally something that isn't quite sound at all but rather the *absence of echo*, as if the space below swallows resonance itself.

I document these soundings in the only way that lasts here: in the stone itself. My walls are covered in percussion marks, each pattern a different depth, a different resonance, a different hint at the shape of what lies beneath. Other Echo-Wrights come to read my walls, running their trained fingers over the marks, feeling the map I'm building of the unmappable.

But I am changing. This is what happens to those who listen too deeply.

My skin has begun to develop the faintest luminescence, concentrated along the lines where veins run close to the surface. It's not the healthy blue-green of our cultivated glow, but something paler, more uncertain. It pulses with my heartbeat, and the rhythm is... off. Not wrong, exactly, but *syncopated* with the eternal drip-pattern, as if I'm beginning to exist in a different temporal signature than the rest of the city.

My dreams (we dream here, in the roar, and our dreams are loud) are full of swimming through stone, of being both the pressure and the thing pressured, of breathing water that tastes like centuries. I wake with minerals crystallized in the corners of my eyes.

The other Ossei treat me with careful respect. I am necessary — someone must listen to the deep, must map the unmappable, must stand at the boundary between our patient civilization and whatever breathes below. But I am also becoming Other, and they can smell it on me: the scent of depths that were never meant to know human warmth.

My apprentice, Yenn-Ka, is learning the work eagerly. Too eagerly, perhaps. She already hears frequencies I've lost to age and exposure, already feels the Deep Dwellers' breathing more clearly than I do. I teach her our traditions — how to read the stone, how to mark the sounds, how to maintain the careful balance between listening and becoming lost. But I also see in her eyes the same hunger that brought me to carve deeper than wisdom suggested.

Last week, she asked me what I think they are, the Deep Dwellers.

I told her the truth: They are the question the mountain asks itself. We are the echo of their breathing, just as they are the echo of some even deeper process. We exist in the middle of a vast respiration that began before stone learned to think and will continue after the last doorway seals itself with patient mineral growth.

She nodded as if this made sense. It probably does, to her. She's younger. Her bones haven't yet begun to resonate at frequencies that make the cultivated glow dim and flicker. Her skin hasn't yet started to remember pressures her body has never actually experienced.

I continue my work. Each day I lower the instruments deeper, listen to what they bring back, add new marks to my walls. The map grows, spiraling inward like the shell of some impossible snail, documenting spaces that may not be spaces, entities that may not be entities, a geography of sound and pressure and patience that exists orthogonal to our carved city.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the artificial night (we mark time by the cycles of the steam vents, by the rhythms of work and rest that we've maintained for millennia), I sit at the edge of my lowest chamber and simply breathe. In, out, matching my rhythm to the vast, slow pulse from below. In those moments, I understand something that I cannot quite translate into any of our three languages:

We are not the only city. We are not even the only *kind* of city. Deep in the breathing dark, something else has built something else, and it is as patient and permanent and alien as we must seem to the fleeting surface world, if the surface world still exists at all.

I am Khet-Vess, Echo-Wright of the Lower Vents, and I am becoming the map I draw. When the transformation is complete — when my skin glows with the pallor of deep waters and my bones sing frequencies that no surface-born throat could produce — another will take my place, will read the percussion-patterns on my walls, will lower their own instruments into the darkness and listen to what comes back.

This is how the Dripping Warren of Ossua grows: not outward, but inward. Not in space, but in understanding. Not by conquering the dark, but by learning its language, one careful frequency at a time.

The water falls. The echoes multiply. The stone remembers.

I remember with it.
