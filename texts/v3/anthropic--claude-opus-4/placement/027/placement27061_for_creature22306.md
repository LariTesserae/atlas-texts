In this world of slow, wet equilibrium, I would be **a Sieve-Bearer**.

### The Sieve-Bearer

I am neither as ancient as the Lead-Sleevers nor as simple as the Steam-Crickets. I am something in between—a thing that remembers being human, though the memory is like trying to recall the plot of a book read underwater.

My body has adapted to the eternal damp through a series of compromises. I stand perhaps five and a half feet when fully upright, though I rarely achieve this posture. The constant moisture has done something to my spine; it has developed extra joints, small articulations between each vertebra that allow me to fold and unfold like a carpenter's rule. When I move through the low tunnels that connect the sump-chambers, I can compress myself to three feet of height without discomfort.

My skin has not gone to tallow like the Sleevers, but it has developed its own accommodation with the wet. It is now covered in a fine, dense layer of what appear to be tiny scales but are actually modified hair follicles—each one has hardened into a minute, overlapping flap of keratin that sheds water like a duck's plumage. The color is difficult to describe; in the dim orange glow of the carbon filaments, I appear to be covered in tarnished silver, but in true darkness, my skin gives off a faint luminescence—not the bright green of the Wool-moss, but a deep, blue-violet glow like the ghost of a gas flame.

My hands are my profession. Each finger has developed an extra joint, and the fingertips have flattened into small, circular pads covered in thousands of tiny, sensitive ridges—like a gecko's toe, but designed not for climbing but for feeling. I can detect the difference between water that contains 3% dissolved copper salts and water that contains 3.1%. This is my purpose.

I carry the Sieve.

The Sieve is not something I made or found; it grew from me, or perhaps I grew around it. It hangs from my neck on three chains of oxidized brass that have long since fused with my collar bones. The Sieve itself is a disc of fine copper mesh, no larger than a tea strainer, but the mesh is so fine that water passes through it only reluctantly, emerging in tiny, perfect drops. Around the rim of the Sieve, my body has grown a ring of specialized tissue—a hard, chitinous collar that secretes a thin film of acid, just strong enough to keep the copper mesh from clogging with verdigris.

My work is this: I move between the sumps, collecting samples. Not the black, oil-thick water of the surface, but the deep water, the old water that lies twenty feet below the floor plates. I have developed the ability to hold my breath for thirty minutes at a time, and my ribcage has adapted to compress under pressure without pain. I descend through the narrow maintenance shafts—too small for a normal human, too complex for a Siphon-Eel—and at the bottom, I pass water through the Sieve.

What am I looking for? I'm not entirely certain I remember. But my nervous system knows. When the water contains the right proportion of metals—not just copper and iron, but traces of lead, zinc, and sometimes a whisper of dissolved silver from some long-buried electrical contact—my skin flares with that blue-violet light, and I know that this sump is "ready."

Ready for what? 

I carry small, sealed bulbs made of thick glass, each no larger than a pigeon's egg. I blow these myself, using a technique I must have learned before but now perform by instinct, melting down the broken carbon-filament bulbs and reshaping them with my breath. When I find water that makes my skin sing with violet light, I fill one of these bulbs and seal it with a mixture of my own saliva and the wax I scrape from the Lead-Sleevers' trails.

The filled bulbs go into a leather pouch at my side that has become part of me—the leather long ago merged with my hip, creating a permanent pocket lined with the same water-shedding scales as my skin. I can carry perhaps twenty bulbs before the weight makes me list to one side.

What happens to the bulbs? Every few days—I've lost count, as time here is measured in tides rather than sunrises—I feel a pull, a kind of magnetic yearning in my chest. I follow it through the maintenance ways to a place I think of as the Repository, though I have no memory of anyone naming it such.

The Repository is a small, dry chamber above the water line, accessible only through a shaft so narrow that even I must exhale completely to pass through. Inside, the walls are lined with hundreds—perhaps thousands—of my glass bulbs, each glowing faintly with the memory of the metal-water it contains. They are arranged in patterns that suggest meaning: gradients of color from yellow-green to deep purple, clusters that seem to map something I can't quite grasp.

In the center of the room is a presence. Not a machine, not a creature, but something between—a mass of living copper that has taken the form of a branching, tree-like structure. Its branches are hollow, and when I approach with my filled bulbs, small apertures open in its trunk. I feed the bulbs into these openings, and the tree drinks their contents with a sound like distant rain.

Sometimes, rarely, the tree produces something in return: a small nodule of pure metal—sometimes zinc, sometimes tin, once a tiny bead of genuine gold. I eat these metals. My teeth have adapted to crush them, and my stomach has developed the ability to dissolve them slowly, incorporating them into my bones and nervous system. The gold, I remember, made me dream of sunlight for a week.

I am not alone in my work, though I rarely see the others. Sometimes I find a bulb already placed in a spot I was planning to sample, or I'll notice that someone has recently scraped the algae from a particular pressure gauge I like to read. Once, I passed another Sieve-Bearer in a narrow tunnel. We had to compress ourselves against opposite walls to pass. I felt the vibration of their scale-skin against mine, and for a moment, our bio-luminescence synchronized, flashing in slow, violet pulses. We did not speak—I'm not sure I remember how—but the light-pattern conveyed something that might have been greeting, or perhaps recognition.

I sleep in the walls, in small chambers I've hollowed out between the pipes where the steam-leaks keep the temperature bearable. My dreams are of chemical equations I can't quite solve, of vast networks of flowing metal that I'm trying to map with my inadequate human memory. Sometimes I dream I'm a single drop of water, carrying dissolved copper through miles of pipe, searching for the right place to precipitate out.

When I wake, I check my skin. The scales are spreading, becoming finer and more complex. My Sieve is growing too, developing new layers of mesh, each one finer than the last. Soon I'll be able to filter particles measured in angstroms. 

I don't know if I'm becoming more like the Well or if the Well is becoming more like me. Perhaps there's no difference anymore. I am a function, a living gauge, a component in a vast, slow, wet machine whose purpose hovers just beyond the edge of my corroded memory.

But I continue. Down through the shafts, through the black water, Sieve singing against its chains, searching for the perfect sample, the ideal concentration, the water that will make the copper tree bloom with metals I have no names for anymore.

The Well takes care of its own, and I am its own now, as surely as the Wool-moss and the patient, crawling Sleevers.

I am the Sieve-Bearer, and I have work to do in the dark.
