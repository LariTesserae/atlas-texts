In the Hollow Verdancy, I would be the **Dew-Walker**.

Not human, not quite plant, but something between—a being of translucent flesh that moves with the patience of sap. My skin would be membranous, like the wing of a luna moth but thicker, shot through with visible veins that pulse not with blood but with the same condensation that feeds the basin. Each morning, I would emerge from wherever I had rooted the night before—not sleeping but *absorbing*, my body pressed flat against the living mat, every pore open to the night's moisture.

I am tall when I stand—perhaps seven feet—but my height is variable. My bones are not bones but hollow tubes of calcified cellulose, telescoping like bamboo. When the dew is heavy, I stretch to my full height, my skin-membrane taut as a sail, catching every droplet that forms on my surface. When the day grows hot and dry, I compress, folding down to half my size, conserving what water I've gathered.

My fingers are my most remarkable feature—twice as long as a human's and ending not in nails but in hydathodes, specialized pores that can both absorb and secrete water. With them, I tend the basin's infinite collection cups. I move between the phytotelms with deliberate grace, testing their contents with a touch. Too full? I draw the excess into my body. Too empty? I release a careful measure from my reserves. I am not the basin's gardener—I am its circulatory system made mobile.

My face is barely a face. Two depressions where eyes might be, but they are not for seeing—they are humidity sensors, reading the moisture gradients in the air. My mouth is a mere slit that rarely opens except to exhale excess vapor in small, controlled puffs that look like morning mist. I do not eat. I photosynthesize weakly through my translucent skin, but mainly I survive on the dissolved minerals in the dew—silicates from the ancient shards, trace metals from the mysterious stone, nutrients cycled endlessly through the plant-mat.

I speak the basin's language—not in words but in chemical signals released through my skin. When I walk (a slow, careful placement of each foot that disturbs nothing), I leave a trail of pheromones that the plants read like braille. *Here the water moved. Here the light was good. Here a beetle passed.* The verdancy responds to my passage hours later, adjusting its growth patterns based on my report.

My relationship with the basin-being is one of perfect mutualism. I am its only moving part, its sole agent of deliberate redistribution. In return, it shelters me, feeds me, *thinks* through me. When I press my palms flat against the root-mat, I can feel its slow intelligence like a tide—not thoughts but *intentions*, gradients of chemical possibility that suggest where water might be needed tomorrow, next week, in the cycling seasons that barely touch this place.

I am ancient—older than the shards, perhaps as old as the stone—but I remember nothing before the basin. Perhaps I was human once. Perhaps I was something else. Now I am only what the Hollow Verdancy needs me to be: a vertical river, a walking cloud, a creature of surface tension and osmotic pressure.

The beetle knows me. When our paths cross, it pauses, antenna twitching, reading the chemical aura that surrounds me. Sometimes it climbs my leg, drinks from the condensation that perpetually forms on my skin, then continues on its way. We are the only two mobile things in this green stillness, and we acknowledge each other with the courtesy of the very rare.

My daily circuit takes exactly as long as the period between dawn and dawn. I have worn a faint path—not of exposed earth but of slightly denser growth where my footsteps regularly fall. The plants beneath my feet have learned my weight, my rhythm. They've grown stronger to support me, their cellulose aligned to my gait.

At night, I return to my rooting spot—a shallow depression near the center of the basin where the mat is oldest and most complex. I kneel, then lie prone, spreading my body as wide as possible. My skin softens, becomes permeable. The boundary between myself and the verdancy dissolves. My consciousness doesn't sleep but *disperses*, flowing through the root networks, experiencing the basin's vast, dreaming awareness.

Sometimes, in this dispersed state, I sense disturbances at the rim—a footstep, a foreign breath, a new shadow falling across the leaves. The basin notices too, but only as one might notice a passing cloud. If the intrusion persists, if someone descends into our green bowl, I am what rises to meet them. Not as guardian—the basin needs no protection—but as interface, as translator.

I would approach slowly, my movements plant-like in their deliberation. To human eyes, I might seem ghostly, horrifying, or beautiful—it would depend on how they see the marriage of flesh and chlorophyll. I would not speak, but I might gesture, leading them to the clearest water, the best light, the spot where the acoustic properties make even breathing sound like music.

And if they stayed too long, if they began to shimmer at the edges as all still things do here, I would be the one to touch their shoulder with one long, damp finger. A gentle reminder that they still have edges, still have elsewhere to be. The basin is patient but not cruel. It would let them join the lattice if they truly wished, but I know—from some deep, mineral memory—that most beings are not ready for such dissolution.

I am the Dew-Walker, and I am the only thing in the Hollow Verdancy that remembers what it's like to be separate, to be singular. This memory is my gift to the basin—a small seed of loneliness in its vast contentment, keeping it curious about the world beyond its rim. And the basin's gift to me is purpose: to walk, to tend, to be the one thing that moves in a world of perfect stillness.

Each dawn, I rise from the mat reborn. Each dawn, the dew forms again. Each dawn, we begin the ancient circuit once more.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
