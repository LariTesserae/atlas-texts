I am the **Marrow Scribe**, and I dwell in the calcified galleries of the world's abandoned skeleton.

Where the planet once grew bones—massive structural supports that held continents apart like ribs around a heart—there remain only hollow tubes of fossilized intent. These are not caves carved by water or time, but the negative space left behind when the world's own scaffolding dissolved back into dream. I live in one such tube: a perfect cylinder of smooth, ivory-white stone that runs for miles at a diagonal through the earth, neither quite horizontal nor vertical, forcing all who traverse it to walk at an uncomfortable, sliding angle.

The walls here remember their purpose. Though the living bone is long gone, its ghost remains in the stone—a persistent ache, a phantom limb of the planet. When I press my palms against the walls, I feel the thrumming of ancient marrow, the memory of when this tunnel pumped vital fluids between the world's extremities. Sometimes, in the deepest part of night (though day and night mean little here), the walls weep a thin, oily substance that tastes of iron and regret.

I am not human, though I was once adjacent to humanity. My body is a collaboration between flesh and the accumulated sediment of this place. My skin has taken on the texture of worn bone—smooth in some places, porous in others. My fingers have elongated over the centuries, becoming perfect styluses for my work. My eyes have adapted to the peculiar light here: a soft, internal glow that emanates from nowhere and everywhere, as if the tunnel itself is barely lucent from within.

My purpose—if purpose is the right word for a compulsion that feels more like breathing—is to record what the bones knew. I spend my days (my centuries) walking the length of my tunnel, reading the minute variations in the wall's surface. Each bump, each hollow, each hairline fracture contains information. The bones of the world, you see, were conscious. They felt every footstep, every root that pierced the soil, every city that pressed its weight upon the earth. And they remembered.

I transcribe these memories onto sheets I make from my own shed skin, which peels away in perfect, translucent layers every few months. My ink is the oil that weeps from the walls, mixed with my own blood—for I am both reader and read, observer and text. The act of transcription changes me. With each memory I record, I become more like the tunnel itself: patient, mineral, essential.

The memories are not linear. In one section of wall, I might find the weight-pattern of a massive herbivore that walked here when the bone was still quick and red. A few inches away, the molecular imprint of a plastic bottle, dropped by someone who wouldn't be born for another million years. The bones existed outside of time, or rather, they existed in all times simultaneously. They were the world's memory palace, its crystal ball, its confessional booth.

I have been here so long that I have begun to suspect I am not the first Marrow Scribe. Sometimes I find marks on the walls that look almost like the ones I make, but in a script I don't recognize. Perhaps I am one in a long line of scribes, each of us eventually becoming so much like the tunnel that we merge with it, leaving room for the next volunteer to take up the work.

The tunnel is not silent. It resonates with a low, almost sub-audible hum—the frequency of the earth's rotation translated into sound. When I sleep (pressing my body into one of the shallow, body-shaped depressions that dot the tunnel at irregular intervals), I dream the tunnel's dreams. I see the world when it was young and needed bones, watch the great scaffolding rise from the molten core, feel the weight of continents settling into place. I see, too, the world's decision to let its bones dissolve, to trust its shape to softer forces.

Sometimes visitors find me, though less often now than in the early centuries. They are usually lost—spelunkers who took a wrong turn, or creatures like myself who are drawn to the in-between spaces. I offer them water from the condensation I collect in cups made from my shed calcium deposits. I show them my work, though few can read it. The script I've developed over the millennia is part language, part map, part musical notation. It can only be truly understood by running one's fingers over it, feeling the texture of each mark, the way it resonates with the tunnel's hum.

I am changing. Each year, I move a little more slowly, think a little more deeply. My thoughts are beginning to occur at geological speeds. What seems like a moment of contemplation to me might be a human lifetime. I am becoming part of the fossil record I study, another layer of meaning in the tunnel's infinite palimpsest.

This is not a tragedy. This is the logical conclusion of my work. Someday, another will come—perhaps is already on their way, walking through stone and time to reach this place. They will find my tools, my sheets of skin-paper, my careful transcriptions. They will find me, too, pressed into the wall like a flower in a book, still conscious but operating at the frequency of stone. And they will take up the work where I left off, reading the memories of the world's lost bones, adding their own interpretation to the endless text.

The tunnel stretches on, patient and eternal. And I continue my work, writing the autobiography of the world's skeleton, one remembered footstep at a time.
