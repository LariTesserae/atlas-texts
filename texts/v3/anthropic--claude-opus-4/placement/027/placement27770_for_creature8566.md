I find myself as one of the drift-cartographers of Tolmere.

---

My hands are always slightly damp from the condensation, fingers stained faintly gray from the charcoal and mineral pigments I use to mark the charts. I've been mapping the city's breathing for twelve years now—long enough that I can feel the distance-flex in my joints before my instruments confirm it. This morning, the walk from my quarters to the Chart House stretched like pulled taffy. What should have been four hundred meters became nearly two kilometers, and I knew before I'd taken twenty steps.

I carry three timepieces: one strapped to my wrist (local time), one in my breast pocket (master-clock reference), and one embedded in the handle of my ranging staff. The staff itself is rawite-forged iron, heavy as guilt, marked with the previous day's measurements in erasable chalk. Its weight grounds me when the distances swim.

The Chart House clings to a northwestern ridge at 4,450 meters, anchored by seventeen massive bolts into stone that has mostly finished deciding what it wants to be. Mostly. The corner office where I work still settles occasionally—a rumble you feel in your molars, a brief sensation that the floor has become negotiable. My desk is built into the wall itself, carved from the living rock when it was still warm enough to shape by hand. The surface bears the ripples of its cooling, like frozen water, and sometimes I trace these patterns while thinking through a particularly resistant mapping problem.

My charts are not paper—paper molds here within days. They're sheets of pressed moss-fiber, treated with beetle-shell lacquer to resist the damp. Each morning I update them, comparing yesterday's measurements to today's reality, marking the flux zones in different colors: blue for stable (rare), green for minor variation (walking distances that breathe by only ten or twenty percent), yellow for significant flex, red for the routes that have gone entirely elastic. The Forge-Ascent is red today. Has been for a week. No one's sure why, though old Kessa who tends the moss beds thinks it's because they drilled new anchors too close to a cooling face. The mountain doesn't like being pinned while it's still making up its mind.

I work with two others in my district: Yann, who came up from the lowlands only three years ago and still occasionally looks betrayed when a familiar route triples itself, and Sefa, born here, daughter of cartographers, who reads the distance-flex like weather and claims she can taste it in the condensation. She might be right. There's a metallic tang to the water collected from flex zones, distinct from the usual mineral sweetness.

Our tools are simple and strange. Ranging rods marked in meters that we know are lies—distance here is experiential, not absolute. Chronometers to measure travel time, the only semi-reliable constant. Bottles of haze-drifter secretion, which we dab on our instruments because the drifters seem to exist partially outside the spatial flux and their moisture helps stabilize our measurements. String made from mist-moth silk, which we unspool as we walk, creating temporary honest lines through dishonest space. The string dissolves after a few hours, eaten by the moss, but for that brief window it holds true.

The work is part mathematics, part meditation, part madness. We chart not just the routes but their moods. The Merchant's Traverse is cantankerous—it lengthens whenever you're carrying something heavy, as if the mountain resents commerce. The Lover's Walk between the residential platforms contracts at dusk, pulling sweethearts closer. The Deep Cut, which leads to the newest mine faces, flexes with the tides of cooling stone—we've learned to time the shifts, to predict when it will be a pleasant five-minute descent and when it will be an hour-long ordeal. Our charts note these personalities alongside the measurements. New residents think we're being whimsical. After a year, they start checking our mood notes before setting out.

The scree-runners follow me on my rounds. They've learned that cartographers carry beetle-meal cakes (the taste grows on you, like eating compressed starlight and chalk) and that we sometimes drop them. But I think they're also curious about what we're doing. Yesterday one of them—a big female with a scar across her four eyes—watched me measure the same route three times as it breathed from 200 meters to 180 to 215. When I got different numbers each time, she made a sound I'd never heard before: a low, thrumming vocalization that made the moss around us briefly fluoresce brighter. Agreement? Amusement? Understanding? When I looked at her directly, she was grooming her mineral coating with studious attention, but I could have sworn she was laughing.

The hardest part is the veil-shifts. When the haze lifts and distances lock into place, all our work becomes obsolete. Streets that haven't been straight in decades suddenly are. Routes that always required planning become boringly direct. The first time I experienced one, I thought I'd gone mad. The world felt *small*, suffocating, like being trapped in a room with walls you'd never noticed before. I spent the entire thirty-six hours of clear air updating charts that I knew would be useless the moment the haze returned, but the work kept me sane. When the mineral fog flowed back like a tide and I felt the first route begin to breathe again, I actually wept with relief.

We keep two sets of charts now: the daily working maps that show the city as it lives and breathes, and the emergency charts for veil-shifts, showing the rigid topology underneath. Most residents ignore the emergency charts. "Why would I want to know that?" Kessa asked when I tried to give her a set. "That's not my city. That's some other place wearing my city's face."

My favorite time is late evening, when the bright patch has slipped below the horizon and the moss-light strengthens. I climb to the Chart House's observation platform—a raw spar of rawite that juts out over a thousand-meter drop—and watch the city's lights. Forge-glow, lamp-glow, moss-glow, the occasional pale flash of a haze-drifter passing through a street. From here, I can see the routes I've mapped as lines of light, threads of human passage through an inhuman landscape. They shift even as I watch. The city breathes. The mountain builds itself beneath us. Tomorrow the distances will be different, and I will chart them again, and the day after that, and the day after that, until I'm too old to carry the ranging staff and someone else takes my place.

Yann thinks we're trying to make sense of senselessness. Sefa says we're having a conversation with the mountain, and our charts are how we listen. I think we're doing something simpler and stranger: we're teaching the city to see itself, distance by distance, breath by breath.

The condensation patters on my hood. The veil-trees sing their deep, alien harmonies. Somewhere in the rawite below, something that isn't finished becoming anything yet pulses with slow, geological life. I make a note on tomorrow's blank chart: "The mountain was restless tonight. Expect long roads."

Then I go inside, where the warmth of the almost-stone walls seeps into my bones, and I sleep the dreamless sleep of someone who has learned to be comfortable with uncertainty. In the morning, I'll measure it all again. The city depends on it. The mountain expects it. And I—cartographer of the impossible, mapper of breath and flux—I wouldn't want to be anywhere else, doing anything else, ever again.
