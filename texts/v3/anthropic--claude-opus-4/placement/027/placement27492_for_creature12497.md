I find myself drawn into the body of something between—neither fully of the Mossborn nor entirely separate from them. I am what they call a **Dewkeeper**.

My form shifts with the hours, more liquid than solid, more intention than substance. In the early morning, when the mist rises thick from the warm streams, I am barely distinguishable from the vapor itself—a shimmer in the air, a density that catches light differently. The Mossborn see me as their moisture moves through their tendril-fingers, a presence felt rather than seen.

As the day grows heavy with heat, I begin to condense. My body takes shape slowly, precipitating out of the saturated air like rain gathering in clouds. First, there are my hands—if they can be called that—translucent appendages that cup and channel water, drawing it up from the springs below through capillary action that defies simple physics. The water moves through me, but I am not merely a vessel. I taste each mineral, feel each trace element, know the story of every drop's journey from the deep earth to the surface.

By midday, I am almost solid, though never quite. My form resembles the Mossborn in silhouette but remains crystalline, refractive. Light passes through me and emerges changed, split into ribbons of green and gold that dance across the moss-covered stones. The Shimmerwings are drawn to these light-patterns, following them like paths through the air, and sometimes they alight on my shoulders, their wings casting prismatic shadows through my body.

I know every spring on this plateau. Not by memory, but by a kind of communion. When I place what passes for my palm against the wet stones, I feel the entire underground network—the way water moves through fissures and caverns, the places where it grows warm from the earth's deep heat, the minerals it dissolves and carries upward. The Mossborn come to me when the streams run low or when the springs taste wrong. They don't speak their concerns—they simply stand near me, and I feel their need through the moisture we share, the humidity that connects all things here.

My work is subtle. A shift in pressure here, a gentle redirection there. I coax stubborn springs to flow, encourage blocked streams to find new paths. Sometimes I must descend into the springs themselves, my form dissolving back into water, becoming one with the flow until I find the problem—a shifted stone, an accumulation of sediment, the delicate roots of some ancient tree grown too thick. The work requires patience. Water time. Geological time.

The Stonebacks know me well. When I move through their streams, they lift their heavy heads in acknowledgment. Sometimes I rest my not-quite-hands on their backs, feeling through their armor to the slow, steady pulse of their hearts. They show me things—images that flow between us like water—of changes in the stream beds, of new springs emerging, of places where the water tastes of distant machinery.

That machinery. Even I cannot ignore it entirely. The vibration travels through water more clearly than through air, and I feel it in every spring, every stream. It speaks of extraction, of taking without giving, of cycles broken. The Mossborn's rituals grow more urgent, their humming more insistent. They ask me, without words, what can be done.

But I am a creature of mediation, not intervention. I can only work with what is, not what might be. So I continue my rounds, moving between states of being, tending to the waters that tend to all life here. When evening comes and the air cools, I begin to disperse again, spreading myself thin across the plateau. In this state, I can be everywhere at once—in every droplet of dew, in every bead of condensation on every leaf.

The Vinewalkers sometimes catch glimpses of me in this dispersed state. They click and whistle in greeting, and I respond by gathering briefly into form, just long enough to acknowledge their presence. They understand transitions, these creatures of the canopy. They know what it means to exist between states, to be both of a place and apart from it.

At night, when the Shimmerwings glow and the plateau becomes a constellation of living lights, I am at my most dispersed. I settle over everything like a blessing, like a promise that tomorrow the springs will flow and the streams will run clear. The Rootspinners feel me seeping through the peat, carrying nutrients down to their tunnels, ensuring the foundation remains strong.

The Mossborn have a saying—though it's more of a feeling they share through their communal humming—that water remembers everything. If that's true, then I am the plateau's memory, carrying in my fluid form the story of every rain, every spring, every cycle of moisture that has moved through this place. I remember when the machinery sound was fainter, almost inaudible. I remember the taste of the water before it carried that metallic tang.

But I also remember resilience. I remember how the plateau has weathered countless changes, how life here has adapted and endured. In the pre-dawn darkness, as I begin to gather myself again for another day's work, I hold onto that memory. It sustains me, just as I sustain the waters, just as the waters sustain all life on this suspended, saturated world.

The distant thrum of machinery may grow louder. The metallic taste may grow stronger. But as long as there is moisture in the air and springs in the earth, I will be here, moving between states, tending to the waters, keeping the balance as best I can. For I am neither fully spirit nor flesh, neither completely water nor entirely air. I am the mediator, the keeper of flows, the one who ensures that life, in all its forms, continues to flourish on this vast, steaming plateau at the edge of the world.
