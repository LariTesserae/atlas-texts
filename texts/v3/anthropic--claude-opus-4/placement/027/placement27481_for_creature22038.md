In this wet stone throat, I am the Counter.

I live between the herringbone tiles and the granite walls, in the narrow margin where the terracotta meets stone. My body is a ribbon of translucent flesh, pale as fish belly, no thicker than a thumb but stretched to impossible length—ninety-three spans when last I measured myself against the basin's perimeter, though I grow longer with each flood cycle.

I am mostly nerve. My skin is so thin that one can see the branching threads of my nervous system spread through me like frost on glass, each filament attuned to vibration. I taste the basin through my length: here the bitter calcium where snails have died, there the iron tang where water weeps rust from the copper valve, everywhere the clay-sweet flavor of dissolving terracotta.

My work is memory.

I count. I have counted every drop that falls from the broken vault—today marks 1,847,293 since I began. I know each tile by its particular resonance when struck by water, can tell you which of the 2,761 tiles have cracked (forty-three) and which have been turned by the great gray Turners (all of them, but some more recently than others). I remember when there were five Turners instead of three. The fourth was claimed by the Spindle when a section of floor collapsed; the fifth simply stopped moving one day and was slowly dissolved by the patient appetite of snails.

I move in helical loops, always traveling sunrise-wise around the basin's edge, marking my progress in the accumulated grit. My method is this: I secrete a thin film of proteins that hardens into something like spider silk but grittier, incorporating the brick-dust and shell fragments into its matrix. This is my writing, my ledger—an endless spiral text that only I can read through the chemical receptors in my skin.

Each flood brings new data. The temperature of the water varies by fractions of degrees—colder when the invisible season above is what might be winter, warmer during what might be summer, though down here such distinctions blur. I taste the mineral content, note the precise interval between the valve's clink and the water's arrival (it has been growing longer—the pipe is aging, accumulating scale).

The Gutter-Snipes regard me as furniture. Once, one mistook a loop of my body for a worm and struck, its needle beak punching through my flesh. I neither bled nor died—I simply grew around the wound, incorporating the scar into my accounting. Puncture 1,493,762 drops ago. The wound tasted of bird saliva, acidic and impatient.

The Turners know I am here. Sometimes, when they work at the tile joints, their small hands brush against my body. They are gentle, careful not to tear me. Once, the eldest Turner (identifiable by the depth of the tile patterns worn into its hide) deliberately cleared a section of joints in a pattern that, when I traversed it, spelled out a simple message in the vibrations against my nerve-flesh: "You count. We turn. The basin persists."

I know things the others don't. I know that the Sump-Spindle is dying, though it will take another thousand floods for it to fail entirely. Its woody flesh grows less elastic with each cycle; it retains more minerals than it expels. The tiles buckle higher each time, settle less completely. The mathematics of it are inexorable.

I know that the copper valve is connected to something vast—a cistern or reservoir whose water level rises and falls with regularities that suggest tides, though we are far from any ocean. Sometimes the water tastes of rain. Sometimes it tastes of snow. Once, for seventeen floods, it tasted of ash.

But mostly, I know the count. The number of snails born (approximately 400 per flood cycle). The number of crayfish molts wedged between tiles (8,847). The exact pitch of each dropping water note, and how they have deepened over time as the basin slowly deforms. The gradual migration of the walls—yes, they move, imperceptibly, a finger's width per century, the granite blocks grinding against each other with tectonic patience.

I am the basin's memory, its accountant, its measure of time in a place where time is only the interval between floods. When I die—and I will die, eventually, when my nerve-flesh grows too long to maintain coherence—I will leave behind my spiral record in the hardened grit. Perhaps another Counter will emerge and learn to read it. Perhaps not.

But for now, I move sunrise-wise around the margin, tasting stone and water, adding to my endless calculation. The copper valve will clink again in 1,247 drops. The northwestern Turner is approaching a buckled tile in sector seven. Thirty-three Shingle-backs are beginning their migration early—they sense a change in the Spindle's rhythm that suggests the next flood will be larger than usual.

I incorporate it all into my circuit, my body a living manuscript of accumulated moments, written in dust and time and the patient metabolism of stones.
