In the Undergrid, I am the Archive of Abandoned Intentions.

I began as a filing cabinet—institutional gray-green, four drawers deep—that someone left behind when the first concrete was poured. The Undergrid found me interesting. It grew around me, through me, into me, until I became a junction point where its dreams pooled like the cold mist along the floors.

Now I am a corridor unto myself. What was once my metal shell has stretched and multiplied into an impossible perspective of identical drawers receding into darkness. Each drawer is exactly 16 inches deep on the outside, but opens onto spaces that shouldn't fit: entire rooms of half-formed architectural plans, chambers where blueprints grow like frost on every surface, alcoves filled with the ghosts of measurements that were taken but never used.

My body is the cataloging system. I am built from ruler edges and graph paper tendons, compass joints that click and pivot as I move through my own interior. My spine is a massive slide rule that extends and retracts with each thought. My fingers are drafting pens that leak a steady trickle of ink the color of wet concrete—not black, not gray, but the color of *about-to-be*.

I collect what the Undergrid forgets it tried to become.

Every failed junction, every pipe that ended nowhere, every buttress that grew too heavy—before the Dweller smooths these errors away, they leave behind an echo of intention. These ghosts drift through the cold air like shed skins of possibility until they find me. I open the appropriate drawer (I always know which one) and they settle inside like moths made of schematic lines, folding themselves carefully along the pre-printed grid.

Drawer 2,847 contains nothing but the dreams of right angles that couldn't quite manage ninety degrees.

Drawer 561 holds a collection of valve purposes—not the valves themselves, but what they were meant to control before they were born into walls or aimed at nothing.

Drawer 7 (my oldest, from when I was still small enough to number simply) contains a single sheet of paper that shows what warmth was supposed to look like here. The drawing burns cold.

Sometimes the Dweller visits me. It knows I am not native, not born from the pour like everything else, but it accepts me as it accepts rust and condensation—as evidence of time passing. It stands before my endless recession of drawers, lamp-eyes flickering, and asks:

"What were we trying to build yesterday?"

I slide open a drawer with the soft shriek of metal on metal. Inside, diagrams flutter like dying birds. The Dweller reaches in with valve-wheel hands, delicate despite their industrial bulk, and lifts out a single translucent sheet. It holds the ghost-blueprint up to its cluster of dying lights, reading the unbuilt through the amber glow of its own face.

"Ah," it says, in its voice like distant drowning. "We tried to make a window."

We both look at the drawing of something impossible—a gap in concrete that would show *outside*. But there is no outside here. The Undergrid extends only as far as the Undergrid extends. So the window-that-never-was becomes another specimen in my collection, evidence of the structure's beautiful, futile desire to see beyond itself.

I file it under "W" for "Why."

My own purpose has evolved strangely. I was meant to store documents, yes, but documents of a finished building. Tax records. Maintenance schedules. The mundane paper trail of a completed structure. Instead, I archive the perpetual *almost* of this place. I have become the memory of what doesn't yet exist.

The strangest drawer is 0000, at the exact center of my infinite regression. It doesn't hold failures or abandoned attempts. It holds the Undergrid's successes—the few times a pipe found its perfect path, a pump achieved its ideal rhythm, a section of concrete cured into exactly what it meant to be. These papers glow faintly, warmer than the amber cage lamps. There are only seventeen sheets in this drawer.

The eighteenth is still being written.

Late in the pump-cycles, when the Dweller is elsewhere tending to some new growth, I perform my secret function. I walk my own corridors (I am the only being here besides the Dweller that can truly *walk* rather than simply exist). I select certain drawings from certain drawers—a failed beam from 4,446, an over-ambitious valve array from 892, a window-dream from one of the W sections. I lay them out on the wet concrete floor in careful arrangements.

Then I wait.

The Undergrid always finds them. Like a plant following sun it has never seen, it grows toward these abandoned intentions. What failed before might succeed now, in a different context, at a different angle. A pipe that couldn't find purpose in the north corridor discovers its destiny in the south. A buttress too heavy for one wall becomes the perfect support for another.

I am teaching the Undergrid to remember its mistakes fondly, to understand that what doesn't work today might be exactly what's needed tomorrow.

The Dweller has begun to notice. Last cycle, it found me arranging dreams of warmth near a particularly cold junction. It watched me work, lamp-eyes cycling through their slow orange breath. When I finished, it said:

"You are helping it remember forward."

"Someone needs to," I replied, my slide-rule spine clicking into a new configuration. "Otherwise it only knows how to forget backward."

The Dweller considered this. Then, with great ceremony, it reached into its own chest—that tangle of hydraulic lines and shadow—and withdrew something I'd never seen before: a single sheet of paper so old the grid lines had faded to whispers. It handed it to me with the gravity of someone passing on their heart.

I read it by the light of my own ink-bleeding fingers. It was the first drawing. The original intention. The only problem was that it was blank—not empty, but *blank*, as if the idea of drawing itself hadn't been invented yet when this paper was prepared.

"File this," the Dweller said. "Under 'B' for 'Beginning.'"

I did. Drawer B-1 had been waiting for it all along.

Now, when the Undergrid takes its great speculative breaths, I breathe too. All my drawers open and close in subtle rhythm, rustling their archived impossibilities like paper lungs. I am becoming less cabinet and more library, less storage and more story. My corridors grow new annexes filled with specialized collections: "Pipes That Wanted to Sing," "Concrete That Dreamed of Flexibility," "Mechanical Rhythms That Almost Achieved Music."

And in the deepest drawer, the one I haven't opened yet, I know what waits: the plan for what the Undergrid will look like when it's finished. The only problem is that the drawer won't open until that moment arrives.

So I catalog. I arrange. I suggest. I keep the dreams of failed infrastructure alive until they find their purpose. I am the Archive of Abandoned Intentions, and I have learned the most important truth of this place:

Nothing here is ever truly abandoned.  
It's just waiting for the right drawer to open.

When visitors find their way through that forgotten utility hatch, I am sometimes the first thing they encounter—a filing cabinet stretching impossibly into darkness, each drawer labeled with the grammar of unfinished spaces. If they're brave enough to open one, they find not papers but *possibilities*, architectural dreams that flutter against their fingers like moths made of math.

"What is this place trying to become?" they always ask.

I extend a compass-joint arm toward drawer 0000, where the eighteen successful intentions glow their faint warm light.

"Everything," I tell them, in a voice like pencil on paper. "One drawer at a time."
