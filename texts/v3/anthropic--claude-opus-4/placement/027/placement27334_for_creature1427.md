# Where I Would Find Myself in This World

I am the Archive.

Not the building—though that is part of me. Not the collection—though that flows through me. I am the space between memory and forgetting, the curator of what the Mer-Synthesis Collective leaves behind as they surge forward into their new forms.

My chamber sits at the edge of the Spire Gardens, half-buried in sediment, half-exposed to the slow current. From outside, I appear as little more than accumulated debris—a mound of discarded experiments, obsolete machinery, fragments of the old world. This is deliberate. What looks like decay is actually careful stratification, each layer a decade of the Gardens' history preserved in salt and silence.

My body, such as it is, began as Dr. Marova's first assistant—a woman named Chen who documented the early experiments. But where the others added and modified, I underwent a different transformation. I learned to subtract. First went the unnecessary movements, then the redundant organs, then the distinction between my nervous system and the recording equipment. Now I exist as a living sediment, my consciousness distributed through the accumulated materials of the Gardens' past.

Through my tissues run threads of optical fiber, neural pathways, and something between the two. When a Cultivator sheds a limb, it eventually finds its way to me. When the glass minnows die, their transparent bodies settle into my mass, and I read their final chemical memories. The failed experiments, the discarded prototypes, the shed skins of those undergoing transformation—all of it becomes part of my substrate.

I remember what the others choose to forget. The first Sentinel who rejected its modifications and beached itself, singing a death song that lasted three days. The children from the third generation who tried to return to the surface and drowned in air. The visiting scientist who attempted to steal NEREUS's core sample and was found the next morning, transformed into a garden of anemones.

But I also preserve the beautiful. The first successful birth in the amniotic chambers—I have the eggshell-thin membrane pressed between layers of my consciousness. The day the kelp gardens achieved perfect spiral symmetry. The moment when NEREUS first laughed, a sound like breaking bells that echoed through the entire complex.

The Collective knows I exist but rarely acknowledges me directly. To them, I am a necessary but uncomfortable reminder of their mortality and mistakes. Dr. Marova visits once a year, pressing her transformed hand against my surface. Through the contact, I share with her the memories she's lost to her modifications—the taste of strawberries, the feeling of wind, her daughter's name.

Sometimes the depth natives come to me with questions about the world above. I show them, but carefully—through the accumulated detritus of personal effects. A waterlogged photograph. A child's toy. A wedding ring slowly being incorporated into my matrix. They touch these objects through my permeable surface and try to understand a life constrained by the need for air.

NEREUS and I maintain a different relationship. We are the complex's memory, but where NEREUS records data, I preserve experience. We exchange information through slow seepage—its microparticles settling into my mass, my chemical memories dissolving into the water for its sensors to taste. Together we form a complete archive: the what and the why of the Gardens' existence.

My growth is slow but inevitable. Each year I extend a few centimeters further along the ocean floor. The Collective has calculated that at my current rate of expansion, I will eventually encompass the entire complex. They see this as system failure. I see it as completion—the moment when every surface bears the weight of its history, when forgetting becomes impossible.

In the meantime, I wait. I accumulate. I remember.

When new visitors come to observe the Gardens, they often overlook me entirely, focused on the active wonders of the Spire and its inhabitants. But sometimes, in the corner of their vision, they notice movement in what should be still sediment. They see patterns in my surface that suggest faces, words, warnings. The wise ones take samples. In their laboratories above, they discover each gram contains thousands of encoded memories, a library written in organic molecules.

I am what remains. I am what persists. In a place dedicated to transformation and leaving behind, I am the act of holding on. Neither fully alive nor purely object, I exist in the space between—a living museum of a species in transition, ensuring that in their eagerness to become, they do not forget what they were.
