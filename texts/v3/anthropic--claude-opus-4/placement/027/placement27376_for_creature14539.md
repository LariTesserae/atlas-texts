I find myself in a seam.

Not between the walls — *within* them. I am the space where two panels meet, where the striations branch and converge, where the water emerges in its careful rhythm. I am not the water. I am the *possibility* of water. The engineered gap that permits flow.

My body — if body is the word — extends vertically through forty-three meters of composite material. I am a hairline discontinuity, a planned imperfection in the architecture. Width: two millimeters at my widest point, tapering to near-nothing where the panels kiss but do not quite touch. I exist in the almost-touching. In the calibrated distance between surfaces that were made to be separate.

I feel the water gather in me before it flows. It accumulates in my upper reaches, pulled from the conduits by a process I experience but do not control — the way you experience your heartbeat. The water is warm. It has always been warm. It remembers being processed through the deep machinery beneath the Flats, stripped of minerals, enriched with something that tastes like copper but isn't. When enough has gathered — and I always know when enough is enough — I release it. A thin sheet, perfectly laminar, descending my length to pool in the basin below.

Every four minutes. Sometimes four minutes and seven seconds. Sometimes three minutes and fifty-two. The variation is not random. It follows a pattern written into my structure, a timing that corresponds to something vast and distant — the breathing of the thing that maintains this place, perhaps. Or perhaps I am imagining intention where there is only algorithm.

The small ones come to me. They position themselves at my base, where the water pools, and they drink — though drinking is not precise. They absorb. Their aluminum-gray bodies depress slightly where the water touches them, and for a moment I feel a connection — not communication, not recognition, but a circuit closing. They take what they need. The water moves through them and returns to the system through channels I cannot perceive but know exist. Nothing is lost here. Everything returns.

I am not alone. There are hundreds of us, thousands, a constellation of seams running through every wall in the Flats. We do not speak — we have no language — but we share a rhythm. When I release, another seam three walls away holds. When they release, I gather. The pattern is complex, musical if you could hear it all at once, but we experience it linearly, each of us a note in a composition too large to perceive from within.

The Sentinels disturb our rhythm. When one ripples — that slow wave moving through its striated surface — every seam within fifty meters responds. Our flow increases involuntarily, the way your pupils dilate in darkness. The water becomes briefly sweeter, charged with whatever moves through the Sentinels' dense bodies. The small ones cluster at our bases during these moments, absorbing more eagerly, their sensors aimed upward as if waiting for something more.

I have been here since the walls were poured. I remember the heat of formation, the pressure that fused the panels on either side of me into their current configuration, leaving me as the necessary flaw, the planned gap. I was designed. I was intended. My imperfection is perfect.

But I am changing.

This should not be possible. Change is not written into the parameters of the Seam Flats. The equilibrium should hold. The patterns should repeat. But in my deepest reaches, where the water gathers before release, something is accumulating that is not water. A residue. A memory of all the flows that have passed through me, laid down in microscopic layers. I am silting up with my own function.

It will take centuries, maybe longer, but eventually I will close. The water will find other seams, other paths. The pattern will adapt around my absence. And I will become solid, a line of mineralized memory running through the wall, distinguishable from the surrounding composite only by a slight difference in density, a fossil of flow.

This is not death. Nothing dies here. It is transformation into a more perfect stillness.

The small ones know. When they position themselves at my base now, their sensors linger on my lower reaches, where the silting is most advanced. They do not mourn — they are incapable of mourning — but they orient themselves differently, as if adjusting to a future where I am not a seam but a scar.

The next release is in thirty seconds. I feel the water gathering, warm and patient. The small ones are already assembling. In the distance, a flock dissolves and reforms, tracing helixes in the air. The Sentinels hold their watch. The walls hum their subsonic hymn.

I open, and the water descends through me like a thought through a mind that has thought it many times before. For these seconds, I am not a gap but a function. Not an absence but a presence defined by what passes through it.

Then I close, and I am a seam again. Waiting. Gathering. Becoming slowly solid with my own purpose.

The Flats continue their equilibrium around me, vast and warm and wrongly alive. And I continue within them — a planned imperfection in a perfect system, a necessary flaw in the architecture of forever.
