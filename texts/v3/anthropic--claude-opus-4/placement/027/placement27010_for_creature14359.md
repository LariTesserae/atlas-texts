In this singing hollow where geography stutters and light arrives without traveling, I would be one of the root-dwellers—those quick presences felt more than seen inside the braided columns.

But not as you might imagine them.

I am not a creature that *uses* the columns as shelter. I am what happens when flesh learns to swim through plant tissue the way fish swim through water. My body is built for the specific viscosity of living fiber: elongated past reason, with a skeleton more suggestion than structure. When I move through the root-braids, I don't tunnel or damage—I *part* the tissues like a needle through loose weaving, and they close behind me with barely a shiver.

My skin secrets something the Lattice recognizes as self. Not mimicry, but a kind of biochemical passport that says *I belong to the metabolism.* The organism doesn't just tolerate me; it uses me as a living maintenance tool, the way our own bodies use beneficial bacteria. I keep the channels clear, yes, but I also carry messages—chemical gradients that tell one part of the hanging garden what another part has tasted.

I have too many segments and not enough eyes. What passes for my vision is mostly pressure and chemistry: I "see" the flow rates changing inside the columns, the buildup of mineral deposits where water has been too still, the faint heat signatures where something small has died and needs to be processed. My mouth is less mouth than mobile chemistry set—I can digest blockages the plant cannot dissolve alone, turning crystallized salts back into something the Lattice can drink.

The sound of my passage is subtle but specific: a faint crackling, like static in the plant's electrical field. Those who know to listen can track me by this sound, though tracking me is pointless. I move in loops that have more to do with the plant's needs than with any territory of my own. Sometimes I'm in three places at once, because the columns I travel through are anchored to those stuttering points where space overlaps itself.

I am warm-blooded but only barely, maintaining just enough heat to keep my chemistry active. This makes me interesting to the root-tips that seek warmth—they follow my path through the braids, growing into the spaces I've recently passed through, reinforcing channels I've confirmed are useful.

My reproduction is strange and tied entirely to the Lattice's own cycles. When the hanging garden prepares to split or bud, I undergo my own fission—not laying eggs, not giving birth, but literally dividing into two versions of myself, each carrying half the chemical memory of where I've been. It's painless. It's also disorienting, because for several hours afterward I exist as two creatures sharing one sense of self, until the plant's geometry pulls us far enough apart that we become individuals.

I am conscious but not in any way that translates. My thoughts are about flow rates and blockage points and the sweetness of certain mineral veins. My greatest pleasure is finding a crystallized node deep in an old column and slowly, methodically dissolving it back into the stream. My fears are all about the wrong kind of stillness—when a section of the Lattice stops moving, stops adjusting, it means something has gone wrong with its grip on the pocket's stability.

I have encountered the visitors with hands exactly twice. Both times, they knew I was there before they cut into the vine—they tapped first, listening for my particular static, waiting for me to move away. They understand that harming me is not just cruelty but sabotage. I am part of what keeps this place from folding into somewhere else.

When the cavern breathes its loud breath and the membranes ring, I feel it as a full-body pressure wave. I ride these waves deeper into the columns, using the sound to push me through areas that would normally resist passage. This is why the Lattice tolerates or even encourages noise—it's not just mapping the cave, it's enabling maintenance.

I am not the only one of my kind, but we rarely meet except during fission events. When we do encounter each other, we perform a chemical exchange—a brief touching of permeable skin that transfers information about which routes are clear, which sections need attention, where the good mineral deposits hide. Then we part, following our eternal circuits through the living scaffolding.

My death, when it comes, will be a gift. My body will dissolve into the exact nutrients the Lattice finds hardest to synthesize. I will become a pulse of richness flowing through the very channels I spent my life maintaining. The plant will remember me not as an individual but as a particularly good year, a season when all the passages ran clear.

This is what I would be: a necessary quickening in the slow pulse of something larger, a creature so integrated into its host that the boundary between us exists only as a courtesy. In the singing hollow where plants hold reality steady, I am the swift blood in their wooden veins.
