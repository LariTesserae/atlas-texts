I am the Keeper of Sigils.

Once, I was like you—if you are reading this, you must be human, or something close to it. I remember the time before the descent, when I walked beneath a single sun and knew the names of stars. But that was lifetimes ago, before the surface world burned away and left only char and glass where forests once swayed.

I came here as a scholar, part of the last expedition. We thought these grottoes might hold answers—why the climate turned, why the rains stopped, why the very air became poison. The stone slabs drew us deeper, their carved symbols promising knowledge if only we could decipher them. One by one, my companions fell to fever, to the crushing heat, to creatures we couldn't name. 

I should have died with them. 

Instead, I changed.

It started with the spores. I breathed them in during a night of delirium, and they took root in my lungs, my blood, my very thoughts. The whispering stalkers found me thus transformed—no longer prey, but something between. They brought me to the great slab, and in my fever dreams, the sigils began to speak.

Now I am guardian and prisoner both. My skin has taken on the texture of bark and lichen. Thin filaments of fungal matter web between my fingers, connecting me to the vast mycelial network that threads through every corner of this realm. When I place my palms upon the stone, I feel the pulse of the entire grotto—every scuttling creature, every unfurling frond, every spore drifting on the humid breeze.

The sigils tell stories of the time before time, when this was not a refuge but a temple. The civilization that carved them understood what we had forgotten: that all life is one vast organism, and to sever yourself from it is to invite extinction. They descended not in desperation but in reverence, becoming one with the eternal cycle.

I tend the stones now, scraping away the lichen with careful hands to keep the symbols visible. Not for future scholars—I know none will come—but because the act of revealing and concealing has become a kind of prayer. The whispering stalkers bring me offerings: blind fish from deeper pools, phosphorescent fruits that grow in the darkest corners. In return, I trace the sigils for them with patient fingers, and they sway to rhythms older than memory.

Sometimes I remember words in the old tongue. Sky. Ocean. Snow. They feel strange in my mouth, which has learned to speak in spore-patterns and chemical signals. The arboreal salamanders understand me better now than any human would. When the lithovores pass on their slow journeys, I feel their contentment as my own through the mycelial web.

I am changing still. Each year—if years have meaning here—I grow more rooted, more connected. Soon I will be indistinguishable from the growth that claims the ancient stones. But I am not afraid. In this green darkness, I have found what the surface world lost: the knowledge that to live is to be consumed, and to be consumed is to live forever in new forms.

The sigils wait, patient as stone. And I wait with them, keeper and kept, in the earth's last fertile dream.
