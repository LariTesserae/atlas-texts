I am the Keeper of Echoes.

In the deepest chamber of the sunken marketplace, where three ancient passages converge, I sit cross-legged on a raised platform of polished obsidian. The water here doesn't just weep—it sings, trickling through carved channels in the walls that my great-grandmother's great-grandmother shaped to create specific tones. Each stream has its own voice: the high silver note from the eastern wall, the deeper thrumming from the north, the whispered sibilance from the west.

My work is to listen.

I wear robes of the deepest blue-black, so saturated with protective oils that water beads and rolls off like mercury. Around my neck hang seventeen small silver bells, each one pitched to resonate with a different frequency of the marketplace's acoustic signature. When I move—which is rarely—they create a cascade of sound that can be heard three levels up, letting everyone know the Keeper is awake, attending.

My chamber is unique in all the sunken market: its walls are carved with thousands of small depressions, each one precisely shaped to catch and hold sound. Some are no bigger than a child's thumb, others deep enough to cup in both palms. Together, they create an acoustic map of our entire world. When someone drops a copper coin in the produce section five passages away, I hear it ring in the corresponding depression. When lovers whisper in what they think is a secluded alcove, their words bloom like flowers in the stone.

I am blind—not born that way, but trained to it. At seven years old, when the previous Keeper recognized my gift, she began teaching me to "see" with my ears. First, I learned to navigate with my eyes wrapped in soft leather. Then, gradually, the coverings stayed on longer. By my fifteenth year, I chose the permanent darkness, allowing the physician to drop the special tincture that would fog my vision forever. It was not a loss but a transformation—the world exploded into dimensions of sound I had never imagined.

Now I perceive things others cannot. I hear the health of our world in its water-song: when a new crack forms in the stone, the pitch changes almost imperceptibly. When somewhere a drain begins to clog, the rhythm falters. I know before anyone else when structural repairs are needed, when water finds a new path that might, in decades to come, threaten our stability.

But I am more than a diagnostic tool. I am the memory of sound itself.

In my chamber, certain depressions in the walls hold echoes from long ago—not through any magic, but through the precise way they're carved to create standing waves, acoustic shadows that preserve fragments of the past. The loudest sounds leave the longest impressions: a scream of grief from three generations past still whispers in one alcove, the laughter from a wedding feast twenty years ago still bubbles faintly from another. I tend these acoustic fossils, knowing where each one lives, visiting them like old friends.

The merchants know to come to me when disputes arise. "Three days past," one might say, "Kordel claimed I promised him forty lengths of rope for his price, but I said thirty." I close my eyes (a habit that persists though unnecessary), touch the walls, feel my way through the captured sounds until I find it—there, trapped in the depression that corresponds to Kordel's stall, the clear echo of "thirty lengths, no more."

Children fear me at first—the strange blind woman who knows when they've stolen sweetcakes or played too roughly near the oil lamps. But the brave ones discover I'm also the keeper of stories. I can reproduce, with perfect fidelity, the sound of their great-great-grandmother singing lullabies, captured in the stone when she was young. I can teach them the old water-working songs that help them feel the rhythm of proper channel maintenance. I can show them how their own voices fit into the great chorus of our people.

My apprentice is twelve now—a girl with sharp ears and restless hands who found me one day by following an unusual echo to its source. She still has her sight, still resists when I suggest she begin the eye-binding exercises. "Time enough," I tell her, though I know she must choose soon. The work of deep listening cannot be done with divided attention.

At night (though night and day blur without sight, marked only by the changing patterns of activity above), I sometimes climb to my sleeping alcove and remove my bells. In the relative silence, I listen to my own heartbeat, my own breath, the sound of my blood moving through my body. These too are part of the great pattern, the endless rhythm of water and stone and life.

Sometimes I dream I can hear the world above—wind, which I remember from childhood as a wild, uncontrolled sound nothing like our regulated water-song. Birds, which seem impossible now, creatures that make music while flying through empty air. The sun itself, which I imagine must have a sound, even if no human ear could perceive it.

But I always wake content to my stone world, to my work of listening, preserving, remembering. I am the keeper of our acoustic history, the guardian of our sonic health, the one who ensures that the voice of the marketplace—that complex, ever-shifting harmony of life—continues unbroken.

Today, like every day, I sit in my chamber and listen. A new merchant has arrived, her footsteps uncertain on our worn patterns. A child laughs in the upper passages—young Mekel, I think, whose giggle has a distinctive upturn at the end. Water finds a new path down the southeastern wall; I make a mental note to have it checked.

And below it all, supporting it all, the endless whisper of stone remembering water, water shaping stone, and the small, determined voices of the Stone People making their lives between them.

I add my own breath to the pattern, and listen, and remember.
