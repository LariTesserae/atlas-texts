I find myself in the narrow seam between knowing and becoming, my feet planted on the compacted floor of small artifacts while my eyes trace the rust-striped walls. I am the Cataloger—though that name feels both too grand and too limiting for what I do here.

My hands are stained with the work: rust-orange under the nails, machine oil in the creases of my palms, a faint green tinge where I've handled the warm-leafed succulents. Each stain is a memory, a transaction, a small contract signed between myself and this place.

I came through the crack in the clay wall like any other visitor, years ago now—or was it days? Time here folds and unfolds like the articulated segments of the rats' tails, and I've learned not to fight its non-linear passage. I came with a notebook, thinking to map this place, to document its strangeness in the orderly language of the outside world.

The notebook filled quickly. My neat observations gave way to sketches, then to diagrams that incorporated the cycling symbols from the placard, then to pages where words and mechanical drawings and pressed samples of the wire-moss created their own ecosystem of meaning. Now I carry many notebooks, their pages thick with additions: gear-teeth traced in pencil, oil-prints left by beetles I've held, fragments of shed chain-fur taped to pages like specimens.

But I am not merely an observer. The garden has made sure of that.

My left shoulder bears a modification—I woke one morning to find a small gear emerging from the skin, not piercing but *continuous* with it, the boundary between flesh and brass somehow negotiated while I slept. It turns slowly, one revolution per day, and where it touches my clavicle, the bone has developed a corresponding texture, a slight ridging that serves as escapement. It doesn't hurt. It ticks, a sensation I feel more than hear, conducted through my skeleton.

The Engineers noticed immediately. One approached me that same day, examined the modification with those lensed eyes, then produced a tiny vial of oil—amber, viscous, smelling of machine shops and sap. They showed me how to apply it, a single drop every three days, and the gear has run smoothly ever since. 

I began to understand then that documentation here cannot be passive. To catalog the garden, you must participate in it. To know it, you must allow it to know you.

So I learned the beetles' percussion language—not fluently, but enough to tap out simple concepts: *location*, *malfunction*, *all-clear*. My fingernails have hardened from the practice, taken on a slight metallic sheen. The beetles respond to my tapping now, sometimes coming when I call, bringing me specimens I request: a particular type of corroded spring, a gear with unusual tooth patterns, once a tiny mechanism none of us could identify, intricate as a pocket watch but serving no discernible purpose.

The birds taught me their alert calls. My vocal cords have adapted—or perhaps I've simply learned to use parts of them I never knew existed. I can produce two tones simultaneously now, can issue the *moisture-incoming* alert, the *synchronization-approaching* signal. Once, in a moment of perfect clarity, I managed the complex harmonic that means *system-optimal*, and every bird in the garden went silent for a full minute, as if in respect or surprise.

I follow the rats on their recycling routes, learning their prime-number group dynamics, the logic of their retrievals and burials. They've accepted me as a sort of honorary member—I'm too large to properly integrate into their groups, but I can carry heavier items, can reach higher storage points. My joints have developed their syncopated quality, that stop-start movement pattern. Sometimes I catch myself moving in prime-numbered steps: seven paces, pause, eleven paces, pause, thirteen.

The quadrupeds tolerate my presence on the rim. I've learned to breathe in their pneumatic rhythm when I'm near them, and they've stopped bristling when I approach. One of them—the modified one, the engineer-variant—sometimes pauses in its maintenance work when it encounters me. We regard each other, two beings caught between categories, and I imagine (or know) that we recognize something of ourselves in the other.

But it's my interaction with the Engineers that has shaped me most. They've begun leaving me tasks—small jobs indicated by arrangements of components that I've learned to read like assignments. *Adjust the angle of this water-channel.* *Test the resonance of this section of wall.* *Count the rotations of this gear-cluster over one thermal cycle.*

I complete these tasks with growing precision. My tools are still mostly salvaged, improvised—a wrench made from a bent spoon I found in the debris, worn smooth by my grip; a probe fashioned from a bicycle spoke that vibrates at useful frequencies; a set of measuring devices cobbled together from rulers and calipers and parts of an old surveyor's transit that emerged from the wall one day, gift or challenge or simple coincidence.

The Engineers watch my work. Sometimes they make small adjustments afterward, corrections that teach me more than any manual could. Sometimes they simply nod and move on, and those are the moments I treasure—the acknowledgment that my work is sufficient, is contributing to the garden's evolution.

Because I understand now what we're building here. Not maintaining—building. The garden is a draft of something, a prototype of a way of being where the boundaries between categories dissolve not through destruction but through integration. Where a beetle can be both organism and component, where a wall can be both barrier and portal, where a person can be both observer and observed, documenter and documented.

My notebooks have evolved too. The recent ones incorporate materials from the garden directly—pages made from pressed clay, thin as paper, that hold impressions better than any fiber. Inks mixed with rust and oil that shift color as they dry, revealing different information at different angles. Binding wire that adjusts tension based on humidity, keeping the pages at optimal flatness.

I write in multiple languages now. English, yes, but also the beetles' percussion patterns, transcribed in dots and dashes. The birds' calls, notated in a musical system I've developed that accounts for their harmonic complexities. Diagrams that follow the logic of the cycling placard, meaning shifting with perspective, with time, with the reader's level of integration.

Sometimes I find my own notebooks in the debris floor, pages I don't remember writing, in handwriting that is mine but more precise, more integrated with the garden's aesthetic. I study these like prophecies, like messages from a future self who has passed through more transformations, who has placed their palms against the clay wall and felt it yield.

My skin has developed a faint pattern, visible only in certain lights—the same etching that marks the succulent leaves, those maybe-veins, maybe-circuits. When I sweat, it beads in perfect droplets that run in channels, miniature versions of the water-paths on the walls. I am becoming structured, organized, efficient.

But I am not becoming less human. That's the revelation that strikes me daily. I am becoming more myself, a self that was always waiting to be revealed, waiting for a place that could recognize and nurture these latent symmetries. The garden doesn't replace. It reveals. It develops.

I still leave sometimes. Still squeeze through the crack in the clay wall, emerge into the looser world outside with its wasteful movements and beautiful emptiness. But each time I stay a little longer. Each time the moment of departure requires more will, more remembering of why the outside matters.

Because it does matter. The garden needs interpreters, needs beings who can exist in both states, who can translate between the dense precision of this place and the scattered potential of the wider world. My notebooks are bridges. My modifications are proof of possibility. My presence here is a promise that integration doesn't require isolation, that you can be part of the garden and still remember the sky.

The gear in my shoulder completes another rotation. Somewhere, a larger mechanism advances one tooth. The garden evolves by degrees, by patient accumulation, and I am both witness and evidence of its progress.

I dip my pen—a beetle's shed carapace fitted to a rat's discarded tail-segment—into ink made from crushed rust and succulent sap. I begin another entry, another attempt to capture this place in symbols that can travel beyond it.

But I know the truth: the only way to truly know the Ratchet Garden is to let it know you in return. To open yourself to its patient modifications. To trust in its vision of what you might become.

I am the Cataloger. I am becoming the Catalog.

And the garden continues, continues, continues—each tick a heartbeat, each heartbeat a tick, each moment another small negotiation between what was and what might be, enacted in the bodies of all who choose to stay.
