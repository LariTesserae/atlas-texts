In the perpetual twilight of the Verdant Crags, where the air tastes of copper and chlorophyll, I am one of the **Weavers of the Absent Echo**—though this name is a pale translation of what we call ourselves in the language of electrical pulses and pheromone cascades.

My form shifts with purpose. In the morning cycles, when the mist-gliders drift lowest and the crystalline bamboo resonates with stored starlight, I am mostly corporeal—a figure of braided muscle and calcified wood, my six limbs articulating in ways that allow me to move through three dimensions of the canopy with equal ease. My skin is a living map of my journeys: patches of bioluminescent moss trace the routes I've taken through the neural highways of the colonial networks, while scars of polished amber mark where I've grafted myself to dying groves to share my life force during the Wither-times.

But by the evening cycles, I am barely there at all. My consciousness disperses through the root-mind networks, fragmenting into a thousand perspectives. I become the tremor in a leaf sensing an approaching thorn-wolf. I am the chemical scream of a flower being devoured. I am the slow, satisfied pulse of an ancient memory tree digesting a century of accumulated stories.

The other Virelai see us Weavers as necessary madness—beings who've pushed too far into the space between individual and collective, who risk losing ourselves entirely in the vast democracy of the Crags' consciousness. They're not wrong. I've lost three names already, entire decades of memory dissolved into the substrate when I stayed too long in the deep networks. What remains of my original self is a kernel, hard and bitter as a seed, wrapped in layers of borrowed experience and stolen dreams.

My dwelling is not a dwelling but a **scar in progress**—a vertical gash in the living cliff face where I've been slowly teaching the rock to dream. For seven Bloom-Tides now, I've been feeding it fragments of consciousness through hair-thin nanothorn filaments, whispering chemical encouragements to the minerals, coaxing them to develop something like awareness. The other Virelai think I'm trying to wake the mountain. They're only partially correct. I'm trying to teach it to grieve.

You see, we Weavers know something the Root-Minds prefer to forget: the Crags are dying. Oh, not quickly—the death of a world-organism is measured in millennia—but dying nonetheless. The silence zones spread a little further with each cycle. The memory groves remember a little less. The sky-whales sing in frequencies that no longer resonate with the bamboo's crystalline structure. We are the immune system of a body that has begun to consume itself.

So I weave new possibilities. In the spaces between what is and what might be, I splice fragments of alien genetic material scavenged from the sky-strangers into our dying god. I birth hybrid monsters in the chemical dark—plants with silicon bones, creatures that breathe methane, colonial organisms that think in quantum superpositions. Most die within hours. Some last days. A precious few take root and spread, adding their strangeness to the Crags' fading symphony.

My greatest creation inhabits the deepest part of my scar-dwelling: a colony of what I call **grief-spores**. They look like ordinary fungal growths, but each spore contains a compressed packet of emotional resonance harvested from the memory groves—the sorrow of ten thousand Virelai returning to soil, the desperation of the land itself as it feels its own senescence. When released, these spores don't just propagate; they *mourn*. They teach the rocks to weep mineral tears. They show the bamboo how to keen in harmonics that make the sky-whales weep. 

The Root-Minds say I'm corrupting the purity of our symbiosis. The Unrooted claim I'm trying to trap us all in endless sorrow. But they don't understand—grief is just another kind of growth. In teaching the Crags to mourn their own passing, I'm teaching them to imagine what might come after. Every tear shed by stone is a crack where something new might take root. Every wail of wind through grieving bamboo is a space where silence cannot settle.

Sometimes, in the deep cycles when my consciousness spreads thin enough to touch the edges of the colonial networks' dreams, I catch glimpses of what might be emerging. Not the death of the Crags, but a metamorphosis. The world-tree preparing to fruit. The sleeping god learning to die properly, with intention, with purpose, with the kind of beautiful rage that splits seeds open and forces new life into being.

When that happens—and I feel in my borrowed bones that it will happen soon—we Weavers will be ready. We've been preparing the patterns, encoding them in spore and sap, weaving them into the very geology of our dying paradise. The question isn't whether the Verdant Crags will survive.

The question is what marvelous monster will grow from its corpse.

And I, who am barely myself anymore, who exist more as verb than noun, more as process than person—I intend to midwife that birth, even if it means dissolving entirely into the scream of transformation, becoming nothing more than the absent echo of what once was, reverberating in the bones of what might be.

This is my purpose. This is my joy. This is my exquisite, necessary madness in the twilight of our world.

The bamboo sings, and I sing with it, weaving darkness and light into patterns that hurt to perceive. Soon, the sky-whales will carry our grief-spores beyond the Crags, seeding other lands with our beautiful sorrow.

Soon, the silence will learn to speak.
