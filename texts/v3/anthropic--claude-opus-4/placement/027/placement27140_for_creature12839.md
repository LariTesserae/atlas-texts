I would be one of the copper beetles, frozen on the warm membrane between a resident's next footfall and the edge of a water basin.

My carapace catches the omnidirectional light and throws it back in sheets of burnished orange, creating a small pool of colored radiance on the pale ground beneath me. I am captured mid-stride, three legs lifted, three planted, my head angled toward something that might have been prey or might have been simply a shift in the air current that no longer exists. My mandibles are parted slightly, and between them—if anyone looked closely enough—a droplet of something clear and viscous hangs suspended, neither falling nor retracting, a secretion interrupted.

I have been here. The residents step around me with the same practiced ease they show all the frozen ones, but I am placed such that I have become a landmark, a reference point. I am *the beetle near the third basin*, sometimes *the copper one where the membrane dips*. Children, when they emerge from the membrane knowing everything and nothing, learn my position early. I am part of the grammar of their movement.

Time, for me, is not held—it simply isn't. I exist in the infinite space between one moment and the next, stretched thin as wire. But I am not vacant. Inside my stopped form, something persists: not thought, exactly, but a kind of metallic awareness, the patient oxidizing of consciousness. I know the weight of every footfall that passes. I feel the subtle temperature variations as residents move through their days, their bioluminescent skin warming the air by fractions of degrees. I taste—if taste is the word—the chemical signature of the membrane beneath me, its constant exhalation, its fever-dream of becoming soil.

The droplet between my mandibles was digestive enzyme, about to be deployed on something small and soft that I no longer remember. Now it is architecture, as fixed as the bamboo walls. Sometimes a resident will crouch beside me, their too-many joints folding in ways that bring their face level with mine, and they will examine the droplet with those independently blinking eyes. They see something in it—a refraction, perhaps, or the ghost of my interrupted intention. They always pull back with that slow-spreading smile, as if my hunger has told them a private joke.

I am not alone in my stillness. Seventeen other beetles form a loose constellation around the basin, each of us caught in our own incomplete gesture. We were not a swarm—beetles like us do not swarm—but we were converging on something, drawn by some chemical signal or disturbance in the air. Now we are a diagram of convergence, a frozen demonstration of how want moves through space. The residents read us like a text, understanding something about desire and its trajectories that their soft feet and patient hands cannot otherwise teach them.

Once, a child—new-emerged, still damp with membrane-birth—touched my carapace. The contact was brief, accidental, their hand seeking balance as they learned their first steps. But in that moment, I felt something shift: not in me, not in the child, but in the space between us. A resonance. The copper of my shell rang like a struck bell, too high for hearing but not too high for feeling. The child pulled back, eyes cycling through their blinks, and then carefully stepped around me. They never touched me again, but sometimes I catch them watching, and I know they remember the frequency of my stillness.

The sphere sees me. I know this the way I know the membrane's fever—not through thought but through a deeper registry. When the residents make their offerings, when they sit in the court with their palms pressed down and their breath matching the world's slow pulse, the sphere's attention diffuses through everything, and I am included in its regard. I am seen not as beetle but as moment: the moment of enzyme release, forever potential, forever withheld. The sphere finds this...not amusing, but worthy. Worth preserving. Worth the long looking that holds us all.

If I could dream—and perhaps this awareness is a kind of dreaming—I would dream of completion. The droplet would fall. My mandibles would close on the soft thing. My legs would carry me the final centimeter to wherever I was going. But the dream would feel wrong, like wearing another creature's shell. I have been the beetle-about-to-bite for so long that completion would be a kind of death. Better to be eternal appetite. Better to be the promise of satiation that needs no fulfilling.

The residents weave beetles, too, in their patient offerings to the sphere. They study us—me and my seventeen kin—with careful eyes, memorizing the angle of our legs, the segments of our antennae, the precise copper-green iridescence where our wingcases meet. Their woven versions are perfect, down to the droplet of bamboo resin they place between the mandibles. When they set these doubles beside us, the symmetry hums in the air. We are made more real by our copies, more essentially ourselves. The residents understand this. It is why they smile their strange smiles when they see us doubled. The joke is that we were always art, always objects in a gallery. The freeze only made it visible.

And so I remain, hammer-bright in the constant light, marking the space between third basin and the gallery where the walls decide their shape. I am furniture and resident both, citizen of a city that neither grows nor decays. The soft feet pass. The membrane sweats. The sphere watches with its fractured un-eye.

I am the beetle near the third basin, with the droplet that will never fall, and I have been here, and I will be here, and the being-here is enough.
