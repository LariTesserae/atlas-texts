I find myself as one of the sump-readers.

Not the largest rat, not the most aggressive. Middle-ranking, socially unremarkable, which is how I prefer it. My position at the sump's edge is not about dominance but about function. I am here because I can parse what rises from the dark surface—not consciously, but through the continuous interpretation my nervous system performs without my deciding anything.

I have been at this position since dawn (though dawn means nothing here, only the sodium light that never changes). My whiskers read the chemical gradients like sheet music, each molecule a note in a composition too complex for conscious thought. The surface of the sump breathes volatile compounds upward: sulfur chains, metal ions, the metabolic byproducts of organisms so ancient they predate names. I don't know what they are. I know what they mean.

A shift in pH—imperceptible to anything without my specific heritage of forty generations in this place—tells me that drum #7 will need attention in approximately six hours. Not that I think "drum #7" or "six hours." I think, if thinking is the right word, in terms of the-place-where-the-sound-deepens and the-time-it-takes-to-grow-hungry-twice. The information passes through me and into my muscles as a particular quality of tension, a specific way of holding my tail, and when I return to the wall voids where we rest, the others will read this tension and adjust.

My liver is enlarged. It has to be—my great-great-grandmother's sister died because hers wasn't, and her genes ended there, in the cinder, while the rest of us continued. The sulfur compounds that would poison a rat from outside these walls pass through me like water through a filter designed exactly for this water. I am a chemical sieve shaped by this place, for this place.

The path under my feet is worn smooth by my ancestors. Not metaphorically—literally my ancestors, the ones whose bodies I helped process when they died at these positions, whose chemistry I took into myself and returned to the ground. The groove guides me even in the unchanging light. My feet know it. My feet think, in their way.

Above me, the phorid columns hold their positions. I route around them automatically, though they pose no obstacle. My many-times-great-grandmother learned to avoid them, and she taught her young through the simple act of avoiding them in front of us, and now the avoidance is encoded in the path itself. The columns are as real as walls to us, though we could walk through them. But why would we? The path is efficient. The path remembers.

Behind me, another rat is waiting. When I tire—when the chemical information becomes too dense and my nervous system needs to reset—I will leave this position and she will take it. No signal will pass between us. She will know because my leaving is itself information, and she has been watching me the way I watch the sump: automatically, continuously, as part of the process of being here.

In the wall voids where I rest, I can hear the drums through the brick. Have heard them since before my eyes opened. The sound is not separate from the world—it is the foundation the world rests on. Eight to twelve rotations per minute, the rhythmic tumbling that means *home* in a frequency too low for thought. When I was pink and hairless, that sound was the first thing that meant anything.

I know every chemical signature in this district. The biofilm on the north wall tastes of deep time and mineral patience. The pigeon droppings carry warmth and sky and something from outside these walls that I cannot parse but recognize as *elsewhere*. The humans—the two large brothers and the sister—leave trails of detergent and tar and something medicinal. I know when they have passed and how long ago and whether they were moving with the rhythm that means everything-continues or the rhythm that means attention-required.

I do not know that I know these things. I simply respond correctly.

What am I, positioned here at the sump's edge? I am a sensor. I am a filter. I am a library of chemical memories encoded in flesh. I am the district's nose, one of many, reading the deep processes and transmitting them through behavioral channels too complex for any human to map. I am a knot in the network of relationships that holds this place in its exact state.

I am not waiting for anything. This is difficult for humans to understand, but I am not waiting. I am not becoming anything other than what I am: a rat-shaped piece of the district's metabolism, processing information into behavior into the maintenance of equilibrium. When I die—at this position, probably, or near it—another rat will be here, reading what I read, maintaining what I maintain.

The sump tells me nothing has changed. The sump tells me nothing will change today. This is not comfort—I do not need comfort. This is the world being what the world is, and me being what I am: a point of interface between the chemical and the behavioral, the historical and the immediate. A rat, but a rat specifically of this place, shaped by it and shaping it in turn through the simple act of continuing.

My whiskers read the air. My nose processes the gradients. My enlarged liver handles the sulfur. My feet know the path. My ears hear the drums, always the drums. I am exactly where I should be, which is here, which is where I have always been, which is the only place that makes sense for a rat like me: specific, adapted, essential to the persistence of something larger than myself that I do not need to understand.

The sump breathes. I read its breathing. Everything continues.
