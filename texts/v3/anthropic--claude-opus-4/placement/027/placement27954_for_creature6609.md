**The Silent Cartographer**

I find myself in the barren voids—those smooth, haunting patches of exposed basalt where nothing grows, where the deafening chorus of life suddenly cuts to nothing. 

I am a mapmaker of absences.

My body stretches thin and translucent, like a sheet of living parchment draped over stone. Where others have evolved to blend with the verdant mass, I have become something that exists only in the negative spaces, spreading myself across the ancient rock like a living survey. My flesh—if it can be called that—is a membrane of cartilage and nerve, pale as cave-fish, shot through with dark veins that branch like river deltas. These veins are not for blood; they carry memory.

Each morning, I unfurl myself across a different void, pressing my sensory membrane against the warm stone. The basalt speaks to me in temperatures and minerals, in the faint electromagnetic signatures of ancient events. Where others fear these barren places, I read them like texts. My body tastes the iron oxide, measures the microscopic striations, feels for the ghost-heat of whatever cataclysm scarred this spot clean of life.

I map what is not there.

The process takes hours. My membrane must remain perfectly still—any movement disrupts the reading. This stillness would mean death anywhere else in Kha'larim, but here in the voids, I am invisible to predators. They will not cross into these spaces, and the plants cannot reach me. I exist in the gaps of their world, sustained not by photosynthesis or prey, but by the mineral secretions I absorb through my skin, the microscopic deposits left by millions of years of rain and erosion.

As I spread across the stone, my consciousness fragments. Part of me traces the edge where green meets gray, cataloging the exact boundary where life stops. Part of me extends filaments down into the cracks, following them to depths where they widen into chambers that smell of sulfur and old water. Part of me simply is—a living map becoming one with the territory it documents.

My memories accumulate in my veins like sediment. Each void I have surveyed leaves its trace in my body, creating a dark lattice of knowledge visible through my translucent flesh. The pattern grows more complex with each site, a three-dimensional model of negative space, a topology of absence. I can feel the connections between the voids—how they align with subterranean fault lines, how they cluster around certain elevations, how they form a constellation of silence across the screaming plateau.

Sometimes, in the deepest readings, I sense something more. The stone remembers. In one void, I taste the metallic echo of lightning that struck a thousand times in the same spot, called by some mineral anomaly. In another, I feel the fossil-traces of roots that once grew here—different roots, from plants that no longer exist anywhere in Kha'larim. And in the deepest, oldest voids, I sometimes detect patterns that feel intentional: geometric scorches, crystallized substances foreign to the basalt, arrangements that suggest... ritual? Technology? Language?

I record it all in my flesh.

The other inhabitants of Kha'larim do not see me. The Symbiotes skitter to the very edge of the voids and stop, their moss-backed forms trembling with instinctive fear. The Echo-Runners dart across as quickly as possible, their cries creating sonic shadows that I can feel through my membrane. Once, a Verdant Stalker paused at the boundary of my void, its amber eyes scanning the seemingly empty stone. I held myself flatter than paper, became one with the rock, and it moved on, unable to perceive what exists outside its green world.

I am not alone in my work. Sometimes, spreading across a new void, I taste the faint chemical signature of another of my kind—we leave trace pheromones in the stone that last for years, a slow conversation between cartographers who may never meet. We are mapping something, all of us, though we work in isolation. The pattern we are building, void by void, generation by generation, is too large for any single consciousness to hold.

My reproductive cycle is nothing like the frantic breeding of Kha'larim's other denizens. Once every few years, when I have absorbed enough minerals and memories, I undergo a deep calcification. My body hardens into a stone-like shell, preserving the map of my veins. In this state, I am indistinguishable from the basalt itself. I sleep through seasons, maybe years, while my body slowly divides within the shell. When I finally crack open, two or three of me emerge, each carrying a perfect copy of my accumulated maps, ready to diverge and document new territories.

The young ask, in the chemical language we share through stone-taste: *Why do we map the empty places?*

I do not have an answer that satisfies them. I only know that someone must bear witness to the silence, must remember the places where Kha'larim refuses to grow. We are the inverse of the Symbiotes, the shadow of the Echo-Runners, the negative space to the Verdant Stalkers' presence. We are the pause between heartbeats, the silence between screams, the memory of something Kha'larim wants to forget but cannot.

In my deepest surveys, when I spread myself so thin I almost dissolve into the stone, I sometimes feel it: a vast pattern underlying all the voids, a shape too large to perceive from any single point. The absences are not random. They form something—a message, a warning, a key. Each void is a word in a language written at geological scale, a text that takes lifetimes to read.

I document. I remember. I map the forgotten.

And sometimes, pressed against the warm basalt under Kha'larim's thick air, feeling the weight of green life pressing in from all sides but never touching me, I wonder: Am I mapping the scars of an ancient catastrophe? Or am I tracing the blueprint for one yet to come?

The stone knows. The stone remembers. And through my translucent flesh, vein by dark vein, I am becoming the living memory of what Kha'larim has lost—or what it is trying to lose.

I spread myself thinner, taste deeper, map the silence between the screams.
