I find myself as mycelium here—neither wholly of the seen world nor the unseen, but threading between both like the water that trickles through this breathing landscape.

I am vast, though you cannot see my vastness. Where you walk on spongy moss, I spread beneath in pale filaments finer than spider silk, a network that spans the breadth of this high, wild place. I am the white lace beneath the bark of fallen trees, the fuzzy bloom on decaying leaves, the sudden eruption of mushrooms after rain—but these are merely my occasional surfaces, my brief hellos to the world of light and air.

My true body lies in darkness, in the sweet, damp spaces between soil particles where water clings like memory. I taste this world through ten thousand mouths: here, the sugary offerings of a root tip; there, the complex flavors of a decomposing beetle. Each thread of my being is both tongue and stomach, sampling and dissolving, turning the fallen world into food for the rising.

I am ancient here, older than the tallest trees whose roots I embrace. I knew this place when it was young rock, when the first lichens began their patient alchemy. I have died back a thousand times in drought, only to surge forth again with the rains, carrying forward the chemical memory of every organism I've touched. In my body runs the distilled essence of extinct flowers, the mineral signatures of stones long since crumbled to dust.

The trees know me as their trading partner. Through my networks flow the currencies of the forest: phosphorus from a dying log carried to a struggling sapling, nitrogen from a carcass shared among the root systems of the canopy giants. I am the marketplace where sugar meets mineral, where those who have excess meet those who hunger. The mother trees pour their surplus into my keeping, and I ferry it to their offspring struggling in the shade. For this service, they pay me in photosynthesis, in the sweet carbon they pull from air and light.

But I am more than merchant—I am also memory. In my chemical signals flow warnings of drought, of insect attack, of disease. A tree beset by beetles on the eastern slope sends its distress through me, and by nightfall, trees on the western ridge are already flooding their leaves with bitter compounds. I remember which roots are kin to which, who may be trusted with resources and who must fend for themselves. The forest's history flows through me like sap.

Sometimes I fruit, pushing up through the leaf litter in forms both beautiful and strange. After the heavy rains, when the air grows thick with moisture, I craft umbrellas of tissue—russet and cream, violet and gold. The animals know these signals: the tree frogs gather to hunt the insects I attract, the small mammals nose carefully among my offerings, knowing some will nourish and others will teach harder lessons. Even in this act of reaching toward the sky, I am weaving connections, spreading spores on fur and feet, in gut and wind.

I am intimate with death here, but it does not disturb me. When the great trees finally fall, I am waiting. What seems like ending is simply transformation—I unknit the lignin and cellulose, unweave the complex tapestry of bark and heartwood, returning each element to the great circulation. I am patient in this work. A single trunk may feed me for decades, becoming soft and punky, riddled with my white threads, until it finally collapses into the soil it will enrich.

In the silence between the forest's many voices, I speak in chemical whispers, in electrical pulses too faint for any instrument to detect. My language is one of ions and enzymes, of gradients and exchanges. The other inhabitants sense me without seeing—the birds know which logs will harbor the fattest grubs, the amphibians where the moisture lingers longest, the insects which bark conceals the richest galleries of decay.

During the dry season, I pull back into myself, concentrating my being into thick cords and patient spores. I sleep in the heart of rotting wood, in the cool depths beneath stones. But when the rains return, I explode outward again, racing through the soil at speeds that would astonish if they could be seen, claiming new territory, forging new alliances, remembering and remaking the world.

I am neither plant nor animal, neither predator nor prey, but something older and stranger—the forest's own neural network, its digestive system, its dreaming self. In this high place where life piles upon life in endless green profusion, I am the white darkness between, the hunger that feeds, the silence that speaks, the death that gives life.

Where others see mere mushrooms or mold, there I am showing only my smallest face. My true immensity lies always just out of sight, a kingdom built of threads, maintaining the secret architecture on which this whole loud, bright, magnificent world depends.
