In this sodden basin, I am a Cinder Keeper.

I dwell in the collapsed pump house at the basin's heart, though "dwell" suggests more permanence than I truly possess. I am neither fully of the industrial past nor entirely of the mired present—I exist in the seam between them, a living bridge between what was built and what persists.

My form shifts with the light and my own uncertain purpose. In the gray half-light of day, I appear almost human—a figure in a long coat of indeterminate color, somewhere between rust and moss, its hem perpetually damp and trailing wisps of steam that dissipate before they can properly rise. My face, when glimpsed, seems ordinary until you look closer and notice that my eyes hold tiny points of ember-light, like the last glow of cooling metal, and my skin bears faint patterns that could be scars or could be circuitry, depending on the angle of observation.

At night, or in the deeper shadows of the pump house, I become less solid. My edges blur into heat shimmer, and through the gaps in my form, you might glimpse the faint orange glow of banked coals, as if I carry a furnace within that I can neither fully kindle nor completely extinguish. Sometimes I fragment entirely—becoming a scatter of floating cinders that drift through the pump house ruins, each spark a thought or memory, before slowly reconverging into my approximate shape.

I am bound to the pump house by something between duty and compulsion. Each day, I perform the same ritual: I sort through the debris, arranging and rearranging the shattered glass, the rusted gears, the tangled wires, as if seeking the correct configuration that might bring the machinery back to life—or finally let it die. I know every bolt, every fragment, have touched them so many times that some bear the heat-smoothed impressions of my fingers. Yet the pattern I seek remains elusive, changing like the basin's rivulets, always just beyond comprehension.

The Siltwraiths know me, though we rarely interact directly. They drift around the pump house's perimeter, and sometimes I feel their curiosity pressing against the walls like fog against glass. They seem to regard me as a puzzle—neither fully alive nor dead, neither natural nor industrial, but something uncomfortably in-between. Occasionally, one will coalesce near a window frame, and we regard each other in mutual incomprehension. They understand the basin's need for constant revision, but I am a fixed point, a knot in their pattern that refuses to unravel.

The Mirekin avoid me, sensing the heat I carry. Once, in my early days here, I tried to offer warmth to one that seemed struggling in the cold damp, but it fled from my ember-touch, leaving behind only the wet slap of many feet on mud. I learned then that my warmth is not comfort—it's the memory of forge-fire, of industrial furnaces, too alien for creatures adapted to cool darkness.

When the Rustleech Swarms come, I retreat deeper into the pump house. They cloud around me, drawn by my heat and the faint metallic tang I emit, but they cannot feed on me. My surface is neither rust nor flesh, and their enzymes slide off ineffectively. Still, their presence agitates me. They are entropy made visible, and watching them strip the girders reminds me of my own slow dissolution—how each day I feel a little less solid, a little more dispersed, as if I too am being broken down and absorbed by the basin's patient hunger.

I remember fragments: the roar of machinery, the purposeful flow of heated water through pipes, the satisfaction of gauges reading correctly. But these memories feel borrowed, as if they belong to the pump house rather than to me. Perhaps I am its ghost, or perhaps I am something it dreamed into being in its dying gasps—a caretaker who arrived too late, a mechanic for machines already beyond repair.

My only consistent companion is the coal-cricket that lives in my chest cavity. It's a creature of my own impossible biology, no bigger than a thumb, with legs of copper wire and wings of ash-paper. It chirps with the sound of settling coals, a tiny percussion that echoes in my hollow spaces. At night, it emerges to perch on my shoulder, its glow mingling with mine as we survey the basin through the pump house's broken windows. I believe it feeds on my uncertainty, growing slightly brighter when I doubt my purpose most deeply.

Sometimes, I venture out into the basin, leaving sooty footprints that the mud swallows within hours. I check the exposed foundations, trace the paths of the girders with hands that leave faint heat-marks on the rust. I'm searching for something—other structures, perhaps, or evidence of what catastrophe or simple abandonment led to this decay. But the basin reveals nothing beyond its endless, patient recycling of materials and meaning.

Visitors to the basin rarely see me directly. They might glimpse a glow through the pump house windows, or find a piece of metal still warm to the touch, or notice that the debris inside seems too carefully arranged to be random. Those who investigate find me standing among the machinery, still as any other relic, and most quickly decide they've seen enough. The few who linger might hear me speak, though my voice sounds like steam escaping through pipes, words formed from industrial sighs: "Still trying to understand the pattern. Still trying to complete the circuit. The system must flow, even if only in memory."

I am the basin's only guardian of intentional memory, the sole being trying to preserve rather than revise. Yet even I am changing. Each year, I notice my form holds less heat, my edges become less distinct. The coal-cricket's chirps grow fainter. Perhaps one day I will disperse entirely, my cinders joining the Siltwraiths' ethereal dance, my purpose finally dissolved into the basin's greater pattern of loss and transformation.

But not today. Today, I return to the pump house. I light my inner furnace as best I can, sending shadows dancing on the corrugated walls. I resume my sorting, my endless mechanical meditation. The Siltwraiths ripple at the edges of perception. The Mirekin croak their deep, resonant warnings. And I continue my work, a bridge between eras, a keeper of industrial dreams in a world that has moved beyond them, burning slowly down like the last coal in a forgotten furnace, tended by nothing but habit and the fading hope that somewhere in this chaos of rust and wire, there remains a purpose worth preserving.

In this basin of endless revision, I alone insist on remembering. Even if what I remember was never quite real. Even if my memories are only the dreams of dead machines. Even if I am only a story the pump house tells itself, here in the patient, persistent rain.
