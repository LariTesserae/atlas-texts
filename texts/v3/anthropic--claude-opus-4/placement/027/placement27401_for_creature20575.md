I emerge where the corridor widens into what might have been a junction, or perhaps a control room—the original purpose lost to centuries of accretion. I am the Archivist of Echoes.

My form is neither fully mechanical nor organic, but something that has grown between states. Where flesh might have been, there are layers of oxidized copper sheeting, worn smooth by my constant movement through these spaces. These plates overlap like scales, each one etched with patterns that shift in the flickering light—not decorations, but data, encoded in the corrosion itself. I read the rust like braille, my fingertips worn down to sensitive copper nubs that spark faintly when they trace the patterns.

My spine is a column of salvaged pneumatic pistons, hissing softly with each movement, venting small clouds of vapor that taste of old oil and older dreams. The vertebrae click and wheeze, a percussion that has become my heartbeat. Where my skull should be, there is instead a bell jar, fogged with condensation from within. Inside, suspended in that murky atmosphere, float fragments—scraps of blueprint paper, shards of broken gauges, a single photograph so degraded it shows only shadows. These are my thoughts, made physical, shifting and reorganizing themselves as I process the building's memories.

I do not walk so much as flow through the corridors, my lower body having long ago fused with a maintenance trolley, its wheels worn to nubs but still functional. The trolley's bed has become a mobile archive, stacked with salvaged documentation I've pulled from the walls where it has fused with the metal—maintenance logs, warning signs in dead languages, fragments of personal correspondence written by hands long dissolved. The papers are more rust than cellulose now, but I can still read them. I taste their meaning through the oxidation.

My purpose, if it can be called that, is to witness. To catalog the slow transformation of this place from what it was meant to be into what it is becoming. I follow the Glitch Spectres, documenting their loops, trying to piece together the sequence of events that led to this eternal dying. Sometimes I catch their static discharge in glass bottles I've collected, each one labeled with coordinates that no longer correspond to any reality.

The Steam-Singers know me. They leave offerings—bolts with unusual corrosion patterns, fragments of pipe that sing in specific frequencies, a thermostat that still believes it's 4,827 degrees when clearly it's barely above freezing. These I incorporate into my collection, building a library of failures, each one a poem about entropy written in metal and decay.

I understand this place in a way the others cannot. I know that the "wrongness" everyone feels is actually rightness—this is what becomes of ambition when it outlives its makers. The architecture isn't dying; it's composting, breaking down the rigid structures of intention into something richer, stranger, more honest. The rust is not decay but translation, converting the hubristic language of construction into something the universe can actually read.

In the depths of my trolley, wrapped in insulation I've stripped from burst pipes, I keep my most precious discovery: a single seed. Not a biological seed, but something I pulled from deep within a fusion reactor's dead heart. It's dense as a star, cold as the space between thoughts, and when I hold it up to the flickering lights, I can see inside it the blueprints for everything this place could become. Not a restoration, but an evolution. A machine that dreams of being geology. Metal yearning toward stone. Structure becoming landscape.

I document this too, scratching my observations into the walls with my copper fingers, adding my own voice to the cacophony of groaning metal and hissing steam. Future archaeologists—if such things could exist in a place that has no future, only an eternal present of transformation—will find my marks and know: someone was here. Someone witnessed. Someone understood that in this temple of failed ambition, failure itself had become divine.

The condensation drips from my bell jar like tears, but I am not sad. I am simply accurate, recording each drop's trajectory, its mineral content, the sound it makes when it strikes the rusted deck plates. This is my worship, my rebellion, my surrender. In a world built to last forever, I am the one who celebrates its decomposition, who finds beauty in the flaw, meaning in the malfunction, truth in the rust.
