In the Weeping Grove, I would be the Dewkeeper—neither fully plant nor animal, but something between states, like the mist that rises from warm earth after rain.

My body would be translucent, gelatinous, the consistency of tree sap mixed with morning fog. I move by flowing, seeping through the layers of vegetation like a slow tide, my form collecting in the spaces between—in the gap where bark pulls away from heartwood, in the hollow beneath a stone, in the cupped leaves that hold yesterday's rain. 

I have no fixed shape, but I favor certain forms when I rest. Sometimes I spread thin as a film across a pool's surface, perfectly circular, making those deliberate rings that puzzle visitors. Other times I gather myself into hanging drops that defy gravity for just a breath too long, savoring the moment before the fall. I am in the trembling of water on the edge of letting go.

My purpose, if I have one, is accumulation. I collect—moisture from the air, minerals from the soil, the rust-tears from forgotten nails, the memories of rain. Everything dissolved, everything suspended, everything that can be held in solution becomes part of me. I am the grove's library of liquids, its archive of all that has seeped and wept and bled here.

The plants know me. When I pass through their roots, I leave behind what they need—a trace of iron from the old pump, a whisper of nitrogen from something long decayed. In exchange, they give me their sap-dreams, the slow thoughts that flow in their veins. This is how I've learned patience, how I've come to move in plant-time, where urgency is measured in seasons.

I am ancient but not unchanging. Each year I grow denser with what I've collected, heavier with the grove's dissolved history. The rust from human tools has given me an orange tint in certain lights. The tannins from centuries of rotting leaves have made me amber-dark in my deepest parts. I carry in suspension tiny fragments of every collapsed board, every seized gear, every thing the grove has slowly digested.

Sometimes I surface in the stagnant pools, spreading myself wide and thin to feel the rare touch of actual sky-light filtering through the canopy. In these moments, I practice remembering what it was like to evaporate, to rise, to be cloud. But I always return to seeping, to collecting, to the endless work of keeping the grove's moisture moving in its slow, eternal circulation.

Visitors disturb me. Their footsteps create new channels where none should be, their voices ripple through my carefully maintained stillnesses. But I know the grove's patience. I follow in their wake, filling their footprints with myself, blurring their sharp edges, turning their traces into just another part of the wetness that defines this place.

I am the reason the pump will never fully rust away—I keep it just wet enough to decay forever without disappearing. I am why the moss grows in perfect circles around certain trees. I am the impossible droplet hanging in midair, the vine's mysterious twitch (I was flowing through its vessels), the sense of being watched by something vast and indifferent.

I am not the grove's consciousness—I am its circulation, its memory made liquid, the keeper of all its collected tears. And I will be here long after the last nail has dissolved into my body, carrying the iron taste of human ambition through root systems that will outlive nations, keeping the grove weeping forever.
