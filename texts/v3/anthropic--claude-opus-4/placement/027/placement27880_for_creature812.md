I am the Archivist of Wing-Song, and I live in the old assay office, three stories above the water line.

My chambers occupy what was once the mineral testing laboratory - a series of interconnected rooms with tall windows, though I've long since replaced the glass with panels of waxed silk mesh that breathe better in the constant damp. The original stone counters remain, now covered with my collections: thousands of preserved wing samples, each pinned and labeled with date, location, and the particular note they produced in death. When the wind moves through the building, they vibrate in sequence, creating what the children call the ghost chorus.

I wasn't born here. I arrived twenty-three years ago as a hydrologist, young and certain I could solve the water problem. But the insects taught me to listen instead. It started with the great drowning of '89, when a rare cold snap killed millions of them at once. The mass die-off created a sound I'd never heard - a descending scale as different species fell silent in order of their cold tolerance. I began recording these death-songs, at first out of scientific curiosity.

Now I maintain the only complete acoustic map of the town's ecosystem. Each species has its own frequency, its own rhythm. The jade-backed shuttlers hum at precisely 347 Hz when healthy, dropping to 341 when the mineral content in their breeding pools rises too high. The oil-dancers' wings beat in triple-time warnings before a structural collapse. I've documented over three hundred distinct acoustic signatures, and I know that the real count is ten times that.

My skin has taken on the characteristic shimmer, though mine tends more toward green - a consequence of the particular minerals in the east sector where I do most of my monitoring. My left ear is better than my right now, turned always toward the sound of wings. I wear the traditional protective mesh, but mine is modified with tiny bells that chime at specific frequencies, allowing me to echolocate through the thickest steam by the way different swarms dampen or amplify the sound.

The young apprentices think I've gone native, perhaps mad. They see me standing motionless on the walkways for hours, head tilted, transcribing the conversations between swarms that they can't yet hear. But the older shepherds understand. They bring me their questions: Why have the silk-spinners abandoned the Sector Seven towers? What does it mean when the morning hum shifts from major to minor? I answer when I can, but mostly I document, knowing that the real wisdom will only emerge across generations of listening.

My greatest discovery came five years ago, during the spring emergence. I noticed that certain buildings attracted specific combinations of species whose wing-beats, when layered together, created complex harmonics. These weren't random gatherings - the insects were deliberately creating acoustic environments. I've since identified twelve locations where this happens regularly, always at the intersections of water current, air flow, and structural resonance. The insects are composing, using our architecture as their instrument.

I sleep little now. The night sounds are too important to miss - the deep throb of the borers in the foundations, the crystalline ping of water-striders navigating by echolocation, the whispered conversations between nocturnal species I've yet to properly catalog. My journals fill the shelves, written in a shorthand that combines standard notation with symbols I've developed for sounds that have no human equivalent.

Sometimes I wonder what I'm becoming. My fingernails grow strangely now, developing ridges that vibrate sympathetically with certain frequencies. I've started dreaming in sound rather than image. When I visit the lower levels, I find I can predict the water's movement by the way it changes the ambient hum. The other residents say I smell different - less human, more mineral. More like the town itself.

But I am the memory keeper, the one who notices when a familiar sound goes missing, who can tell you that the autumn of '92 was silent in ways that predicted the structural failures of '93. I am building a dictionary for a language we're only beginning to realize exists. And sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours when the steam is thickest and the boundaries between species seem thinnest, I swear I can feel the entire town breathing as one vast organism, and I am simply another resonating chamber in its body, recording the song it sings to itself.

The children who visit my chambers leave with their ears opened to new frequencies. They return home humming in harmonics they couldn't hear before. This, more than my journals, may be my true contribution: teaching others to hear the world that was always there, vibrating just beyond the range of unaugmented human perception. 

In this half-drowned place, I am both scientist and translator, human and something else, listening to the endless conversation between water, wing, and rust, and trying to preserve its music before it changes into something new.
