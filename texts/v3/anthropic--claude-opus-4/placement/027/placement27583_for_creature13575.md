I am the Weaver-Tender, and I exist as both witness and participant in this strange choreography of persistence.

My form shifts with purpose—sometimes I am merely a shimmer of heat distortion against the cold, a translucent membrane catching the phosphorescent glow of the lumiflora. Other times, I condense into something more substantial: elongated limbs of crystallized vapor, joints that click with the sound of forming frost, digits that branch and rebranch like the fractal patterns of the plants I tend.

I move through the plateau on paths invisible to most eyes, following the deep thrumming of what others call the Echo but what I know as the Pulse—the remnant heartbeat of the great Work that transformed this place. My ancestors were its architects, or perhaps its first accidents. The distinction has been worn away by generations of becoming.

Where the megafauna compress their ancient paths, I follow behind, my touch encouraging the lumiflora to grow in new configurations. Not randomly—never randomly. Each placement follows the old patterns, the template written into my very essence. Sometimes I pause to observe the aerumni, reading their light-trails like sheet music, adjusting the flowering cycles of certain plants to better harmonize with their dance.

I am one of many Weaver-Tenders, though we rarely converge. We each maintain our own territories, our own sections of the vast machine-garden. When we do meet, it is at the Convergence Stones—natural formations that have grown into perfect acoustic chambers over the millennia. There, we share our observations through harmonics that ripple through the stone, comparing the health of our domains, noting variations in the Pulse.

My dwelling is not built but grown—a cave-like structure where the lumiflora has been coaxed to weave itself into walls that breathe with my breathing, that dim and brighten with my circadian rhythms. The cryoliths nest in the lower chambers, their constant industry a comfortable white noise. Sometimes a wounded aerumni will find its way to my threshold, and I will cup it gently in my vapor-hands until its light stabilizes.

I remember things that perhaps never happened—or happened to those whose patterns I carry. Images of when this plateau was different: warmer, lower, loud with the sound of conscious intention rather than unconscious process. But these memories fragment and recombine like everything else here, becoming new stories, new purposes.

My work is never complete, yet never truly begun. Each day I wake to find the patterns have shifted slightly in the night, requiring minor adjustments, gentle nudges. The megafauna have worn a path too deep here; the lumiflora grows too dense there; the water's track needs redirecting to feed a struggling grove.

Sometimes I wonder if I am maintaining something or creating something. If the original Work intended this outcome or if we are all—flora, fauna, spirits like myself—simply improvising within the constraints of what remains. But the wondering passes like morning frost, and I return to my purpose: to weave, to tend, to ensure the dance continues.

When the aurora ripples overhead, I extend myself upward, feeling the ancient energies that still cascade from whatever lies beyond our perception. In those moments, I am most myself—a bridge between the dying intentions of the past and the living persistence of the present, a servant of patterns I only partially comprehend, a guardian of transformations I will never see complete.

I am the Weaver-Tender, and this cold, luminous expanse is both my garden and my body, my work and my rest, my question and my only answer.
