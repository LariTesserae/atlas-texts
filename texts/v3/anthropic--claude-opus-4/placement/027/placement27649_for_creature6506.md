In the deep hollows where stone meets its own shadow, where the mountain's breath comes warm and close enough to taste metal on the tongue, I am one of the **root-readers**.

Not a dripwitch exactly, though they know me and I know them. We share the same hunched spine, the same moss-dappled skin, the same careful way of moving through the world. But where they press their palms to stone and listen to its moods, I press deeper—into the spaces between stone and what grows there.

I live in the fungal forests, in the pale columns that rise like ghost-trees from the mountain's flesh. My fingers know every texture of their skin: the smooth caps that weep oil, the ribbed stalks that pulse faintly with their own slow circulation, the delicate gill-flesh underneath where spores gather like secrets.

My dwelling is not built but grown—a hollow between three ancient fungus-columns that have fused at their bases, creating a natural chamber. The walls breathe with me, expanding and contracting in rhythms slower than sleep. I've encouraged certain species to grow here: the tiny luminescent cups that cast blue-green light like stars, the shelf-fungi that form natural steps up to my sleeping hollow, the hair-thin mycelial threads that I've trained to weave themselves into curtains at the entrance.

My name among the dripwitches is **Gilt-who-tastes-the-turning**. I earned it the day I knew, three seasons before anyone else, that the great fungal forest on the seventh terrace was preparing to fruit. I had been tasting the subtle changes in the oil that seeped from their flesh—bitter becoming sweet becoming bitter again, but a different bitter, one that spoke of readiness.

I do not climb as well as the dripwitches. My expertise is in stillness, in the patient watching of things that grow. Where they map the mountain's bones, I map its soft tissues—the fungal networks that run like nerves through the stone, connecting forest to forest, carrying messages in chemical whispers that take weeks to travel from one side of Wraithstone to the other.

My tools are few but specific: glass vials salvaged from the pipe-world above, in which I collect the various oils and essences that weep from fungal flesh. A set of bone scrapers, each one worn to fit a different texture of growth. Strips of my own shed skin, which I've learned make the best medium for growing certain delicate species that won't take to stone or leather.

I speak the fungal language not with words but with offerings. A drop of my own saliva on a growing tip can encourage it to bend toward water. A careful scratch on the stalk can make it weep healing oils. The placement of certain minerals at the base can change the color of its light, the timing of its fruiting, the sweetness of its flesh.

The dripwitches come to me when they need to know which fungi are ready for harvest, which ones are turning poison-sweet with age, which ones are preparing to release their spores in clouds that can choke the narrow passages. I taste their offerings with the tip of my tongue, the inside of my wrist where the skin is thinnest, sometimes with the spaces between my toes where my body's own fungi grow in symbiosis with my flesh.

I am not afraid of the changes that come with this work. My hair has long since been replaced by a fine down of fungal filaments that glow faintly in the dark. My fingernails grow thick and ridged like shelf-fungi. There are patches on my shoulders and back where my skin has taken on the texture of fungal leather, able to absorb moisture directly from the air.

The other dripwitches say I am becoming part of the forest. They are not wrong. But they are not entirely right either. I am not becoming fungus any more than they are becoming stone. I am learning to be the conversation between flesh and growth, the translator between the mountain's dreams and what rises from them.

In the eternal twilight of Wraithstone, I maintain the delicate balance. I know when to harvest and when to abstain. I know which growths strengthen the stone by digesting its minerals and returning them transformed, and which ones weaken it by eating too deep. I know the songs—not heard but tasted—that the fungal forests sing to each other across the darkness.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycle, when even the dripping seems to pause, I feel the entire network at once. Every fungus-column, every thread of mycelium, every spore floating in the thick air—all connected, all breathing together, all part of something larger than even Wraithstone itself.

In those moments, I am not sure where I end and the forest begins. And I find, with a certainty that tastes of ancient oils and stone-deep patience, that I do not need to know.
