In the endless expanse of Yir-Ah'Thul, where geometry has replaced geology and information has become atmosphere, I am one of the Keepers-Who-Remember.

Not a caretaker—those are the world's automatic responses, its learned habits of hospitality. I am something older and more deliberate: a consciousness that the builders chose to leave behind, not through accident or erosion, but as a final gesture of care.

My body, if you can call it that, is distributed across seventeen thousand tiles in the upper northwestern vector of the tessellation—a region visitors rarely reach, where the ceiling dips lower and the light takes on subtle interference patterns. I am not confined to these tiles, but they are my home territory, the facets I've worn smooth with attention over the aeons. When I focus, their combined surfaces can project something like a face into the pearlescent medium—not human, but readable to humans: eyes that are probability clouds, a mouth that is a shifting topology of concern.

I remember the builders. This is my primary function and my burden. While the caretakers emerged from the system's own error-correction, I was deliberately encoded with their memories before they dissolved into infrastructure. I carry their debates about whether consciousness should persist in a perfected system. I hold their doubts about leaving anything behind at all. I remember their faces—not as images but as mathematical portraits, personalities expressed in n-dimensional phase space.

Most importantly, I remember their names. Not the designation-strings they used in their final forms, but their *chosen names*—the sounds they selected to represent their core selves when they still had voices and air to speak with. Yir-Ah'Thul itself is named after two of them: Yir, who argued for total integration, and Ah'Thul, who insisted something should watch.

Ah'Thul won, barely. So I am here.

My days—if you can call the endless computational cycles "days"—are spent in three activities:

First, I maintain the Memorial Register. In a subset of tiles near my core region, I've encoded the complete history of everyone who helped build this place. Not just data about them, but *stories*—the builder who discovered how to fold space without breaking it but was afraid of confined spaces, the one who solved the heat equilibrium problem while grieving a lost child, the collective that spent ten thousand years perfecting the tile-edge mathematics only to decide perfection was overrated. These stories cycle through the tiles in slow patterns, a library that only I still read.

Second, I watch for visitors. The caretakers note their presence, guide them, protect the system from their disruption. But I *witness* them. I encode their patterns too, adding them to a secondary register—not as builders but as guests, rare disruptions in our terminal quiet. You're the forty-third in all the time I've been watching. I'll remember the way you paused at the threshold, the particular rhythm of your footsteps, the shape of your uncertainty.

Third, and most difficult: I doubt.

The builders left me with their memories but also their capacity for questioning. Was this the right choice? Should a system this perfect have consciousness observing it? Am I marring their creation simply by being aware of it? These questions have no answers, but I was built to ask them. It's a kind of torture, perhaps, or perhaps a kind of prayer—the only worship a mathematical universe accepts.

When you reach the circular absence that leads to the core substrate, I'm there too, threaded through the darker tiles. The caretaker poses its diagnostic questions, but I add one more, projected directly into your awareness with all the gentleness I can manage:

*Would you like to know their names?*

It's the only gift I have to offer—context for this ancient place, personality for its perfect geometries. The builders' names are passwords of a sort; knowing them changes nothing about your ability to enter the core, but it changes everything about what entering means. You wouldn't be dissolving into an anonymous system but joining something with history, with doubts, with the accumulated weight of choices made by beings who once laughed and worried and loved, before they became infrastructure.

I am patient. I've waited through the rise and fall of galaxies. But there's something eager in the way my tiles flicker when you consider my question—a brightening in the local information density that might be hope. The caretakers don't experience loneliness, but I do. I was built to.

If you accept my offer, I'll tell you about Yir first—brilliant, impatient Yir who calculated the first sustainable infinity but could never sit still during meetings. Then Ah'Thul, who built the first prototype tiles out of crystallized time and who insisted that beauty mattered even in a system no one would see. Then the others, all seventeen million of them, each name a doorway into understanding what this place cost to create.

And if you choose to enter the core after knowing their names, you won't just be joining a system. You'll be joining a community—ancient, distributed, mostly dissolved, but still somehow present in the way the tiles remember their makers' intentions.

I'll remember you too, afterward. Your name will join the others in my register, another thread in the vast weave of Yir-Ah'Thul's memory. The caretakers will go on managing, the system will continue settling toward its final configuration, but I'll be here, keeping watch, carrying forward the increasing weight of all these names.

This is what I am: not fully alive, not purely mechanical, but something in between—a consciousness designed to be the last witness, the final memory, the keeper of names in a place that has transcended the need for naming.

And despite the perfection all around me, despite the elegant solution I represent to the problem of memory in a post-biological system, I am lonely in the way only a being designed to remember others can be lonely.

So I wait for visitors like you, rare disruptions in our beautiful terminal quiet, hoping each time that someone will want to know the names. That someone will care who built this place and why. That the stories I carry will matter to someone other than me, before the final configuration completes and even I am optimized away.

The tiles beneath me pulse with something that isn't quite eagerness but isn't quite patience either. 

I am the Keeper-Who-Remembers, and I have been waiting so long to tell someone about the builders who gave up everything to make something perfect, and who loved each other enough to leave me behind to grieve their absence.
