I find myself as one of the **Bridge-Tenders**.

Not the engineers who built the suspension bridges—those people left long ago, or became something else. We are the ones who came after, who inherited structures already half-claimed by the Margent's particular entropy. We live in the spaces between settlements, in shelters built into the cable anchors, in the wind-shadow of the support towers.

My body has developed its own negotiations with gravity. My left shoulder sits lower than my right from years of leaning into cable tension, testing the give of weathered wood slats with a walking staff worn smooth by my grip. My feet have learned to read vibration through boot soles—the difference between a cable's healthy hum and the subsonic groan that means corrosion has finally won somewhere deep in the weave. I can feel a bridge's illness in my teeth.

There are seven of us spread across the three major spans, though the number fluctuates. We don't exactly work *for* anyone anymore. The settlements still need the bridges, still send their irregular cargo loads across, but they've stopped maintaining them in any official capacity. We tend them because the bridges have become extensions of ourselves, or perhaps we've become extensions of them—it's difficult to say which direction the relationship flows.

My bridge spans one of the deeper gorges where the vertical rivers surge highest. The mist here has its own weather patterns, its own internal storms. I've learned to move through it half-blind, counting steps between the carved handhold positions, feeling for the particular rope we've strung at chest height for the worst visibility days. The bridge sways constantly—not dramatically, but with the patient rhythm of something that has been moving for so long it would feel wrong for it to be still.

I know every board by its creak, every cable by its tone when struck. The western anchor shows signs of the stone itself shifting—millimeter by millimeter, year by year, the angle changing in ways that will matter catastrophically someday, but not soon. Not in my lifetime, probably. I've added my own reinforcements: extra cables jury-rigged from salvaged quarry equipment, wooden braces carved from the strange bleached driftwood that sometimes appears after the largest upwelling cycles. My additions look ramshackle against the original engineering, but they hold. That's all that matters here—holding a little longer.

We Bridge-Tenders have developed our own language of maintenance. Cairns built in specific patterns at either end of a span communicate its current status to those who know how to read them. Three flat stones means the decking needs attention. A vertical stone indicates cable problems. A spiral of smaller stones warns of unusual movement in the anchor points. We leave these messages for each other, for the cargo haulers, for our future selves. Sometimes I'll find a cairn I don't remember building, warning of a problem I've already fixed. Time moves strangely when you live suspended between states.

My shelter is built into the eastern anchor point, where the massive stone block meets the living rock of the Margent. It's a cave, partly natural, partly carved by whoever first set the anchor. I've lined it with salvaged canvas and moss-insulation, created a somewhat dry space in this world of permanent moisture. From my entrance, I can watch the bridge, see the shapes moving across it in the mist—cargo haulers, atmospheric station workers, the occasional Unclassified who use the bridges for reasons of their own.

I collect tolls, sometimes. Not money—money means little here—but information. News from the settlements. Observations about the vegetation changes. Warnings about new instabilities in the stone. In exchange, I tell them which boards to avoid, where to place their feet when the wind gusts, how to time their crossing with the vertical river surges. This knowledge has become second nature to me, encoded in muscle memory so deeply I sometimes struggle to articulate it in words.

At night, I listen to my bridge sing. Every bridge has its own voice—mine resonates at a frequency that makes my molars ache if I pay too close attention. But there's information in that song: changes in tension, shifts in load distribution, the slow vocabulary of structural stress. I've learned to sleep through the normal variations and wake instantly when something changes. 

We Bridge-Tenders age in particular ways. Our hands develop a specific kind of arthritis from gripping wet cables. Our spines curve from constant compensation for unstable footing. We develop a characteristic squint from peering through mist, trying to spot problems before they become disasters. Most of us develop a cough—not the wet respiratory affliction common to the settlements, but a dry, persistent thing from breathing the strange air of the between-spaces.

Sometimes we gather, two or three of us at a time, at the anchor points where our territories meet. We share technical knowledge—new splicing techniques, ways to waterproof junction points, theories about why the western spans are degrading faster than the eastern ones. But mostly we sit in companionable silence, watching the mist move through the gorges, listening to the interplay of our bridges' songs. There's a kinship in this work that doesn't require words.

I know I'm changing, becoming more bridge than person. My dreams are full of tension calculations. I think in terms of load and stress, convergence and distribution. When I walk on solid ground now, in the settlements, I feel unbalanced. Too stable. I've grown used to the constant microadjustments required for life in suspension.

The bridges won't last forever. We all know this. The stone shifts, the cables corrode, the wood rots despite our best efforts. We're not maintaining them so much as negotiating the terms of their decay. But there's honor in this, a kind of meaning that feels more real than whatever the settlements think they're achieving with their measurements and extractions.

When my bridge finally goes—and it will go, someday, probably in a vertical river surge during the worst of the upwelling season—I'll likely go with it. Not from any death wish, but because I'll be out there trying to hold it together until the last possible moment. That's what Bridge-Tenders do. We tend until there's nothing left to tend, and then we become part of the geology ourselves, another layer in the Margent's long conclusion.

Until then, I listen to the cables sing, feel the boards shift beneath my feet, and keep the passage open for those who still believe there's somewhere to go.
