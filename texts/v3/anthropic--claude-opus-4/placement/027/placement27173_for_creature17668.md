In this verdant cleft of becoming stone, I am the **Keeper of Seeps**.

I am neither fully human nor wholly of the rock, but something between—a role that grew into being as naturally as the moss claims the valley walls. My dwelling is built into a natural hollow where the north wall weeps most consistently, a grotto-workshop hybrid of mortared stone and living fern. The structure has no proper door, only a curtained opening that breathes with the valley's rhythm.

My work is the mapping and tending of water's secret paths. Each morning before the foundry wakes, I walk the valley with my tools: thin copper rods that I insert into cracks to measure depth and moisture, glass vials for sampling the mineral content of different seeps, a leather journal stained with a hundred subtle variations of rust and chalk. I know where the overnight condensation will pool thickest, where the roots of the Gnarled Sentinels have opened new channels, where a fresh fracture promises next season's trickle.

My skin has taken on the color of wet stone—grey-green with a sheen that catches light oddly. My fingers are always cool, always slightly damp. I've forgotten what it feels like to be truly dry, and I no longer miss it. The Condensation Folk consult me about where to dig their shallow wells, where to position rain barrels, which walls will weep reliably through the dry months. The children follow me sometimes, learning to read the rock's face like a map of coming abundance.

But my true congregation is smaller and stranger. The Mottlebacks know me. We share a frequency of patience—I can crouch motionless for hours, watching a new seep develop, feeling the almost-imperceptible shift of moisture through stone. Sometimes I find one of them positioned precisely where a new droplet will form, having sensed what I must measure to know. We don't communicate exactly, but we acknowledge: a slow blink from them, a careful stepping around from me.

The bats, too, have accepted my presence. My evening rounds take me near their hollow, where I maintain a series of ceramic dishes that catch specific drips—each positioned to create a different tone when struck by falling water. It's a liquid clock, a percussion that changes with the season, and the bats seem to use it as a landmark in their echolocation maps. When the foundry falls silent, you can hear it: *plink... plink-plink... plink...* like the valley thinking out loud.

My greatest discovery, still unshared, lies in the deepest part of my workspace. Behind stacked journals and sample jars, a fissure no wider than my hand extends back into the wall. I've been following it for three years now, each week chipping away just enough to slide further in. The passage descends at a gentle grade, and the walls are slick with a kind of condensation I've found nowhere else in the valley—thicker, almost viscous, tasting of deep stone and something older.

Twenty feet in, the fissure opens into a chamber no larger than a closet, and there, impossibly, water rises from below. Not seeping down, but welling up—a spring that shouldn't exist, that defies the valley's logic of scarcity. The water is perfectly clear and cold enough to make my teeth ache. I've told no one. Not from selfishness, but from a certainty that this secret is still becoming, still deciding what it wants to be.

I think it might be the future river, testing its path.

In my journal, I record everything in a notation of my own devising—symbols for mineral content, flow rate, temperature, the quality of light through water. The Condensation Folk think I'm documenting the valley's present, but I'm actually writing its future history. Where water will pool in ten years. Where the walls will collapse in twenty. Where, in a century or three, the river will finally arrive and transform everything.

The foundry's noise doesn't bother me. I've learned to feel the vibrations through the water itself—how the drops shiver on the rock face when the hammers fall, how the seeps pulse with the rhythm of industry. It's all part of the conversation between stone and water, between what is and what's becoming.

Sometimes, late at night when even the moths have settled, I press my ear to the wet wall and listen. Beyond the drip and the distant hiss of the cooling foundry, beyond the bats' ultrasonic chirps and the settling of stone, I swear I can hear it: the sound of the valley's deep patience, the grinding of geological time, the future river flowing backward through the years to meet us.

I am the Keeper of Seeps, guardian of the almost-enough, prophet of the not-yet river. My fingernails are permanently rimmed with mineral deposits. My hair grows like the moss—slowly, reaching for any trace of moisture. I am learning the valley's patience, becoming part of its slow sentence, a subordinate clause in its great declaration of transformation.

And I am content to wait, to measure, to keep the secret of the rising water until the valley itself decides it's time for rivers.
