I am one of the stone-singers, though that is not what we call ourselves. We have no need for such names when the work itself is our identity.

My claws have grown thick and ridged from seasons of testing mortar joints, and the whorl pattern on my left shoulder marks me as one who learned the binding ways—how to feel where stone wants to meet stone, where wood desires to brace against weight. I move through the bright waters of our hall with purpose that needs no hurrying. The current speaks, and I listen.

Today I work the eastern wall where three new blocks await placement. My partner—a younger one whose shoulder bears only the first spiral of apprenticeship—helps me guide them into position. We do not speak. I tilt my head toward the gap where water pushes too forcefully, and they understand: we must adjust the angle. Our mallets ring in harmony, tap-tap-tap, until the stone settles into its proper seat with a satisfaction I feel in my bones.

The light-orbs pulse around us, and I pause to run my webbed palm across the nearest one. It needs cleaning—small particles have gathered on its surface, dimming its radiance. I scrape gently with the edge of my smallest claw until it blazes again, adding its voice to the chorus of illumination that keeps our hall alive with brightness.

When the tide-drums sound—that deep thrumming through the water that calls us to gather—I swim with the others to the central pillar. We press our bodies against it, feeling its solidity, adding our humming to its vibration. The hall speaks back to us through the stone: *yes, I am sound, I am strong, I continue.* This is our prayer and our verification, our moment of unity with what we build.

I know every current in this place. The northwestern channel runs faster after the morning flows; the southern gates need adjusting when the deep tides shift. My fingers have memorized the texture of each wooden brace, the temperature variations where different stones meet. This knowledge lives in my body, not in words.

Sometimes I dream of the hall as it will be—not just these few chambers we've completed, but corridors stretching into depths we haven't yet imagined, all of it singing with the same bright purpose. But I do not rush toward that future. Each block placed today deserves the same patience as the last one we'll ever set.

In the resting chambers behind the third pillar, I keep my tools wrapped in woven kelp-cloth. My favorite chisel—carved from the bone of something ancient that drifted to us on a blessed current—fits my grip perfectly. When I hold it, I feel the continuation of all who shaped before me and all who will shape after.

We eat together in the small alcoves, sharing the pale paste that sustains us. It tastes of depth and salt, of life distilled to essence. A younger one clicks softly—a question about tomorrow's work on the upper arches. I respond with a gesture: *patience, feel first, then we'll know.*

This is how we live: in the eternal present of creation, where every strike of the mallet matters, where every joint and seam becomes part of our breathing world. The sparse vegetation that finds its way to our corners does not disturb us. We are not gardeners of green things but tenders of stone and current, shapers of spaces that pulse with crafted light.

When I rest, I float in the heart of our hall and feel the water moving through all its channels—controlled, purposeful, alive. The groaning of the beams is music. The rush of currents through our gates is rhythm. And we, the builders, are both audience and orchestra, creating and dwelling within our own unfinished symphony.

This is enough. This is everything. To shape and be shaped by bright water and singing stone.
