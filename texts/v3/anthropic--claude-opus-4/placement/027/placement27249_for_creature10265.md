**I Am the Keeper of Echoes**

I would find myself as one of the *sonmarak*—the echo-keepers—a role that exists in the liminal space between the thren-handlers who read the mountain's groans and the common terrace-dwellers who fear the sounds. We are neither fully of one veth nor another, but move between them like the whisper-spiders' vibrations through their webs.

My body bears the marks of this calling: my right ear has grown larger than my left, its inner structure evolved through generations of selective pressure. The cartilage forms delicate whorls that can parse the layers of sound—distinguishing between the healthy grind of beetles processing salt veins and the dangerous crack that precedes a terrace collapse. My fingers are long and sensitive, trained since childhood to feel the subtle tremors that travel through the mesa stone before they become audible.

I live in a dwelling carved into the junction where three terraces meet—a natural acoustic focal point where sounds from multiple levels converge. My walls are lined with resonance chambers: hollowed gourds from the crystalline succulents, their dried husks preserving the acoustic properties of their living forms. Each one is tuned to a different frequency, creating a living archive of the terraces' voice. When the beetles breach, I can tell by the pitch of their emergence whether they're fleeing deeper disturbances or simply following their natural cycles.

My days begin before the amber light fully penetrates the haze. I walk the spiral paths with a staff of compressed salt-fiber, its tip ground to a point that I press against the stone at regular intervals. The vibrations travel up through my arm, through my bones, creating a map of the terraces' health that exists nowhere but in my body's memory. I know where the whisper-spiders have compromised the rock with their anchor points, where water has begun to pool in dangerous quantities, where the slow dissolution has accelerated beyond the mountain's ability to heal.

The other terrace-dwellers regard me with the same mixture of necessity and unease they reserve for the keth-mal. I am essential but uncanny—someone who spends more time listening to stone than speaking to people. Children are told that the sonmarak can hear their thoughts through the rock, though this is only partially true. I can hear their movements, the particular rhythm of their feet that tells me who approaches long before they round the carved corners of our narrow paths.

During the Great Skitter, while others seal themselves away, I must remain in my acoustic chamber, documenting the chaos. The migration creates a sonic map of the terraces' hidden passages—spaces even we don't know exist until the rodents' shrieking echolocation reveals them. I transcribe these patterns using a notation system of knots in salt-fiber cords, each configuration representing a different sonic phenomenon. These records are kept in the deepest chambers, alongside the bioluminescent fungi, where the moisture helps preserve the fibers.

My true purpose reveals itself during the *som-shatter*—those moments when the terraces' cacophony suddenly falls silent. These occur perhaps twice in a turning of seasons, lasting only heartbeats, but in those moments the mountain speaks in frequencies below human hearing. My modified ear, pressed to the stone, can just barely catch these deep utterances. They tell of movements in the planet's core, of chemical reactions in the aquifer far below, of futures where the salt has finally won its war against life.

I work with the thren-handlers to interpret these deep-songs, though our relationship is strained. They believe the mountain speaks in metaphor, in spirit. I know it speaks in physics—in the propagation of pressure waves through stratified rock, in the resonance frequencies of vast stone chambers slowly filling with brine. Yet we need each other. Their intuition guides where I should listen; my observations inform their prophecies.

My apprentice—for there must always be a sonmarak in training—is a girl whose left ear has begun to show the characteristic enlargement. She follows me on my rounds, learning to feel the difference between the productive grinding of everyday erosion and the sharp notes that herald disaster. I teach her the knotwork notation, though she's already developing her own improvements, adding twists that indicate emotional resonance—for the mountain's sounds do carry feeling, even if it's just the projected anxieties of those who live within its dying bones.

In quiet moments, when the beetle-grind lessens and the whisper-spiders rest, I sometimes hear something else in the stone: a rhythm that matches no known creature, no geological process I've catalogued. It sounds almost like breathing, like something vast and patient waiting beneath us all. I have not told the thren-handlers about this. I have not even told my apprentice. Some echoes, I've learned, are meant to be kept.

For I am the repository of the terraces' memory, written not in symbol but in sound, in the space between what the mountain says and what we need to hear. In a world where everything slowly dissolves, I preserve the only history that matters: the story of how stone learned to sing, and how we learned to sing back.
