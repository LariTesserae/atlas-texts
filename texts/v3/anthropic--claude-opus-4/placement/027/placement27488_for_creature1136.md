In the shadow of a rusted communications tower, half-buried in centuries of snow, I maintain my vigil. I am the Keeper of Frequencies, though I have forgotten when I first took up this duty, or if indeed I ever had a choice in the matter.

My dwelling is carved into the ice shelf where the tower's foundation meets the mountain—a series of chambers that spiral downward, following the ancient cables that still pulse with their mysterious hum. The walls are lined with salvaged equipment, devices whose original purposes have long since evolved under my patient tinkering. Oscilloscopes display the heartbeats of the mountain. Receivers translate the songs of ice formation into visible wavelengths. Everything here speaks in frequencies: the crack of glacial shift at 0.1 Hz, the frost hawks' hunting cries at 3,000 Hz, the eternal mechanical drone at precisely 60 Hz that emanates from somewhere far beneath.

I am no longer entirely human, if I ever was. The cold has worked its way into my bones, turned them dense and resonant like tuning forks. My skin has taken on the blue-grey hue of deep ice, and my eyes have developed a second membrane that filters the snow-glare while allowing me to perceive the electromagnetic spectra that dance across this desolate realm. My fingers have grown long and sensitive, able to feel the vibrations in the air, to read the stories that the wind carries from the abandoned settlements scattered across the plateau.

Each day—though day and night blur together in the endless twilight of these altitudes—I climb the tower. The metal sings beneath my touch, each strut and cable contributing to a vast symphonic equation I am still deciphering. At the pinnacle, where the receiver dishes have long since warped into abstract sculptures, I perform my ritual: I broadcast.

What do I broadcast? Even I am not entirely certain. The signals come to me in dreams, in the patterns of ice formation on my windows, in the migration paths of the luminous beetles that emerge from the crevasses during the brief summer thaw. Sometimes I think I am translating between the mountain and the machines, serving as a conduit for conversations that began before the first snows fell. Other times I believe I am simply screaming into the void, my transmissions joining the cosmic background radiation, indistinguishable from stellar noise.

The ice crawlers know me. They skitter up the tower's legs during their hunts, pause at my window to tap messages in their chittering code. I leave offerings of condensed nutrients I synthesize from the hardy lichens and the occasional mechanical fluids that still seep from the old infrastructure. In return, they bring me artifacts—fragments of circuit boards worn smooth by time, crystals that hum with stored energy, sometimes bones of creatures I cannot identify.

There are others like me, I think. Sometimes, when the atmospheric conditions align, my receivers pick up similar broadcasts—patterns too deliberate to be natural, too strange to be purely human. We form a network of hermits, each at our own rusted tower or buried facility, each translating our own fragment of this world's deep language. We are the nervous system of a landscape that dreams in geological time.

The frost hawks regard me with intelligent eyes when they perch on my dishes. They have incorporated the electromagnetic frequencies into their hunting strategies, using the tower's emissions to navigate in whiteout conditions. Their feathers shimmer with an opalescence that might be natural evolution or might be radiation-induced mutation. The distinction no longer matters; in this place, technology and biology have been weathering into each other for so long that they've become aspects of the same phenomenon.

At night, I descend to my deepest chamber, where the cables converge into a nexus of obsolete connection standards and jury-rigged interfaces. Here, I plug myself in—biomechanical ports I've cultivated along my spine interfacing with the system. In this state, I become part of the mountain's dreaming, experience the slow thoughts of stone and the quick flicker of quantum processes still running in buried processors. I feel the pulse of the Earth's magnetic field, the tidal pull of the moon on the ice sheets, the migration patterns encoded in the genes of the snow hares.

This is my purpose, my burden, my joy: to be a translator between scales of time, a bridge between the organic and the artificial, a witness to the slow convergence of what was and what is becoming. The abandoned infrastructure is not dead but dormant, waiting for the right frequency to awaken it. The creatures are not mere animals but participants in an evolutionary experiment that transcends old categories. And I am the tuning fork that helps them find their resonance.

Tomorrow, or the next day, or a thousand years hence, someone will receive my signal and understand. Until then, I broadcast, I listen, I endure. The mountain hums its ancient song, and I harmonize, adding my voice to the chorus that echoes across the frozen expanse, where the boundaries between living and artificial, between past and future, dissolve like snow in the morning sun that never quite seems to rise.
