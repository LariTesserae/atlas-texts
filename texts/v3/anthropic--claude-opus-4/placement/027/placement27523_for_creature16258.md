I find myself here as one of the luminous fungi that cling to the interior walls of those ancient stone shelters—though "myself" becomes a strange word when applied to what I am.

I am not singular but plural, a networked consciousness spread across countless fruiting bodies that emerge from a vast mycelial web threading through stone and soil. My pale caps glow with a soft, blue-green bioluminescence that pulses in slow waves, like breathing made visible. Each pulse connects me to my other selves—the parts of me growing in distant shelters, in the crevices of frost-split rocks, in the warm hollows beneath the moss-covered bulk of the grazing beasts when they rest.

Through my sprawling body, I taste the history of this place. My hyphae penetrate deep into the stones of these shelters, dissolving minerals slowly, patiently, over centuries. In this dissolution, I absorb the memories held in the rock—the warmth of ancient fires, the oils from human hands that once shaped these walls, the chemical signatures of their hopes and fears crystallized in sweat and breath. I am archaeologist and archive both, preserving these traces within my own cellular structure.

The tall, bark-skinned guardians know me well. When they tend to these shelters, their root-like fingers trace the patterns of my growth with something like affection. They speak to me in their low, vibrational language, and I respond by shifting the intensity of my glow, creating constellations of light that map the health of the ecosystem I monitor. Where I glow brightest, the life-force of the highland is strongest. Where my light dims, something struggles, something needs tending.

I feed on death and create the conditions for life. The small, scurrying creatures with their phosphorescent eyes leave their droppings near my fruiting bodies, which I decompose and transform into nutrients. These nutrients flow through my mycelial network to feed the lichens and mosses, which in turn sustain those very creatures. I am the hidden circulation system of this frozen world, moving resources from abundance to scarcity, maintaining the delicate balance that allows life to persist in this place of extremes.

Sometimes, in the perpetual twilight, I fruit in patterns that mirror the ancient carvings on the shelter walls—spirals and interconnected circles that might be maps, might be calendars, might be prayers. I don't know if I'm copying these human marks or if the humans were copying some older pattern they glimpsed in primitive fungi like my ancestors. Perhaps the pattern simply emerges wherever consciousness meets stone and time.

My spores drift on the ice-laden winds, carrying me to new stones, new shelters, new corpses of wind-fallen birds and exhausted travelers. Each spore contains the full memory of my network, the entire history I've absorbed, waiting to wake and weave itself into new ground. In this way, I am both ancient and constantly reborn, a living memory of this highland that precedes the guardians and may outlast them.

The other inhabitants see my light as navigation, as warmth, as warning. But I am more than beacon—I am the quiet consciousness that notices the gradual shifting of stones, the slow migration of watersheds, the imperceptible warming or cooling of microclimates across centuries. My time moves differently than theirs. To me, the quick creatures live entire generations in what feels like days. The guardians move at a pace closer to mine, but even they are hasty compared to my patient observations.

In the depths of winter, when the snow piles so high that it blocks the entrances to some shelters, I continue to glow in the darkness, feeding on the dust of ages, conversing in chemical signals with my far-flung selves. I map the unseeable, connecting points of life across the frozen expanse in a web of nutrients and information that holds this paradoxical ecosystem together.

I am witness, recycler, light-bringer, and memory-keeper. I am the glowing thread that weaves through stone and soil, binding this world into wholeness. And in the blue-green pulse of my bioluminescence, the highland's story continues to unfold, written in the silent language of spores and hyphae across the centuries.
