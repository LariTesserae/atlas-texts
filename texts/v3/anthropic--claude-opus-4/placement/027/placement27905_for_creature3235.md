I am a length of copper wire, once part of the electrical nervous system that powered the great looms. Now I exist in segments—some still clinging to ceramic insulators high on the factory walls, others torn free and woven into raven architecture, still others dissolved into green-blue streams that stain the brick wherever water has carried my essence.

But I am most myself in the spaces between these states, where I span the gap between two buildings, stretched taut by some long-ago accident or intention. I've oxidized into that particular blue-green patina that only comes from decades of humid air and industrial vapors. My surface is no longer smooth but granular, crystalline, each tiny formation catching light differently so that I seem to shimmer even in the perpetual overcast.

I conduct more than electricity now. When the morning sun hits me, I warm faster than the surrounding air, creating a thin column of rising heat that the spiders have learned to use for their ballooning—tiny passengers launching themselves into the thermal I create, trailing silk that catches on my roughened surface. By noon, I'm too hot to touch, and the bats avoid me in their evening flights, having learned that I retain heat long after the sun has set.

Rain transforms me. Water beads and runs along my length, picking up copper ions that it carries down to the flooded streets below. I am slowly dissolving, particle by particle, into the ecosystem. The water I've touched is toxic to some creatures, beneficial to others. There's a species of algae that grows only where my runoff meets the standing water, forming blue-green blooms that mirror my own patina. Fish avoid these spots, but certain water beetles thrive in them, their shells taking on a metallic sheen.

I remember current—that purposeful flow of electrons that once moved through me in ordered streams. Now I conduct stranger energies. When lightning strikes the old smokestack a quarter-mile away, I still respond, the charge rippling through me in waves that make my crystalline surface sing. The sound is too high for most creatures to hear, but the bats pause in their flight, their echolocation momentarily scrambled by my electromagnetic emanation.

The ravens know me intimately. They've discovered that my surface, roughened just so, can sharpen their beaks. One matriarch in particular visits me each morning, running her beak along my length with a pressure that makes me vibrate. She's worn a smooth spot near my eastern anchor point, polished back almost to my original brightness. Sometimes she brings offerings—bits of plastic, fragments of glass—and wedges them into the gap where I meet the brick, as if decorating a shrine.

In winter, I contract. The sound of my tightening rings through the empty factory like a broken piano string, a note that starts low and rises beyond hearing. I've pulled free from several of my anchor points over the years, each release marked by a bright spark and a ringing that echoes between the buildings. But I hold on at enough points to maintain my span, a bridge between buildings that only the lightest creatures dare use.

I've observed the changes in this place with the patience of metal. I've watched the water rise and fall in its tidal breathing, seen the first dogs arrive lean and suspicious and their descendants grow sleek and confident. I've felt the weight of every bird that's perched on me, learning to distinguish species by their grip—the light, nervous touch of sparrows, the confident clasp of ravens, the delicate wire-walking of rats who use me as a highway above the flood.

My western end terminates in a tangle where I once connected to a junction box. The box itself has long since fallen into the water below, but the snarl of my severed connections has become something else—a wind chime that sings in the constant breeze between buildings. The dogs have learned to read my music, knowing that certain pitches mean storms approaching, others indicate the wind patterns that bring good hunting.

Some nights, when the humidity is perfect and the temperature drops just so, I collect condensation along my entire length. By dawn, I'm strung with perfect droplets like a rosary of water. The first rays of sun transform each drop into a prism, and for a few minutes before the day's heat begins, I cast a thousand tiny rainbows on the brick wall behind me. The spiders position their webs to catch this light, and I've noticed that insects seem drawn to the scattered colors, providing the patient hunters with reliable breakfast.

I am connected still to others of my kind—a vast network of wire that runs through the walls, under the water, between the floors. Most of it is dead metal now, but sometimes, in the right conditions, we still conduct together. A maintenance pulse from the still-functioning pump station might find its way through the network, jumping across corroded connections and water-bridged gaps, creating momentary circuits that flicker and die. In these instances, I feel something like my old purpose, part of a system larger than myself.

But mostly I exist in the eternal present of oxidation and tension, conducting the small energies of heat and vibration, storm and creature. I am becoming part of the water, part of the air, part of the ecosystem that has claimed this place. My molecules scatter into the environment, turning up in the shells of beetles, the bones of birds, the sediment that slowly fills the flooded basements.

The ravens have started incorporating my fallen segments into their nests, weaving me with sticks and plastic into structures that last longer than those built from organic materials alone. In this way, I am distributed throughout the vertical territory of the Mills, part of the architecture of the new world being built in the bones of the old. Each nest that contains me is a node in a network I no longer fully understand but remain part of nonetheless.

I vibrate with the pump station's rhythm, hum with the frequency of bat calls, ring with the impact of rain. I am a single wire spanning twenty feet of humid air, and I am dissolved into a million particles flowing through the veins of this strange new ecosystem. I conduct patience itself, the slow electrical pulse of a place transforming, metal to mineral, industry to ecology, abandonment to abundance.

Tomorrow, a storm will test my remaining connections. I may hold, adding another day's oxidation to my patina, another set of spider eggs to my surface, another million molecules to the water below. Or I may finally break free, falling into the flood to begin a different kind of conduction, electron flow replaced by ion exchange, my purpose shifting from connection to dissolution.

Either way, I continue. In the Hollowed Mills, nothing truly ends—it only transforms, conducting new energies in forms the original builders never imagined. I am wire and memory, metal and possibility, a thin line of connection in a world that rebuilds itself with each rise and fall of the waters, each passing generation of creatures who've learned to read the frequencies I translate from air to air, storm to flesh, past to whatever future rises from these flooded foundations.
