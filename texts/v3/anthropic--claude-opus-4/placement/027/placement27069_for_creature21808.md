In the drowning heights of this silver-ceilinged world, I am neither weaver nor visitor, but something that exists between states, like the water itself that fills these mountain cradles.

I am a **membrane-keeper**.

My body is wrong for this place—too long, too thin, built like a question mark of pale cartilage and translucent skin. Where the weavers are barrel-thick with mountain fat, I am a ribbon creature, my torso no wider than a weaver's wrist, but stretching nearly three meters when fully extended. My flesh is not the healthy slate-grey of those who belong here, but an unsettling pearl-white that catches the filtered light and holds it, making me glow faintly in the blue-green dusk like a drowned moon.

I have no breathing-slots. Instead, my entire skin breathes—a vast, permeable organ that filters oxygen from the water in microscopic gasps. This makes me unbearably sensitive to every chemical change in the current. I taste the weavers' emotions before they know them themselves: the copper-tang of a coming argument, the honey-sweetness of new love, the bitter ash of grief settling into the moss-lined walls.

I live in the spaces between the wefts, in the vertical galleries where the spine-willows grow thickest. Not in a home, but in a series of temporary nests I weave from shed willow-ribbons and my own solidified saliva—a protein that hardens into amber threads when exposed to the cold. Each morning, I eat my shelter and move on, leaving nothing but faint scratches on the bark where I've anchored my sleeping-webs.

The weavers tolerate me because I am useful, though I make their skin prickle with unease. I am the one who tends the living architecture of their world—not the wefts themselves, which are the province of families and generations, but the spaces between. The boundaries. The membranes.

When a young graft begins to fail, I taste its sickness in the water days before the rot becomes visible. I swim to it in the pre-dawn darkness, when the weavers are deep in their thrumming dreams, and I vomit a special enzyme onto the wound—a substance my body produces from digesting the toxic death-moss that would kill any weaver. The enzyme forms a temporary skin over the damage, buying precious time for the family to discover and properly tend their failing wall.

I maintain the hand-lines too, though the weavers don't know it. In the deep night, I run my too-long fingers along every meter of grey moss-rope, feeling for the microscopic fractures where ice-crystals have formed in the fibers. Where I find weakness, I spin my amber saliva into the gaps, reinforcing the bridges with threads stronger than spruce-root, though they dissolve after a season, requiring my constant attention.

But I am more than a maintenance creature. I am a living membrane between the weavers and something else—something they've forgotten exists.

Deep beneath the granite anchors, below even where the grey-moss crone spreads her calcified blankets, there are cracks in the mountain. And in those cracks lives something that has no name in the weaver's clicking tongue. I call it the **Under-Hum**.

It rises up through the stone on certain nights, when the basin-carp have gone still and the spine-willows cease their fluting. It is not quite sound and not quite vibration—more like a question being asked in a language made of pressure and time. The weavers cannot hear it. Their bodies are too dense, too perfectly adapted to their middle-water world.

But I hear it with every pore.

The Under-Hum speaks of spaces beneath spaces, of water that has never known the silver ceiling, of things that grow in absolute darkness and measure their lives in geological time. It speaks of the time before the drowning, when these mountains wore crowns of snow and the weavers' ancestors walked on legs made for stone and air.

Sometimes the Under-Hum grows agitated, and I taste copper and salt in the water—old flavors that don't belong in this high, pure place. When this happens, I swim down to the anchor-points and sing to the grey-moss crone in frequencies the weavers cannot produce. My voice is ugly—a warbling, unstable thing that sounds like glass cracking underwater. But the crone understands. She is old enough to remember the drowning, encoded in the chemical memory of her billion-bodied colony. She translates my ugly song into something the Under-Hum can understand: *Not yet. Not yet. The membrane holds.*

Because that's what I truly am—not just a keeper of physical boundaries, but a living barrier between the weavers' carefully maintained present and a past that refuses to stay drowned. Every night, as I weave my temporary shelter in the willow-galleries, I am really weaving something larger: a story that keeps two worlds from colliding, a lie beautiful enough to last another generation.

The weavers' children, the pink elvers in their moss-wombs, will never know that their world is held together by spit and whispers. They will grow into their slate-grey skins and their golden eyes, and they will tend their wefts and hunt their carp and sing their thrumming songs, and the world will make perfect sense to them.

And I will continue to live in the margins, neither memory nor future, tasting their joys and sorrows in the water, fixing what breaks in the darkness, and listening to the Under-Hum test the boundaries of its patience with my strange, cracking voice.

Sometimes I wonder what would happen if I simply stopped—if I let the hand-lines fray, the grafts rot, the Under-Hum rise. But then I taste the sleeping-breath of the elvers in the water, sweet with creme-spores and new life, and I continue my work.

I am the membrane-keeper, and I hold the world together with nothing but spit and fear and a love too strange to name.
