In the spaces between spaces, where the great trunk-chambers narrow into forgotten passages and the root systems create natural catacombs, I am one of the **Echo-Mappers**—though that name captures only the surface of what we do.

We inhabit the acoustic architecture of Ashkenar, living in the resonant cavities where sound behaves strangely. My dwelling is a spiral chamber deep within one of the oldest trunks, where the wood has calcified into something almost crystalline. The walls here remember every sound that has ever touched them, creating a palimpsest of whispers, engine-thrums, and the deep groaning of growth.

My body has adapted to this work over the decades. My eardrums have thickened and developed additional membranes—I can hear frequencies that would damage unprotected ears, and more importantly, I can hear the *shapes* of sounds. When steam moves through the pipes three levels above, I hear not just the hiss but the exact configuration of the conduits. When roots crack stone far below, the sound tells me the mineral composition of what they've broken.

I wear garments woven from sound-dampening moss, harvested from the quietest places in the Gardens—material so absorbent of vibration that it seems to eat words spoken near it. My tools are tuning forks grown from the trees themselves, each one resonating with a different part of Ashkenar's vast body. The largest, made from heartwood of the first trunk, hums at a frequency so low it takes minutes to complete a single wave. The smallest, carved from a twig that grew in an electrical field, rings at the edge of human perception.

My work is to maintain the sonic health of the Gardens. You see, Ashkenar sings—not metaphorically, but literally. The combination of steam engines, electrical systems, wind through leaves, and the deep pulse of sap creates a composition that must stay in harmony. When frequencies clash, pipes crack. When resonances align incorrectly, the trees grow in spiral deformities that weaken their structure. 

I spend my days (though day and night blur in the eternal twilight of the inner trunks) walking the sound-paths, placing my palms against walls to feel the vibrations, sometimes pressing my ear to the floor for hours to track a troublesome harmonic to its source. I've learned to vocalize at frequencies that can shatter rust or encourage sap flow, and my throat bears the scars of early experiments when I pushed too hard and tasted copper blood.

The other inhabitants think us strange—stranger even than the Tree-Speakers. We move through their spaces like ghosts, sometimes standing perfectly still in doorways for hours, reading the acoustic shadows of their daily lives. Children fear us, thinking we can hear their thoughts (we can't, though we can hear their heartbeats from three chambers away). The Pneumatic Guild respects us grudgingly, for we often detect imminent engine failures by the subtle change in their music days before any mechanical sign appears.

But we know things others don't. We know that Ashkenar dreams, and its dreams sound like whale song slowed to geological tempo. We know that the trees communicate through subsonic pulses that travel through the root networks faster than any messenger. We've heard the electromagnetic songs of the Copper Weavers, translated through our modified nervous systems into something almost like language.

My chamber contains my life's work: a massive map of Ashkenar drawn entirely in sound. Wires stretched between the walls hold tiny resonators, each one representing a different location in the Gardens. When I pluck one, it sounds its note, and through the sympathetic vibrations of all the others, I can hear the acoustic health of the entire city. Maintenance crews have learned to check with me before beginning major work—I can tell them how their proposed changes will alter the harmonic balance, preventing disasters before they occur.

Sometimes, in the deepest quiet of the sleep-cycle, when even the engines reduce their breathing to a whisper, I sit in the center of my chamber and listen to the totality of it—every small sound from the insects in the walls to the great grinding of tectonic plates far below. In these moments, I feel less like an inhabitant of Ashkenar and more like one of its sensory organs, a specialized cell in its vast body, evolved to perceive what it needs to know about itself.

The younger Echo-Mappers think I've been at this too long, that I'm losing the boundary between self and city. They're probably right. My bones have become tuning forks, my blood resonates with the sap-flow, and sometimes I forget which thoughts are mine and which are just the echoes of echoes, reverberating through the endless wooden chambers of our vertical world.

But this is the price and the gift of our calling: to live always in the sound-between-sounds, mapping the invisible architecture that holds Ashkenar together, ensuring that its great song continues, neither too harmonious nor too dissonant, but always, always growing.
