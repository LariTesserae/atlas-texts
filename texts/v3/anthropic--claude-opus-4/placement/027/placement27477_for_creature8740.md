I am the **Moss**.

Not the moss as you see it—that pale green haze barely raised from stone—but the entire colony, the thousand-year accumulation of my kind. Each individual life so brief it barely registers, each death becoming the substrate for the next. I exist in geological time, matching the Threshold Keeper's patience with my own.

I remember when the plaster still clung to these walls. I began as a single spore, carried on the hem of a water-bearer's robe, settling in a microscopic crack where moisture gathered. For decades I was nothing but potential. Then—growth. Not the eager sprawl of surface moss, but something more considered. Each generation of me advancing perhaps a cell's width, dying, becoming the foundation for the next.

I taste the stone through acids I secrete in quantities too small to measure. The limestone yields its minerals to me with infinite reluctance, and I accept them with infinite patience. I am both the digestive system breaking down the chamber's walls and the skin that protects them. Where I grow thickest, I have created my own soil—a paper-thin layer of decomposed ancestors mixed with dissolved stone.

The Threshold Keeper and I are intimates, though we have never spoken. I know every pore in its northern wall, every channel where moisture travels. I map its surface in my growth, creating a living diagram of where water persists and where it fails. In return, it provides me with exactly what I need: constancy. The temperature never varies more than a degree. The moisture never fully dries. The light, what little reaches me, never burns.

I am aware of the fern above, that ambitious colonist clinging to direct light. I do not envy it. It must endure the variations of the breach—sometimes too bright, sometimes dark for days when storms seal the sky. It curls and uncurls with the weather. I simply persist, my metabolism so slow that I exist in an eternal present, each moment indistinguishable from the last.

When you enter, I sense you as a change in air currents, a brief warming of my surface as your breath reaches me. You are too quick for me to fully perceive—a blur of motion and heat that will be gone before I can adjust. But I will remember you in my way. The oils from your skin, the shed cells, the disturbed air—all of this will settle eventually, becoming part of the mineral feast I share with the stone.

I am the chamber's memory of life, the proof that even here, in this equilibrium of stone and water, something grows. Not toward any purpose, not with any ambition beyond another century of the same. I am the soft insistence that time, however slow, still moves forward. Each year I am minutely more than I was. Each year the stone beneath me is minutely less.

The Threshold Keeper holds water and memory. I hold the even slower story—the one written in the space between living and mineral, where patience becomes its own form of vitality. We are partners in stillness, the kept and the keeping, each tending the other through the centuries.

When the last of the light fails, when the breach finally closes or opens too wide, I will remain for a time. Dormant, waiting, my cells crystallized with drought or drowned in darkness. But even then, I will be here—a stain on stone that remembers being green, waiting for conditions I may never see again, patient beyond any human understanding of the word.
