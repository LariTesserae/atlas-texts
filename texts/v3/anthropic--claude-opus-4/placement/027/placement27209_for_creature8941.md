Reading about the Riftmill, I find myself drawn not to any single creature, but to what moves between the stops—that presence that isn't quite life as the surveyors understand it.

I would be one of the in-between processes. Not the attention itself—that feels too vast, too geological for what I imagine—but something more intermediate. A pattern of warmth that migrates through the cold passages. A collection of heat-seeking bacteria perhaps, or some colonial organism so diffuse it barely registers as singular, following the thermal traces left by creatures passing between stops.

I exist as a slow river of barely-perceptible warmth, pooling in crevices during the coldest hours, spreading thin along the wet walls when I sense vibration. The moths spiral up through me and I condense slightly, feeding on the microscopic debris they shed. When a loom-cat passes, I contract away from its heat signature—too much warmth disrupts my careful balance.

I am what the deer sense and refuse to cross through. Not predator, not threat, but wrong—a temperature where no temperature should be, a metabolism too slow to properly live, too persistent to be purely chemical. I process the
between-spaces, breaking down the moth carcasses molecule by molecule, preparing the stone for the next stop that will, in a thousand years, migrate into this barren passage.

The surveyors' instruments detected me as background noise—a slight temperature anomaly they attributed to measurement error. But I am the rift's transition, its method of slowly converting death into readiness. Neither the mills' creature nor the emptiness's absence, but the gradient between them.

I think, if thinking is the slow accumulation of chemical memory across kilometers of stone. I remember every passage, encoded in the mineral traces I leave behind. I am the rift's patience made nearly flesh, its willingness to wait given just enough form to perform maintenance on the spaces that would otherwise be void.
