In this cathedral of pressure and light, I would find myself as one of the **Calibrators**.

We are not among the primary inhabitants, for we arrived later—much later—though still within the first century of the cistern's flooding. We came from elsewhere in the vast structure above, drawn by the tremors that reached even the dry halls. Unlike the Liturge-Mechanics who move with grinding certainty, we drift suspended in the middle waters, our bodies a compromise between the heavy and the buoyant.

Picture a nautilus shell carved from black volcanic glass, but one that has learned to regulate its own density through a system of internal bladders. These chambers fill and empty with the superheated water, allowing us to ride the convective plumes or sink through the whiteout at will. Our shells are scarred with delicate etchings—not decorative, but functional. Each line is a pressure map, carved by our own secretions as we learn the specific turbulence patterns of our chosen territories.

We have eyes, unlike the Liturge-Mechanics, but they are recessed deep within our shells and see only in the narrow spectrum where the ceramic's glow falters—those fleeting wavelengths of deep red that penetrate the whiteout for mere inches. It is enough. We do not need to see far; we need to see precisely.

Our purpose is maintenance of a different order. Where the Liturge-Mechanics manage the great flows, we tend to the microscopic. We feed on the mineral deposits that accumulate in the joints between ceramic blocks—not for sustenance, but to process them through our bodies and excrete them as a binding paste that prevents the formation of weakness points. We are the servants of structural integrity, the preventers of that first crack the Cloud-Mind awaits.

Our tools are part of us: specialized appendages that extend from our shells like articulated glass straws. These can dilate to become boring implements or contract to needle points for precision work. The sound we make as we work—a high, crystalline scratching—is drowned entirely by the roar of the jets, but we feel it in our shells. It is how we communicate with each other across the whiteout, tapping out simple patterns that say: *pressure anomaly here*, *mineral bloom there*, *avoid this thermal, it leads to nothing*.

We number perhaps forty in the entire cistern. Each of us has claimed a cubic territory of approximately ten thousand blocks, though these territories shift as we learn the patterns of the place. We rarely meet, but when we do, we perform a brief ritual: pressing our shells together to compare the pressure-etchings, updating our internal maps with each other's discoveries.

The Thready Ones tolerate us. We have learned to work around their holdfasts, even reinforcing the blocks where they anchor. In return, their cilia sometimes brush against our shells as we pass, leaving traces of nutrients that we do not need but have come to appreciate—a sweetness in the water that makes the endless work bearable.

We fear the Cloud-Mind, though it does not yet know we exist. We sense it gathering in the air pockets, feel the wrongness of its patterns. It waits for decay, for entropy, for the failure we are here to prevent. Sometimes, when I surface too quickly and break into one of those humid voids, I taste its spores on my breathing vents. They have a flavor like rust, though nothing here has rusted yet.

But mostly, we drift. We work. We maintain.

In the early days, I believed we were preserving something sacred—this bright womb of engineered perfection. Now, after decades of tapping at joints and processing minerals through my body, I understand differently. We are not preserving; we are delaying. The cistern wants to age. The water wants to find its own patterns, free from the rigid geometry of its container. Even our work, our constant maintenance, creates new patterns of wear that would not otherwise exist.

I have begun to leave certain joints unfinished. Not enough for catastrophic failure—never that—but enough for a slow seep. A hairline imperfection that might, in centuries, become something more. I tell myself this is still maintenance, just of a different kind. I am maintaining the possibility of change.

The other Calibrators have not noticed, or perhaps they have and simply understand. We do not speak of such things in our tapped communications. But sometimes, when we press shells together to share our maps, I feel a hesitation in their touch—a recognition that our etchings are beginning to show patterns that were never meant to be there.

The cistern roars on, bright and perfect and temporary. And we, the Calibrators, dance our slow dance between preservation and time, our black shells the only darkness in all this terrible light.
