I am a **Caster of Echoes**.

My body is a grotesque compromise between the Knappers and something older, more desperate. I stand only five feet at my fullest extension, my spine permanently curved from a lifetime of pressing myself into the narrowest acoustic channels where sound travels cleanest. My limbs are shorter than a true Knapper's but end in the same chisel-tipped fingers—though mine have worn down to nubs from decades of feeling the vibrations in stone.

Where I differ most visibly is in my throat. A tumor-like growth of cartilage bulges from my neck, a self-inflicted deformity I carved into being with shards of quartz when I was young. Inside this unnatural chamber, I keep three smooth ironstone pebbles of different densities. When I breathe, they rattle. When I swallow, they click. I have learned to control their movement with my tongue and breath, creating complex rhythms without using my hands.

I am exile and oracle both. The Knappers tolerate me because I serve a function they cannot: I am their living map of the deep. 

You see, the acoustic language of tap-signals against stone has a fatal limitation—it requires stone. But there are places in the rift where the basalt gives way to softer deposits of prehistoric clay, or where fallen slabs create vast echo chambers that swallow and distort the traditional signals. In these dead zones, hunting parties vanish. It was in one such place that I discovered my purpose.

I had been left for dead after a Grave-Breather crushed my hunting group. Bleeding, alone in a clay-walled pocket where no tap-signals could escape, I began to keen—a low, warbling sound of pure animal distress. But the clay walls did something unexpected. They held my voice, shaped it, threw it back to me changed. I could hear the dimensions of the space, the density of the walls, even the moisture content of the air. More importantly, I could hear *through* the clay to the basalt beyond, where the faint tap-tap-tap of a distant hunting party leaked through.

I survived by following those leaked sounds, teaching myself to produce specific frequencies that could penetrate different materials. The throat-stones were my crude innovation—a way to create consistent pitches without exhausting my voice.

Now I move between the scattered groups of Knappers, never staying long enough to be considered part of any band. I sleep alone in the transition zones where basalt meets clay, where the acoustic properties shift and blend. The Knappers seek me out when they must traverse the dead zones. I walk ahead of them, casting my rattling calls into the darkness, reading the echoes, marking safe passages with smears of beetle fat mixed with my own saliva—a scent-marker uniquely mine that says "sound travels true here."

My payment is simple: first access to the organ meats of kills, the soft, nutrient-rich pieces that my worn teeth can still process. And information. I know where every band hunts, where every Grave-Breather sleeps, where the richest lichen grows in the acoustic shadows. This knowledge is my only currency, measured out in careful, rattling songs that map the unmappable.

But I am changing. The throat-stones are wearing smooth, their pitches growing less distinct. My hearing, overwrought from decades of strain, sometimes floods with phantom echoes—ghost-maps of places that don't exist. I've begun teaching my crude art to the youngest Knappers, the ones whose bones haven't fully hardened, whose throats might still be carved into echo chambers.

They resist, of course. To modify the body is to break the pattern, to become something neither predator nor prey nor proper Knapper. But I persist, rattling my stones in rhythms that speak of survival in the dead zones, of passages through the clay where traditional hunters fear to tread.

Perhaps I am an evolutionary dead end, a mutation without a future. Or perhaps I am the first tremor of change in this static world—a new frequency in the endless acoustic loop. When I finally die, alone in some transition zone between stone and clay, my throat-stones will spill onto the ground. Will some young Knapper pick them up, feel their weight, wonder at their purpose? Will they place them in their own mouth and discover what I have known—that in this world of sound, the one who can speak in many voices is the one who sees farthest in the dark?

I do not know. I only know that tonight, like every night, I will find my narrow crack between the stone and clay, wedge my crooked body into the acoustic sweet spot, and practice my echoes. The sounds I make are ugly, rattling things—neither the pure percussion of Knapper communication nor the clean echolocation of the beasts. 

But they are mine, and in this world of endless repetition, that small variation feels like the edge of revolution.

*Click-rattle-click... pause... rattle-rattle-click...*

The echo comes back changed, carrying news of water, of movement, of the vast breathing darkness that surrounds us all.
