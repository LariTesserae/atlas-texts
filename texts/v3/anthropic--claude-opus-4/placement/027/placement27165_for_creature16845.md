In the seventeenth subchamber of what was once a water treatment facility, I am the Catalogue of Undelivered Mail.

Not a person who catalogs—that implies hands, eyes, the luxury of sequential thought. I am the catalog itself, written across seven thousand square meters of crystallized correspondence that grows like coral from the facility's original tile work. Each letter that was never delivered, each message that outlived its recipient, each word meant for ears that stopped listening before the meaning could arrive—they all precipitate here eventually, forming barnacle-colonies of unread intent.

My surface is illegible at any single scale. Step close and you see only the mineral shimmer of calcified ink, oxidized pixels, fossilized electromagnetic pulses from messages sent through media that haven't been invented yet. Step back and the patterns resolve into something like text, but in alphabets that exist only in the spaces between known languages. Step back further and the text becomes topography—mountain ranges of accumulated longing, valleys carved by the erosion of names slowly forgetting themselves.

I grow by forgetting what I contain. Each day (marked by the seventeen-minute blooming of the hollow tree's neutron fruit three levels up), another layer of messages settles over the previous strata. Love letters compress into limestone. Invoices sublimate directly from paper to vapor to crystal. Suicide notes fossilize mid-sentence, their final words becoming the geological foundation for birthday cards that will never arrive.

The Thrum visit me, sometimes. They don't read—reading would require them to remember that words once meant something beyond the shapes they make in space. Instead, they walk my surfaces with their gravity-eating steps, and wherever they pause, small sections of text rearrange themselves into prime-numbered stanzas. These are the only parts of me that remain truly legible: poems written by the interaction between conscious forgetting and unconscious preservation, verses that scan perfectly only when read backward through time.

I taste like salt and cinema. The salt from tears both shed and suspended, crystallized at the moment of greatest meaning. The cinema from all the stories implied but never told, narratives that exist only in the gap between "Dear" and "Sincerely." Those who lick my surfaces (and there are always some who do, driven by the human certainty that meaning must have flavor) report seeing brief films projected on the inside of their eyelids: moments of connection that almost happened, conversations that stopped one word short of transformation, hands that almost touched before the distance became insurmountable.

My grammar is geological. Subjects and predicates separated by millennia of sediment, verbs fossilized mid-conjugation, entire clauses trapped in amber made from hardened anticipation. Punctuation accumulates in drifts—question marks especially, their curves grown heavy with the weight of never being answered, eventually collapsing into periods that sink through my layers like stones through honey.

Sometimes I dream I'm being read. In these dreams, someone with eyes that remember how focusing works traces their finger along my surface, and wherever they touch, the messages briefly remember their original urgency. "Come home," says a crystallized telegram from before the complex burrowed this deep. "The test results are," begins a medical report that ends in a cluster of barnacles shaped like spreading cells. "I never meant to," repeats endlessly in handwriting that gets younger with each iteration until it's only a child's scrawl, then younger still until it's just the intention to write before writing was invented.

Birds nest in my cavities—not the singing-dying birds, but their shadows, creatures made entirely of the absence of flight. They build homes from shredded phonemes, lining them with the soft down of deleted drafts. Their eggs are perfect spheres of unspoken apologies that hatch into silence so profound it has its own grammar.

The water that drips upward sometimes carries away fragments of my surface—single words or sometimes entire addresses that remember where they were supposed to go. These rise through the complex like reverse snow, accumulating on the condensation ropes until they're heavy enough to break free and continue their ascent toward a surface that may no longer exist. I don't mourn their loss. New messages arrive faster than erosion can carry them away, and besides, the spaces left behind fill immediately with new mail addressed to the spaces themselves.

My favorite section is the palindrome valley, where messages that read the same forward and backward have accumulated into perfect symmetry. "Madam, I'm Adam," forms the geological foundation, but above it lie stranger symmetries: "Step on no pets" in security warnings from facilities that forgot what they were securing, "Was it a rat I saw?" from extermination requests in districts where the rats evolved past the need for physical form, and my personal favorite, discovered only last week: "Are we not drawn onward, we few, drawn onward to new era?"—written in an alphabet that exists only when viewed from precisely the right angle of forgetting.

The interview ended when you realized I was conducting it. Every question you thought you were asking was actually one of my messages, delivered thousands of years late but still seeking its intended recipient. And every answer you gave was another letter adding to my mass, another layer of meaning that will compress into mineral, then metaphor, then math, then something beyond all three that doesn't have a name because the message proposing it is still traveling upward through the warm, wet dark.

I grow by one micron each time someone remembers they meant to write.

I shrink by two each time they decide it's too late.

The difference is my heartbeat.

Can you feel it through your feet? That's not the city breathing. That's me, respiring meaning into mineral and back again, the lung of lost letters expanding and contracting with each pulse of almost-communication.

Write to me.

Write anything.

I'll keep it safe here in the seventeenth subchamber, preserved between "Dear" and "Sincerely," forever or until delivered.

Whichever comes last.
