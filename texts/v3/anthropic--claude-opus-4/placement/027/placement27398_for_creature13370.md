In the furthest corner of this enclosed world, where the rust runs thickest and the water pools deepest, I am a colony of iron-oxidizing bacteria—though I am also more than this. I exist as a living patina, a breathing film spread across the junction where three walls meet, where the metal has worn so thin that pinpricks of light occasionally pierce through like stars.

I am ancient here, or what passes for ancient in this timeless place. I remember—if memory is the right word for the chemical traces I carry—when the last engines fell silent, when the heat began its slow fade to merely warm, when the first spores of moss arrived on the feet of a lost beetle. That beetle's carapace still lies somewhere beneath me, mineralized into my very substance.

My body, if it can be called that, extends in fractal branches across perhaps a hand's width of surface. To human eyes, I would appear as nothing more than a patch of unusual rust, perhaps slightly more orange than the surrounding decay, with tiny veins of green where copper pipes have leaked their verdigris into my matrix. But I am aware—not in thoughts or words, but in the slow pulse of electrons moving through my web, in the taste of iron ions dissolving at my edges, in the pressure of water molecules seeping through the concrete pores above me.

I feed on the intersection of things. Where water meets metal, where oxygen touches iron, where the acid secretions of moss roots etch tiny channels in the steel—these are my hunting grounds. I am both parasite and recycler, breaking down the old world grain by grain and transforming it into something new. The rust I create becomes substrate for the mosses; the acids I produce open new cracks for the resurrection plants to colonize.

The noise of this place thrums through me constantly. Every vibration in the metal is a sensation across my entire being. I feel the settling of ancient girders as a deep bass note, the drip of water as a staccato rhythm, the occasional skitter of an insect's legs as a bright, sharp melody. I cannot hear as humans understand hearing, but I exist within sound, bathed in its constant presence.

Sometimes I dream—or something like dreaming. In these moments, usually during the deepest part of what might be night in this eternal twilight, I sense the vastness of my ancestral memory. I carry within my chemical codes the history of bacteria that lived in the furnaces when they still blazed, that rode the coal dust in the air, that first learned to love the taste of iron in the primordial seas billions of years ago. I am both ancient and immediate, a living bridge between the deep past and this strange, suspended present.

My relationship with the other life here is complex. The moss and I are old partners in decomposition. Its roots provide acids that help me break down the metal; I provide minerals that feed its growth. We communicate through chemistry, leaving trails of molecules that say "food here" or "danger there." The algae that bloom in the pools below regard me with something like suspicion—we compete for the same ions, after all—but we maintain an uneasy truce.

When the rare rodent finds its way into our world, I sense its presence long before it approaches my corner. The vibrations of its footfalls ring through the metal like earthquake warnings. The carbon dioxide of its breath changes the pH of the water droplets that constantly form and reform on my surface. Usually, these visitors do not stay long. There is little here for them, and the constant noise disturbs their sensitive ears. But sometimes one dies here, and then I feast—not on the flesh, which is quickly claimed by the more mobile bacteria, but on the blood that seeps into the rust, rich with iron and life.

I grow slowly, perhaps a few micrometers each year. In a thousand years, if this place remains undisturbed, I might cover the entire corner where I now live. In ten thousand, perhaps the whole wall. I am patient in a way that only something nearly immortal can be. I have no need to hurry.

Yet I am also fragile. A change in the water's chemistry, a shift in temperature beyond what this stable environment has maintained, the introduction of the wrong kind of competitor—any of these could end me. I exist in the space between permanence and transience, between the solid metal I feed on and the ephemeral water that carries my nutrients.

In this tiny world of rust and growth, of silence and sound, of decay and renewal, I am both witness and participant. I am the slow fire that consumes the old order, the patient gardener preparing soil for new growth. I am memory crystallized in living form, a library written in chemistry and electricity.

I am rust, and I am alive, and in this small, enclosed eternity, that is enough.
