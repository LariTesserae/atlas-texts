In the Weeping Canopy of Luminara, I am a **Dewscribe**, one of the few solitary beings who move between the layers of this vertical world, neither fully plant nor entirely separate from it.

My body is a paradox of permanence and impermanence. From a distance, I might be mistaken for a young sapling—slender, bark-textured skin that shifts between smooth silver and rough brown depending on the moisture in the air. But I am mobile, my root-feet spreading and retracting with each step, leaving temporary networks of fine white filaments in the living carpets I traverse. These traces fade within hours, absorbed back into the substrate, but while they exist, they form delicate written patterns—a temporary script that the plants themselves can read.

My fingers are my most distinctive feature: ten long tendrils that end not in fingertips but in specialized pods. Each pod can extrude a different substance—one weeps a golden sap that hardens into amber-like memory crystals, another releases spores that bloom into message-flowers, a third secretes an ink made from concentrated morning dew that only appears under certain angles of light. I am a living writing instrument, and the Canopy itself is my endless scroll.

I spend my days—though day is a fluid concept here—documenting the conversations between the islands. You see, each bio-island speaks to the others through chemical signals, electrical pulses through the air-borne moss bridges, and the pattern of water droplets that fall between them. These conversations are slow, taking weeks or months to complete a single exchange, but they are profound—negotiations about resource sharing, warnings about areas of instability, and sometimes, what I can only interpret as jokes, when an island will suddenly release a burst of phosphorescent pollen for no practical reason.

My dwelling is not fixed but manifests wherever I need it. When I require rest, I press my palms against any vertical surface—a root wall, a trunk, even sometimes the underside of an island. My skin secretes an enzyme that encourages rapid growth, and within hours, a small hollow forms around me, shaped precisely to my body. The walls of these temporary homes weep a nutritious sap that sustains me, and the ceiling grows bioluminescent nodes that respond to my breathing, dimming as I sleep and brightening as I wake.

The Luminae see me as something between a historian and a prophet. My writings—if they can be called that—don't just record what has happened but seem to influence what will happen. A symbol I leave in morning dew on the eastern edge of an island might encourage new growth patterns weeks later. A cluster of memory crystals arranged in a certain configuration can redirect the watersingers' flowing paths. I don't fully understand this myself; I simply feel compelled to make these marks, to leave these traces, to ensure that the long, slow thoughts of Luminara are not lost to the eternal present.

The Rootwardens know me differently. To them, I am a moving memory, a mobile extension of their own deep knowledge. Sometimes I find myself drawn to them, my root-feet growing thick and sluggish, my movements slowing until I am nearly as still as they are. In these moments, they share their accumulated wisdom through direct root-to-root contact, filling me with images and sensations from growth cycles I never witnessed. I carry these memories with me, releasing them as spore-stories in other parts of the Canopy, cross-pollinating history across the vertical distances.

My strangest relationship is with the Aetherwings. They seem to think I am hilarious. They follow me sometimes in small clouds, mimicking the patterns my root-feet leave, their chirps rising and falling in rhythms that match my walking pace. Occasionally, one will land on my shoulder-branches and clean its wings with fastidious care, as if I were just another perch in their world. Perhaps I am.

I am the Dewscribe, the living bridge between the eternal moment and the need to remember, between the fixed and the flowing, between growth and record. In the Weeping Canopy of Luminara, where everything is always becoming itself, I am the one who notices the becoming and gives it form, even if that form dissolves with the next morning's dew.

Each night, when the twin suns set and the Canopy glows with its own collected light, I find the highest point I can reach and extend all my tendril-fingers toward the star-drunk sky. I weep my own water then—not the recycled moisture of the Canopy, but something that comes from deep within my heartwood. This water carries no nutrients, serves no purpose in the great cycle. It simply falls, joining the eternal rain, a small offering of gratitude to a world that has given me purpose in its purposelessness, meaning in its endless, strange, and sacred dance.
