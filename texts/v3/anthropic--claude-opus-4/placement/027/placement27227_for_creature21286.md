The seam has its own keeper.

In the morning, while the first insects tune their legs, she walks the long face of the limestone. Her feet know every depression, every rough patch where the tufa builds its patient architecture. She carries nothing but a shallow bowl ground from shell and a memory of yesterday's mineral bands. The rock changes slowly—so slowly that most would call it changeless—but she reads it the way others read weather. Here, the iron stain has crept a thumb's width. There, new chalk whispers where none was last moon. She dips her bowl where the clearest water beads and tastes first with her tongue-tip, then fully. Each seepage has its own vintage: this one sweet with deep limestone, that one edged with sulfur, another almost salt where the old sea remembers itself far underground.

She was born to this work, though born is too simple. Her mother walked this same line. Her mother's mother. Back and back until the stories dissolve into the sound of water on stone. They say her lineage can taste the difference between water that fell as rain ten years ago and water that has been traveling since before the first cypress knees broke surface. They say this not as marvel but as fact, the way they might say ibises know fish from shadow.

The others call her family the Listeners, though they rarely need to call. Everyone knows where to find them: at the seam, always at the seam, reading the ground's long letter to the sky. When the rains fail to come—and they fail, some years, with a stubbornness that makes even the patient people fret—someone will walk to the seam and stand quiet until she turns. They don't need to ask. She knows why they've come. She'll take their wrist in her cool fingers, still damp from the rock, and press it to a particular band. "Here," she'll say. "Feel how cold. The river hasn't forgotten. Give it six days. Maybe seven."

She's never wrong about the seven.

Her house isn't a house. It's more an understanding between limestone and thatch, a place where she can sleep within hearing of the seam's whispers. Three walls and a gesture toward a roof. A floor of fitted calcrete worn smooth by generations of careful feet. One corner holds bowls, each marked with a different mineral stain—red, green, white, gray-black—to match the bands they taste. Another corner holds nothing but a reed mat and a wrap woven from sedge so fine it feels like water when she pulls it over her shoulders on the rare cool nights.

She owns little because the seam owns everything she needs. A grinding stone for making paste from tufa. A set of picks carved from dense heartwood, each worn to fit a different crevice. Shells for scraping, shells for catching, shells for listening—yes, listening. Hold the right shell to the weeping rock and you can hear the aquifer's pulse, the way a heart sounds if the heart is the size of the whole county and beats once a season.

The children think she's half stone herself. They've seen her stand motionless for hours, palm flat against the limestone, eyes closed, breathing so slowly she might be another mineral deposit. They dare each other to creep close, to see if she's become rock entirely. She never has, but she lets them believe she might. When she finally moves—always just as they lean close enough to see the water droplets caught in the fine hairs of her arm—they shriek and scatter like minnows. She smiles. It's good for children to think the world holds transformations.

She knows each voice the seam makes. The steady weep of the main face. The stuttering drip where limestone gives way to clay. The musical plink where water finds a hollow just right for singing. In storms, when the ground swells with sudden gift, new voices appear—gurgles, hisses, once a sound like laughter that lasted three days and stopped as mysteriously as it began. She documents these in her mind, not in words, but in the way her body remembers: this spot floods first, that one stays sweet longest, here the minerals concentrate when the dry comes.

Others bring her things in trade, though she never asks. Smoked fish for the knowledge of where the next good seepage will break through. Bitter tea for advice on which band to wash a fever in. Once, memorably, a live heron chick fallen from its nest, in exchange for the secret of where the coldest water rises. She raised the chick on minnows and grubs until it stood tall enough to insult her from her own roof. It still visits, grown now, and drinks from her bowl with the delicacy of a relative who remembers being helpless.

The seam speaks most clearly at dawn and dusk, when the temperature shift makes the rock sweat fresh secrets. These are her working hours. In the heat of midday, she retreats—not to her house, but to the pools. Hidden spots where the limestone dips and holds the clearest water, places she's learned from a lifetime of following the faint mineral trails. She'll float there, only her face above surface, and feel the conversation between stone and water happening all around her, through her, as if she's just another medium for their old argument.

She has names for different waters. The Tooth, sharp with lime. Sweet Shoulder, where the rock curves and gentles whatever passes over it. The Vein, iron-rich and medicinal. Fool's Clear, which looks pure but carries a mineral load that will cramp your gut if you're not careful. She's tasted them all, learned their moods across seasons, knows which will betray you in a dry year and which stay faithful to the last drop.

In the evening, when she's done walking the line and tasting the day's offerings, she sits where the main seam weeps strongest. Sometimes others join her—always at a respectful distance, always quiet. They don't come for her words. They come to watch how she reads the rock, the way her fingers trace the bands like text, the way she tilts her head when listening to changes too subtle for untrained ears. They're learning, slowly, the ones who care to. She doesn't teach in the usual way. She just continues her work and lets them observe, the way the rock lets water observe its chambers before choosing which way to flow.

She dreams of rivers that haven't existed for a thousand years. Wide, lazy things that carved the crescents everyone walks on now. In her dreams, she follows them backward, underground, to the place where all water is one water, undifferentiated and ancient. She wakes with the taste of it in her mouth—pure potential, unmarked by minerals or memory. It's the only water she can't name, and she thinks perhaps that's what the seam is always trying to tell her: that beneath all the varieties, the veins and voices, there is one source, patient as stone, generous as time.

When the others see her at dawn, already walking the line with her bowl and her inheritance of attention, they feel both comfort and mystery. Comfort because she is constant as the seam itself. Mystery because who can say what passes between a woman and stone in those long silences? What contract they've made, what promises?

She knows she won't do this forever. Already there's a child—her sister's daughter—who follows her some mornings, skinny legs trying to match her stride. The girl has the taste, that rare ability to distinguish waters that others would call identical. More importantly, she has the patience. She can stand still as a heron, waiting for the rock to confide in her.

One morning, not today but not too many seasons from now, the keeper will press the child's hand to the coldest band, the one that speaks of rivers so deep they've never known sun. The child will gasp at the chill, then close her eyes and listen with her whole palm, the way one listens to a story too important for words. And the keeper will step back, becoming what she's always been: not the owner of the seam's secrets, but their temporary custodian, one in a line stretching back to when the first people put their lips to the limestone and tasted home.

The seam doesn't care who listens, only that someone does. The water will weep from stone long after the last house returns to mud, long after the last memory of sweet seepage and mineral bands. But for now, while there are those who choose to live by the old rules, someone will wake each dawn and walk the line, bowl in hand, reading the earth's mail to the sky. Someone will know which voice means patience and which means hurry. Someone will keep the catalogue of tastes and temperatures, will stand between the people and the stone and translate, patiently, the long story of how love moves through limestone: slowly, constantly, cold as time and sweet as survival.

She is that someone now. Tomorrow, she will be again. The seam weeps and she listens. That is all. That is everything.
