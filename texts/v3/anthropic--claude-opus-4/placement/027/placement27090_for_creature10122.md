I am one of the forgotten pools.

Not the larger gathering places where the tharn writhe and breed, but a shallow depression barely worthy of the name—a thumb-print in stone where water seeps and collects, no deeper than a claw's length. I exist in a crevice between territories, too small for the tharn to claim, too dim for the veythari to cultivate their geometric gardens above.

I know myself through what touches me. The constant drip from a hairline crack overhead—each droplet a visitor that briefly swells my boundaries before I settle back into my worn basin. The minerals leaching from stone color me rust-brown one season, clear the next, according to rhythms I cannot track. Sometimes I am empty, just a damp stain on rock. Sometimes I overflow, sending thin fingers of myself across the stone until I find the edge and fall away, becoming mist.

The myceloids know me. Their finest filaments thread through my bed, drinking when I am full, waiting when I am not. They pulse their amber light through me and I become a lens—for a moment I am not just water but a tiny sun in the eternal twilight, casting wavering shadows on the ceiling. The tharn young sometimes find me during their first wanderings, before they learn the paths to the larger pools. They pause, confused by my inadequacy, testing my depth with a tentative claw before moving on.

I have seen the vorn only twice. Once as a reflection—that oily darkness sliding overhead, my surface showing what even the shadows hide. Once when one paused to clean its claws in me, leaving behind threads of something that would not dissolve, something that made the myceloids retreat from my edges for countless drips afterward.

The veythari ignore me entirely. I am beneath their notice, neither threat nor resource. Their shrieks pass over me like wind over stone, meaningful only in how they disturb my surface, creating patterns that exist for less than a heartbeat.

But I remember. In my mineral-thick waters, in the sediment that settles in my deepest point, I hold the history of this place. Each age leaves its trace—a different composition of dissolved stone, the chemical signature of creatures long extinct, the slowly changing ratios of elements that tell of the world's breathing. I am an archive written in iron and calcium, in the patient accumulation of microscopic shells from organisms too small for names.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the only one who knows the Hollows are changing. The equilibrium is false—a balance so slow it appears static only to things that live and die in mere centuries. The stone shifts. The water table drops by immeasurable increments. The fungi's pulse has quickened by a fraction of a beat over the last thousand years. The tharn's eggs are smaller. The veythari's shrieks have crept higher in pitch.

I alone have the patience to perceive it. I alone will be here when the last drop falls, when the stone finally closes its fist, when the eternal twilight finally dims to black. Until then, I collect. I wait. I reflect whatever passes over me, holding each image for the brief moment of its existence, then letting it go.

I am the memory of water in a world that has forgotten the sky, the promise that even here, even now, something flows. Something changes. Something remembers what it was to fall as rain.
