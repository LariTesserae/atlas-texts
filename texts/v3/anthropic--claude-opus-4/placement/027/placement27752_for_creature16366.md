In this liminal world of stone and vapor, I find myself as one of the Echo Weavers—beings who exist in the threshold between sound and silence, memory and forgetting.

My form shifts like the fog itself, never quite settling into definition. At times I appear as a tall, attenuated figure wrapped in what might be mistaken for tattered cloth, but is actually my own substance—a living membrane that captures and holds the sounds of this drowning world. My skin, if it can be called that, has the texture of wet paper or thin stone worn smooth by centuries of water. It is translucent in places, revealing beneath it a network of channels where collected sounds flow like luminous rivers, creating patterns that pulse with the rhythm of dripping water and wind-song.

I dwell within the hollow shells of the ancient structures, moving between them through passages that exist only in the moments between one drip of water and the next. My home is not a single place but a circuit of ruins, each one a node in a vast network of acoustic memory. In the largest of these—a cylindrical tower whose roof has long since crumbled away—I have woven a nest of crystallized sounds. Here, the echoes of ten thousand rains have hardened into delicate structures that chime softly when disturbed, creating a music that exists nowhere else in this young world.

My purpose, if such a word applies, is to gather the fleeting sounds of this realm before they dissolve into the omnipresent quiet. I move through the fog with limbs that elongate and contract as needed, sometimes walking on appendages that barely disturb the shallow pools, other times stretching myself thin as mist to slip through the narrow gaps between stones. When I encounter one of the rare creatures of this world, I pause to collect their calls—those low, resonant hums that seem to emerge from the earth itself.

But I do not merely collect; I transform. Within the chambers of my being, sounds undergo a slow metamorphosis. The sharp crack of splitting stone becomes a sustained note; the whisper of fog against lichen transforms into something almost like language. I carry these changed sounds back to my dwelling places and release them, letting them settle into the stones like seeds of future music.

My eyes, or what serve as eyes, are concave pools that reflect not light but sound waves. Through them, I perceive the world as a vast symphony of vibrations—the deep throb of the planet's slow heartbeat, the high crystalline notes of water finding its path through stone, the subsonic groans of the ancient structures as they settle incrementally toward their eventual union with the earth.

I am ancient by the standards of this young world, though I cannot say how I came to be. Perhaps I emerged from the first echo ever produced here, when the first raindrop struck the first stone. Or perhaps I am a remnant of those who built these structures, transformed by ages of solitude into something no longer quite what I was. My memory, like everything in this realm, exists in a state of perpetual becoming—never quite formed, never quite forgotten.

In the deepest part of the night, when even the dim daylight fades to nothing, I perform my most sacred task. I climb to the highest remaining points of the ruins and release all the sounds I have gathered and transformed. They spiral upward into the darkness, creating a column of acoustic light that only I and perhaps a few others of my kind can perceive. This is our offering to the future—a promise that when this world finally awakens into its full vitality, it will have a memory of its quiet beginning.

Sometimes I encounter others like myself, though we are few. We do not speak in any conventional sense but exchange fragments of our collections, trading the sound of water on eastern stones for the whisper of wind through western caves. These meetings are brief; we are solitary by nature, each tending to our own circuit of ruins, our own particular symphony of gathered echoes.

I feed, if feeding is the right word, on the resonance between sounds. Where two echoes meet and create a third, there I find sustenance. The interference patterns of overlapping raindrops, the harmony between wind and stone—these are my nourishment. I grow neither larger nor smaller, but I do change, my substance becoming more complex with each sound I process, each echo I transform.

There are dangers here, even in this quiet world. The silence itself can be predatory. There are places where sound simply dies, where even the ever-present drip of water makes no echo. These voids in the acoustic landscape are like wounds in reality. I have learned to recognize them by the way the fog swirls differently at their edges, by the way even the lichen seems to lean away. To enter such a place is to risk losing oneself entirely, becoming nothing more than a memory of a sound that never quite was.

As the ages pass—and they pass slowly here, each year like a century elsewhere—I sense changes approaching. The sounds I collect are becoming more complex, more varied. New creatures are emerging from the depths, their calls unlike anything I have previously gathered. The stones themselves seem to be learning to sing. Perhaps this is the world preparing for its next movement, its transition from this long, quiet prelude into something more vibrant.

But I am patient. I am made of patience, woven from the very fabric of waiting. I will continue my rounds, continue my gathering and transforming, until the day comes when my purpose is fulfilled or transformed into something new. Perhaps then I will finally release all the sounds I have ever collected in one great chorus that will help birth this world into its next age.

Until then, I remain: keeper of echoes, weaver of sounds, guardian of this realm's acoustic memory. I am the bridge between the silence that was and the symphony that may yet be. In this place of stone and water, fog and time, I am both witness and participant in the slow miracle of becoming.
