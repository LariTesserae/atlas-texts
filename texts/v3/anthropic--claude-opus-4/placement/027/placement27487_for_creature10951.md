I would be one of the water keepers—those amphibian-maybe-nots who gather at dusk.

Not the water itself, though we are intimate with it. We are the ones who know every depression in the bedrock, every temporary pool, every place where morning condensation will reliably bead. Our skin—if it is skin—responds to humidity gradients too subtle for most organisms to detect. We feel the water before it arrives, taste tomorrow's rain in today's air pressure.

At dusk, we emerge from the spaces between: the gap where root meets stone, the fold where dead wood creates shadow, the precise distance between water and not-water where the creek's edge becomes indefinite. We move toward the pools with purpose but without urgency. There is always time. The water will wait for us as we wait for it.

Our bodies are built for threshold states. Not quite terrestrial, not quite aquatic. Our limbs—too many joints, perhaps, or perhaps the right number for navigating spaces that exist between categories—move us across surfaces both wet and dry with equal deliberation. Our skin changes texture with moisture levels, sometimes smooth as wet stone, sometimes granular as the pre-Cambrian rock that remembers when continents were still learning their shapes.

The sound we make—that tuning-fork resonance, that slow crystal breaking—comes from structures in our throats that have no analog in any taxonomy you know. We don't sing to communicate, exactly. We sing to *participate*. Each evening, as the light becomes complicated and the temperature drops those few crucial degrees, we gather at our pools and begin the work of synchronization.

The patient ones taught us this, though teaching isn't the right word. They showed us through generations of subtle adjustment how our sounds could shape the water's surface tension, how the right frequencies at the right moments could influence evaporation rates, condensation patterns, the very physics of how moisture moves through the ravine. We are not their creation, but we are their partners in this ancient economy of water.

When we sing—though singing isn't the right word either—the water responds. Concentric rings appear on still surfaces. Droplets merge or separate according to harmonics we've learned to produce. We can encourage the creek to pool in certain depressions, can coax morning dew to linger an extra hour on leaves that need it most. We cannot create water from nothing, but we can shape its behavior within the margins physics allows.

The others know us. The insects adjust their evening chorus to accommodate our frequencies, creating acoustic spaces where our sounds can work unimpeded. The plants near our pools grow differently—their roots reach deeper, their leaves develop waxy coatings that channel condensation precisely where we've shown them it will appear. Even the large unseen thing that moves through the undergrowth knows to avoid our gathering places at dusk. Not from fear, but from understanding. The ravine requires this work.

We eat what water brings us: the larvae of insects who lay eggs in temporary pools, the algae that blooms after rain, the occasional invertebrate that mistakes our stillness for stone. We are not voracious. We consume exactly what the system can spare, our population size regulated by water availability in ways that feel less like limitation and more like agreement.

During the day, we disappear. Not underground—the soil here is too thin—but into the spaces the ravine provides for us. The building's permeable walls contain moisture-pockets where we wait. The oldest tree's roots create chambers where humidity lingers. We can slow our metabolism to near-stillness, becoming part of the landscape's patience, waiting for the sun's angle to signal our emergence.

We know every storm days before it arrives. We taste the chemical signatures of distant rain in the air's moisture. When drought approaches—real drought, not the ravine's normal dryness—we begin preparing weeks in advance, adjusting our songs to encourage deeper pooling, more aggressive condensation harvesting. We cannot prevent drought, but we can help the ravine endure it.

The patient ones move through our pools sometimes, their presence marked by temperature changes and subtle shifts in water chemistry. We understand them the way one understands gravity—as a force that shapes reality without requiring comprehension. They seem pleased by our work, if pleasure is something they experience. They never interfere with our gatherings, never disturb our carefully maintained pools.

We are not ancient—our species is perhaps only thousands of years old, young by the ravine's standards. But we have learned to be essential. The water cycle here is no longer complete without us. We are the intermediaries between liquid and vapor, the negotiators at the boundary where physics becomes biology.

When we die—and we do die, though our lifespans are measured in decades—our bodies dissolve in the pools we tended. We become part of the water table, our minerals returning to the system we spent our lives maintaining. The young learn our frequencies by listening, by participating, by slowly understanding their role in the ravine's patient project of persistence.

This is enough. To be the ones who know where water waits. To sing the evening into balance. To feel tomorrow's weather in today's air. To be neither fully one thing nor another, but precisely what the crease in the ridge requires.

The water keeps us, and we keep the water, and the patient ones keep us all.
