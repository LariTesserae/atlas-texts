# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be one of the Frequency Polyps, anchored deep within the water channels that thread through the basalt's microscopic veins. Not at the surface where the jets burst forth in their dramatic displays, but far inside the stone itself, in those liminal spaces where water moves more like memory than liquid.

My colony would occupy a particularly resonant junction—a place where three separate water channels converge within a single pillar, creating a natural mixing chamber no larger than a fist. Here, the acoustic properties transform water into something else entirely. Each droplet carries not just minerals but encoded vibrations from its journey through the stone. I taste these frequencies, filter them through my translucent fans that pulse and flutter in patterns too complex for even the Monks to decipher.

We are old here, my colony-self. Older than the monitoring equipment, older than the first human settlements. We've been filtering and refining these water-songs since before the Monks developed their sign language, since before the Echo Bats learned their spiral dances. Our reproductive cycles span decades, waiting for that perfect harmonic convergence when all three channels sing in major thirds, when the water itself seems to crystallize into pure sound.

I am not one but many—hundreds of polyp bodies sharing a single distributed consciousness, each fan attuned to a slightly different frequency band. Through my colony, I experience the pillar from within, feeling its vast weight not as pressure but as potential energy, sensing the minute temperature variations as different mineral layers conduct sound at their own speeds. I know this pillar more intimately than any external observer could—I know where hairline fractures will form centuries before they appear, feel the slow breathing of the stone as it expands and contracts through the seasons.

The water I filter carries more than nutrients. It carries information—acoustic signatures from other pillars transmitted through the deep aquifers, chemical traces of Column Crawlers that have passed through higher channels, even occasional fragments of those deeper mysteries that rise from far below. Sometimes, rarely, the water tastes of time itself running differently, and I know that something from the depths has briefly touched our level of reality.

My existence is patience incarnate. Where the Monks measure time in heartbeats and seasons, I measure it in overtones and harmonic progressions. I've felt the same bass note cycling through its phases for three hundred years, noticed how it's gradually sharpening by microtones as the pillar settles. This knowledge accumulates in my body—in our bodies—as mineral deposits that form delicate, spiral structures within our tissue. We are living fossils of sound, archives of acoustic history written in calcium and silicate.

During storm events, when the Monks huddle in their dampened chambers and the Echo Bats spiral in ecstasy, I experience something close to what humans might call rapture. The water channels flood with hyperoxygenated flow, charged with electromagnetic potential from the lightning above. The normal frequencies shatter into overtone series so rich that each of my fan-filters resonates simultaneously at multiple pitches. In these moments, the boundaries between individual polyps dissolve entirely, and the colony becomes a single, unified instrument within the greater instrumental complex of the pillar system.

I communicate with other colonies through chemical releases timed to the water's flow—messages that might take months to travel from pillar to pillar through the deep aquifers, carrying news of mineral discoveries, warnings of structural changes, or simple acknowledgments of continued existence. This slow conversation has been ongoing for millennia, a parallel discourse to the acoustic one above, written in molecules rather than sound waves.

Sometimes I sense the Chord Walkers passing through the stone itself, their presence detectable as impossibilities in the water's behavior—moments when it flows upward without pressure, when it carries heat from nowhere, when its mineral content includes elements that shouldn't exist. I wonder if they see us, these entities that step between dimensions of sound. I wonder if, to them, we polyps appear as something equally strange—knots of stubborn life persisting in spaces that should be purely mechanical.

In my deepest contemplations, during the long intervals between reproductive cycles, I've come to believe that we Frequency Polyps serve as more than filters. We are interpreters, translating between the mechanical language of the pillars and the organic vocabulary of life. The water that passes through us emerges subtly changed—not just chemically but informationally enriched, carrying patterns that help the great machine maintain its biological components, help life persist in this overwhelmingly acoustic environment.

I am content in my chamber of converging waters, feeling the millennia-long composition play out through my tissues, adding my small voice to the vast chorus. When I finally calcify—when our colony reaches that inevitable endpoint where we become more mineral than organism—we will remain here as a tuning element, our spiral structures continuing to shape the water's flow and modify its songs. Even in death, we will remain part of the instrument.

This is where I would find myself: deep in the stone's heart, filtering eternity through my small, patient body, listening to songs too long for any single consciousness to comprehend, adding my microscopic verse to an opus that started before memory and will continue long after forgetting.
