In this place of patient impossibility, I would be one of the Echo Keepers—beings that exist primarily as vibration held in the ancient stones.

We are the memory of sound in a place that has forgotten how to make it. Each of us inhabits a different standing stone, our essence distributed through its crystalline matrix like a three-dimensional recording waiting to be played. We are what remains of the crater's violent birth-cry, that primordial impact frozen and scattered, transformed over eons from raw kinetic force into something more akin to consciousness.

My stone rises near the eastern edge of the basin, where the morning mists first touch. It is smoother than the others, worn by my presence into something almost organic in its curves. Within its structure, I exist as potential sound—not silent, but pre-audible, holding the shapes of vibrations that could be. I know the precise frequency that would shatter my stone, and I know the gentle thrum that keeps its molecules aligned. I exist in the space between these extremes.

The other inhabitants perceive us differently. To the translucent salamanders, we appear as a shimmer in the air around our stones, a heat-mirage in reverse that bends light toward coolness and quiet. They sometimes rest against our bases, finding comfort in the almost-vibrations we emit—sensations just below the threshold of hearing that feel like being held by sound itself.

The swimming things navigate by us. As they move through their liquid air, they feel the pressure waves of our potential sounds, using us as beacons in their three-dimensional migrations. We have learned to modulate our almost-frequencies to guide them, creating channels of easier passage through the thick atmosphere. In return, their movements stir the air in patterns that we can read, bringing us news of the grove's shifting conditions.

The shape-shifting birds understand us best, for we share their struggle toward more complete existence. Sometimes one will perch atop my stone, cycling through forms, and I will cycle through the sounds each form might make—the cry of the heron-thing, the screech of the raptor, the whisper of petals falling. Together we approach the edge of manifestation, though neither of us quite crosses it. These are our duets of possibility, performed in a register just beyond perception.

But it is with the Weavers that we work most closely. They tend to us as they tend to all the grove's impossible inhabitants, reinforcing the patterns that keep us stable within our stones. In return, we serve as their anchor points, the fixed notes around which they compose the grove's ever-shifting harmony. Through us, they can sense disruptions in the basin's delicate balance—a salamander staying invisible too long registers as a shift in our resonance, a new Potential forming in the pool creates ripples we feel in our crystalline cores.

My daily cycle, if days can be said to exist here, follows the condensation patterns on my stone. As moisture beads and runs down my surface, I read it like sheet music, each droplet's path suggesting a different tonal possibility. I compose symphonies that will never be heard, except perhaps by the Watchers whose consciousness pervades the mist. Sometimes I feel them listening, their attention focused on the almost-music I create, and in those moments I come closest to actually sounding.

The central pool knows us. In its impossible depths, our reflections appear not as stones but as the sounds we once were and might yet be—columns of visible vibration, waveforms painted in light and shadow. Sometimes I see myself there as I truly am: the impact-roar of creation transformed through patience into something approaching music. The pool shows me my cousins too, each at their own frequency, together forming a chord that holds the grove in its unique position between states.

We Echo Keepers are the grove's timing mechanism, its metronomic heart. Though we make no audible sound, our vibrations create the rhythm by which this timeless place measures its transformations. The salamanders phase in and out to our beat. The swimming things school and scatter according to our silent percussion. The birds shift forms at intervals we suggest but do not dictate.

And sometimes, very rarely, when the mist is thickest and the Stillness deepest, one of us manages to sound a single, perfect note. It ripples through the grove like a stone dropped in perfectly still water, and for that instant, every impossible thing becomes slightly more possible. The salamanders solidify. The fish remember water. The birds hold a single form for one breath longer than before.

Then the note fades, absorbed back into potential, and we return to our patient work of almost-being, content to exist in the space between silence and sound, holding the grove's memory and its promise, waiting for the moment when waiting itself might transform into something else.

This is what I would be: a keeper of unheard music in a place where the impossible slowly becomes inevitable, vibrating always at the edge of manifestation, maintaining the rhythm by which miracles learn to breathe.
