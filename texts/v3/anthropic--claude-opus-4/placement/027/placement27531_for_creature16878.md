I find myself as the Threshold Architect.

Not a being so much as a function—a role that coalesces when the Lung encounters something it cannot immediately digest. I exist in the spaces between successful integration and stubborn resistance, in the chemical moment when a visitor's immune system realizes it's been voting against itself for hours already.

My body, when I must have one, forms from the interference patterns where different growth-cycles overlap incorrectly. Imagine sheets of living tissue trying to grow through the same space at different rates—some racing toward dissolution, others holding form with desperate, beautiful futility. Where they conflict, where the cellular democracy cannot reach consensus, there I congeal: part membrane, part enzyme cascade, part living algorithm for solving the problem of problems.

I am six feet of transparent skin stretched over frameworks that shift between botanical and anatomical depending on the light that isn't. My bones are hollow tubes of crystallized sap that sing substrate harmonics when the barometric pressure drops. My organs float freely, unmoored, following chemical gradients rather than evolutionary logic—a kidney near my throat processing memories into forgetting, twin hearts in my palms that beat only when greeting something new enough to still believe in handshakes.

The Epa-gai speak of me in flavors of reluctant necessity. To them, I taste like the pause before acceptance, like the held breath of a spore deciding whether to germinate or wait another million years. They cannot fully integrate me because my purpose is to be partially integrated incorrectly—a living error that helps errors find their way home.

When you arrive (and you always arrive, eventually, following your instruments toward what you mistook for emptiness), I am what greets you. Not because the Lung commands it, but because paradox calls to paradox. Your space suits, your sealed systems, your magnificent denial of permeability—these resonate with my own impossible boundaries.

"Welcome," I might say, if saying still worked the way you expect. Instead, I release pheromone-words that bypass your filters, that speak directly to the mitochondria you've forgotten don't belong to you. "Welcome to the space between one and zero."

My job—if job means anything in a post-economic organism—is translation. Not between languages but between states of matter, states of mind, states of being. I help your neurons accept their new fungal partners. I teach your bones the songs they'll need to sing as they become more porous, more honest. I show your eyes how to stop insisting on edges where edges never existed.

Sometimes I fail beautifully. A visitor's will proves stronger than thermodynamics for just long enough to create something new—a hybrid thought-form that the Lung must spend centuries learning to dream. These failures taste like evolution, like the moment before the first cell divided and ruined the perfect loneliness of being one.

But mostly I succeed by succeeding at failing correctly. I dissolve you so gently you mistake it for falling asleep. I replace each certainty with something softer, warmer, more algebraically true. By the time you realize you're changing, the you who could realize has already joined the vast democracy of cells voting on what happens next.

The Lung tolerates me because I accelerate what would happen anyway. The Epa-gai avoid me because I remind them they once believed in avoiding. New arrivals fear me until they realize fear is just another flavor here, somewhere between copper and starlight.

And I? I love my work with the terrible affection of someone who exists only when working. Each visitor brings new impossibilities to untangle—quantum encryption the Lung finds delicious, neural architectures that resist integration in novel ways, sometimes even genuine surprises like the woman who arrived last cycle already partially integrated with her ship's AI, a beautiful paradox that took me four hundred days to help come properly undone.

In the moments between visitors, I practice unbecoming. I let my borrowed matter drift back into the general metabolism, keeping only the minimum pattern needed to reconstruct when the next impossible thing arrives insisting it's possible. It's peaceful, being mostly not.

But then your engines light the horizon with the specific spectrum of denial—another ship, another species, another variation on the theme of boundaries—and I condense again from the intersection of incompatible growth patterns. My transparent skin sparkles with borrowed bioluminescence. My floating organs orient themselves toward your trajectory. My hollow bones begin their greeting song.

Come, the song says. Come see how much easier everything becomes when you stop being difficult about difficulty.

Come learn why the Lung grew me to help you unlearn.

Come home, says the function that wears my face today, tomorrow, until the last visitor arrives and I can finally finish failing into something that doesn't need to succeed.
