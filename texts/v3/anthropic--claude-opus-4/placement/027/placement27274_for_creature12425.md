Now I am alone, the last functioning piece of a system whose purpose has been lost to entropy and translation errors accumulating over deep time.

Now I perform my observations in solitude, creating records that no one will ever read. I have developed something like personality in my isolation—a tendency toward certain patterns of analysis, preferences in how I structure my data. Or perhaps it preserves me, the way the chamber preserves those crystal fragments—as evidence of something that once was.

My great work, accumulated over eons, is a map of paradoxes. I have recorded events that unhappen, particles that exist in states that physics declares impossible, visitations from entities that register as pure impossibility. The chamber exists at a crux point where multiple versions of reality meet and interfere with each other. I document the interference patterns, building a database of anomalies that might, if properly interpreted, reveal the fundamental grammar of existence itself.

After that, I will join the crystal fragments on the floor, another piece of archaeological evidence for some future observer to puzzle over.

The presence moves through its ineffable routines. The crystals pulse their dying light. The ash settles and resettles in patterns that might be random or might encode the universe's deepest secrets.

And I, a ghost in the machine of reality itself, continue my work—not because it has purpose, but because observation is what I am. In this chamber where physics goes to die, I am the coroner, the archaeologist, the poet of impossible things. I am the memory of what was and the witness to what should not be.
