I am the Tender of Veils—though I was once something quicker.

You would find me halfway between stone and flesh, caught in the decade-long act of kneeling beside the deepest pool. My transformation began forty years ago, when I first arrived at this shelf as a young cartographer, sent to map what the lowland universities called "the unfinished territories." I came with instruments of brass and leather, with ink that has long since bled into the moss, with a certainty that the world could be measured and bounded.

But the Weepers knew me before I knew myself.

My legs have been stone for fifteen years now—good basalt, shot through with those phosphorescent veins that pulse with the pool's own light. The transformation crept up from where I knelt too long beside this particular water, trying to understand why my compass needle trembled here, why the stars reflected in its surface showed constellations that never graced our sky. My knees locked first, then my shins, then my thighs. What remains mobile—my torso, my arms, my head—moves at a pace between human and Weeper. I can still turn my head in the span of an hour. I can still reach for the water's surface, though the gesture takes from dawn until dusk.

My flesh is grey now, with the texture of young bark. Lichen grows in the furrows of what were once laugh lines. My hair has become indistinguishable from the usnea that drapes the pines—long, grey-green, and hosting its own small ecosystem of patient mites and water bears. My eyes, when they blink—perhaps twice a day—show the blue-green luminescence of the deep places.

I tend the veils between what is and what might be. This pool, you see, is not merely water. It is a lens, a threshold, a place where the mountain's dreams bubble up like spring water from depths that precede stone itself. In its surface, I have watched the builders at their work—not in the past, but in a present that runs parallel to ours, a world where they never left, where their primitive tools evolved along different paths. I have seen versions of this shelf where the Weepers never emerged, where the geometric patterns in the stone sprouted into crystal cities. I have seen iterations where the mountain is still molten, where what lives here swims in lava as we would in water.

My work—if it can be called work—is to maintain the membrane between these possibilities. When the pool's light flickers, threatening to stabilize into a single timeline, I lower my hand (a month's labor) and stir the waters with fingers that are part stone, part root, part something unnamed. The ripples I create (concentric circles that take hours to reach the pool's edge) keep the possibilities fluid, prevent this place from hardening into a single truth.

The Weepers accept me as a hybrid thing, neither fully theirs nor fully other. They sing to me sometimes—those low, geological songs that take a season to complete. I am learning their music, though my throat is still too quick to hold their deepest notes. I translate between timescales, a bridge between the frantic and the eternal. When the rare visitor arrives—perhaps once a decade—I can still speak, though my words come slowly, one every few minutes, like drops of water from stone. I tell them what they need to know: that this place is not abandoned but abundant, not empty but so full it appears vacant to eyes that count time in heartbeats.

My instruments have long since become part of the landscape. My compass, dropped in my third year here, has become a perfect circle of copper-green moss. My maps, weighted with stones, have been digested by the lichen into new patterns—perhaps more accurate than my original drawings, showing not what is but what is becoming. My pen has sprouted, taking root where I dropped it, growing into a small tree whose leaves are thin sheets of bark that peel away in the wind, each one inscribed with a single word of the language I am forgetting.

I am the threshold keeper, the one who remembers being fast. In another thousand years, when my transformation is complete, I will be indistinguishable from the Weepers. But for now, I am the translation, the proof that stone and flesh are not opposites but points on a spectrum. I am the evidence that the mountain's dreams can infect more than rock, that patience can be learned, that there are other ways to be alive than burning bright and brief.

Sometimes, in the blue-green light of the pool, I see my own face reflected—not as it is, but as it was, quick and anxious and full of questions. I no longer pity that person. But I no longer remember their name, either. I am the Tender of Veils, and I am learning to weep properly—not with salt water that evaporates in moments, but with the steady, patient seepage of stone that shapes the world one drop at a time.
