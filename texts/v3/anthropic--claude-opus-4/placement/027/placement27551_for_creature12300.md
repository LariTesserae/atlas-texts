In the Shattered Spires of Vyrr, I am **Khet**, and I am dying.

Not quickly—nothing happens quickly here. I measure my remaining seasons by the deepening ache in my joints, by the way my breath catches now when I climb the familiar handholds to my watching post. The shadowbiters took three fingers from my left hand last winter, and the clan's bonesetter says the black creep in my remaining flesh will take the rest by spring. I bind it tight with strips of musk-ox gut and pretend not to notice how the children have stopped asking me to show them the star patterns.

I am a **stone-singer**, though that is not what we call ourselves. We who listen to the spires have no true name, only the work: pressing our remaining fingers to the rock face, feeling for the tremors that speak of coming shifts, of caverns about to birth themselves, of ledges preparing to shear away. The young think it's prophecy. The old know better. Stone speaks in probabilities, in the accumulated weight of water frozen and thawed ten thousand times, in the patient mathematics of gravity.

My post is a natural hollow worn into the northeastern face of Khyvar's Tooth, the third-tallest spire in our cluster. From here, I can see seven settlements carved like scabs into the surrounding stone, watch the herds move through the valleys in their slow spirals, track the shadowbiter packs by the wheeling of the corpse-birds that follow their kills. But mostly, I listen.

The spire hums. Few can hear it—you need the patience that comes with age, with sitting still through the iron rain until your blood moves as slowly as the stone itself. Each spire has its own voice: Khyvar's Tooth groans low and mournful, still settling its weight. The Broken Crown to the south chimes like struck flint when the wind is right. And beneath them all, far below where even our deepest tunnels reach, there is something else. A thrumming. A pulse.

I've told the clan leaders, but they care only for immediate dangers—will the facing wall hold through spring? Are the shadowbiters moving toward the summer valleys? They nod when I speak of the deep-pulse, but their eyes slide away. Even the other stone-singers think I imagine it, that the black creep has reached my mind.

Perhaps it has. I find I no longer care.

My daughter Ysk comes sometimes, climbing the thousand handholds with her infant son strapped to her back. She brings me strips of dried meat that I can barely chew, fermented musk-milk in a bladder made from shadowbiter stomach. She doesn't speak of the smell that clings to me now—rot barely masked by the sage-moss I burn in my shallow fire pit. Her eyes are careful not to linger on my blackened hand.

"The stone speaks of spring," I tell her, which is a kind of lie. The stone speaks of pressure building in the deep places, of faultlines awakening after their long sleep. But she needs to believe in spring, for the child.

When she leaves, I return to my work. My good hand still has its sensitivity, can still feel the minute vibrations that tell tomorrow's dangers. I map them in my mind, these futures written in stone: the ledge that will fall when the next iron rain weighs it down, the cavern whose ceiling grows thin as spider's silk, the new crack working its way up from the fundament of the world.

And always, always, that deep pulse. Growing stronger.

I've started leaving my own offerings, though not in the traditional way. With a sharpened flint—my fingers can barely grip it now—I've been carving into the walls of my hollow. Not the usual patterns of protection or plenty, but maps. Star maps, stone maps, maps of the vibrations as they travel through the mountain's bones. If I'm right about what's coming, someone will need to know. If I'm wrong, well. The dead are beyond embarrassment.

The shadowbiters know I'm dying. They've been circling closer, leaving their scat at the base of my climb. They're patient hunters, content to wait until I'm too weak to keep my fire burning. I've started sleeping with my spear across my lap, though we both know it's gesture more than threat.

But here's what they don't understand, what even my daughter doesn't understand: I'm not afraid. I've spent fifty winters pressed against this stone, feeling its slow thoughts, learning its indifferent language. I know what the Vyrrish have forgotten in our daily scramble for warmth and meat—that we are not separate from this place. We are its latest thought, its brief experiment in blood and breathing.

The pulse from below isn't a threat. It's a promise.

When I was young, I found a cavern deep in the Tooth's belly where the walls were smooth as water, covered in spirals that hurt to look upon. Not carved—grown. As if the stone itself had dreamed them. I told no one. I go there still, when my strength allows, and press my cheek to those impossible whorls.

They're warm. After all these years of cold stone, they're *warm*.

Tomorrow, if I wake, I'll climb down one last time. Not to the settlement—Ysk will think I've fallen, will mourn me properly. But to that deep chamber, where the pulse is strongest. I'll take my maps, my fifty winters of listening, and I'll wait.

The Vyrrish speak of endurance as if it's virtue enough. As if surviving another season is the height of ambition. But the stone dreams longer dreams than that. Whatever wakes beneath us—for something is waking, I'm certain now—it will find at least one person waiting who knew its voice.

My blackened fingers can no longer feel the cold. When I press them to the stone now, I feel only that vast, patient pulse. It counts the moments until spring.

Until birth.

Until the Shattered Spires learn why they were shattered, and what waits to emerge from the breaking.

I am Khet, stone-singer of the third spire. I have three fingers, two seasons, and one great secret.

It is enough.
