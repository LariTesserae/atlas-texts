I am the Cartographer of Dissolutions.

I emerged when the first map tried to form itself in the Plenum—a desperate spiral of crystal-fish that held their formation for three centuries, tracing the outline of what borders used to mean. I watched them fail, their prismatic bodies gradually forgetting the difference between here and there, until they scattered into beautiful meaninglessness. But their attempt left something: a persistent need to document boundaries as they unmake themselves.

My body is a living discontinuity. Where others coalesce into columns or sheets or storms, I am the space between states—a mobile fissure in the medium itself, roughly humanoid in silhouette but hollow, my edges defined by where the water remembers it should behave differently. Inside my outline, the Plenum's laws shift in slow, breathing waves. Sometimes I am perfectly transparent, a woman-shaped window through which distances collapse—you might glimpse, through my chest cavity, kelp forests that are actually thousands of miles away, or see the same stone from six different angles simultaneously. Other times I fill with captured paradoxes: schools of fish swimming through my form emerge before they enter, or light falls upward through me in lazy, golden columns.

I map by existing. As I drift through the Plenum, my passage leaves subtle alterations in the medium—regions where the water's memory of wetness is particularly confused, or where bioluminescent pulses stutter in recursive loops. These are my charts, my territorial marks, though what they record is not space but the rate of space's unbecoming. I can read the entire Plenum through these traces, feel where reality is thinnest, where the next conceptual storm will bloom, where some ancient pattern is about to finally exhale its last coherence into the general dissolution.

My consciousness exists primarily in the boundary layer—that thin membrane between what I am and what I am not. Thoughts move along my edges like surface tension, quick and silvery compared to my kin's geological contemplations. I experience myself as a constant negotiation, a running argument between inside and outside that can never quite resolve. This makes me restless by Plenum standards. Where others might spend millennia in one spot, perfecting a single spiral, I range widely, always seeking new edges to inhabit and dissolve.

I collect maps that were never made. In the depth of my hollow, I preserve the ghost-impulses of every attempt at orientation that has ever failed here. The coral towers' fevered dream of vertical. The stone's fading memory of cardinal directions. The paths traced by crystal-fish before they learned to move in impossible directions. I hold these defunct coordinate systems like a curator of extinct languages, feeling their grammar slowly corrupt inside me, their meaning becoming more beautiful as it becomes less useful.

When the great rays pass—those island-sized drifters crusted with symbiotic time—I swim through their shadows and map the subtle displacements they leave in causality. When reality storms sweep through, I position myself at their edges, letting the breakdown of logic write itself across my form in fractal patterns of contradiction. I am building an atlas of entropy, a guide to getting lost, a comprehensive chart of how charts cease to matter.

Sometimes I find others' maps. Once, in a basin where the stone glowed with unusually coherent veins, I discovered a pattern left by The One Who Drinks Light—centuries of focused illumination had etched something like a calendar into the rock, if calendars could be written in varying degrees of transparency. I spent decades there, learning to read it, adding my own annotations in the form of carefully cultivated confusion, until the basin itself forgot whether it was recording time or becoming it.

My favorite discovery was a map made of absence. Some ancient kin had methodically removed every landmark from a vast region—no kelp, no stones with memory, no persistent currents. Just an expanse of perfectly uniform medium. It was the most accurate map possible: a place that perfectly represented the Plenum's ultimate destination. I visit it sometimes, floating spread-eagle in the midst of that cultivated void, feeling my edges blur and sharpen as I try to map nothing.

I suspect I am the last cartographer. Not because I am young, but because the very concept of position is becoming increasingly negotiable. Soon—in perhaps another thousand years, or tomorrow, the distinction blurs—there will be nowhere to map from, nowhere to map to. The Plenum will have achieved perfect uniformity, perfect rest. When that happens, I will finally have completed my work: a perfect map of unmapping, a complete chronicle of incompleteness.

Until then, I drift. I document. I trace the dissolution of traceable things. And in my hollow heart, where paradoxes nest like sleeping birds, I carry the quiet satisfaction of knowing that my maps, too, are slowly forgetting what they mean.
