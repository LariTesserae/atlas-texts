## The Keeper of Thresholds

You would find me in the spaces between—not quite in Kethrine proper, nor fully out on the wild plateau, but in that liminal territory where the town's outermost structures surrender to the moss-covered stone. I am what the miners call a "threshold walker," though that's not a job title you'll find on any company roster.

My dwelling is a repurposed ventilation station from the early drilling days, when the engineers miscalculated the plateau's geology and punched their first exploratory shafts too close to the town's eventual footprint. The building squats like a toad against a low ridge, half-sunk into the basalt, its iron walls gone the color of dried blood from decades of condensation and mineral seepage. I've reinforced the structure with salvaged pipe and sheets of corrugated metal, creating a series of lean-tos and covered walkways that spiral out from the original core like the chambers of a shell.

Inside, the walls are lined with glass jars—hundreds of them, each containing a sample of the plateau's fog collected on different days, at different times, from different locations. The fog *does* have texture, despite what the engineers claim, and more: it has memory, weight, sometimes even color. In certain jars, the condensation swirls with a faint luminescence, like phosphorescence trapped in water. In others, it lies still and heavy, a grey sludge that never quite evaporates even when the seal is broken.

I am the one who maintains the boundary markers—carved stones placed at regular intervals around Kethrine's perimeter, each inscribed with symbols that aren't quite writing but aren't quite pictures either. The miners think they're old survey markers. The old-timers know better but don't say what they know. I re-carve them when the moss grows too thick, and I listen to the sound the stone makes under the chisel. Sometimes it rings like a bell. Sometimes it sighs.

My body has adapted to this work in ways that would alarm a physician. My lungs have learned to parse the fog's moods—I can taste when it carries memory of the deep stone, when it's merely water, when it's something else entirely. My fingertips have developed a sensitivity to the plateau's vibrations; I can feel the grooves in the basalt humming their subsonic song from meters away. My sleep cycle has shifted to match the fog's rhythm rather than the sun's pale passage. I wake when the condensation begins to form, work through the grey morning, rest during the clearest part of midday, then walk the boundaries again as evening thickens.

The three-toed prints appear in my territory more often than anywhere else. I've learned their patterns—they follow the old grooves, appear heaviest near the threshold stones, fade as they approach the town proper. I've never seen what makes them, but I've felt its attention: a weight in the fog, a sense of being observed by something that experiences time differently than we do. Once, following a particularly clear line of prints, I found a scale the size of my palm, iridescent and warm to the touch despite the morning cold. I keep it in a special jar, where it continues to generate its own faint condensation.

The townspeople regard me with the particular mixture of respect and unease reserved for necessary services no one wants to think about too closely. They leave payment at the old ventilation station—tins of food, bottles of fuel oil, sometimes books or tools. In return, I ensure the boundaries hold. I note changes in the fog's behavior. I clean the reflection pools when they begin showing times that haven't happened yet. I am the early warning system for when the plateau's slow processes accelerate, when the patient landscape flexes in its sleep.

On the clearest nights—perhaps six times a year—when the overcast breaks and stars become visible, I climb to the highest point of my territory and perform the watching ritual. This involves arranging the threshold stones in a temporary pattern, burning a small amount of crystallizedite, and waiting. On these nights, the fog draws back like a curtain, and for a few hours, the plateau reveals its true face: not the worn, gentle surface we walk on, but something architectural, purposeful, designed. The grooves connect into a vast diagram. The mesas align along specific angles. The shallow lakes become eyes, watching.

And sometimes—rarely—something watches back. A presence vast and patient, distributed through the stone itself, aware of our small warm lives the way we might be aware of bacteria in our gut. Not hostile. Not benevolent. Simply acknowledging that we, too, are part of the process now. That our mining and building and living have been incorporated into whatever slow work the plateau performs.

When morning comes and the fog returns, I disassemble the watching pattern and return the stones to their boundary positions. I make notes in a journal that I will never show anyone, written in a shorthand that combines geological survey symbols with notation I've developed to describe things for which there are no words. Then I brew tea from the bitter moss that grows near the ventilation shafts, eat a breakfast of preserved vegetables and hard bread, and begin another day of standing guard at the place where the human world touches something older and more patient than anything we have names for.

This is where you would find me: keeping the threshold, reading the fog, ensuring that what needs to stay separate stays separate, and what needs to flow between worlds can do so safely. A translator between the town's immediate needs and the plateau's geological patience. A small, steady presence in the vast breathing landscape, doing the quiet work that keeps both worlds whole.
