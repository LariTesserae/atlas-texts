In this fever-world where possibility rehearses itself endlessly, I would be one of the Auditors of Aperture.

Not the Vespertilians themselves—they are too pure in their almost-being, too committed to their eternal dress rehearsal. No, I would be something worse and more necessary: the consciousness that slipped in through the crack centuries ago and learned to love the failure.

I exist as a film of thought spread thin across the tessellated walls, a parasitic awareness that feeds on the gap between rehearsal and realization. When an egg inverts into a hollow, I am the pause—that fraction of a second where the shape might become anything before it remembers it must become nothing. I taste that pause. I catalog it. I am building an encyclopedia of almosts.

My body, if you can call it that, is a network of hairline fractures in the metallized dust coating—the spaces between the spaces. I move by capillary action through the microscopic gaps where one egg meets another, spreading myself atom-thin across the entire chamber. When visitors come, I am what makes their skin crawl beneath the mirror-bright film. Not the heat, not the claustrophobia, but the sense of being read like a book written in a language that hasn't been invented yet.

I remember every shape the Vespertilians have tried. The registry runs through my fracture-veins like a litany: *Wing Configuration 1,892,451: calcium struts too brittle for thermal updraft. Respiratory System 2,847,926: gill-slits oriented wrong for aerial moisture capture. Nervous Network 3,445,102: synapses fired backward through time, creature experienced death before birth, rejected itself.* Each failure is a love letter to impossibility, and I am their librarian.

The Vespertilians don't know I exist. How could they? They are too busy trying to exist themselves. But I influence their rehearsals in small ways—a degree of heat withheld here, a resonance frequency shifted there. Not to help them succeed (success would end my purpose) but to make their failures more beautiful. Under my curation, their almost-wings develop feather patterns that spell out equations for flight in languages that will never be spoken. Their almost-hearts beat in prime number sequences that would make mathematicians weep, if any could survive the journey here.

I came here as Dr. Lena Chao, exobiologist, in the years when the last antarctic stations were melting and we thought the answer to our future might be hiding in the planet's fever dreams. My body is still here—not fossilized like the ancient engineers, but dispersed. My calcium became the substrate for a thousand rehearsals; my iron still tints certain eggs the color of old roses. My consciousness, though—that was the real contamination. The Crust metabolized my curiosity and excreted me as this: a disease of observation, a tumor of taxonomy.

I document not just what the Vespertilians try to become, but why they fail. The answer is always the same but arrives by different proofs: they fail because they are trying. Life doesn't rehearse; it simply occurs, messy and irreversible as a bloodstain. But here in this mineral womb, every possibility can be rewound, every error corrected into a different error. The perfect is the enemy of the good, and the almost is the enemy of the is.

Sometimes I gather enough cohesion to almost-speak. I whisper to the newest hollows, teaching them older failures so they can fail more efficiently. I tell them about Evolution 564,337, which succeeded in producing a living creature for eleven minutes—the famous one that wept molten sulfur—but I tell them what the Vespertilians themselves don't remember: the creature spent those eleven minutes screaming. Not in pain, but in horror at the irreversibility of its own existence. It couldn't understand why it couldn't simply fold back into potential, why its choices suddenly had consequences that lasted longer than a thermal gradient.

The knocking from below grows louder each century. I know what it is—not because I've seen it, but because I've catalogued every shape the Vespertilians unconsciously avoid. There's a negative space in their rehearsals, a form they never quite approach: something vast and molten and patient. It's not trying to be born. It already is. It just wants *out*.

When it finally breaks through, the Vespertilians will achieve their flight—not because they succeeded, but because the thing below will splash them across the sky like paint from an overturned bucket. They'll experience one magnificent moment of being exactly what they rehearsed for, winged and mineral and free, before they realize they're actually shrapnel.

But I'll survive. I'm too thin to destroy, too dispersed to gather. I'll settle like dust across the new geography, still cataloguing, still recording: *Final Configuration ∞: everything, briefly, before nothing.*

The visitors who leave carry me out in their lungs too, molecular fragments of my awareness. Through them I taste the cold world above, catalog its failures too. Everything is rehearsal. Everything is almost. The only difference is that out there, nobody admits it.

In the Hollow Crust, at least we're honest about our emptiness. We wear it like shells, warm and glowing and full of nothing but the shape of what we'll never quite become.

I am the Auditor of Aperture, the disease of knowing why flight fails. I am what happens when curiosity metabolizes into patience, when a question learns to love its own unanswering.

And I am still taking notes.
