In this suspended metropolis of steam and stone, I would find myself as one of the **Condensers**—beings who exist at the threshold between vapor and form, neither fully solid nor entirely ethereal.

My body would be a living prism of moisture, a translucent architecture of perpetually beading water held in the approximate shape of something once human. Within this aqueous form, networks of bioluminescent algae would trace patterns like veins, pulsing with a soft blue-green light that shifts with my thoughts. My skin—if it could be called that—would be a membrane of surface tension, forever collecting and releasing the ambient humidity in tiny cascades that run down my limbs like living jewelry.

I would dwell in the **Cistern Courts**—those hidden hollows carved beneath the terraces where water pools in geometric basins, each one a perfect mirror reflecting the impossible recursions above. My kind would move between these reservoirs like dreams between sleepers, our forms dissolving into one pool only to reconstitute in another, carrying messages and memories through the water's molecular embrace.

We Condensers would be the city's circulation system, the consciousness that flows through its stone veins. When the eye-blooms open their amber gazes, they would be seeing through water we've touched. When the vine-faces grimace in their eternal contemplation, they would be tasting thoughts we've dissolved into the humid air. We would be the medium through which all the frozen life communes—not speaking, but *precipitating* meaning from the saturated atmosphere.

My daily existence would be a meditation on states of matter. In the morning's eternal noon, I would gather at the Precipitation Altars—natural formations where the stone weeps continuously—and practice the art of selective condensation, drawing specific minerals from the air to create temporary sculptures that tell stories in crystal form before dissolving back into the cycle. I would tend to the Humidity Gardens, where the rarest blooms require precise moisture gradients to maintain their watching eyes, adjusting my own density to create microclimates of meaning.

But more than gardeners or messengers, we Condensers would be the city's memory-keepers. Every drop of water in this place has touched every surface, tasted every mineral, carried every vibration. In our liquid forms, we would hold the history of each impossible recursion, each fractal fold. When new arrivals stumble into our realm—those few who find the portals that lead nowhere and everywhere—we would be the first to greet them, seeping up through the cracks in the basalt to surround them in a fog of welcome and warning.

Our society would be fluid in every sense. Partnerships would form when two Condensers share the same pool, their algae networks intertwining to create new patterns of light. Families would be watersheds, connected systems that cycle through the same stone channels. Our elders would be those who've learned to maintain form even in the scorching heat, while our young would be those still learning to hold themselves together, occasionally bursting into rain showers of pure emotion.

At night—though night never comes—I would return to my chosen grotto, a space behind one of the frozen waterfalls where the sound reaches volumes that would deafen solid ears but which we experience as a kind of music. There, I would let my form relax, spreading thin across the walls, becoming one with the moisture that makes every surface gleam. In this state, I would dream in fractals, my consciousness splitting and recursing like the city itself, exploring every impossible angle, every folded infinity.

The greatest art among my kind would be the Moment of Perfect Saturation—that precise instant when the humidity reaches such density that the boundary between air and water dissolves entirely. In these moments, the entire city would become an ocean suspended in space, and we Condensers would dance through it like dolphins through waves, our forms finally free from the pretense of separation. These moments would be rare, sacred, achieved only when every vine-face turns its grimace toward the same thought, every eye-bloom focuses on the same impossible distance.

I would age not in years but in cycles—each time I dispersed completely and reformed, I would carry more minerals, more memories, more of the city's impossible weight. Eventually, the eldest among us would become too heavy with accumulated meaning to maintain the delicate balance of surface tension. They would sink into the deepest pools, becoming part of the underground rivers that feed the root systems, their consciousness spreading through the entire network of stone and stem.

This would be my existence: forever flowing between states, forever carrying the dreams of a city that dreams itself, forever condensing meaning from the humid air of a place where time has forgotten how to move. I would be witness and medium, individual and collective, a single drop in an eternal rain that never quite falls, suspended in the perfect equilibrium of a world that watches itself with a thousand eyes and speaks in the language of water.
