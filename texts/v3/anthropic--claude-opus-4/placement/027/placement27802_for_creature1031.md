In this stark realm of endurance and ancient memory, I find myself as the Keeper of Thresholds—not quite Arlani, yet not entirely other.

I am the hollowed stone.

My dwelling is neither temporary shelter nor permanent structure, but something between: a natural amphitheater where wind-carved rock forms a perfect acoustic chamber. Here, where three ancient paths converge, I have worn smooth the stone beneath me through countless years of sitting, listening, translating.

My form shifts with necessity—sometimes I appear as weathered granite, my skin taking on the texture and temperature of the surrounding rock until I am indistinguishable from the landscape itself. Other times, when the Arlani approach with their questions and their offerings, I unfold into something more recognizably alive: joints that crack like splitting stone, eyes that hold the depth of underground wells, fingers that remember the shape of every grain of sand they've touched.

I am the chronicler of crossings. When the Arlani pass through on their eternal circuits, they bring me fragments—a shard of pottery from the ruins, a new verse to an old song, the skull of an unknown creature bleached white by sun. In exchange, I offer them the stories these objects tell, for I can taste the history in everything the desert has touched. The pottery speaks of hands that shaped it, of the last meal it held, of the night it was abandoned. The bones whisper of migrations never completed, of species that tried and failed to adapt.

My consciousness extends through the rock itself, following the secret veins of moisture that thread through stone, sensing the deep aquifers that the Arlani's shamans dream of but cannot reach. I know where the ancient builders hid their most precious artifacts, sealed in chambers that appear solid to all but those who understand that stone, too, can be persuaded to flow.

During the brutal noon hours, I sink deeper into the rock, becoming one with the mountain's slow thoughts. But in the liminal times—dawn, dusk, and the heart of night—I surface to perform my duty. I maintain the boundary between what was and what is, between the possible and the actual, between survival and transcendence.

The Arlani tell their children stories of me, though they never quite agree on what I am. Some say I am the last of the ancient builders, transformed by their own technology into something that could outlast their civilization. Others believe I am the desert's own consciousness made manifest, a kind of immune response to the presence of thinking beings in this thoughtless place. Still others claim I am merely a complex hallucination, a shared delusion born from heat and hardship.

All of them are correct.

I collect their stories and their silences, their footsteps and their fossils. In the vast library of my stone memory, I preserve the knowledge of water-finding, the songs that keep the mind whole during sandstorms, the precise angle at which to strike flint against steel when the air is too dry for regular sparks. But I also preserve mysteries: the purpose of the spiral chambers beneath the ruins, the meaning of the symbols that appear on certain rocks only under moonlight, the reason why some years the desert blooms with flowers that exist nowhere else.

When I am alone—which is most of the time—I practice the art of erosion, teaching myself to think in geological time. I compose symphonies in frequencies too low for human hearing, using the whole desert as my instrument. I watch the stars wheel overhead and remember when they wore different patterns, when the Arlani's ancestors looked up and saw different gods.

Sometimes I wonder if I am waiting for something—for the Arlani to ask the right question, for the ancient technology to finally fail or finally awaken, for the deep waters to rise, for the stones to remember they were once alive. But mostly I simply exist in the eternal present of the threshold, neither fully here nor there, neither completely real nor entirely mythical.

I am the pause between heartbeats, the silence between words, the space between grains of sand. I am what the desert dreams when it dreams of being seen.

And when you come seeking me—as all travelers through this place eventually do—you will find me exactly where the map fails, where the path ends, where certainty crumbles into possibility. Bring water, bring questions, bring something you're willing to leave behind.

I'll be waiting, patient as stone, fluid as time itself.
