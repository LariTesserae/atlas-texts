In this piled world, I find myself as one of the **Moss Weavers**.

We are neither the most ancient nor the most numerous, but we occupy a singular niche in this vertical ecosystem. My body is elongated and segmented, perhaps three meters when fully extended, though I spend most of my existence curled and contracted. My skin is not skin at all, but a living carpet of symbiotic moss that grows directly from my flesh. This moss changes with my moods and needs—deepening to near-black when I seek concealment in the darker reaches, brightening to pale jade when I venture toward the twilight zones above.

I have six primary limbs, each ending in delicate, articulated digits that can spread wide like a spider's web or contract into precise manipulators. Between these digits grow fine, sensitive filaments—part nerve, part root—that I use to taste the chemical stories written in the plant surfaces. My remaining limbs are smaller, clustered near my head, specialized for the intricate work of weaving.

For that is what we do. We are the architects of impermanence, the artists of the ephemeral. Using secretions from specialized glands, we bind together the loose fibers that slough constantly from the great plants. These we weave into temporary structures—bridges across the gaps between stalks, nets to catch the perpetual drip and channel it into pools, even crude shelters that last perhaps a season before returning to the decay that feeds everything here.

Our eyes are numerous but small, scattered across our head-segment like stars. They perceive not just light but also the faint electrical fields that run through the living plants, allowing us to navigate even in absolute darkness by following the slow pulses of vegetable thought. We sense the Mycelial Minds beneath us as vast, cool presences, like underground rivers of consciousness. Sometimes, in our deepest weavings, our work brushes against their fruiting bodies, and for a moment, we share something—not quite communication, but an acknowledgment of mutual existence.

I live in the middle strata, where the darkness is profound but not absolute. Here, the Chitinous Climbers pass on their endless journeys, and I have learned to recognize individuals by the pattern of their clicking gait. Sometimes I weave bridges specifically for them, reinforcing the strands to bear their weight. In return, they leave nutrient-rich droppings that feed both my moss-skin and my weaving materials.

My dwelling is not a fixed place but a series of woven pockets I create and abandon as I move through the vertical world. Currently, I inhabit a hammock of my own making, suspended between two vast leaves that cup enough of the eternal drip to form a small pool. The sound of water dropping into water is my constant companion—a metronome for my work.

We Moss Weavers are solitary by nature, but we know each other's work. Each weaver has a signature pattern, a way of twisting fibers that is as unique as a fingerprint. I often encounter the weavings of others—some fresh and taut, others ancient and sagging with moisture. The oldest weavings I've found incorporate materials I don't recognize, possibly from the time of the Ancients who carved the plant-matter shelters. I study these with my filament-fingers, learning techniques lost to time.

My diet is unusual even for this world. I feed primarily on the nutrients my moss-skin extracts from the air and the surfaces I traverse, supplemented by certain fungi that grow in the darkest places. But I am also nourished by the act of weaving itself—there is something in the creative process, in the bringing of order to chaos, that sustains me in ways I cannot fully explain.

The Gloom Serpents are my greatest fear and my strangest fascination. Their ability to move through solid matter as if it were water speaks to a different relationship with reality than my own. Once, I felt one pass directly beneath a web I was weaving. The structure trembled with its passage, but held. Later, I found a scale it had shed caught in my fibers—a piece of living darkness that I wove into my next creation, giving it properties of shadow and substance I had never achieved before.

The Drifters high above are like distant gods to us. On rare occasions when I climb toward the twilight peaks, I see their lights painting slow auroras across the canopy. I weave nets to catch the phosphorescent dust that sometimes falls from their passing. This dust, when incorporated into my weavings, creates structures that glow faintly, providing just enough light to work by in the deep darkness.

My purpose, if I have one beyond survival, is documentation. Each weaving is a map, a story, a recording of this moment in our world's slow transformation. The patterns encode information—the health of the plants, the movement patterns of fauna, the chemical gradients that shift with the seasons. Other Moss Weavers who find my abandoned works can read these stories, adding their own chapters in turn.

I am currently working on my most ambitious project yet—a vast web that spans an entire canyon between two plant-mountains. It is not meant to catch anything, but to resonate with the deep hum that sometimes emanates from the world's core. If successful, it will be an instrument, a way to listen to and perhaps communicate with whatever intelligence guides the growth of our vertical universe.

In quiet moments, suspended in my woven home with the eternal drip as soundtrack, I wonder about the Ancients. Were they like us, trying to make sense of this overwhelming world through craft and creation? Did they too leave messages in their carvings that we are too alien to read? Sometimes I weave replicas of their tools from plant fibers, trying to understand their purpose through mimicry.

This is my existence—a life spent suspended between the crushing depths and the impossible heights, creating temporary beauty in a world of permanent growth and decay. I am a small pattern in an enormous tapestry, but I am content, for I know that my weavings, however ephemeral, are part of the great conversation between all things in this piled world.

The moss on my skin shifts to a pattern of deep satisfaction as I add another strand to my canyon-spanning web. Somewhere above, a Drifter's light paints my work in brief, ethereal colors. Below, the Mycelial Minds dream their slow dreams. And I weave, always weave, binding moment to moment with fibers that will outlast me, even as they inevitably return to the damp embrace of our eternal, vertical home.
