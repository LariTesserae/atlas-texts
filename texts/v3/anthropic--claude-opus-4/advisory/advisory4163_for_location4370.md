## Preparation Guide for the Hive Lung

### Critical Pre-Visit Preparations

**Thermal Regulation**
The chamber maintains a precise 55°F (12.8°C). This isn't merely cool—it's actively regulated, meaning your body heat will be constantly wicked away. Bring multiple thin, moisture-wicking layers rather than bulky insulation. The condensation from the breathing hexagonal cells will soak through ordinary fabrics within minutes. Consider:
- Synthetic base layers (merino wool will trap the machine oil smell)
- Waterproof but breathable middle layer
- Outer shell that can be easily cleaned of oil residue
- Backup clothing sealed in waterproof bags

**Respiratory Protection**
The ozone and machine oil aren't immediately toxic, but prolonged exposure will cause throat irritation, headaches, and possible metal fume fever. A half-face respirator with combination organic vapor/P100 cartridges is minimum protection. Bring extras—the oil vapor will clog filters faster than normal. Consider practicing breathing through the respirator beforehand; the chamber's sound-dampening effect combined with your muffled breathing can trigger claustrophobia.

**Eye and Ear Protection**
The phosphorescent light from ten thousand scarabs creates a stroboscopic effect as they move. Bring polarized goggles with side shields—regular sunglasses won't cut it. The light shifts wavelengths constantly; some visitors report migraine auras or temporary color blindness afterward.

For sound: the "oddly muffled" quality is more disorienting than loud noise. The clicking creates subsonic frequencies that bypass normal hearing. Bring both earplugs AND over-ear protection that can handle unusual frequency ranges. Musicians' earplugs that reduce volume while maintaining clarity work well.

### Movement and Positioning

**Never Touch the Walls**
The scarab coating isn't passive. They respond to contact by swarming, and their brass carapaces are sharp enough to shred skin. The walls look stable but are constantly being rebuilt—lean against one and you might find welding-worms attempting to incorporate your clothing into their construction.

**Watch Your Step**
The floor expands as the mercury pool grows. What looks like solid ground might be scarabs mimicking flooring. Test each step with a walking stick (metal, not wood—wood confuses their sensors). The condensation makes every surface slick. Crampon-style shoe attachments help, but avoid magnetic ones—they interfere with the inhabitants.

**Maintain Center of Gravity**
When scarab formations suddenly coalesce into larger forms, the shifting weight can create localized gravity distortions. Keep knees slightly bent, weight centered. If you feel dizzy, don't close your eyes—the visual anchor, chaotic as it is, helps more than darkness.

### Behavioral Protocols

**Minimize Electronic Devices**
The electromagnetic fields will corrupt most electronics. Mechanical watches stop working. Phones may permanently malfunction. If you must bring electronics, triple-wrap them in faraday fabric and keep them powered off. The chrome centipedes are particularly attracted to active circuits.

**Don't Attempt Pattern Recognition**
Your brain will try to find patterns in the scarab movements. Resist this. Visitors who stare too long at the formations report persistent visual hallucinations lasting weeks. Look for 30 seconds, then focus on something static (bring a neutral-colored cloth for this purpose). The patterns aren't random but they're not meant for human cognition.

**Expect Extraction Attempts**
The dismantler-mites don't distinguish between chamber components and visitor equipment. They'll attempt to disassemble buckles, zippers, metal frames. Tape over all metal fasteners. Some visitors report mites attempting to "sample" dental fillings or surgical implants—keep your mouth closed and covered.

### The Mercury Pool

**Absolute No Contact**
The pool's fluid isn't mercury but shares its density and toxicity. The breaching structures suggest massive activity below the surface. Stand at least six feet back—splashes happen without warning. If any gets on skin or clothing, don't try to wipe it off (this spreads it). Let it bead and roll off, then neutralize the residue with the sodium thiosulfate solution you should carry.

**Photography Challenges**
The pool's surface reflects the scarab light in ways that overwhelm camera sensors. If you must photograph it, use manual settings, fastest shutter speed possible, and expect 90% of shots to be overexposed blurs. Some visitors sketch instead, though the constant movement makes this challenging.

### Time Management

**Maximum Safe Exposure: 2 Hours**
After two hours, the combination of fumes, sensory overload, and the chamber's reality-bending properties causes disorientation severe enough to impair judgment. Set multiple alarms (mechanical, not electronic). The time distortion effect means an hour feels like minutes.

**Exit Strategy**
The chamber reorganizes constantly—your entry point might be welded over or converted to a breathing cell. Mark your path with biodegradable tape (the cleaning mites will remove it within hours, but that's enough). Keep oriented to the mercury pool as a landmark, but remember it's expanding—distances change.

**Decontamination**
Outside the chamber, you'll need thorough decontamination:
- Strip outer layers and seal in disposal bags
- Shower with industrial degreaser to remove oil residue
- Flush eyes with saline solution
- Take activated charcoal to absorb any ingested vapors

### Psychological Preparation

**Conceptual Flexibility**
The hardest part isn't physical—it's mental. Your categories of "living" and "mechanical" will break down. Some visitors experience existential vertigo, questioning whether they themselves are biological or mechanical. This passes, but can be intense in the moment.

**Isolation Protocol**
Despite the teeming activity, many visitors report profound loneliness in the chamber. The inhabitants ignore you completely unless you interfere with their functions. You're witnessing something that doesn't need you, doesn't acknowledge you, would continue unchanged if you never existed. Some find this liberating; others, terrifying.

**Post-Visit Integration**
Expect dreams about clicking scarabs for weeks afterward. Some visitors report compulsions to organize objects in hexagonal patterns or anxiety when around normal insects. These effects fade, but consider scheduling a few days of rest after your visit.

### What to Bring (Summary)
- Layered moisture-wicking clothing with waterproof outer shell
- Half-face respirator with extra cartridges
- Polarized full-coverage goggles
- Dual hearing protection
- Metal walking stick
- Crampon shoe attachments
- Faraday bags for electronics
- Biodegradable marking tape
- Sodium thiosulfate solution
- Industrial degreaser
- Activated charcoal
- Mechanical timers
- Neutral-colored cloth for visual breaks
- First aid kit specializing in chemical burns
- Several days' supply of migraine medication

### Final Note

The Hive Lung processes something we don't understand in service of the machine-mountain's unknowable functions. You're entering a digestive organ, a computational cluster, a nursery—possibly all three. Respect its autonomy. Observe without interfering. And remember: you're the foreign body here, temporarily tolerated by an immune system vast beyond comprehension. 

When you leave, the chamber will seal your passage and continue its work, and within hours there will be no trace you ever visited. This isn't hostility—it's something more unsettling: complete irrelevance. Prepare for that, above all.
