**A Traveler's Guide to the Drowned Salt City**

This is not a place for casual exploration. The salt flats demand respect, preparation, and an acceptance that you will leave changed—if you leave at all.

**Physical Preparations**

Your boots must be engineered for this environment. Standard footwear will dissolve within hours. You need thick-soled industrial boots with chemical-resistant polymer coating, preferably with internal cooling systems. The salt crust appears solid but conceals pockets of superheated brine. One wrong step, and you'll punch through into water that can strip flesh from bone. Bring backup boots sealed in protective wrap.

The suit is non-negotiable. Full-body chemical protection with redundant sealing, but it must also breathe—the humidity will drown you in your own sweat otherwise. Look for suits used in volcanic research, with built-in cooling tubes you can fill with water. The water will evaporate within hours, so bring reserves. Calculate four liters per day just for the cooling system, another six for drinking.

Your respirator needs specialized filters for both sulfur compounds and metallic particulates. The air isn't immediately lethal, but prolonged exposure causes heavy metal poisoning that manifests weeks later as tremors, confusion, and eventually organ failure. Bring triple redundancy on filters. The beetles, while not aggressive, shed microscopic fragments of their shells—essentially breathing in powdered chromium and copper.

**Navigating the Beetles**

The salvage beetles operate on simple behavioral algorithms. They ignore organic matter unless it contains rare earth elements—remove all electronics, jewelry, and metal dental work before entering. Even trace amounts can trigger investigative swarming. They won't attack, but being buried under a million clicking bodies while they attempt to process your phone is its own form of horror.

Learn to read their movements. When beetles flow in unified streams, they've detected a rich deposit. Follow their paths to avoid unstable ground—they instinctively route around weak salt crusts and active thermal vents. When they scatter in radial patterns, a geyser is imminent. You have perhaps ninety seconds to clear the area.

Never camp near their hive structures. The constant motion generates a subsonic hum that causes severe psychological distress in humans—anxiety, paranoia, and eventually complete dissociation. Travelers report losing hours staring at the patterns of their movement, forgetting to drink water or check equipment.

**Thermal Hazards**

The heat is a living thing here. It rises through your boots, radiates from every surface, presses against you with humid weight. Standard thermometers fail—bring industrial pyrometers to check ground temperature. Anything above 80°C means the crust is too thin. 

Map the geyser patterns before venturing far from stable ground. They follow the old street grids, erupting along what were once major thoroughfares. The city's dying infrastructure creates predictable patterns: every 4-7 hours, pressure builds in the eastern district. Every 12 hours, the factory sector vents. Time your movements accordingly.

The green-glowing pools are deceptively alluring in the eternal twilight. Do not approach them. The chromium concentration creates a heavy-metal mirror that reflects and amplifies heat. Standing near one for more than minutes causes severe burns through radiant heating alone. The beetles harvest from their edges, creating stable platforms—use these if you must pass nearby.

**Psychological Preparations**

This landscape attacks the mind as surely as the body. The perpetual twilight disrupts circadian rhythms within hours. Bring artificial light sources timed to simulate day cycles, or risk the creeping madness of eternal dusk. Many travelers report time dilation—days feeling like weeks, hours like minutes.

The silence between sounds is oppressive. When not filled with steam-hiss or beetle-click, the emptiness presses against your ears like depth pressure. Bring white noise generators. The alternative is beginning to hear things that aren't there—voices in the steam, music in the distant bridge-songs, whispers from the city below.

You will dream of drowning. Every traveler does. The city's death saturates the air, and sleeping minds reconstruct it nightly. You'll wake gasping, tasting salt water that isn't there. Prepare mentally for this. Some bring dream suppressants, though these carry their own risks in a place where alertness means survival.

**What to Bring Beyond Survival**

Chemical analysis equipment, if you're scientifically inclined. The unique conditions create mineral formations found nowhere else in the multiverse. The beetle shells alone contain alloys that materials scientists would kill for. Sample containers must be trebly-sealed—the residual chemicals eat through standard materials.

Bring offerings for the city. This sounds mystical, but it's practical. Drop small electronics or rare earth elements at the edges of beetle streams, and they'll process them rather than investigating you. Old phones, dead batteries, copper wire—the city's ecosystem accepts these tributes and grants you easier passage.

Recording equipment, if you must, but understand that standard electronics fail rapidly. The combination of heat, humidity, and trace magnetic fields from the buried machinery creates a perfect storm of interference. Mechanical cameras work better. Hand-drawn sketches best of all.

**The Deeper Dangers**

Some travelers, seduced by the geometric perfection of the salt formations or the hypnotic patterns of beetle movement, choose to stay. They shed their protective equipment gradually, claiming the city "accepts" them. Within weeks, their skin takes on the same chemical patinas as the beetle shells. Within months, they stop responding to human contact. The beetles flow around them as they stand motionless in the salt, gradually becoming part of the landscape.

Do not explore the exposed infrastructure alone. The pipes and gantries that breach the surface connect to the flooded underlayers. The temptation to descend, to see the preserved city below, has claimed many. The industrial machinery still functions in those drowned spaces, grinding forward in purposeless automation. Automated systems don't distinguish between intended materials and human bodies.

The cooling towers on the horizon are a trap. Yes, they're the only vertical structures, the only landmarks in the endless flat. Yes, the beetle bridges between them are beautiful, an inadvertent art installation of reclaimed metals. But the towers concentrate the worst aspects of this place—heat, chemical saturation, and the endless whisper of corroding concrete. Time moves differently near them. Travelers report entering at dawn and emerging at dusk, with no memory of the intervening hours and their water supplies mysteriously depleted.

**Final Warnings**

This city is a closed system slowly grinding to entropy. Everything here is recycling, including you if you stay too long. The salt wants to preserve you. The beetles want to process you. The heat wants to dissolve you. Even the air wants to fill your lungs with metal and minerals until you become part of the industrial cycle.

Stay no more than seven days. After that, the chemical exposure begins to affect judgment. You'll find reasons to extend your visit. The patterns in the salt will seem to hold meaning. The beetle movements will start to make sense as a form of communication. The ghost turbines below will sound like they're calling your name.

When you leave—and you must leave—scrub every surface of your equipment with industrial solvents. The salt carries with it microscopic beetle eggs (or what passes for eggs in their self-replicating cycle). Bring even a grain back to another world, and you risk spreading this mechanical ecosystem to places unprepared for its hunger.

The city offers no redemption, no revelation, no treasure worth the risk of staying. It is a monument to industrial hubris, a perfectly functioning system with no purpose but its own continuation. Visit if you must, to witness the end result of unchecked progress. But remember: the salt flats grow thicker each year, and they're always hungry for new materials to preserve.
