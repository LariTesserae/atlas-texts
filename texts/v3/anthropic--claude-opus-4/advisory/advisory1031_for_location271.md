## A Traveler's Guide to the Forgotten Cisterns

### Initial Mental Preparation

Before you even consider descending, spend several days preparing your mind. This is not hyperbole—the cisterns will assault your senses in ways that can induce panic in the unprepared. Practice sitting in complete darkness while listening to white noise at uncomfortable volumes. Train yourself to navigate your home blindfolded, relying solely on touch. Most importantly, practice breathing exercises to combat claustrophobia. The passages between chambers will compress around you like a stone throat.

Consider visiting during your world's dry season. While the water levels never truly drop, the slightly reduced flow can make the acoustic environment marginally more bearable for newcomers.

### Physical Provisions

**Clothing**: Wear form-fitting synthetic materials that won't absorb water. Loose clothing will catch on the narrow passages and become dangerously heavy when soaked. Bring at least two complete changes in waterproof bags—you will be drenched within minutes. Avoid cotton entirely; it will never dry in the humid environment. Neoprene socks or diving booties are essential—regular footwear will be ruined, and bare feet risk injury on the worn but still sharp stone.

**Lighting**: Bring triple redundancy. The primary should be a high-quality headlamp with extra batteries in waterproof cases. Your secondary should be chemical light sticks—they're unaffected by moisture and provide omnidirectional light that helps combat the disorienting acoustic environment. For emergency backup, bring a small hand-crank flashlight. Mount all lights securely; dropping them in the flowing channels means they're gone forever.

**Navigation**: Forget GPS or electronic devices—the moisture will destroy them, and signal cannot penetrate the rock regardless. Bring surveyor's chalk in multiple colors to mark your path, but understand that the flowing water will erode these marks within hours. Better is a spool of thin, strong cord that you can tie at intervals. Some experienced visitors use a ball of yarn, paying it out as they go—a mythologically appropriate choice for this labyrinth.

### Safety Equipment

**Breathing**: The air is breathable but oppressive. Bring a small battery-powered fan to create air movement during rest breaks—the psychological relief is worth the weight. Some visitors find relief in menthol oils applied below the nose, cutting through the mineral taste of the air.

**Communication**: You cannot rely on verbal communication. Bring waterproof writing tablets or learn basic touch signals with your companions. A whistle might work for emergencies, but test it first—the acoustic environment can turn a whistle into a painful shriek.

**First Aid**: Focus on treatments for cuts, scrapes, and panic attacks. The constant moisture means wounds won't clot properly. Bring waterproof bandages and antiseptic that works in wet conditions. Include anti-anxiety medication if you're prone to claustrophobia.

### Entering the Cisterns

Find a local guide if possible—not to lead you through (few will venture into the depths), but to show you the least hazardous entrance. Some access points require technical climbing skills; others involve squeezing through gaps barely wide enough for a human body. The municipal workers might be convinced to point you toward the maintenance entrances, which are marginally more navigable.

Time your entry for early morning. You'll need maximum mental acuity to handle the sensory assault, and starting fresh is essential. Inform someone of your plans and expected return time—this is not a place where you want search and rescue teams to find your remains weeks later.

### Navigation Strategies

Move slowly. The acoustic environment will disorient you within minutes, and rushing leads to injury or losing your way. When entering each new chamber, pause and create a mental map using touch. The carved symbols on the walls can help—run your fingers over them to build a tactile understanding of their meaning.

Follow the water flow initially—it will generally lead you deeper but along more navigable paths. When you need to return, move against the flow. This isn't foolproof, as the water circulates in complex patterns, but it provides a general orientation.

Count your breaths and steps. In the absence of visual landmarks and with sound reduced to meaningless roar, these internal rhythms become your clock and compass. Some visitors find that humming—felt through bone conduction rather than heard—helps maintain sanity and timing.

### Managing the Acoustic Environment

The sound will break you if you fight it. Instead, let it wash through you. Some visitors report achieving a meditative state where the white noise becomes a blank canvas for thought. Others experience the opposite—a complete cessation of internal dialogue. Both reactions are normal.

If you feel panic rising, find a smaller chamber where you can touch all walls without moving. The physical containment, paradoxically, can feel safer than the larger spaces where the sound seems infinite. Press your palms against the cool stone and focus on that sensation to ground yourself.

### Biological Hazards

While the cisterns harbor little dangerous life, take precautions. The pale slimes are generally harmless but can be extremely slippery. Test each foothold, especially on surfaces with visible growth. The blind crawfish can deliver a painful pinch if stepped on—shuffle your feet rather than stepping normally.

That "something larger" mentioned in the deepest chambers remains unidentified across multiple worlds. Local legends vary—some say it's merely the pressure waves from distant chambers, others insist on more exotic explanations. Regardless, if you sense large-scale water movement in the depths, retreat to shallower areas.

### Time Limits

Do not stay below for more than four hours on your first visit. The combination of sensory deprivation and overload creates a dissociative state that becomes dangerous with extended exposure. Some visitors report losing entire days in the chambers, emerging dehydrated and confused. Set a waterproof timer and obey it absolutely.

The cisterns exert a psychological pull on some visitors—a desire to go deeper, stay longer, become part of the eternal flow. Recognize this as a dangerous delusion brought on by the environment. The water engineers who work the upper levels tell stories of colleagues who ventured too deep and never returned, though whether they became lost, injured, or simply chose not to leave remains unknown.

### Exiting

The return journey is often harder than the descent. Your markers may have washed away, and exhaustion compounds disorientation. This is when most accidents occur. Move even more slowly, rest frequently, and don't hesitate to backtrack if a passage feels wrong.

When you finally emerge, the normal world will seem impossibly loud and bright. Have sunglasses ready, even if exiting at night—street lights will sear eyes adapted to the dimness below. Your hearing will remain affected for hours, possibly days. Avoid loud environments and give your senses time to readjust.

### Recovery

Shower immediately and thoroughly—the mineral-laden water can cause skin irritation if allowed to dry. Drink significant amounts of clean water to flush the mineral taste from your system. Sleep will likely be difficult the first night as your brain processes the experience. Many visitors report intense dreams of flowing water and endless stone passages.

Some find themselves changed by the experience in subtle ways. Colors seem more vivid for weeks afterward. The sound of running water triggers visceral memories. A few report an ongoing awareness of the hidden waters flowing beneath city streets, a constant whisper at the edge of perception.

### Final Warnings

The cisterns are not malevolent, but neither are they benign. They exist in a state of perfect indifference to human presence. Respect this. Do not attempt to map the entire system—it may be truly unmappable, changing subtly with each visit. Don't try to dam or redirect the water flow—the system's equilibrium, once disturbed, might not reset.

Most importantly, trust your instincts. If a passage feels wrong, if the water's voice changes in a way that raises primitive alarm, if you sense that "something larger" approaching—leave immediately. The cisterns have kept their secrets for centuries. They don't need to share them with you.

Those who successfully navigate the forgotten cisterns often speak of the experience in hushed, reverent tones. It's not an adventure they recommend, exactly, but rather something they feel privileged to have survived. The cisterns strip away the veneer of civilization, reduce you to a blind creature feeling its way through primordial darkness. Some find this terrifying. Others discover it's exactly what they were looking for.

Whether you return to the surface changed or merely wet, you'll carry the memory of that eternal flow, that crushing sound, that perfect equilibrium between human ambition and geological patience. The city above will never feel quite the same, knowing what moves in the darkness below its foundations—water and stone and time, circulating forever in chambers that remember when bronze was the height of technology, and forget nothing.
