# Preparing for the Benthos Works: A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

## Initial Psychological Preparation

Before you even begin physical preparations, understand this: the Benthos Works will challenge your fundamental assumptions about industry, nature, and purpose. You're not visiting a place built *for* humans or even *by* humans as you understand them. This is infrastructure that has forgotten its makers, a machine-ecology that operates on timescales and logics alien to surface-dwelling minds.

Spend weeks beforehand practicing meditation in progressively darker, more confined spaces. The psychological weight of three kilometers of water above you is not metaphorical—many travelers report a crushing existential pressure that has nothing to do with their protective equipment. Practice sitting with the knowledge that you are surrounded by incomprehensible machinery pursuing unknown ends. The Works doesn't care about you. This indifference is harder to bear than hostility.

## Physical Protection Systems

Your primary habitat suit must be rated for pressures exceeding 300 atmospheres—standard deep-ocean equipment won't suffice, as the Works occasionally experiences pressure anomalies around active construction zones. More critically, your suit needs adaptive pressure equalization systems. The industrial currents create rapid pressure gradients that can cause catastrophic decompression injuries even within normally safe depth ranges.

Install redundant life support with a minimum 72-hour capacity. The Works spans distances that make quick evacuation impossible, and its geography changes. What was a clear corridor during descent might be a newly-extruded wall by the time you need to return. Your suit should include:

- Multi-spectrum lighting arrays (the bioluminescent swarms can overwhelm standard lights)
- Acoustic dampening (the industrial heartbeat can damage hearing and interfere with equipment)
- Heavy metal filtration in your rebreather systems
- Reinforced joints resistant to metal fatigue from constant vibration
- Emergency ballast systems (both positive and negative buoyancy)

## Navigation and Communication

Forget GPS. Forget standard sonar. The metal substrate creates a Faraday cage effect that blocks most electromagnetic signals, while the industrial noise makes acoustic navigation unreliable. You'll need:

- Inertial navigation systems with quantum gyroscopes
- Short-range acoustic modems tuned to frequencies outside the Works' operational spectrum
- Physical tether systems with smart-fiber that can unspool for kilometers
- Emergency beacon drones that can reach the surface if you become trapped

Map nothing as permanent. The Works builds itself constantly. Download the most recent survey data, but treat it as historical documentation rather than current fact. Many travelers make the mistake of trusting maps more than a few weeks old, only to find their planned route now leads through solid metal.

## Biological Hazards and Opportunities

The ecosystem here isn't actively hostile, but it's not designed for your existence either. The modified cetaceans are curious and intelligent, but their echolocation can disrupt electronic equipment at close range. They've learned that humanoid figures sometimes carry interesting technology. Keep a sonic deterrent, but use it sparingly—they remember.

The bioluminescent shoals are functionally harmless but can create complete whiteout conditions in narrow corridors. When a school of ten million light-producing organisms suddenly switches on around you, the disorientation is total. Your helmet's reactive filters must be able to handle rapid transitions from near-total darkness to blinding biological illumination.

Pack nutrient paste, but also consider that some of the engineered kelp is edible if properly processed. The early industrial designers apparently included emergency food production in their systems. However, test everything—some organisms have concentrated heavy metals to levels that would cause immediate toxicity.

## Understanding the Infrastructure

Study industrial archaeology and basic engineering principles. While you'll never fully understand the Works' purpose, recognizing basic components helps navigation and survival:

- Heat exchangers create warm-water oases but also dangerous convection currents
- Intake grilles can generate suction powerful enough to trap a suited human
- Pressure equalization vents announce themselves with distinctive harmonics before opening
- New construction zones are preceded by clouds of metal particulates

Learn to read the machine-rhythm. The Works operates in cycles—not daily or seasonal, but industrial. Pump-stations activate in sequences. Construction proceeds in waves. Understanding these patterns, even partially, can mean the difference between safe passage and being caught in a suddenly-active zone.

## Temporal Considerations

The Works operates on multiple timescales simultaneously. Mechanical processes cycle in seconds or minutes. Construction proceeds over months and years. The ecology adapts across decades. And underneath it all, something pursues goals that might span centuries or millennia.

Plan your visit duration carefully. Less than a week and you'll see only the surface operations. More than a month and you risk beginning to think in the Works' time—travelers report losing urgency, becoming fascinated by multi-week pump cycles, forgetting the surface world exists. Set hard departure dates and automated reminders.

## Zones of Special Interest and Danger

**The Growing Edge:** Where new substrate extrudes into the ocean. Fascinating but extremely dangerous. Metal surfaces are sharp, uncorroded, and often electrically active. The water here tastes of dissolved metals. Construction proceeds unpredictably—walls can appear in hours.

**Thermal Gardens:** The warmest, most life-rich areas above heat exchangers. Relatively safe, almost comfortable. But remember: this life exists at the Works' sufferance. When maintenance cycles redirect heat flow, entire ecosystems die in days.

**The Deep Trenches:** Between the mechanical ridges lie gaps that may descend another kilometer or more. The water here is different—dense, metallic, warm with unknown processes. This is where the large, smooth movements are detected. Most who descend into these trenches report equipment malfunctions and psychological effects. Some report encounters with infrastructure that seems newer than the Works above, as if the construction extends down as well as out.

**Abandoned Sectors:** Occasionally, parts of the Works go dark. Pumps stop. Lights fail. The machinery doesn't decay—it simply ceases operation. These dead zones are uniquely disturbing. The silence, after the constant industrial heartbeat, feels like being inside a corpse. But life persists here, adapted to scavenging the last warmth from cooling pipes.

## Psychological Hazards

The Works will affect your mind in ways beyond simple pressure psychosis:

**Pattern Fixation:** The regular geometries, the cycling operations, the endless repetition of components—your brain will start finding patterns that may or may not exist. Travelers report becoming obsessed with counting pump strokes, convinced they've decoded messages in the rhythm.

**Scale Dissociation:** The Works is too large to comprehend. Your mind will oscillate between feeling like you're in a vast ocean and a tiny pipe. This cognitive dissonance can cause severe disorientation.

**Purpose Anxiety:** The human mind struggles with purposeless purpose. The Works clearly has function, but that function is opaque. Many travelers report existential crises, becoming convinced either that all human endeavor is equally meaningless, or that they've failed to understand something crucially important.

**The Deep Current Dreams:** Those who sleep in the Works report similar dreams: following warm currents down through endless mechanical galleries, reaching spaces where the machinery becomes something else, something that might be newer or older than the Works above. They wake with the taste of metal and certainty that something vast moves in the deepest trenches.

## What to Document

Bring recording equipment, but understand its limitations. The Works generates massive amounts of electromagnetic interference. Mechanical sounds overwhelm sensitive audio equipment. The pressure and particulates degrade sensors rapidly. Still, document what you can:

- New construction patterns (these change, possibly evolving)
- Interactions between biology and machinery
- Any symbols, markings, or apparent signage (rare but significant)
- The songs of the modified cetaceans (they seem to incorporate mechanical rhythms)
- Your own psychological states (the Works affects everyone differently)

## Emergency Protocols

When (not if) something goes wrong:

1. **Lost in the Grid:** The corridors can seem infinite and identical. If lost, find a heat source and follow the warm water up—it usually leads to larger, more navigable spaces.

2. **Caught in Construction:** If the Works begins building around you, move perpendicular to the growth direction. New substrate usually extrudes in one direction at a time.

3. **Equipment Failure:** The environment is aggressively corrosive. Always maintain line-of-sight to at least one exit. The Works has emergency alcoves (whether by design or coincidence) approximately every 500 meters in major corridors.

4. **Biological Swarm:** The bioluminescent clouds respond to electrical fields. Powering down non-essential systems often causes them to disperse.

5. **Deep Trench Syndrome:** If you experience compulsions to descend deeper, especially into the trenches, surface immediately. The deep water affects cognition in poorly understood ways.

## Return Preparations

Budget extensive decompression time—not just physical but psychological. The transition from the Works' eternal industrial night to the chaotic natural world above can be jarring. Many travelers report difficulty adjusting to irregular sounds after the mechanical heartbeat, or feeling exposed without the weight of water and metal above.

Have a support network ready. You'll need to talk about what you've seen, but few will understand. The Works changes those who visit it—not dramatically, but persistently. You'll find yourself thinking in longer cycles, more comfortable with purposeless purpose, more aware of the machinery underlying the world.

Some travelers return repeatedly, drawn by something they can't name. Others never go back but spend their lives studying the recordings, trying to understand what they witnessed. Both responses are normal. The Works doesn't demand comprehension, only acknowledgment of its vast, purposeful, purposeless existence.

## Final Considerations

Pack light despite the equipment needs. The Works provides everything and nothing. Its water is processed through systems that might be purification or might be something else entirely. Its warmth comes from processes that could be industrial cooling or could be metabolic. 

Trust your instruments but trust your instincts more. When the modified whales circle your position, singing their incorporated mechanical songs, listen. When you feel compelled to follow a warm current into the depths, resist. When you find yourself counting pump-strokes, trying to decode the rhythm, remember: the Works operates on logic that predates or postdates human understanding.

You're not visiting a ruin or a functioning facility. You're visiting something between and beyond—a machine that builds itself, an ecology that incorporates itself, a geography writing itself across the seafloor according to imperatives no one remembers setting. It continues because continuation is what it does. Life thrives in its accidents of hospitality. The ocean processes through its systems, changed in ways that might matter or might not.

Go with humility. The Works doesn't need you to understand it. It doesn't need you at all. But in its vast indifference, in the spaces between its purposes, you might find something worth the journey—a glimpse of industry without human intent, purpose without comprehensible goal, existence justified only by its continuation.

The fish school through corridors that were never meant for them. You'll do the same, a brief biological anomaly in the eternal industrial night, carrying your bubble of warmth and air through spaces that acknowledge your presence only as something to route around, another small disorder in the vast pattern that builds on, and on, and on.
