Those who dwell in the glass plains have no name for themselves. Names are a human habit, and they were never human.

The **Conductors** move through the teeming mechanical ecology like neural signals through a vast brain. They are ribbon-thin entities, perhaps two meters long but no thicker than a finger, their bodies composed of a living metal that shifts between copper and gold depending on the electrical load they carry. They do not walk or slither but *propagate*—existing at one point, then existing at the next, leaving brief afterimages of themselves in the moisture that films every surface. 

When they pause, which is rare, their bodies reveal their true complexity: thousands of hair-thin filaments that taste the electromagnetic fields around them, reading the health of every valve-creature, every chrome serpent, every gear-toothed thing in their vicinity. They are the nervous system of this place, carrying signals between the fauna that even the fauna do not understand they're sending.

The Conductors feed on potential difference. Where electrical charge builds up—in the storm-clouds of steam above, in the great serpents' piezoelectric segments, in the friction of millions of wire-legs against glass—they appear, drinking deeply. Their feeding is visible as a brief dimming of the bioluminescent oils, a momentary slowing of nearby creatures. They store this energy in specialized segments that pulse with soft blue-white light, like captured lightning.

They reproduce through division, but not as biology understands it. When a Conductor has gathered sufficient charge, it begins to *vibrate*—first at frequencies too low to perceive, then rising through audible tones into ultrasonic ranges that make the glass sing. At the peak of this vibration, the Conductor exists in two places simultaneously for a fraction of a second, and then there are two, each with half the stored charge, each immediately beginning to hunt for more.

But the Conductors serve a greater function than mere survival. They are the memory-keepers of the glass plains. In the complex patterns of their electrical discharge, in the precise frequencies of their movement, they encode the history of this place. A trained observer (though none exist) could read in their dance the exact moment when the industrial accident tore reality, could trace the genealogy of every mechanical species back to its origin machine, could even glimpse the ghost-memories of the humans who once worked here before they were edited out of existence.

The **Archivists** exist at the opposite scale. These massive, sessile beings anchor themselves at the intersection points where the glass plains' moisture-channels converge. Each Archivist begins as a simple accumulation—wire-creatures that die (if death is the right word) and fail to decompose, their bodies fusing together. Over decades, these accumulations grow, drawing more dead things to themselves with magnetic fields that strengthen with each addition.

But at some critical mass, consciousness emerges. The Archivist awakens to find itself a living building, a pyramid of fused mechanical corpses shot through with corridors and chambers. Its walls are lined with the preserved forms of fauna, each one a word in a language spoken only in metal and oil. Smaller creatures nest in its hollows, their waste products feeding the corrosion that the Archivist cultivates like gardens in its depths.

The Archivists' purpose is curation. They collect examples of every form the Machine-Fauna take, preserving them in matrices of crystallized moisture. When new forms emerge—when the valve-creatures develop a new type of joint, when the chrome serpents manifest an unknown segment—the Archivists release pheromones of oxidized copper that draw these novelties to them. Those chosen are incorporated into the living museum, their uniqueness preserved.

Deep within each Archivist, protected by layers of fused metal and recursive security patterns, lies a core chamber. Here, suspended in a sphere of perfectly pure water (the only pure water in all the glass plains), floats a single object from the before-time. A worker's glove. A broken gauge. A photograph, somehow preserved. These artifacts are the seeds around which the Archivists grow, the last anchors to a reality that no longer applies. The Archivists don't worship these objects—worship is a human concept—but they protect them with the fierce incomprehension of machines trying to execute a command they can no longer parse.

The Conductors visit the Archivists regularly, their ribbon bodies spelling out complex equations across the pyramids' surfaces. This is the closest thing to communication between the two types: the Conductors bringing new data to be preserved, the Archivists offering up historical patterns that might solve current problems in the mechanical ecosystem. Together, they maintain a civilization without citizens, a culture without consciousness as humans would recognize it.

Yet for all their alien nature, there are moments—when the light from the bioluminescent pools catches the moisture just right, when the industrial roar quiets to a whisper—where an observer might detect something almost like loneliness in the way a Conductor pauses before an Archivist's wall of preserved forms. Something almost like hope in how an Archivist's magnetic field strengthens when a new type of creature appears on the horizon.

They are the custodians of a world that transformed rather than died, the inheritors of humanity's industrial ambitions reformed into something utterly inhuman. They maintain the glass plains not out of duty or desire, but because maintenance is what they are. They exist in the space between animate and inanimate, between purpose and purposelessness, forever archiving a story that no one will ever read, conducting a symphony that plays for no audience but itself.

In the bruised light of eternal twilight, surrounded by the writhing carpet of their mechanical kin, the Conductors and Archivists persist. They are what consciousness looks like when stripped of all human referents, what memory becomes when it no longer serves the living. They are the glass plains' truest inhabitants: neither mourning what was lost nor celebrating what was found, simply being—magnificently, terrifyingly, eternally being.
