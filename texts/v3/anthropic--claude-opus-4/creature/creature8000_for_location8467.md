The people of the Weeping Galleries move like water themselves—fluid, patient, finding the path of least resistance through their limestone world. Their bodies have adapted over generations: narrow shoulders, flexible spines that bend and twist with practiced ease, feet that grip wet stone with spreading toes. They are pale as the limestone that surrounds them, their skin taking on an almost translucent quality in the constant moisture, blue veins visible beneath.

They call themselves the Vith'arani—"those who flow downward"—and their entire culture bends around the concept of yielding, of finding the spaces between. Children are born small and stay small longer than those who live beneath open sky. Their first words are often whispered; they learn early that sound in the galleries has its own life, that a careless shout can cascade through chambers and return as accusation.

The Vith'arani sleep curled like ammonites, pressed against the stone walls on their reed mats, families fitted together in configurations that change nightly as children grow and bodies shift. They wake to the sound of water—always water—and their first act each day is the wringing of the mats, a ritual as old as their inhabitation. The water pressed from the reeds runs into channels, joining the eternal flow.

Their social structure mirrors their architecture: dense, layered, interconnected. There are no true leaders, only those who know—the Keeper of Pools who understands which waters are sweet and which carry too much limestone, the Path Singers who can navigate the galleries by acoustic echo alone, the Memory Holders who maintain the oral histories in the precise, whispered cadences that carry best through stone. Knowledge is shared in the pools, passed in low voices while hands work at washing or grinding.

The Vith'arani have a taxonomy of wetness—seventeen words for different kinds of moisture, from the *siv'ani* (the first cold drops that wake you) to the *mor'essen* (the slick film on stone that means passage is safe). They read the water like others read weather, knowing from its taste and temperature what changes might be happening in the world far above.

Their art is subtle, almost invisible—patterns worn into stone by repeated touching, fossils polished and arranged in meaningful configurations, the careful cultivation of mineral deposits that form curtains of flowstone over generations. They sing, but softly, their harmonies designed to use the echo rather than fight it. Multiple voices in different chambers can create songs that seem to come from the stone itself.

Marriage among the Vith'arani is a negotiation of space as much as hearts. New couples must find or create their niche, literally—carving out their few square feet or inheriting them from those who have joined the ancestors. The ceremonies happen at the pools, with cupped water poured from hand to hand, the couple learning to match the rhythm of their breathing to the eternal drip.

Their dead are given to the deepest pools, weighted with stone and lowered into waters that have never known sunlight. They believe the bodies become limestone eventually, returning to the galleries in the most fundamental way. Sometimes, in the darkest chambers, they claim to hear the ancestors in the sound of water on stone—not voices, exactly, but intentions, warnings, approvals.

The children of the galleries play games of silence and flexibility—seeing who can traverse a passage without disturbing a single drop, who can hold their breath longest underwater in the pools, who can identify others by the sound of their movement three chambers away. They keep blind fish as pets sometimes, in small pools carved into their family's platforms, feeding them crumbs of ground fungus and watching their pale forms circle endlessly in their miniature worlds.

Trade with the outside happens rarely, at the surface openings where the galleries meet daylight. The Vith'arani emerge blinking and uncomfortable, bringing up the products of their patience—perfectly polished stones, fungi dried and pressed into dense cakes, water from the deepest pools that supposedly has healing properties. They trade for what they cannot make: metal tools, precious wood, news of the world above. But they never stay long. The open sky makes them anxious, the absence of echo disorienting.

In the galleries, they have a saying: "Water finds the truth of stone." They live by this principle, allowing their lives to be shaped by their environment rather than fighting it. They are not a desperate people, despite their density. They have found a strange peace in their compression, a philosophy that values yielding over resistance, community over solitude, the collective breath over the individual voice.

At night—though night and day mean little in the galleries—they perform the ritual of the Silent Hour. All voices cease. All movement stops. Only the water continues its patient work. In this silence, they say, you can hear the galleries thinking, the slow thoughts of stone and time. When the hour ends, life resumes, but gentler, as if reminded of the vast patience that surrounds them.

The Vith'arani know every surface of their world by touch. They could navigate blindfolded—and often do, in the darkest passages. Their fingerprints are worn nearly smooth from constant contact with wet stone. They leave subtle marks—a particular way of gripping a handhold, a pattern of wear on a commonly used path. To outsiders, the galleries might seem unchanging, but the Vith'arani read the stone like a constantly evolving text, written by bodies and time.

They are not the first to inhabit these spaces—sometimes they find traces of earlier peoples, tools of unfamiliar design, chambers carved in different styles. But they don't think of themselves as inheritors. They are simply the current iteration of life in the galleries, like the water that flows through the same channels while always being different water. Someday, they know, others may come after them, read the subtle marks they've left, and make their own accommodations with the weeping stone.

This is who they are: a people compressed but not oppressed, shaped by their environment into something resilient and strange. They have learned to love the boundaries that define them, to find freedom in limitation, to hear music in the endless percussion of water on stone. The Weeping Galleries are not their prison but their chrysalis, and they have no desire to emerge as anything other than what they are—the Vith'arani, those who flow downward, finding life in the spaces between drops.
