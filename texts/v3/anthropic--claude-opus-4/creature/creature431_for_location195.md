The Tenders move through the perpetual mist like gray herons through marsh fog, their regulation suits modified far beyond company specifications. They've sewn strips of the phosphorescent moss into the seams, creating constellation patterns that help them recognize each other in the machine-haze. Some have replaced their standard-issue helmets with cobbled respirators that wheeze and click in counterpoint to the industrial symphony, filters packed with dried moss that they claim tastes better than the recycled air.

They rarely speak above the din, having developed a language of light-signals using their helmet lamps and the reflective patches they've adhered to their gloves. Quick flashes mean different things: *pressure drop in Sector Seven*, *collection arm malfunction*, *something moving in the sealed sections again*. They walk with an odd gait, having learned to match their steps to the rhythm of the great extraction engines—it reduces fatigue, they say, to move in harmony with the Deep Pulse.

Most Tenders work alone, each claiming a territory of pillars like shepherds with mechanical flocks. They know their columns individually, can tell by the pitch of the condensation nets' ringing whether the moisture content is optimal. They've named them, too—*Old Weeper*, *The Twins*, *Broken Crown*—and leave small offerings at their bases: interesting stones from the excavation debris, origami folded from water-resistant maintenance manuals, tiny shrines of wire and moss.

But in the spaces between shifts, in the maintenance corridors where the sound dampens just enough for conversation, they gather. They share thermos-coffee that tastes of minerals and trade stories of what they've seen in the fog. They speak of the Drain Dancers—figures glimpsed between the pillars who move too fluidly to be human, who seem to know where the next equipment failure will occur. They whisper about the Deep Listeners who press their ears to the sealed barriers and claim to hear singing from the other side, a sound that makes the moss glow brighter.

Some Tenders never leave. They build hidden shelters in the dead spaces between machinery, little nests lined with heat-reflecting emergency blankets where they sleep between shifts rather than face the journey topside. They've grown pale as cave fish, their eyes huge behind their fogged visors. These Deep Dwellers know every echo, every shadow, every place where the temperature pockets create small oases of comfort. They've learned to read the moss patterns like weather forecasts, to predict equipment failures by the way condensation pools in certain corners.

The company tolerates them because they're effective. The Deep Dwellers can diagnose problems before the sensors register them, can coax failing machinery to last another few weeks through careful adjustments and whispered encouragements. They leave notes for the surface crews in a cramped script, written on moisture-proof paper: *Third turbine bearing developing harmonic—needs love, not replacement* or *Collection pool Twelve-Alpha has started dreaming—check pH levels*.

New Tenders last three months on average before requesting transfer topside. But those who stay longer begin to change. They start hearing music in the machinery. They develop preferences for certain fogs over others. They begin to see patterns in the condensation drops that fall from the nets, spend their breaks creating elaborate charts trying to decode what they insist is a message. They report conversations with other Tenders in sectors that have been unmanned for weeks.

The children of the Drain Works—those born to Tenders who chose to stay—are something else entirely. They play in the mist like it's their natural element, racing between the pillars on jury-rigged scooters that run on compressed air diverted from the pneumatic systems. They attend makeshift schools in repurposed control rooms where they learn to read system diagrams alongside ancient poems about rain. They know which mosses are safe to eat and which will make you see colors that don't exist topside. They've never known sunlight, but they speak of the surface world with the detached interest of anthropologists discussing a foreign culture.

The oldest of them, now in their teens, have begun to explore the sealed sections. They claim the barriers were meant to keep something in, not out. They've found ways through, spots where the industrial welding grows thin, where the moss grows so thick it's dissolved the metal beneath. They return with strange artifacts—pieces of machinery that predate any known manufacturing technique, crystals that hum in harmony with the extraction engines, water samples that refuse to freeze no matter how cold the storage units run.

The Maintenance Chiefs pretend not to notice. The quarterly efficiency reports show the Drain Works operating at peak capacity. The water flows upward. The city drinks. And in the depths, something that might have once been purely human continues its slow adaptation to the rhythms of artificial rain, finding grace in the spaces between purpose and survival, between the designed environment and whatever insists on growing there anyway.
