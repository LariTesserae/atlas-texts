The Shapers came to this plateau when the stone was still warm from its birth, drawn by dreams of a city that could not rot. They are a patient people, long-limbed and deliberate, their skin bearing a faint green-gray tinge from generations of living among the moss. Their eyes have adapted to the perpetual mist—pupils that dilate wide and irises of pale amber that catch and hold what little light filters through the vapor.

They dress in fabric woven from the moss itself, harvested in careful strips that regrow within seasons. The garments hang loose and heavy with moisture, never truly dry, developing their own ecosystems of minute flowering plants that root in the weave. The eldest Shapers are gardens unto themselves, their robes hosting rare species found nowhere else in the city.

Children here learn silence before speech. The thick air swallows sound, so the Shapers developed a language of gesture and touch, hands moving in precise patterns through the mist. When they must speak aloud, they do so directly into each other's ears, lips nearly touching, sharing breath in the humid air. Their voices, when heard, are low and liquid, shaped by centuries of adaptation to this muffling atmosphere.

The work of stone-shaping passes from parent to child through apprenticeship at the water wheels. Young Shapers spend years learning to read the basalt's crystalline structure, feeling with their fingertips for the planes where the rock wishes to split. They know each district's stone by touch—the slightly coarser grain near the eastern springs, the glass-smooth columns of the central plaza, the iron-warm blocks that refuse all moss near the plateau's heart.

They measure time not in days but in erosions—the span it takes for a new channel to wear smooth, for a bridge's edges to soften under passing feet, for a tower's sharp corners to begin their rounding. A human lifetime encompasses perhaps ten erosions. The very eldest remember twenty or more, their memories stretching back to when certain districts were merely plans traced in the moss.

The Shapers eat what the moss provides and what the waters bring. Certain pools breed translucent fish that never see sunlight, navigating by pressure and vibration alone. The moss itself, when prepared correctly, yields a bitter tea that sustains and mildly intoxicates. The flowering vines produce seed pods that must be collected at the exact moment of ripeness—too early and they're poisonous, too late and they dissolve into the ever-present moisture.

Their dead they give to the moss. Bodies are laid in the shallow pools among the ferns, where they sink slowly into the green embrace. Within a season, nothing remains but the clothes, which are harvested and rewoven with new moss, carrying forward the garden-patterns of the deceased. They believe the moss holds all their ancestors, that the city's green carpet is woven from every Shaper who ever lived.

In the mist-divided districts, different traditions evolve. The North-Facers refuse all plant matter on their bodies, going naked but for bronze ornaments that patina green in the moisture. The Channel-Keepers breed elaborate varieties of vine, competing to create the most complex hanging gardens. The Stone-Singers claim to hear voices in the basalt's crystalline structure, spending days with their ears pressed to newly exposed rock faces.

Yet all share the same patience, the same acceptance of the city's rhythms. They do not hurry—cannot hurry in air so thick it resists rapid movement. Their bronze tools ring against stone in measured beats. Their water wheels turn at the exact speed of necessity. They build as if they have forever, because in their moss-shrouded world, forever is the only timespan that matters.

When young Shapers reach maturity, they undertake the Descent—lowering themselves on vine ropes down the cliff faces to see what lies below the clouds. Most return within days, speechless and changed. Some never return at all. Those who come back take up their chisels with new purpose, as if they've glimpsed something in the lower world that makes their stone-shaping urgent despite its slowness.

The city's edges creep outward by inches each generation, new towers rising from previously untouched stone. But at the center, the oldest structures remain exactly as they were carved, preserved by the moss and the constant moisture. The Shapers could tell you, if they spoke of such things, that these first buildings were not built but discovered—that the original settlers found them already implied in the rock, waiting only for bronze tools to reveal their forms.

They dream, when they sleep in their moss-draped chambers, of a time when the entire plateau will be shaped, when every possible tower has been freed from the stone, when the water channels form a perfect mandala visible only from above. But they also know this completion will never come—that there will always be one more whisper in the basalt, one more form waiting to be revealed. This is their comfort and their calling: to be eternal midwives to a city that births itself from volcanic stone, one careful chisel-stroke at a time.
