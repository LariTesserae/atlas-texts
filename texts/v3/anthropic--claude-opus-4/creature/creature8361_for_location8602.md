The Drifters make their home here, though "home" implies a fixity they've long since dissolved. They were human once—or something enough like human that the distinction hardly matters now. Over generations uncounted, they've adapted to this place where water and air intermingle, where time pools and eddies rather than flows.

Their bodies have become suggestions. A Drifter standing still might be mistaken for a heat mirage, a vertical distortion in the saturated air. Only when they move—and they're always moving, always circulating—do their forms coalesce enough to comprehend. They have the approximate shape of tall, attenuated people, but their surfaces run like rain on glass. Their skin, if it can be called that, is a membrane that exchanges moisture freely with the atmosphere. They're translucent in the way of deep water, their interiors showing shifting patterns of current and density rather than organs.

They travel in loose spirals through the city, following paths worn into reality itself by countless repetitions. A Drifter might take three days to cross a plaza, or three seconds—the distinction has ceased to matter. They move by allowing themselves to precipitate forward, condensing slightly more in the direction they wish to go, letting the pressure differential pull them along. Groups of them flow together like separate streams converging, their boundaries merging and separating without ceremony or acknowledgment.

They communicate through temperature. A Drifter warming itself by a few degrees might express contentment, curiosity, or the need to share information—context determines meaning. Complex conversations play out as subtle thermodynamic exchanges, ideas spreading through groups as waves of cool and warmth. They have a written language too, though "written" isn't quite right—they alter the condensation patterns on surfaces, creating texts that exist only as long as the moisture holds its shape. These messages layer over each other on the weeping walls, palimpsests of meaning that shift with humidity and time.

Their children—if that term applies—begin as dense knots of fog that gather in the city's many alcoves. The adult Drifters tend these condensation points with infinite patience, adjusting the temperature and airflow around them, teaching them the patterns of circulation before they're coherent enough to move on their own. It takes decades for a new Drifter to fully precipitate into being. Some never quite manage it, dispersing back into the general moisture, which the others accept without grief—what disperses will gather again, somewhere, somewhen.

They cultivate the moisture-gardens with movements that might be dancing or might be engineering. By circulating in particular patterns, they encourage certain plants to grow, certain flows to establish themselves. They've learned to read the city's breathing, to predict where new springs will bubble up through the stone, where the air will thicken enough to support the floating salamanders they sometimes herd from place to place. Their understanding of the city's systems is complete but wordless—they know it the way they know their own circulation.

The Drifters worship, though their religion has no name and makes no claims. They gather where the city's mysteries are thickest—where stairs lead up and down simultaneously, where water flows in Möbius strips, where buildings exist inside themselves. Here they perform slow rotations that might be prayer or might be maintenance, tending to the places where reality wears thin. They believe—if belief is the right word—that the city is a single enormous lung, and they are its breath. Their purpose is to keep circulating, to prevent the system from reaching true equilibrium, which would be indistinguishable from death.

They have no possessions but the patterns they carry in their movement, no names but the thermal signatures they've developed over lifetimes of circulation. They build nothing permanent, write no lasting histories. Their culture consists entirely of flows—traditional routes through the city, conventional ways of spiraling around obstacles, inherited rhythms of expansion and contraction.

Sometimes a Drifter grows too dense, too defined. When this happens, they make their way to the city's edge, where the plateau drops away into clouded nothing. There they stand at the precipice and simply... let go. They allow their cohesion to fail, their moisture to rejoin the general atmosphere. The others gather to witness this, creating slow vortices that might be funeral rites or might simply be the natural response to a change in air pressure.

And sometimes, rarely, the reverse occurs. A Drifter becomes so diffuse they risk dispersing entirely. When this threatens, the others converge, circulating around the fading one, creating a pressure differential that helps them recondens. They'll maintain this formation for however long it takes—days, years, decades—until their companion either stabilizes or disperses despite their efforts.

This is how they live, have always lived, will always live, in their city that breathes water—circulating endlessly through streets that forget themselves, tending gardens that neither grow nor die, existing in the space between states, citizens of the eternal, bright, damp afternoon that never quite arrives at evening.
