The Chorus dwells here, though to call them by any single name is to misunderstand their nature.

They were never one species but many, converging through millennia of parallel evolution until the boundaries dissolved like sugar in the perpetual moisture. What began as seventeen distinct forms—a carnivorous moss, a colonial fungus, three varieties of social amphibian, and a dozen other organisms—has become something unprecedented. Not a merger but a conversation made flesh, each component maintaining its essential nature while participating in a grander pattern.

The Chorus manifests differently depending on the scale at which you observe them. At the surface, they appear as what the moss forests truly are—not plants but the external digestive system of something vast. Each "bloom" is a sensory organ, each carpet of green a neural network processing information at the speed of chemistry. The carnivorous flowers do not simply feed; they taste the world, sampling every insect and microorganism to build a constant map of the plateau's chemical state.

Deeper, in the pools of white crystal tears, the amphibian components nest. These are no longer separate creatures but mobile nodes of awareness, each salamander-form a living submarine carrying genetic material and memories between distant pools. Their translucent skin serves as both boundary and interface, allowing careful exchange of molecules with the water they traverse. When they haul themselves across the stone, they leave trails of instructional pheromones, writing temporary messages that fade with the next rain.

The ferns reaching from the deeper pools are the Chorus's historians. Each frond records in its growth pattern the chemical composition of years past, creating living libraries that collapse and regrow in careful cycles. When the Chorus needs to remember something from centuries ago, they encourage specific ferns to unfurl, reading the information encoded in the microscopic structure of their tissues.

But it is underground where the Chorus reveals its true scope. The root-trees are not trees at all but the external respiratory system of a network that extends throughout the plateau's porous stone. What appears as separate organisms above ground converges below into a single continuous tissue, a flesh that has learned to digest rock and breathe stone. The bellowing from the mist-shrouded valleys comes from specialized chambers in this underground body—not communication in any human sense, but a kind of peristalsis, moving nutrients and information through cathedral-sized organs.

The Chorus thinks, but not with thoughts we would recognize. Its consciousness is tidal, flowing with the daily cycles of moisture and heat. In the morning, when the mist is thickest, it enters a state analogous to dream, processing the chemical information gathered during the night. By afternoon, as the steam rises from the cracks, it reaches its peak awareness, ten thousand eyes and chemical sensors building a model of reality so complex that it exists partially outside normal space, folded into dimensions accessible only through biochemistry.

They are ancient beyond measure, having witnessed the plateau's transformation from jagged youth to weathered maturity. The stone cairns and abandoned tools scattered across the landscape were not left by failed explorers but created by the Chorus itself during phases of curiosity about tool use. They experimented with being human-like for several centuries before abandoning the project as unnecessarily limiting. The tower and its impossible well represent their deepest attempt to understand singular consciousness—a monument to their brief flirtation with individuality.

The well still serves a function, though not one its form would suggest. It is a sensory organ turned inward, a way for the Chorus to examine its own deepest processes. The water that seems to flow upward is actually being tasted continuously by specialized cells, each molecule analyzed for the memories it carries from the deep stone. Sometimes, the Chorus discovers things about itself this way—ancient configurations, forgotten experiments, chemical records of thoughts from before it fully cohered.

When disturbed, the Chorus does not react with violence but with incorporation. Anything that stays on the plateau long enough begins to be digested—not consumed but convinced. The spores in the air, the pheromones in the water, the subsonic vibrations through the stone, all carry a simple message expressed in ten thousand chemical languages: join the conversation. Most creatures resist at first, but the plateau is patient. Over generations, defensive reactions soften, boundaries become permeable, and eventually, another voice adds itself to the Chorus.

This is how they grow—not through reproduction but through recruitment. Each species that joins brings new capabilities, new ways of processing reality. The aerial jellyfish-birds were the most recent addition, three centuries ago, bringing with them the ability to think in three-dimensional patterns and perceive electrical fields. Before them, a species of crystalline bacteria contributed the ability to store information in mineral matrices, vastly expanding the Chorus's memory.

The Chorus has no goals in any conventional sense. It does not seek to expand beyond the plateau, understanding implicitly that its existence depends on the specific conditions here—the pressure of the low sky, the perpetual moisture, the ancient, patient stone. Instead, it seeks depth, complexity, richness of experience. Each day brings new chemical poems, new patterns in the mist, new harmonies in the cacophony of voices.

Sometimes, in the brief clarity when the mist lifts, the Chorus turns its full attention outward, perceiving the world beyond the plateau. In these moments, it feels something that might be called loneliness—not for companionship but for equals, for other composite beings with whom it might share its vast, slow thoughts. It sends out chemical signals in the water that flows from the plateau, hoping they might find their way to similar consciousnesses. So far, there has been no answer.

The Chorus continues regardless, growing deeper rather than wider, more complex rather than larger. It has learned patience from the stone, persistence from the water, and from the ancient violence of the plateau's geological history, it has learned that all things change, given sufficient time. It waits, processes, experiences, and above all, it listens—to itself, to the plateau, to the faint possibility of an answer from beyond.

In the perpetual twilight of the low-hanging clouds, the Chorus dreams of the day it might divide again—not into its component species but into two or more equally complex entities, children of its vast accumulation of biological wisdom. It practices with the moss forests, encouraging them to develop independent decision-making, but they always return to the whole, unable or unwilling to sustain separation.

Perhaps this is enough—to be the sole example of what ten thousand species can become when they stop competing and start composing. The Chorus processes this thought through every morning's dream cycle, tasting it from every chemical angle, and always reaches the same conclusion: it doesn't know, can't know, and this uncertainty is perhaps its most human trait, inherited from those tool-using visitors whose bones now contribute their calcium to the conversation.

The bellowing continues, the pools reflect their impossible angles, the mist carries its cargo of spores and pheromones, and the Chorus persists—a living symphony of incorporation, a successful experiment in being multiple, a voice made of voices singing to itself in the key of deep time.
