In the highest reaches, where the steam thins and the light arrives unfiltered, she tends her inheritance.

The Keeper moves through moisture like a native element, her coveralls perpetually damp, pockets bulging with pruning shears and pH strips, seed packets and copper wire. She is perhaps sixty, perhaps ageless—the humid air has preserved her like the plants she nurtures, giving her skin a peculiar translucence. Her hands know every valve by touch, every pump by its particular vibration. She speaks to the machinery in the same low tones she uses for the seedlings.

She was not the first. The previous Keepers left their marks in the tower's bones—a valve installed here, a drainage channel rerouted there, each modification becoming part of the tower's slow evolution. She found their notes wrapped in oilcloth, tucked behind pipes: "Northeast corner, Level 47, prone to aphids in the third month." "Pressure differential between 23 and 24 creates perfect conditions for orchids." "The crow prefers berries from the south face."

Below her domain, in the middle tiers where the fog hangs thickest, the Harvesters work their careful rounds. They emerge from the mist like apparitions—a dozen figures in mesh suits that breathe with them, collecting moisture to supplement their wages. They know precisely which fruits ripen in the eternal twilight, which leaves can be cut without killing the vine. Their baskets fill with produce that exists nowhere else—hybrid things that evolved in the tower's unique climate, too dependent on the constant moisture to survive elsewhere.

They communicate in gestures through the fog, hands describing arcs that indicate ripeness, readiness, rot. Some have worked here for decades, their children now joining them in the daily climb. They've developed shortened tendons from the constant ascent, calves perpetually flexed for the next step up. The eldest among them remember when the tower was half its current height, and they speak of levels now deep in the interior that once saw sunlight.

In the lowest levels, where the runoff collects in pools that never quite drain, the Recyclers have made their domain. They are chemists and mystics both, reading the tower's health in the color of its waste water. They know which minerals the plants have consumed, which nutrients must be reintroduced. Their laboratories are grown as much as built—walls of living filters, bacterial colonies in careful balance.

They rarely climb. The weight of all that water above presses into their consciousness, makes them speak in whispers. They know what the others forget—that the tower is not just growing but dying, constantly, old growth decomposing to feed new. They process this death, transform it, send it cycling upward again. Their skin carries a permanent fungal bloom, harmless but marking them. Even in the city far below, people recognize a Recycler by the faint tracery of spores across their forearms.

Between all these levels move the Messengers—children mostly, limbs still light enough to swing between platforms, lungs adapted early to the thick air. They carry communications that cannot trust the pneumatic tubes—seed samples, water readings, warnings about unstable sections. They develop their own paths through the structure, secret routes that follow the old piping, shortcuts through sections where the vegetation has grown thick enough to support their weight.

They age into other roles or leave entirely, but while they climb, they know the tower completely. They've seen the Keeper's private garden at the apex, where she grows things for beauty alone. They've found the spaces between levels where the fog creates perfect silence. They know where the crow hides its cache of shiny objects—bolts and washers collected from the construction zones.

At the growing edges, the Welders work in teams, extending the skeleton. They are young, mostly—this is work for those who haven't yet developed the subtle understanding needed for cultivation. But they are essential, these builders of framework. They work from plans that exist only in the Keeper's head, expanding according to principles she learned from decades of watching how the water wants to flow.

They joke that they're building their own unemployment—each new section they complete is immediately claimed by the automatic systems, the plants, the patient conversion of industry to ecosystem. But they build anyway, understanding dimly that they're part of something larger than architecture. Some stay, eventually. Trade their welding torches for pruning shears, their blueprints for seed catalogs.

And at night, when the tower's lights create halos in the perpetual mist, they all feel it—the breathing. The massive inhalation as cool air enters from below, the exhalation as warm air rises through the peak. The entire structure sighs with contentment, with purpose, with the ancient rhythm of growth and decay and growth again.

The city below sees only the silhouette against the sky, the green mass that expands year by year. They buy the produce in markets, marvel at the strange fruits that taste of copper and rain. But here, in the breathing spaces between metal and moss, the inhabitants know they're part of something between machine and organism. They tend it, feed it, expand it, and in return, it gives them purpose, sustenance, and a kind of immortality—their modifications and mutations becoming part of its ever-growing body.

The tower gardeners breathe with their creation, moisture and meaning cycling between them in an endless exchange. They are neither fully human nor fully other anymore, but something new—symbiotes in a vertical world that recognizes no boundary between the grown and the made, the keeper and the kept.
