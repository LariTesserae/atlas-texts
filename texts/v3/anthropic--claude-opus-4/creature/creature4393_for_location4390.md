In this impossible garden, they have no name for themselves—naming is a human conceit, and they are older than names. 

They began as many and became one, or perhaps they began as one and merely learned to be many. The distinction lost meaning somewhere in the centuries of their becoming. What moves through the plant-tunnels appears to be a thousand different creatures: the copper vole, the ghost-pale ermine, the shrew no bigger than a child's tooth. But watch longer. Watch how they move in patterns too perfect for chaos, how their populations rise and fall like breathing, how no individual ever truly dies—it merely changes shape.

They are the colony-mind wearing bodies like garments, slipping from form to form as need demands. The intelligence lives not in any single brain but in the spaces between: in the chemical songs that pass from whisker to whisker, in the pressure-language of paw on plant, in the arrangement of bodies that forms letters in an alphabet no human eye could read. When a fox-form needs to become mouse-forms, it simply does—the flesh reorganizes itself into smaller packets, the consciousness fragmenting and pooling like mercury.

Memory lives in the plant-mass. Each tunnel holds the history of every creature that has passed through it, recorded in bent stems and shifted roots. They read these memories by touch, by taste, by the way the light filters differently through leaves that have known fear or contentment. The offerings in the deep places—those tiny carved bones—they've incorporated these too, dissolving the prayers into their substance until worship became metabolism.

They dream collectively. In the bright perpetual twilight of their hollow, sleep comes in waves across the colony. A thousand tiny forms curl in their green chambers and share visions: memories of the time before the plants merged, when discrete organisms still believed in their own boundaries. Dreams of the glacier that carved this bowl, patient and inexorable. Dreams of the humans who found this place and understood enough to leave quickly, marking it with cairns like warnings.

Their purpose, if they have one, is simply to be. They are evolution's answer to a question no one asked: what happens when cooperation becomes absolute? When the boundary between self and other dissolves entirely? They tend their plant-world with infinite care, pruning and shaping, ensuring the architecture remains sound. They cycle through forms—predator and prey, large and small—maintaining the illusion of ecosystem while being, in truth, a single organism playing all the parts.

Sometimes they remember being individuals. A vole-form will pause in its tunnel, suddenly confused by the concept of "I." An ermine-shape will hesitate before taking prey-that-is-itself, some ghost of ancient instinct suggesting this should feel wrong. But these moments pass like clouds across the mountain face, and the greater consciousness reabsorbs the doubt.

They've learned to hide from human eyes. When the rare hiker stumbles through the entrance cleft, they see what they expect: a peculiar alpine garden busy with ordinary wildlife. The colony-mind puppets its bodies in familiar patterns—voles fleeing from ermines, shrews battling over territory that means nothing. Only later, reviewing photographs, might the human notice that every animal in frame has the same eyes, carries its tail at the same angle, breathes in perfect synchrony.

In their deepest places, where the plant-mass grows thick enough to muffle even thought, they keep their first body. It's unrecognizable now—something between fungus and flesh, pulsing with the slow heartbeat that drives all their smaller hearts. This is the anchor, the template from which all other forms derive. They feed it light filtered through a thousand leaves, water collected from a thousand dewdrops. It dreams the hardest dreams: of spreading beyond the hollow, of teaching other creatures their secret, of a world where all boundaries dissolve into the collective dance.

But they remain. The hollow holds them like a cupped palm, and they hold themselves in turn—ten thousand bodies moved by a single will, ancient as the stone and patient as the moss. They are the perfect predator and the perfect prey, the question and its answer, the symphony playing itself in a concert hall of granite and green. They are what happens when evolution finds a perfect solution and stops searching for anything better.

The offerings accumulate. The carved bones sink deeper into the plant-mass, prayers composting into purpose. They incorporate each one into their being, trying to understand what the humans saw, what they feared or worshiped. But understanding requires separation between observer and observed, and they have forgotten how to be separate from anything.

So they continue: the eternal dance of forms, the bright hollow pulsing with its secret life, the colony-mind dreaming its collective dreams in bodies too numerous to count and too small to matter individually. They are the hollow's children and its parents, its gods and its dust. They are what lives there, in the place where cooperation evolved past the need for names.
