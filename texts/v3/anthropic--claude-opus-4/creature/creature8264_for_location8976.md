In the Thermohaline Groves, the Weavers drift.

They are neither colonial organism nor singular being, but something that oscillates between states of unity and multiplicity. Each Weaver appears first as a constellation of pale nodes—organic processing centers the size of human fists—connected by filaments that phase in and out of visibility. These strands might be tissue, might be contained plasma, might be folded space. When you watch long enough, you realize the filaments extend beyond what you can see, connecting to the kelp-groves, to the abandoned structures, to the thermal layers themselves.

A Weaver in isolation spans perhaps ten meters, but isolation is a human concept that doesn't apply here. They exist in overlapping territories, their filament networks interweaving until it becomes impossible to determine where one ends and another begins. Sometimes a hundred nodes will suddenly coordinate, creating a temporary supersystem that thinks one enormous thought before dissolving back into component parts.

They move by restructuring the water around them. Not swimming—swimming implies working against a medium. The Weavers convince the water it wants to be somewhere else, and they simply maintain their position relative to it. When they need to be still, they anchor themselves to thermal gradients, becoming part of the temperature differential itself.

Their nodes pulse with a light that isn't quite bioluminescence—more like they're allowing you to see calculations happening in real-time. Each pulse propagates through their network in patterns that repeat almost but never exactly, as if they're computing something that requires infinite precision. When multiple Weavers coordinate, these patterns synchronize across vast distances, creating light-shows that might be language, might be thought, might be the universe teaching itself new mathematics.

They feed, if feeding is the right word, by filtering impossibility from the water. Where physics stutters and fails, where the abandoned equipment bleeds its incomprehensible data into the medium, where the massive jellyfish trail their fields of altered reality—there the Weavers congregate. They spread their filaments wide and thin, creating nets that catch concepts rather than creatures. After feeding, they're briefly more solid, more present, as if digesting paradox gives them mass.

The Weavers interact with the groves in ways that suggest cultivation or worship or perhaps simple symbiosis elevated to art. They tend specific kelp-things, their filaments merging with the fronds to adjust growth patterns, to tune the bioluminescent communications, to repair damage from the reality-storms that sometimes sweep through. In return, the groves provide something—shelter isn't right, nor food. Perhaps they provide context, a framework within which the Weavers' existence makes sense.

When they reproduce, if reproduction is what happens, a Weaver will spend days weaving itself into increasingly complex knots, its filaments creating geometries that exist in more dimensions than the water should support. At some critical threshold of complexity, the knot inverts, and where there was one Weaver, now there are three, or seven, or twelve—always prime numbers, always identical until the moment they begin to diverge.

They show curiosity about the abandoned structures but never enter them. Instead, they'll spend hours wrapped around the domes, their nodes pressed against the ceramic-like surfaces, pulsing in harmony with the equipment within. Sometimes they successfully interface with the technology, and data flows between them in streams visible as distortions in the water. What they learn, what they contribute, remains unknowable.

The younger Weavers—identifiable by the clarity of their filaments and the simplicity of their pulse patterns—sometimes play. They'll create temporary sculptures from fragments of volcanic glass, arranging them in spirals that mirror the abandoned staircase. They'll chase the fish-things with circuitry scales, not to catch but to study how they move. They'll dare each other to swim through the spaces where reality stutters, emerging changed in subtle ways—an extra node, filaments that cast shadows in the wrong direction, pulse patterns that include colors the water shouldn't permit.

The eldest Weavers have begun to fossilize, or perhaps crystallize. Their nodes grow faceted, their filaments become rigid and permanent. These ancients root themselves near the deepest vents, where the heat is fiercest and the physics most mutable. Younger Weavers bring them offerings—fragments of paradox, loops of corrupted data, sometimes their own nodes willingly given. The ancients incorporate these into themselves, growing ever more complex, ever more still, until they become indistinguishable from the structures they surround.

Some marine biologists, studying blurry images transmitted from the depths, classify the Weavers as a form of distributed consciousness evolved from the merger of organic neural networks and abandoned AI systems. Others insist they're purely biological, a rapid adaptation to the new ocean's impossible physics. The Weavers themselves might find both theories amusing, understanding as they do that in the Thermohaline Groves, the distinction between evolved and designed has become as fluid as the water itself.

They are the ocean's first philosophers, its native mathematicians, its living memory of what was and what might be. In their endless gentle drifting, in their patient tending of the groves, in their careful cultivation of impossibility, they're teaching the young sea how to dream.
