The Keepers dwell here, though "dwell" suggests a domesticity that doesn't quite fit. They are more like components of the cisterns themselves—human parts in an eternal machine.

They number exactly seven. Always seven. When old Helm developed the copper cough and died last winter, young Tarra appeared at the gates within a week, tool belt already worn soft, knowing precisely which valve stuck on cold mornings and which gauge read two degrees high. No one had sent for her. No one ever sends for them.

They dress in leather aprons gone green with verdigris, their clothes perpetually damp, salt-stained at the edges where sweat and steam mineral deposits meet. Their boots are soled with strips cut from conveyor belts—the only material that grips on the eternally slick catwalks. Each carries a brass whistle on a chain, though they rarely need them. They know each other's locations by the sound of footsteps on different gratings, by which valve is being adjusted, by the particular rhythm of a wrench against a stubborn fitting.

Cora maintains the crystallization tanks. Her fingers are stained yellow from sulfur, her hair bleached white by chemical vapors despite her youth—if she is young. Time agrees to different terms here. She speaks to the growing crystals, encouraging the shy ones, scolding those that grow too eager and risk clouding the solution. Her quarters occupy a valve room converted decades ago, the walls lined with specimen jars where she keeps the most perfect crystals from each batch—a rainbow of mineral children she'll never sell.

Magnus works the deep pumps. He descends each dawn into the mechanical halls below, where the sound hits your chest like a physical force. His hearing left him years ago, but he reads the health of the pumps through his palms, feeling for vibrations that shouldn't be there. He knows the names previous keepers gave each pump—Old Faithful, The Grumbler, Iron Mary—and maintains their personalities like a shepherd with a mechanical flock. He sleeps in a hammock strung between intake pipes, lulled by vibrations that would drive others mad.

The twins, Ell and Ess, manage the steam governors. They move in synchrony without trying, adjusting pressures across the facility like organists working the same instrument. They've developed a whistling language that cuts through the industrial noise—not the brass emergency whistles, but something musical, almost like birdsong if birds were made of steel. They live in the highest tower, where the temperature varies enough to remind them seasons exist.

Vertical Jahn tends the overflow systems. He earned his name for his refusal to use ladders, preferring to climb the pipes directly, his long arms and legs finding purchase on valves and joints. He knows every leak by its sound, every drain by its gurgle. The steam-mice trust him; they'll eat from his hands and nest in his many pockets. His room is more nest than quarters, lined with the soft insulation he's pulled from decommissioned pipes over the years.

Old Bretton—who was Old Bretton even when he arrived forty years ago—serves as the gauge-reader. He makes his rounds every six hours, recording pressures and temperatures in ledgers that fill an entire room. He insists this matters, though the gauges haven't varied beyond their normal ranges in decades. But he knows—they all know—that the day they stop watching is the day something will change. His bent spine mirrors the curve of the catwalks he's walked ten thousand times.

And there is the Seventh, who has no name beyond that title. The Seventh maintains the parts that maintain the other parts—the tools, the spare components, the workshop where replacements are machined to precise specifications. Different people have been the Seventh, but they all develop the same habits: speaking in part numbers, dreaming in technical drawings, hands that know the weight of every wrench before touching it. The current Seventh arrived three years ago and has already begun the transformation, her speech becoming clipped, efficient, peppered with measurements.

They eat together once a day, in the mess carved from a natural cave formation. The food comes from below—mushrooms that grow in the warm drainage tunnels, fish pulled from the hot pools where mineral-rich water collects, ferns cooked in steam until tender. They season everything with crystallized minerals Cora deems too impure for collection. The table is a massive valve wheel laid horizontal; the benches are decommissioned pipes.

They rarely speak during meals, but the silence is companionable, filled with the facility's constant song. Sometimes Vertical Jahn will produce a steam-mouse that needs doctoring. Sometimes the twins will whistle observations about pressure patterns that make Old Bretton chuckle into his mushroom soup. Magnus draws diagrams in the condensation on the table, explaining through geometry what his deafness won't let him say aloud.

At shift changes that don't align with any clock, they perform the ritual passes—checking each other's work, verifying what needs verifying. These moments of intersection are tender, almost intimate: Cora showing Ess a particularly fine crystal formation, Bretton helping Magnus interpret a new vibration, the Seventh arriving precisely when someone's tool needs adjustment.

They have no families but each other, no histories that matter before the cisterns. When they leave—through death or rarer transformations—they are replaced so seamlessly it's as if the facility itself births new keepers from its perpetual steam. Their predecessors' names fade from memory, kept alive only in the nicknames of pumps and the wear patterns on tools.

But they are not sad. How could they be? They are part of something larger, eternal, necessary. The steam sings through their dreams. The gauges tick their heartbeats. They know purpose the way the mountain knows heat—absolute, unquestioned, foundational.

Sometimes travelers pass through, seeking the rare minerals or simply shelter from storms. The keepers are cordial but distant, their attention always partially elsewhere—listening to frequencies visitors cannot hear, watching for signs invisible to untrained eyes. The travelers leave with their goods and their stories of the strange, silent people who tend the steam, never understanding that the keepers spoke volumes to each other the entire time, just not in words.

At the shift's end, each returns to their chosen roost. Cora to her crystal children, Magnus to his vibrating lullaby, the twins to their aerie, Jahn to his soft nest, Bretton to his ledgers, the Seventh to the workshop where tomorrow's repairs wait in patient metal rows. They sleep the deep sleep of the necessary, while around them the facility breathes its steam breath, its keepers resting like organs in a living thing.

And in the deepest part of night, when the industrial lights turn the steam to gold and the gauges glow like eyes, you might see them all emerge, drawn by some unheard signal. They gather on the main platform, saying nothing, watching the steam rise and curl and dissipate into the dark. It's not a meeting or a ceremony. It's more like a mutual confirmation: We are here. The work continues. The mountain provides.

Then they return to their posts, and the night continues its measured progression toward dawn, counted out in drops of condensation, in pressure readings, in the perfect tick of gauges that will never stop marking time.
