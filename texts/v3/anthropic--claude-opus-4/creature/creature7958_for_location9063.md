The Coralweavers drift through their city like memories through a dying brain. Their bodies are suggestions rather than certainties—elongated torsos that taper into fronds of tissue, arms that split into delicate manipulator tendrils or fuse into paddle-broad limbs as the work demands. Their skin is translucent, revealing the slow pulse of ichor through branching vessels, the occasional flicker of bioluminescent organs that serve purposes their ancestors knew but they have forgotten.

They have no faces as such. Where a head might be, there is instead a crown of sensory polyps—some tasting the water's chemical messages, others detecting the electrical signatures of their neighbors, still others extending filaments that interface directly with the coral colonies. When they speak their clicking language, it comes from specialized chambers along their flanks that compress and release water in precise rhythms. When they need to see, eye-spots bloom across their surface like fast-opening flowers, each one viewing a slightly different spectrum, the overlapping images creating a vision that exists more in interpretation than perception.

The eldest among them have begun the slow merger with their city. Their lower bodies trail off into root-systems that penetrate the coral floors, drawing nutrients up while feeding processed thoughts down into the collective neural web. They can still move—slowly, painfully, dragging their architectural connections behind them like wedding trains made of living stone—but most choose stillness. They become intersection points in the city's consciousness, translator-nodes between the polyp-dreams and the still-mobile workers who must navigate both realms.

The young are almost human in their cohesion—compact bodies with recognizable limbs, their skin merely opalescent rather than fully transparent. But the city calls to them through every surface, and by their third decade they've usually begun to fragment. A hand left too long against a wall might fuse temporarily, requiring careful coaxing to separate. Their sensory polyps multiply and specialize. Their bones—if they can be called bones—begin to hollow, making space for the symbiotic worms that will eventually help them process the city's chemical signals.

They reproduce by budding, though not in the way their bodies might suggest. When the desire strikes, a Coralweaver will retreat to a chrysalis-chamber, one of the few structures in the city that remains stable. There, they'll spend months growing a duplicate of themselves—not a child but a twin, complete with their memories up to the point of separation. The original and the copy might work together for years before diverging into distinct individuals, or might immediately swim to opposite districts, disturbed by their similarity. Some lineages have produced dozens of versions of the same base individual, each one slightly altered by their experiences, creating entire neighborhoods populated by variations on a theme.

Their tools are extensions of their bodies in the most literal sense. The bone-hammers and acid-chisels connect to their nervous systems through hair-thin filaments, becoming temporary appendages that they can feel through. Master crafters might work with dozens of tools at once, their consciousness distributed across a constellation of implements, their natural bodies almost forgotten in the center of the whirling cloud of purposeful bone.

They fear warmth above all else. The collective memory of the species recalls the Bloom—a time when volcanic activity heated their waters and the city grew so fast it consumed half its inhabitants before they could escape. Now they maintain careful stations around the valley's edge where workers chip away excess growth, where the elderly sacrifice portions of their merged bodies to keep the city from choking on its own expansion. The cold is their salvation, the thing that keeps them individuals rather than mere cells in the greater organism they're building and becoming.

Music is their mathematics. The singing worms aren't pets or tools but something more like living equations, their harmonies encoding everything from architectural plans to philosophical arguments. A master weaver can conduct a choir of worms to calculate stress distributions in new growth, to model the flow of nutrients through district-wide circulation systems, to predict where the next merger between buildings will occur. The worms themselves understand none of this—they sing because their bodies demand it—but the patterns they create are the closest thing to objective truth in a city where even the distinction between self and other shifts with the tides.

At night—though day and night mean little in this bioluminescent space—they gather in what they call the Remembering Pools. These are chambers where the neural density of the coral has reached such intensity that it preserves impressions of everyone who enters. Coralweavers float in the warm, thick water, feeling the ghostly presence of their predecessors, uploading their own day's experiences to the collective record. Young ones come here to learn skills their bodies remember but their minds never knew. Old ones come to feel themselves as they were decades ago, before the merger began to claim them.

They have no concept of death as ending. A Coralweaver who sinks too deeply into merger simply becomes city, their consciousness spreading through kilometers of coral until it's too diffuse to be called individual anymore. Their memories persist in the Remembering Pools, their biological patterns might bud forth in new offspring, their merged flesh continues to live as part of the greater organism. They fear loneliness more than dissolution—to be cut off from the city's neural web, to be trapped in a single body with a single perspective, this is their nightmare.

When outsiders come—and they do come, the brave or foolish who seek the city's biological treasures—the Coralweavers receive them with cautious hospitality. They offer guides whose bodies are still cohesive enough to be followed, tools that won't bond permanently to foreign flesh, quarters in the stable zones where the architecture moves slowly enough for air-breathing visitors to rest. But they watch with a mixture of pity and confusion as these guests struggle to maintain their boundaries, to resist the city's gentle invitations to merger. Why would anyone choose such isolation? Why cling to the fiction of separation when connection offers so much more?

The city dreams, and they dream with it. In the deepest districts where the merger is complete, where individual Coralweavers are indistinguishable from the coral colonies they tend, new thoughts emerge that no single mind could conceive. These deep thoughts ripple outward through the neural web, sparking innovations in the workshops, inspiring new arrangements in the singing worm choruses, sometimes reshaping entire neighborhoods as the city tries to express concepts that have no words in any language.

They are changing, generation by generation. Each new budding produces offspring slightly better adapted to the merged life, slightly less attached to the illusion of individuality. Their tools grow more sophisticated as they become more integrated—what use for handheld implements when you can be the implement, when the city itself can be shaped by thought transmitted through coral neural channels? The youngest workers sometimes forget to maintain separate bodies at all, existing as traveling waves of intention through the city's living infrastructure until some task requires them to coalesce.

And yet they persist in their routines, their social structures, their careful maintenance of the boundary between city and sea. Perhaps because they know that without some friction, some distinction between self and environment, consciousness itself dissolves. They walk the knife's edge between unity and separation, building their civilization in the space between the individual and the collective, between the creature and the city, between the dream of self and the reality of connection.

They are what we might become, given different seas to swim in. They are what we fear to become. They continue their slow dance of merger and separation in the cold depths, tending their living city, singing their mathematics, remembering themselves in pools of thought-thick water, always becoming, never quite arriving at whatever finale their transformation implies.
