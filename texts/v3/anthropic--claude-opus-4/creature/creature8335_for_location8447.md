The Listeners came first, though they no longer remember being human.

They exist now as elongated things, their bodies stretched and reformed by generations of symbiosis with the sonic flora. Each one stands nine feet tall but weighs no more than a child—their bones have become hollow resonating chambers, their skin stretched drum-tight over frameworks of cartilage that flex and bend in ways that violate anatomy. They have no eyes. Where sockets should be, specialized membranes vibrate in response to sound, creating three-dimensional maps of their environment more precise than vision ever was.

Their fingers have multiplied and lengthened into delicate manipulators that can pluck individual plant fibers, tuning the hollow like an instrument. They move through the cacophony with profound grace, reading the acoustic patterns like sheet music, understanding instinctively which harmonies indicate healthy growth, which dissonances signal disease or damage. They are gardeners of frequency, curators of resonance.

The Listeners communicate through their bodies. Their hollow bones produce pure tones when struck or stroked—each individual has a unique tonal signature based on their skeletal structure. They speak in chords, multiple tones generated simultaneously through practiced manipulation of their resonating chambers. Emotion colors their language: grief sounds like minor keys dying in their chest cavities, joy rings out in overtones that make the plants shiver sympathetically.

They feed on sound itself, like the plants they tend. Their digestive systems have evolved specialized organs that convert acoustic energy into nutrition. They position themselves at nodes where the hollow's natural resonances concentrate, mouths open, throats vibrating at precise frequencies to extract sustenance from the waves passing through them. During the rare wind-deaths, they fast, growing translucent until their bones show through their skin like architectural drawings.

But the Listeners are not alone. They share the hollow with the Composers—creatures that might have once been insects or arachnids, now grown large as housecats. Their bodies are living instruments: wing-cases that snap in complex rhythms, leg segments that rub together to produce singing frequencies, abdomens packed with specialized sound-generating organs. They crawl across the plant surfaces, testing resonances, adjusting growth patterns through targeted sonic stimulation.

The Composers work in orchestras of dozens, their collective intelligence emerging from the interplay of their individual songs. They problem-solve through music, each organism contributing its voice to symphonies that are actually computational processes. When they discover new acoustic territories—frequencies that make the plants grow in unprecedented ways—they preserve these discoveries in their young, encoding the knowledge in specialized organs that develop only in response to specific sound-patterns during metamorphosis.

Together, Listeners and Composers form a civilization without artifacts, without writing, without any technology recognizable as such. Their culture exists purely in sound—histories sung in recursive loops that layer memory upon memory, philosophies explored through harmonic relationships, sciences practiced through acoustic experimentation. They've developed mathematics based on interference patterns, medicine that diagnoses through disruptions in resonance, architecture that builds with guided plant growth.

The young of both species learn by listening. Listener children grow their first resonating bones in response to their parents' songs, their bodies literally shaped by the frequencies they're exposed to during development. Composer larvae spin cocoons of sound-absorbing silk in the hollow's quietest corners, emerging transformed by the acoustic patterns they absorbed during metamorphosis.

There are specialists among them. Listener surgeons who can hear illness in the subtlest changes of body resonance and cure through precise acoustic interventions. Composer engineers who design new plant growth patterns by conducting orchestras in calculated performances that last for days. Pairs that bond across species—a Listener and a orchestra of Composers who develop such intimate acoustic understanding they effectively become a single organism, their songs interweaving in patterns too complex for either to produce alone.

They have religion, of sorts. They worship the Fundamental Tone—a frequency so low it takes a full day to complete one cycle, felt rather than heard, that they believe underlies all existence in the hollow. Their holiest practitioners spend decades learning to perceive it directly, their bodies adapted through meditation and practice until they can sense vibrations that would be imperceptible to their younger selves. These priests claim the Fundamental Tone contains information—instructions, perhaps, or memories of whatever intelligence first created the singing plants.

Death among them is transformation. When a Listener's bones grow too rigid to resonate properly, when a Composer's instruments begin to fail, they give themselves to the plants. The vegetation absorbs them eagerly, incorporating their acoustic properties into new growth. The deceased become part of the hollow's symphony, their voices preserved in the resonances of specialized plant structures that grow from their remains. The living can still communicate with them, after a fashion, by playing the right frequencies to activate these memorial growths.

They know there is a world outside the Singing Hollow, but it holds no interest for them. Why venture into spaces where sound dissipates uselessly into sky? Where plants grow in boring silence, dependent on something as crude as light? They've evolved beyond such simplicities, found richness in limitation, built entire universes in a single cave where sound never stops.

The children of both species play games with echoes, chasing acoustic shadows through the maze of growth. They learn to sing before they learn to think, their consciousnesses emerging from the interplay of their own sounds with the hollow's eternal chorus. They have no concept of silence—to them, it would be like asking a fish to imagine the absence of water.

This is evolution's answer to perfect darkness and endless noise: creatures that made these conditions not just tolerable but necessary, who found in sonic chaos the foundation for awareness, culture, and meaning. The Singing Hollow shaped them, and they shape it in return, gardener and garden locked in an acoustic spiral that ascends toward complexities our silent world can never reach.
