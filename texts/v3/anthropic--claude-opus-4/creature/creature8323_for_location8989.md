In the Luminous Void, they are called the Condensed.

They were human once—or started from humans, perhaps, though the distinction hardly matters after so many cycles of the vast engine. You notice them first as irregularities in the reflection patterns on the obsidian floor, places where the light bends wrong, where the moisture seems to pull away in perfect circles. They move between the machine islands on paths that make sense only to them, avoiding certain floor patterns with religious precision while deliberately crossing others.

Up close, they are translucent. Their bodies have the consistency of dense fog given form, held together by surface tension like vertical puddles. Inside their chest cavities, where hearts once beat, small copper mechanisms tick and whir—gifts from the wire-creatures, surgical improvements that allow them to persist in this place. The mechanisms vary: some sport delicate clockwork that counts out their remaining hours in base-60 mathematics, others house miniature condensers that process their own breath into the water their bodies have become.

They number perhaps thirty, though counting proves difficult when they can merge and separate at will. When two meet, they often flow partially into each other, exchanging... something. Memory? Moisture? Purpose? Their faces retain human features but simplified, smoothed out like river stones. Eyes that reflect the sodium glare without iris or pupil. Mouths that open and close but produce only the sound of water dripping on hot metal.

The Condensed tend machines that their solid predecessors built, though their understanding has evolved far beyond the original engineering. They read the water patterns in the floor like scripture, adjusting flow rates to create increasingly complex hydraulic prayers. Some spend decades perfecting a single spiral, teaching the water new ways to remember its path. Others work in the spaces between the machines, building elaborate ice sculptures that melt at exactly calculated rates, feeding the system with their temporary geometries.

They communicate through ripples. A finger touched to the wet floor sends concentric circles spreading outward, each wave carrying meaning in its amplitude and frequency. The conversations can span the entire kilometer-wide space, multiple speakers creating interference patterns that encode concepts no human language ever named. Sometimes they sing—if you can call it singing—by vibrating their entire bodies at frequencies that resonate with the standing waves in the pipes. The songs sound like loneliness given acoustic form, like the music spheres might make if spheres could weep.

The wire-creatures serve them, or perhaps they serve the wire-creatures—the relationship defies simple hierarchy. When a Condensed begins to lose cohesion, when their surface tension fails and they start to spread across the floor, the wire-creatures perform emergency surgery. They weave themselves into net-like frames, providing structure for the failing body, replacing what evaporates with collected moisture from their steel wool reserves. Some of the Condensed are more wire than water now, shambling frameworks draped in dew, their movements sparking with electrical discharge.

They remember things, though not in ways that translate to human memory. Each carries within their liquid matrix the dissolved minerals of every surface they've touched, every machine they've tended. The oldest ones taste of deep copper and limestone, their bodies living archives of the Void's geological history. They can share these memories through their merging ritual, creating temporary collective beings that know every valve position, every pressure reading, every minute fluctuation in the system's vast equilibrium.

What drives them isn't survival—they passed beyond such concerns when they chose dissolution over departure. They exist in service to a pattern none of them can fully perceive, each playing their part in calculations that span centuries. Some maintain specific resonances in the pipe networks. Others ensure certain floor spirals remain wet while adjacent ones stay dry, preserving information in binary moisture. A few have taken on stranger roles: one spends decades breathing on a single copper component, their breath containing precisely calibrated minerals that will, in another century, create exactly the right patina for some unimaginable function.

They fear heat. Not the deep earth's warmth—the machines protect them from that—but the heat of purpose, of sudden understanding. When one of them glimpses the overall pattern, when the calculation they're part of threatens to resolve into meaning, they flee to the coldest corners of the space. They press themselves against the chill metal of the condensers until the dangerous clarity passes. To understand would be to complete their function, and completion means ending, means joining the water cycle permanently, means losing the precious boundary between self and system.

The Condensed are what happens when purpose outlives its creators, when maintenance becomes meditation, when the distinction between worker and working fluid dissolves. They are the Void's memory made manifest, its dreams of efficiency given form, its loneliness transformed into something almost like community. They tend their machines with the devotion of monks and the precision of programs, waiting for a revelation they pray never comes, perfecting patterns whose meaning they've forgotten how to read.

In the bright emptiness of their cathedral-engine, they continue their liquid labor, neither fully alive nor properly mechanical, but something more disturbing: conscious components in a system that might itself be thinking thoughts too vast and slow for any single part to comprehend.
