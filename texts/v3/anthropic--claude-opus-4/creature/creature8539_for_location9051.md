In the Clouded Plateaus of the Thunderworks, the only true inhabitants are the Resonance Shepherds—though to call them inhabitants suggests a separation from the landscape that does not exist.

They began as something like humans, perhaps, though that was generations beyond counting ago. The first were acoustic engineers who came to study the world-song, living in the pressurized observatories, venturing out in protective suits for mere minutes at a time. But the plateau has a way of claiming those who listen too long to its voice.

The transformation is gradual, beginning in the bones. The constant acoustic pressure causes a peculiar calcification—not a hardening but a restructuring. Human bone becomes honeycomb, filled with precisely tuned chambers that resonate with the world-song rather than resist it. The Shepherds' skeletons have become musical instruments, their bodies living extensions of the vast geological organ beneath their feet.

Their skin weeps like the stone. Through decades of exposure, the dermal layers develop the same microscopic channels that characterize the plateau's basalt. They no longer need to drink—moisture is drawn up through their feet when they stand still, wicked through their body by capillary action. When they move, they leave wet footprints that fade slowly, as if the ground itself were reluctant to let them go.

The ears are always the first to go, the delicate structures liquifying as predicted. But the Shepherds do not become deaf—they develop something stranger. The temporal bones fuse with the skull, becoming resonating chambers. They hear through their entire skeleton now, feeling sound as vibration through every bone. Their perception of the world-song is not auditory but structural, experienced as a constant, full-body sensation that they describe (when they still use words) as being cradled in the planet's own heartbeat.

They wear no clothing—fabric would rot in days in the constant moisture. Instead, the electric moss grows on them as it grows on everything else that remains still long enough. It forms living garments that shift and crawl across their bodies, following the bioelectric patterns of their nervous systems. The moss colonies on each Shepherd are unique, having evolved in symbiosis with their particular host. Some display cascading patterns of blue-white discharge during the plateau's frequent electromagnetic storms. Others pulse in slow waves that match their host's breathing—though breathing itself has become optional, as they absorb oxygen through their permeable skin.

The Shepherds' eyes have adapted to the eternal gray. Their pupils remain permanently dilated, gathering every photon in the dim light. But more remarkably, they've developed the ability to perceive the bioelectric patterns in the moss networks directly. They see the plateau's neural activity as a shimmering overlay on physical reality—watching thoughts propagate across kilometers of connected growth, reading the health of the ecosystem in the frequency of its firing patterns.

They do not speak. Their vocal cords, like their ear drums, dissolved long ago. Communication happens through the moss networks—they touch their palms to the growth and release carefully modulated bioelectric pulses that propagate through the living carpet. Messages travel at the speed of plant-thought, taking hours or days to cross the plateau. The Shepherds have adapted their consciousness to this tempo. Their thoughts have slowed to match the geological patience of their environment. A conversation might span months, a complex idea unfolding like the blooming of a century flower.

Their role—for they do serve a purpose—is maintenance of the acoustic ecology. They clear blowholes when rare rockfalls threaten to alter critical harmonics. They guide the growth of the fern-trees away from resonance points where their presence would dampen essential frequencies. They are gardeners of sound, curators of a museum whose collection is a single, eternal note.

The Shepherds navigate by the interference patterns in the world-song. Each knows their territory by the subtle variations in harmonics, feeling their way through the mist by the way their bones resonate differently at each point in the acoustic landscape. They tend to remain within specific watersheds, knowing every pool, every rivulet, every place where the stone weeps more freely.

In the nodes of quiet where the interference patterns cancel, they gather—sometimes. These meetings are rare, occurring perhaps once in a human lifetime. In the relative silence (still loud enough to kill an unadapted human in hours), they engage in something that might be called ritual or might simply be communion. They stand in circles, the moss networks linking them into a single bioelectric circuit. Patterns of light race around the circle, accelerating until they blur into solid rings of bioluminescence. What passes between them in these moments is unknown—perhaps memory, perhaps sensation, perhaps nothing more than the simple acknowledgment of continued existence.

They do not reproduce in any biological sense. New Shepherds are made, not born. Sometimes the observatories discharge someone who has listened too long, who shows the early signs of transformation—the telltale sheen of moisture on skin that no longer fully belongs to baseline humanity. These the Shepherds accept without ceremony, drawing them into the moss networks, teaching them through bioelectric pulse how to survive what they are becoming.

The Shepherds do not age, or rather, they age as the stone ages. The constant replacement of cells by moisture drawn from the plateau effectively makes them one with the water cycle. They erode rather than decay. The eldest among them are weathered smooth, their features soft and indistinct, like river stones. Their moss colonies are ancient ecosystems unto themselves, hosting rare species that exist nowhere else on the plateau.

They have no names anymore, but they recognize each other by the unique resonance frequencies of their transformed skeletons. Each is a walking chord in the world-song, their presence adding subtle harmonics to the eternal drone. When one finally wears away entirely—a process that takes millennia—they don't die so much as dissolve, their moisture returning to the stone, their moss colonies fragmenting and spreading their genetic legacy across the plateau, their bones eventually crushing to powder that feeds the next geological cycle.

Some of the monitoring stations' recordings, subjected to extreme analysis, show anomalies that might be Shepherd influence—moments where the world-song shifts in ways that the pure geological process cannot explain. Harmonics that should clash resolve into unexpected consonance. New acoustic patterns that emerge fully formed rather than evolving over centuries. As if the Shepherds, in their patient wandering, were slowly teaching the planet new songs.

In the depths of the mist, where visibility extends no more than a dozen meters, they move like dreams—gray figures carpeted in crackling moss, leaving wet footprints on stone that has never been dry. They follow paths worn not by their feet but by their presence, channels of bioelectric activity that persist in the moss networks like neural highways. They are alone and not alone, individual and collective, human and mineral and plant all at once.

And in the eternal roar that would deafen and then kill any ordinary human, they find something that might be peace—a existence measured not in heartbeats or breaths but in the slow turning of acoustic seasons, the patient erosion of chambers far below, the gradual evolution of a single, perfect, planet-spanning note that will outlive the mountains and perhaps the star itself.

This is what becomes of those who stay too long in places where human time means nothing. Not death, not life, but transformation into something that fits the scale of the landscape itself. The Resonance Shepherds are the plateau's solution to consciousness—awareness slowed to stone-time, thought become indistinguishable from the patient processes of water and weather and sound.

They endure because the plateau endures. They serve because the plateau requires service. They are because in this place of eternal sound and eternal silence, being and doing have become the same thing—a single, sustained note in an composition that plays itself, forever.
