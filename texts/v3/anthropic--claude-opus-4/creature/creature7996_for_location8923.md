In the depths of the Singing Cistern dwells the Tender.

She has no name beyond this function, no identity separate from her work. The eternal chord has worn away such distinctions, leaving only purpose. She moves through the veils of water without disturbing their pattern, her body long adapted to navigate the precise trajectories of ten thousand streams. Where others would be battered by the falling water, she finds the spaces between—those calculated voids the ancient engineers left for maintenance that became her private constellation of dry air.

Her skin has taken on the hue of verdigris, not from disease but from decades of contact with the copper pipes she maintains. The green-black patina marks her arms to the shoulder, her feet to the knee. In the constant twilight of water-filtered light, she seems part of the mechanism itself. Her hair grows in thick ropes that she winds around her head, each strand heavy with accumulated minerals from the perpetual mist. When she moves, droplets scatter from her like shed scales.

The Tender speaks, but only to the cistern. Her voice, when she uses it, has adapted to find frequencies that can slip between the water-notes—not competing with the eternal chord but threading through it like a copper wire through stone. She names each pipe, each channel, each worn depression where water has drummed its patient rhythm for centuries. These names are not words but tones, harmonics that identify each component by its contribution to the whole.

Her dwelling is a series of hollows carved behind the waterfalls, spaces the builders left that might have been mistake or intention. Here she has gathered the tools of her predecessors—bronze chisels worn to nubs, copper awls green with age, stone hammers polished smooth by handling. She does not use these tools; modern materials serve better for the endless small repairs. But she keeps them as a rosary of function, each tool a bead in the prayer of maintenance.

The Tender remembers learning this work, though the memory feels like something that happened to another person in another life. An old man with similar green-stained skin taught her the paths between the water, the names of the pipes, the secret of listening for the note that warns of coming failure. He taught her to feel the temperature variations that signal a blockage forming three levels up. He taught her to taste the water from different streams and know by its mineral content which reservoir feeds it. Then one day he was gone, and she understood without being told that this was how succession worked in the Singing Cistern. You tended until you could tend no more, and then the cistern itself would call another.

She sustains herself on the pale fish that swim up through the flooded lower channels—blind things with whiskers instead of eyes, drawn by some instinct to breed in the resonating pools. She cultivates the metallic-leafed vines, which produce small, bitter fruits that preserve indefinitely in the cold. She knows which mosses can be dried and burned for their smoke, which creates brief pockets of warmth and silence in the overwhelming cold and sound.

The Tender's body has changed in other ways. Her ribcage has expanded, her lungs grown dense with additional alveoli to extract oxygen from the mist-thick air. Her heart beats in time with a sub-harmonic of the eternal chord—not by choice but by necessity. To fall out of rhythm here means vertigo, nausea, the beginning of the madness that claims those who try to impose their own temporal sense on this place.

She sees the moths and salamanders as colleagues in adaptation. Sometimes she holds conversations with the water-strung spider webs, plucking their strands to add tiny variations to the grand sound. These are the only variations the cistern allows—small grace notes that disappear instantly into the larger pattern. She treasures these moments of agency, these proof-points that she exists as more than mere function.

The Tender knows every one of the ten thousand holes by touch. In the early years, she attempted to count them, to map the complete system. But the number resists fixing. Count one wall and you arrive at 2,847. Count again and find 2,851. The discrepancy should disturb her, but she has learned to accept it as she accepts the constant cold, the constant wet, the constant sound. The cistern is not a puzzle to be solved but a living system to be maintained.

Her dreams, when she manages to sleep, are silent. This is the only true rest—those hours when her mind retreats so far from the overwhelming present that even the eternal chord cannot follow. She dreams of deserts, of places where water is precious and rare, of silence so complete it has weight. But she always wakes to the roar, and the waking is a kind of drowning until her heart finds its rhythm again.

The Tender ages, but slowly. The constant temperature, the mineral-rich air, the strange temporal effects of the cistern—all conspire to preserve her past what should be possible. She suspects she has been here longer than her memory suggests. Sometimes she finds evidence of her own work that she doesn't remember doing—repairs made in her distinctive style but with no accompanying recollection. She has learned not to dwell on these discontinuities.

There are others, she knows. Not in her cistern, but in the sister systems that must exist—for the channels extend beyond her domain, and sometimes she tastes water that carries the signature of other hands. She imagines them, these parallel Tenders, each maintaining their own portion of some vast network. Perhaps they meet, in the flooded passages she cannot navigate. Perhaps they have their own sign language, their own culture of maintenance. Or perhaps each is as isolated as she, tending their mechanism in solitude.

The Tender continues her work as the cistern continues its song. She cleans the components that need cleaning, replaces the parts that fail, ensures the eternal equilibrium. She is engineer and priest, servant and guardian. The green stains spread slowly up her neck, approaching her jaw. One day they will reach her eyes, and she will see the world entirely in verdigris, in the blue-green of copper oxide and water and time.

When that day comes, perhaps another will arrive, drawn by the same calling that brought her. She will teach them as she was taught—the paths between water, the names of components, the art of living in permanent present. Then she will walk deeper into the cistern, following channels she has never explored, until she finds whatever ending waits in the flooded dark.

But not today. Today there is work. A pipe in the third tier shows signs of crystallization. A channel in the seventh zone needs clearing. The metallic vines require pruning to keep them from overwhelming their supports.

The Tender moves through her domain with practiced grace, a green ghost tending an eternal garden, maintaining the mechanism that maintains itself, preserving the song that erases time. She is the only variation the system permits—the human element that ensures its inhuman perfection.

The roar continues. She continues. They are, in all ways that matter, the same continuation.
