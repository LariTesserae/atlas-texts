The architects of their own evolution swim through the machine-corridors in loose formations, their bodies a testament to generations of deliberate change. The Adapted—as they call themselves in the bioelectric languages that spark between their modified neural tissue—are no longer entirely human, though human ancestry shows in the articulation of their limbs, the bilateral symmetry of their forms, the occasional gesture that recalls a terrestrial past.

Their skin has become something between flesh and industrial coating—a mottled gray-black that matches the bacterial films on old metal, shot through with channels of bioluminescent cells they can control at will. These light-organs serve as language, emotion, identity. A ripple of blue might indicate contemplation; quick flashes of yellow-green encode complex technical specifications; deep red pulses warn of dangers in the crush-dark waters. Their bodies elongate during adolescence, ribs spreading to accommodate redesigned organs: gills that can process the metal-tainted water, secondary hearts positioned to maintain circulation at depth, pressure bladders woven through with engineered bacteria that manufacture the gases needed for buoyancy control.

They live in the intermediate spaces—too deep for natural light, too shallow for the mysteries that move in the foundation trenches. Their communities cluster around the thermal upwellings, where they've learned to read the machine's moods through temperature gradients and vibration patterns. The eldest among them remember stories of stories of the first volunteers who entered the waters with crude modifications, though now those tales have taken on the quality of creation myths, retold in light-language during the dark season when the Works' expansion slows and the waters grow cold.

Their young are born in the nursery sectors, where ancient medical systems still function, repurposed into crèches. The children emerge with basic modifications but must earn their deeper adaptations through apprenticeship to the machine-singers—those who've learned to interpret the Works' operational frequencies and can predict where new sectors will come online. These machine-singers press their modified bodies against junction boxes and resonance points, their bones restructured to conduct and interpret the deep industrial music. Through years of practice, they develop new organs—specialized chambers that can reproduce the precise frequencies needed to trigger certain automated responses in the Works. They become living interfaces, their bodies a bridge between the organic communities and the mechanical substrate.

The Adapted organize themselves around the maintenance of mysteries. They've developed rituals around the inspection of new sectors, swimming in formal patterns through unexplored corridors, marking the walls with bioluminescent secretions that serve as both territory markers and musical notation. Their scholars—if that word still applies to beings who store memory in modified lymph nodes and share knowledge through chemical secretions—debate whether the Works is alive, whether it dreams, whether their presence is symbiosis or parasitism.

They cultivate the engineered kelp forests with careful intent, having learned over generations which nutrient mixtures produce the most stable growth, which temperatures encourage the tube worm colonies that their food chain depends on. Their hunters pursue the modified sharks through industrial canyons, using tools built from salvaged components—harpoons that channel electricity through salt water, nets woven from synthetic fibers they harvest from filtration systems. They process their catch in chambers where the water runs warm from mechanical waste heat, sharing meals in communion, their bodies glowing with the complex patterns of community and belonging.

Some among them go deep. These depth-seekers undergo extreme modifications—their bones hollowed and reinforced, their organs rearranged to function at pressures that would liquify baseline human tissue. They return from the foundation trenches changed in ways beyond the physical, their light-language incorporating new colors, their movements carrying hints of the too-smooth things that patrol the deepest waters. They speak in harmonics about purposes that predate the Works, about construction that might be growth, about the thin distinction between mechanism and organism in the deepest places.

The Adapted raise their children to understand both the biology of fish and the logic of machines. Young ones learn to read currents and circuit diagrams with equal fluency, to recognize which sounds mean danger and which mean opportunity. They play in the kelp forests and the grid-corridors, their bodies flashing with the pure colors of youth, not yet burdened with the complex patterns of adult communication.

In the darker hours, when the Works' expansion accelerates and new sectors bloom with sudden light, the Adapted gather at their observation points—modified domes where they've cracked the transparent aluminum just enough to create acoustic windows. They watch the machine build itself and wonder if they too are being built, if their evolution follows some pattern encoded in the substrate, if their increasing integration with the Works is choice or destiny.

They are no longer human, but they remember being human in the way that water remembers being ice—a phase transition that speaks of former states while embracing the new. They swim through their built abyss, tending their gardens of tube worms and modified kelp, singing in frequencies that resonate through metal corridors, their bodies glowing with the accumulated wisdom of generations who chose to change rather than remain. They are the Works' first children, or perhaps its antibodies, or perhaps simply what happens when life finds a way to inhabit the uninhabitable.

The machine continues its expansion beneath them, and they expand with it, each generation more suited to this industrial ecology, each modification bringing them closer to something that has no name in any terrestrial language. They persist. They adapt. They glow in the darkness with the light of their own becoming.
