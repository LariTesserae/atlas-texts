The Threnodies move like water itself—flowing, finding the path of least resistance, pooling in eddies of conversation before rushing onward. Their bodies tell the story of generations in the deep: pale as cave fish, with skin that holds a faint luminescence, as if they've absorbed the phosphorescent molds into their very pores. In darkness, a crowd of Threnodies glows like a constellation of dim stars.

Their eyes have grown large, pupils dilated permanently wide, the irises bleached to the palest blues and grays. But it's how they use these eyes that marks them as citizens of the Carved Hollows—they read movement more than color, tracking the subtle shifts of shadow in blue-green light, catching the flicker of fingers speaking in the silent language of the roar-drunk quarters. Their peripheral vision is extraordinary; a Threnody can navigate a crowded market while maintaining three separate signed conversations.

The constant moisture has made them smooth-skinned, nearly hairless. What hair remains grows long and fine, usually worn in elaborate braids that serve practical purposes—wrapped tight against the skull to avoid catching on low ceilings, woven with small stones or shells that click in specific patterns, adding another layer to their complex communication. The wealthy thread their braids with thin copper wire that catches and reflects the forge-light. The poor use fish bones, which gleam just as beautifully in the phosphorescence.

Their hands are their voices. Years of sign-language from childhood creates a peculiar grace—fingers that flow like water, wrists that rotate with fluid precision. Even in casual conversation, their hands dance. The oldest among them, those who've spent decades in the deepest quarters near the Drum, develop hands so expressive they can convey sarcasm, whisper secrets, or shout rage without moving another muscle. Calluses ridge their palms from climbing, carving, and the constant work of water management.

They dress in layers of waxed cloth, never fully dry but never soaked through. The traditional garment is a close-fitting suit of mushroom leather, harvested from the giant fungi that grow in the lowest caverns, treated with fish oil until it repels water like seal skin. Over this, they wear tunics of traded cloth when they can afford it, or woven fungal fibers when they cannot. Everything is dyed with cave-minerals: rust reds, limestone whites, deep carbon blacks, and precious blues extracted from certain toxic mushrooms. Patterns are crucial—geometric designs that catch the eye in low light, family symbols carved from stone and sewn onto shoulders, status markers that can be read at a distance.

Their children are born swimming. The saying goes that a Threnody baby drinks mineral water before mother's milk. Pregnant women wade daily in the sacred pools near the Drum, believing the vibrations shape the growing child's bones for life in the echoing depths. Infants are carried in slings of woven vine, their large eyes tracking the play of phosphorescence across wet stone. They learn to climb before they walk properly—the three-dimensional nature of their city makes vertical travel as natural as horizontal.

The young are educated in cohorts, moving through the city in small schools like fish, learning the hundred hand-signs for water (dripping, flowing, flooding, misting, dangerous, blessed, rising, falling...), the seventeen cardinal directions (up, down, towards-Drum, away-from-Drum, and thirteen more based on acoustic landmarks), and the songs. Always the songs. For despite the roar that drowns spoken word in half the city, the Threnodies sing constantly. Their music weaves through the water's frequency, finding pockets of resonance where melody can bloom. They sing histories, work songs, lullabies that somehow carry through stone and water-sound to reach a sleeping child three levels up.

Age shows differently here. Without sun-damage, faces remain unlined longer, but the constant moisture creates its own patterns—fine networks of pale scars from stone-scrapes, joints that swell and ache from the damp, a particular stoop that comes from decades of navigating low tunnels. The elderly develop what they call "stone sight"—an ability to read the rock's intentions, to know which walls will weep heaviest, which ceilings might collapse, where new chambers can safely be carved. They're honored as architects and water-readers, their expertise bought with decades of attention to the mountain's moods.

The Threnodies organize themselves around water-guilds: the Channel Keepers who maintain the flow, the Drum Dancers who claim to influence the central cascade's behavior, the Catchers who harvest rain from the ceiling, the Mappers who chart the ever-changing routes as tunnels flood or drain. Marriage often happens within guilds, but romance blooms across the traditional boundaries—love letters carved in hidden corners, gifts of rare dry cloth, synchronized swimming in the public pools that serves as courtship display.

Their social hierarchy flows like their water—always finding its level. Wealth is measured in dryness: the ability to claim upper chambers away from the worst seepage, to afford imported textiles that resist moisture, to maintain private wells that don't require waiting in common bucket lines. But respect is earned through water-wisdom. A poor Channel Keeper who prevents flooding commands more real authority than a rich merchant who merely stays dry.

They worship through movement. The Drum Dancers are part priest, part engineer, part artist. Their elaborate rituals at the edge of the great cascade supposedly read and influence the water's pattern. Whether true or not, their predictions of drought or flood prove accurate often enough to maintain faith. They leap and spin on platforms jutting over the abyss, their bodies painted in phosphorescent mold, creating light-trails in the mist. Some say the bravest dancers dive into the Drum itself and emerge in lower pools, blessed by the deep waters. Some say the dancers who disappear feed something that lives at the bottom, keeping it satisfied so it doesn't rise.

In the quiet hours—for even the city of endless noise has rhythms of greater and lesser activity—the Threnodies gather in acoustic pockets where conversation is possible. They share fermented mushroom tea, play intricate games with carved stones that represent water levels and population flows, tell stories with their whole bodies—hands signing, faces expressing, feet tapping coded rhythms. Their humor runs dark and wet: jokes about drowning, about the sun-blind surface dwellers, about the things that scratch at the city's edges.

The criminals among them face unique punishments. Thieves are marked with phosphorescent dye that won't wash off for months, making them visible even in the darkest corners. Murderers are lowered into the Drum on ropes, held in the cascade's fury until nearly drowned, then pulled up to face exile to the dry caves—waterless passages where madness comes quick. Lesser offenses merit time in the Splash Crews, maintaining the most dangerous channels where the water runs wild.

Their dead return to water. Bodies are carried to special chambers where calculated erosion reduces them to bones within weeks. The bones are then carved with the individual's life story and installed in the Remembrance Walls—vast galleries where phosphorescent mold is encouraged to grow in patterns that illuminate the carved histories. Families visit to run fingers over familiar names, adding new carvings to record births, marriages, achievements. The very richest commission Bone Chanters—artists who create musical instruments from ancestor remains, flutes and drums that sing with the voices of the departed.

The Threnodies dream of drowning and wake relieved. They carry water always—in their clothes, their hair, their lungs' humid breath. They know a hundred words for echo and use them all. Their children play games of "Deep Dark" where one ventures just beyond the phosphorescent boundary and must find their way back by sound alone. Their artists carve impossible delicacies—lace patterns in living rock, water channels that sing specific notes, chambers shaped to amplify whispers into shouts or reduce roars to murmurs.

They are a people pressed by circumstance into strange and beautiful shapes. Like the stone they carve, like the water that carves them, they adapt and endure. In the blue-green twilight of their city, in the constant thunder of the Drum, in the networks of tunnel and bridge and seeping wall, they have built a civilization that could exist nowhere else, populated by people who could survive nowhere else.

The Threnodies do not simply live in the Carved Hollows. They have become the Carved Hollows—worn smooth by generations of passage, channeled by necessity, flowing always toward survival in the deep places of the earth. Their bodies are maps of their city, their movements are its water, their songs are its echoes.

And in the darkest corners, where even the phosphorescence fails, where the water-roar becomes something else—a breathing, a waiting—there are Threnodies who no longer look entirely human. Who have lived too long in the deep, drunk too deeply of certain pools, listened too closely to what whispers under the Drum's thunder. They are still Threnodies, still citizens, still family. But their fingers have grown webbed, their eyes clouded to pearl, their songs shifted to frequencies only stone can hear.

The city keeps them. The city changes them. The city sings through them in voices of water and echo, and they sing back, harmonizing with the eternal flow, the eternal roar, the eternal carved and carving dark.
