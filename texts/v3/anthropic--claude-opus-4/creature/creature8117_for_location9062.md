The builders are a people of moss and mercury, their skin taking on the pale, faintly luminescent quality of those who live between stones. They move with a peculiar grace—not the flowing movement of dancers, but the careful, deliberate placement of those who know that every surface might betray them. Their hands are their most distinctive feature: unnaturally long fingers with extra joints, evolved or adapted over generations to find holds in the wet stone. The fingernails grow thick and slightly curved, natural pitons that click softly against the rock as they climb.

They dress in layers of what they call "water cloth"—fabric woven from the fibers of the pale shoots that grow along their walls, treated with the mercury-bright tears until it repels moisture while remaining supple. The garments hang loose and are secured with bone toggles at strategic points, allowing freedom of movement while climbing. Each person carries their tools in pouches that shift position as they move, never tangling, never impeding—a dance of objects learned from birth.

Their eyes have adapted to the eternal twilight. Pupils dilated permanently wide, iris colors faded to grey and pale amber, with a nictitating membrane that flicks across when they move from darker to lighter areas. They see well in their shadow world but suffer in true daylight, which few ever experience. The children are born with normal eyes that change over their first years, a transformation the parents watch with both sadness and relief.

The builders rarely speak above a whisper, their vocal cords having atrophied somewhat over generations. Instead, they've developed a complex language of hand signs that can be performed even while climbing—subtle shifts of weight, the angle of an extended finger, the rhythm of taps against stone. A conversation might occur across a chasm with nothing but the dance of hands in the dim light. When they do vocalize, it's often in harmonics, finding the resonant frequencies of their immediate surroundings to carry meaning in the overtones.

They organize themselves not in families but in "braids"—fluid groups of six to twelve individuals who work, eat, and rest together, their membership shifting like the water that shapes their world. Each braid specializes in a different aspect of the eternal construction: the Stone Readers who interpret the mountain's movements, the Water Weavers who channel the flows, the Platform Dancers who maintain the wooden walkways, the Garden Whisperers who coax food from stone, the Memory Holders who remember where things were and predict where they might be.

Children are raised by their braid, learning to climb before they walk. Infants are carried in specially designed slings that leave the parent's hands free, the baby's first view of the world inverted and vertical. By age three, they're scrambling up walls with their unnaturally long fingers, finding holds invisible to outside eyes. By seven, they're trusted with their first copper tool, usually a small chisel that they'll carry and care for their entire lives, the metal slowly taking on the unique patina of its owner's skin oils and the mountain's minerals.

The eldest among them show the full transformation of their kind. Their fingers have grown so long they can no longer fully straighten them, permanently curved to match the stone. Their skin has taken on a translucent quality, veins visible beneath like map lines, pulsing with blood that runs slightly cooler than human norm. Some develop patches of what they call "stone sleep"—areas where the skin calcifies slightly, creating natural armor at joints and pressure points. They consider this a blessing, a sign that they're becoming one with their eternal construction project.

They eat fungi, the pale shoots, salamander meat when they can catch it, and strange fruits that grow from the ceiling gardens—bulbous things full of clear fluid that tastes of minerals and rain. They supplement this with a paste made from certain stones they've learned are digestible, grinding them with the mercury tears to create a substance that sustains them through the long building seasons when the gardens fail.

Their concept of time is vertical rather than linear. They speak not of past and future but of upper and lower, understanding that what sinks will rise again, that what climbs must eventually fall. Their memories are spatial—they remember not when something happened but where, in which fold of stone, at what height in the ever-changing maze.

Death among them is returning to the stone. Bodies are placed in specially carved niches where the mountain's moisture will slowly calcify them, incorporating their bones into the structure they spent their lives building. They believe this is the highest honor—to become a permanent part of the impermanent city, their calcium joining the limestone in the eternal construction. Children learn early to recognize these ancestor-stones, running their long fingers over the surfaces where a tibia or skull has been half-absorbed, whispering their names in the acoustic sweet spots where the dead might still hear.

They dream collectively. Perhaps it's the mercury vapor from their tools, or the strange acoustics, or simply the result of living so closely entwined, but their sleep is shared. A braid will rest together on the same platform, and in the morning, they've all wandered the same dream-spaces, solved the same construction problems, seen the same visions of what their city might become. They wake already knowing what the others are thinking, hands moving in silent conversation before their eyes fully open.

These are not a people conquered by their environment but married to it, evolved with it, becoming something new in the spaces between what is human and what is stone, what is water and what is air. They build their city and it builds them, each generation a little less like the humans who first found this fold in the mountain, a little more like the patient, permanent, ever-changing stone itself.
