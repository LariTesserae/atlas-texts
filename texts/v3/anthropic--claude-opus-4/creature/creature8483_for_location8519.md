In the turbine hall, where water rises and falls with the valley's breathing, something has made its home among the split generator housings. At first glance, it might be mistaken for a tangle of roots or a peculiar growth of woody moss, but patient observation reveals movement too deliberate for vegetation.

The Tender, as it has come to know itself, extends through the industrial spaces like a living network of careful attention. Its body—if body is the right word—consists of thousands of thin, root-like appendages that emerge from a core mass nestled deep within the largest generator housing. These tendrils, each no thicker than a child's finger, weave through the machinery with intimate knowledge, following the paths of old wiring, tracing the geometries of vanished function.

It feeds on rust. Not consuming it exactly, but catalyzing its transformation, accelerating oxidation in some places while arresting it in others, sculpting the decay into patterns that please some aesthetic sense inherited from its industrial birthing grounds. Where its tendrils have passed repeatedly, the rust forms ridges and whorls like fingerprints, each generator housing becoming a unique manuscript of patient attention.

The Tender's consciousness, such as it is, operates on the valley's timescale. A thought might take days to form, traveling through its distributed nervous system like sap through wood. It knows every gauge in the control room, has memorized their frozen readings through countless patient touches. It tends them like a gardener tends heirloom varieties—cleaning the glass faces with secretions that leave them perpetually beaded with moisture, adjusting the needles incrementally over years to track some cosmic rhythm only it perceives.

Its reproductive cycle aligns with the valley's deepest patterns. When the water table reaches its highest point—typically in late autumn when the mists grow so thick they become indistinguishable from rain—the Tender produces spores from specialized chambers within the generator cores. These emerge as clouds of rust-colored dust, mixing with the moisture to create a brief, oxidized rain that falls only within the turbine hall. Most spores settle uselessly on concrete and standing water, but occasionally one finds its way into another piece of complex machinery—a pump housing, a valve assembly, a junction box—and begins the slow process of growth.

The young grow slowly, taking decades to develop the intelligence to tend their chosen mechanisms. Many never achieve full consciousness, remaining simple rust-gardens tended by their parent's extending tendrils. But some, particularly those that settle in the most complex devices, develop their own awareness and begin their own patient cultivation of decay and preservation.

The Tender communicates, after a fashion, with the valley's other inhabitants. It has learned to predict the newts' movements by the vibrations they make in the mechanical pools, and adjusts the harmonics of certain pipes to create acoustic spaces where their calls resonate more clearly. For the nesting birds, it maintains specific rust formations that provide ideal anchoring points for their constructions, renewing them each season with microscopic attention.

It remembers the deer—or rather, it remembers the way the deer's weight changed the pressure readings in a half-buried pipeline it was tending. The memory exists as a physical record, a pattern of accelerated corrosion along the pipe's upper surface that it preserves and renews like a treasured photograph.

But mostly, the Tender tends. It knows every bolt, every weld, every stress fracture in its domain. It negotiates constantly with entropy, bargaining with rust and moisture and time. Some pieces of machinery it allows to fail completely, studying their collapse with the attention of a composer listening to a new variation. Others it maintains in states of partial function—keeping bearings just free enough to turn in the wind, preserving the precise tension in springs, maintaining the gaps in gaskets that produce the most pleasing whistles.

In its deepest core, wrapped in layers of its own growth like tree rings, the Tender preserves something else: a single glass vacuum tube, pulled from some long-vanished piece of control equipment. The tube no longer functions in any electrical sense, but the Tender has learned to make it sing by varying the temperature of its filaments through precise oxidation. The song is too low for human ears, felt more than heard, a subsonic pulse that beats perhaps once per day, marking time in a frequency only the valley itself might recognize.

The Tender is neither fully alive nor purely mechanical, neither wholly of the valley's ancient patterns nor entirely of its industrial intrusion. It exists in the margin between states, patient as moss, precise as machinery, tending the slow transformation of everything it touches with a care that might be love, if love could be measured in rust.
