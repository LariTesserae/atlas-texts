In the foundry district live the Tenders—those who have learned to read the language written in rust patterns and the songs hidden in steam. They move through their days with a peculiar grace, their fingers stained permanent blue-green from handling the fungi, their hair carrying perpetual droplets of mist like tiny jewels.

The eldest among them is Marva, who has worked the same furnace for forty-three years. Her hands have grown thick and sure, her palms immune to heat, her knuckles swollen into shapes that perfectly fit the tools she wields. She speaks little, but the younger workers watch how she tilts her head to listen to the metal, how she can tell from the color of smoke whether an alloy will hold. At night, she tends a garden of the luminescent fungi in her courtyard apartment, cultivating new strains that burn cleaner, longer. The cats bring her gifts—bent nails, coils of copper wire, the occasional perfect gear—leaving them at her door like offerings.

Children here grow up amphibious. They swim in the canals before they can properly walk, diving for the brass scraps that fetch a penny per pound at the recycling works. Their skin develops a faint shimmer, a barely perceptible scales-pattern that helps shed the constant moisture. They know which drainage grates lead to which underground chambers, which fungi to suck for sweet water when thirsty, which cats will lead them to safety when lost in the maze of alleyways. They play games with the steam vents, learning to read pressure changes, to predict when a blast will come. The clever ones become apprentices. The truly clever ones become water readers.

The water readers are a strange breed, even here. They taste the canals each dawn, rolling the water across their tongues, noting the chemical signatures of each workshop's discharge. Young Tem has been doing this for seven years now, and his eyes have taken on the same iridescent quality as the carp. He can predict which workshops will have accidents, which are cutting corners on their filtration, which are producing something new and untested. He reports to no authority—the district governs itself through whispers and witnessed trust—but workshops that ignore his warnings tend to find their workers mysteriously absent, their supply chains disrupted by surprisingly coordinated cat activity.

The night shift belongs to creatures harder to categorize. Mrs. Chen, who claims to have been young when the district was founded (impossible—that was ninety years ago), operates a tea shop that's only open from midnight to dawn. Her patrons include the lamplighters, yes, but also the things that follow the lamplighters: shadows that move too independently, figures in coats that drip continuously but never seem to empty. She serves them all with the same careful courtesy, accepting payment in currencies not always recognized by daylight: a perfect spider's web, a vial of unclouded rainwater, a story told backwards.

The dreamcatchers work in pairs, one to sleep and one to transcribe. They position themselves in the charged spaces where reality has worn thin—usually near the workshops where the most ambitious inventions are pursued, where inventors push materials and concepts beyond their intended limits. The dreamer drinks a tea of specific fungi, lies on a copper mesh mat connected to ground, and allows their consciousness to drift into the spaces between. What they find there—visions of machines not yet invented, solutions to problems not yet encountered, warnings of failures yet to come—gets recorded by their partner in a mixture of technical drawing and poetry. The factory owners pay well for these glimpses, though they've learned not to push too hard or too fast toward what's been seen. The district remembers those who tried to force the future. Their workshops stand empty now, overtaken by the most vigorous ivy, their walls pulsing faintly with bioluminescent warnings.

In the deepest parts of the district, where the oldest workshops cluster around courtyards gone wild with overgrowth, live those who have been here so long they've become part of the architecture. Old Soren's skin has the texture of oxidized copper, green-black and rippled. He hasn't left his workshop in twenty years, but somehow knows everything that happens in the district. Some say he's learned to see through the eyes of the cats. Others believe he's become one with the mycelial networks that run beneath the cobblestones. When young workers come to him with problems—a machine that won't run true, an alloy that won't hold—he doesn't speak. Instead, he hands them items from his vast collection: a gear that turns against entropy, a flask of water from before the factories came, a map drawn on fungus-leather showing the district as it dreams itself to be.

The merchants arrive at dawn, their boats struggling against the sluggish current, their holds full of raw materials and empty of understanding. They dock at the Weeping Quay, where condensation drips constantly from the ironwork above, and conduct their business quickly. Most refuse the traditional cup of fungal tea, unwilling to risk what changes it might bring. But those who drink, who stay, who listen to the rhythm of hammer on anvil long enough to hear the melody beneath—they sometimes don't leave. The district claims them, as it claims all things, patiently and completely.

And threading through it all are the cats. No one remembers when they became organized, when they started their patrols, when they began delivering messages in exchange for fish. But now they're essential—as essential as the steam engines, as the water wheels, as the morning mist. They sun themselves on warm pipes, their fur patterns matching the patina of the metal beneath them. The oldest ones have eyes that glow faintly in the dark, the same blue-green as the fungi. Some say they're no longer truly cats, that they've become something new, something that could only exist here where the boundaries between natural and manufactured, between quick life and slow decay, have blurred beyond recovery.

This is who lives in the foundry district: the changed and the changing, the patient and the persistent, those who have learned that adaptation is not surrender but conversation, that to survive here is to become part of the great work—not just the work of production, but the deeper work of transformation, of finding the harmony in the discord between what humanity builds and what the world wants to become.
