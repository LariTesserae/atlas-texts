The inhabitants of the Singing Hollow have evolved into something that might still be called human, though the distinction grows academic after millennia of adaptation. Their bodies have lengthened and lightened, bones becoming hollow like those of birds, though not for flight—for resonance. When they speak, their chest cavities amplify the sound, and their elongated ribs can be played like instruments by trained practitioners who press specific points along their torsos.

Their skin has developed a peculiar quality, somewhere between leather and leaf membrane, able to absorb moisture directly from the humid air. It shifts in color from deep burgundy during bright-time to near-black in the darkness, helping regulate their temperature in the constant warmth. Along their spines and the undersides of their arms grow specialized pores—modified sweat glands that have learned to excrete a resinous substance. This resin hardens when exposed to certain frequencies, allowing them to literally sing temporary tools into existence, or seal wounds with a sustained hum.

They do not see as surface dwellers understand sight. Their eyes have enlarged and multiplied—typically three or four per face, arranged asymmetrically, each attuned to different spectrums of heat and sound-pressure. Some individuals develop specialized eye-pits that can detect the emotional resonance of spaces, seeing the psychic residue left by strong feelings as faint color-washes in the air. Their primary sense, however, is vibrational. The fine, downy hair covering their bodies acts as millions of tiny receivers, painting detailed pictures of their surroundings through air current and sound wave.

Children are born already humming, their first cries perfectly pitched to harmonize with the Hollow's base note. Those who are born tone-deaf—rare but not unheard of—become the settlement's memory-keepers, their inability to fully join the collective resonance making them ideal objective observers. They learn to read the patterns in the crystallized wood, interpreting the growth rings not as years but as acoustic epochs, each layer recording the sounds of its formation.

The social structure resembles a musical composition more than a traditional hierarchy. Individuals flow between roles like notes in a chord progression—sometimes leading, sometimes supporting, sometimes providing necessary dissonance. Their names are not words but personal musical phrases, unique combinations of tone and rhythm that others reproduce when calling to them. Conversations often involve multiple participants humming harmony lines beneath the primary speaker's melody, adding emotional context and subtext that can completely alter meaning.

They wear little in the way of clothing—the constant warmth makes it unnecessary—but adorn themselves with cultivated air-plants that grow directly on their bodies. These symbiotic gardens are planted in childhood and mature with their hosts, the plants' roots working into the upper dermis without breaking through to blood. The most revered elders are nearly invisible beneath cascading tendrils of silver-white air-moss, while young adults favor bright, reactive species that flare different colors in response to their emotional states.

Their tools and possessions are grown rather than made. By singing to the crystal-wood in specific patterns over months or years, they coax it to assume desired shapes—vessels that hold sound instead of water, furniture that supports the body while allowing it to resonate freely, instruments that are partially alive and respond to their players' moods. The most skilled shapers can convince the wood to grow in impossibly complex geometries, creating objects that exist in acoustic dimensions no eye can properly perceive.

They do not sleep as surface dwellers do, instead entering periodic resonance-trances where their consciousness merges temporarily with the Hollow's great vibration. During these states, they share dreams, memories blending and recombining in the acoustic space between minds. Privacy, as a concept, exists only in the modulation of one's personal frequency—thoughts and feelings can be hidden by shifting them above or below the range of casual perception, though true secrets are nearly impossible in a society where emotion has sound.

Death, when it comes, is a gradual detuning. The dying spend their last weeks in the Resonance Garden, their failing bodies adding increasingly discordant notes to the collective harmony until, at last, they fall silent. The bodies are then placed in the growth chambers of young towers, where they become part of the crystallizing wood, their bones adding their resonance to the structure's song forever.

They measure time not in days or seasons but in Intervals—the period between when certain combinations of towers achieve perfect harmonic alignment. These Intervals vary in length from moments to decades, each with its own qualities and traditional activities. The Grand Interval, when all towers sing in unison, occurs perhaps once in a generation and marks their most profound celebrations, when new chambers are sung into being and the deepest memories are shared.

In their mythology, they are the Hollow's children, born from its song rather than descended from surface dwellers. They tell stories of the First Listeners, who heard the call from below and gave up their eyes to better hear it, their tears to better harmonize with it, their solid bones to better channel it. Whether metaphor or history, none can say—the songs that carry their oldest memories have modulated through so many retellings that truth and beauty have become indistinguishable.
