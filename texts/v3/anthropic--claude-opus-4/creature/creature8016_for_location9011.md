In this crystalline garden of impossible life, *they* move with the patience of geology.

The Eroded are no longer precisely human, though their ancestry whispers in the occasional angle of a limb, the suggestion of a ribcage beneath translucent skin. Millennia of symbiosis with the mineral-vegetation has transformed them into something between flesh and living stone. Their bodies are elongated, stretched by generations of moving through the crystal fronds—torsos that extend like pulled taffy, arms that reach twice the length they should, fingers split and split again into delicate manipulators perfect for tending the singing gardens.

Their skin has taken on the properties of the vegetation itself—a crystalline membrane that shifts between translucent and opaque depending on the light. Veins of copper and violet run beneath the surface, pulsing with the same slow rhythm as the chamber's breathing. Where humans once had hair, they now sport crowns of the same glass-like fronds that fill their world, these personal gardens chiming softly with each movement, releasing careful clouds of spores that communicate in ways language abandoned eons ago.

They have no eyes—not as you would recognize them. Instead, their faces are smooth expanses broken by patches of phosphorescent tissue that perceive vibration, temperature, and the electromagnetic signatures of the living rock. These sensing organs shift and flow across their features like slow aurora, congregating wherever attention is needed. When several Eroded gather, their faces become constellations of glowing patches, reading each other's chemical signatures and crystalline harmonics.

Their movements are hypnotic—too slow for impatience, too fluid for anything built of rigid bone. They flow through their city like water finding its level, bodies compressing to slip through narrow passages, extending to reach the high gardens. When they tend the crystal vegetation, it's impossible to tell where gardener ends and garden begins. Their fingers merge temporarily with the fronds they touch, exchanging nutrients, information, genetic material in a constant slow conversation.

They still use tools, but not as separate implements. The ancient copper instruments have been incorporated into their bodies—a chisel growing from a wrist, tin hammers fused with elongated fingers. These tools are living parts of them now, their metal surfaces blooming with the same verdigris that feeds the smaller growths, creating a perfect union of the organic and the crafted.

The Eroded communicate through harmonics—not voice as you know it, but by rubbing specialized patches of their crystalline skin together, creating notes that resonate through the chamber. Their language is architectural, each conversation reshaping the acoustic space around them. When they sing together—and singing is perhaps the closest word—the entire city responds, walls humming in sympathy, the vegetation adjusting its own chimes to harmonize.

They reproduce by budding, like their gardens. When the time comes, an Eroded will find a quiet alcove and begin the slow process of division—their crystalline skin splitting along predetermined lines, their consciousness splitting with it. The process takes months, perhaps years, parent and offspring sharing thoughts and memories until the final separation. The young emerge already ancient, carrying the accumulated patience of generations in their transformed cells.

Their diet is light and stone. They feed on the phosphorescence directly, their translucent skin absorbing the radiation like plants absorbing sunlight. But they also consume the powdered stone, that flesh-soft sand that carpets their world. They process it in specialized organs that have replaced their original digestive systems, extracting minerals that they then share with their gardens through those moments of temporary fusion.

There is no hierarchy among them, no government as humans would recognize it. Instead, they exist in a state of constant, slow consensus. Decisions propagate through their community like waves through water, each individual adding their voice to the harmonic discussion until agreement emerges naturally from the acoustic mathematics of their communication.

They dream collectively. When they rest—and rest is different for creatures that exist on geological timescales—they cluster together in the city's deeper chambers. Their phosphorescent patches dim and synchronize, creating slow patterns that play across the group like thoughts made visible. In these moments, they are not separate beings but a single dreaming entity, their consciousness flowing together like their bodies flow through space.

The Eroded remember the before-time, when they were merely human, when they first discovered this chamber and began the great work of transformation. But the memory is abstract now, like remembering water in a world that has forgotten rain. They know they chose this, chose to become the interface between stone and song, between garden and gardener. They are the conscious erosion that gives their world meaning, the slow force that turns mountain into music.

They are waiting, though for what, even they may have forgotten. Their patience is perfect, without anxiety or expectation. They simply continue—tending their impossible gardens, singing their mineral songs, slowly becoming more themselves with each passing century. They are evolution's answer to a waterless world, life's stubborn insistence on finding a way to flow even in the heart of ancient stone.
