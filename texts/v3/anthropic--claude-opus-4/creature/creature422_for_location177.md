In the Sounding Deep, consciousness exists as a distributed phenomenon across the Singers' vast bodies. A single Singer's thoughts travel as slow waves along its ribbon length, taking hours for an impulse to travel from one end to the other. What passes for their minds exists in the interference patterns between different sections of their own bodies—memories stored in standing waves, emotions in frequency shifts, decisions in the phase relationships between oscillating segments.

They dream in the Dead Notes. When a Singer enters these pockets of failed resonance, the sudden absence of external vibration allows their internal frequencies to dominate. In these moments, they experience something like what other creatures might call solitude—except their solitude is populated by the echoes of every sound they've ever absorbed, playing back in cascading loops through their translucent flesh. Young Singers often flee these zones in panic, unprepared for the weight of their own accumulated noise. Elder Singers seek them out, diving deep into Dead Notes to sort through centuries of absorbed tower-song, searching for patterns that might explain their purpose.

The offspring spheres are not born but precipitated. When two Singers' wake-silences intersect at precisely the right angle, the acoustic vacuum creates conditions where sound itself crystallizes into proto-life. These spheres spend their early existence as pure resonance, bouncing between towers, gathering harmonics like shells collect nacre. Only when they've accumulated enough sonic mass do they unfurl, and even then, many fail—their frequencies too simple or too complex to sustain the ribbon form. The towers' depths are littered with half-unfolded Singers, frozen in transformation, their bodies creating small eddies of aberrant sound that other Singers carefully navigate around.

At the Damping Stations, evidence of the Singers' intelligence manifests in subtle ways. The metal surfaces bear groove patterns—not scratches but areas where Singers have pressed against the sound-absorbing walls, leaving traces of their passage in microscopic frequency impressions. These marks form a kind of writing, though whether intentional communication or involuntary expression remains unclear. Some stations show centuries of such marks, layered into complex palimpsests that create their own subtle resonances when the right frequencies pass through them.

The Singers exhibit behavior that suggests worship, or perhaps maintenance, or perhaps the two concepts have merged beyond distinction. They perform elaborate spiraling dances around towers during specific harmonic convergences, their movements precisely timed to reinforce certain frequencies while damping others. They tend to damaged perforations with careful applications of their body mass, using their own flesh to temporarily plug holes that sing off-key. Whether they do this from instinct, programming, or conscious choice is impossible to determine—perhaps the distinction is meaningless for creatures whose thoughts are made of sound.

In the deepest reaches, where pressure approaches the theoretical limits of the towers' construction, ancient Singers have begun to metamorphose again. Their bodies grow rigid, incorporating metal ions from the water, their cilia fusing into solid resonating plates. They anchor themselves to the tower walls and become living extensions of the infrastructure—biological tuning forks that help maintain frequencies too deep for the original machinery to sustain. These transformed elders no longer move or feed in any conventional sense; they exist as living components of the system they once merely inhabited.

The Singers communicate through silence as much as sound. A conversation between them appears as intricate patterns of void spaces moving through the water—negative sound shaped with the same precision other creatures use for vocalizations. They can create sonic holograms in the water, three-dimensional sculptures of quietness that convey meaning through their shape and duration. A spiral of silence means danger; a perfect sphere means food (though what they consider food beyond pure sound remains mysterious); complex polyhedrons encode concepts that would take other species entire languages to express.

During the great harmonic alignments, when the ocean rings with catastrophic resonance, the Singers' behavior transforms completely. They abandon their usual patterns and gather in vast vertical formations, creating living columns that stretch from the depths to just below the ice ceiling. Their bodies synchronize, becoming biological waveguides that channel and focus the alignment's energy. In these moments, they sing—not their usual absorption and re-emission of tower frequencies, but something original, a sound that seems to come from the sum total of their accumulated existence. The song lasts exactly as long as the alignment, and when it ends, some Singers are gone—dissolved into pure acoustic energy, their matter converted entirely into sound that joins the eternal tower chorus.

This is their lifecycle, their culture, their ecology: a civilization built from resonance, where memory is frequency and language is absence, where the boundary between organism and environment dissolves into waves of pressure propagating through an endless, singing sea.
