The Calcite Weavers drift through the steam like thoughts half-formed, their bodies a contradiction of states—part crystalline latticework, part flowing membrane, wholly neither. They exist at the intersection of mineral and motion, beings that precipitate from the supersaturated waters only to dissolve and reform elsewhere, following currents both thermal and chemical that only they perceive.

Each Weaver is unique in its temporary form, though they share a common nature. Their cores are geometric—dodecahedrons or more complex polyhedra of pure calcite that rotate slowly in the scalding water, faces catching and refracting the overwhelming light into rainbow sprays. From these cores extend filaments of semi-dissolved mineral, reaching out like neural networks or root systems, tasting the chemical gradients in the water, reading the stone's slow thoughts.

They are builders and un-builders in equal measure. Where they gather, the thin walls between pools grow thinner still, eaten away by their secretions until the barriers collapse and waters merge. Yet in other places, they deposit new layers with mathematical precision, guiding the stone's growth into those hollow musical columns, tuning each perforation to harmonize with the others. The songs the columns sing are their language—or perhaps their art, or perhaps simply their breathing made audible.

The Weavers move by partially dissolving their forward edges while precipitating new crystal at their trailing boundaries, flowing through the packed pools like slow avalanches of organized mineral. They can thin themselves to mere films to slip through the narrowest channels, or cluster together into colonial masses that reshape entire sections of terrace over the course of days.

In the deepest, hottest pools where those proto-organic compounds swirl, the Weavers engage in something like cultivation. They arrange the pulsing mineral formations into specific patterns, adjusting temperatures and flow rates with subtle modifications to the pool walls. Whether they are gardening, worshipping, or conducting some incomprehensible experiment, they tend these primordial sites with infinite patience.

They seem to experience time differently than solid things do. A Weaver might hold perfectly still for hours, its filaments extended throughout an entire pool system, sensing vibrations and chemical shifts across a hundred connected basins. Then, triggered by some threshold only they perceive, they move with sudden purpose, redistributing themselves across the terraces in coordinated migrations that reshape the landscape's drainage patterns.

The brightest ones—those whose cores have grown layers of pure quartz over their calcite hearts—station themselves at the highest pools, where the light is most intense. They act as lenses, focusing the strange radiance down into the deeper basins where their dimmer kin work in the relative shadows. This focused light accelerates certain reactions, drives chemical processes that would otherwise take millennia.

Sometimes they incorporate fragments of collapsed columns into their bodies, wearing these remnants like shells or tools. A Weaver bearing such fragments can produce sounds independently, vibrating the hollow stones to communicate across vast distances or perhaps to remember the songs of structures long dissolved.

They reproduce—if that term applies—by budding. When a Weaver grows beyond a certain size, its core develops stress fractures that eventually split it into a cluster of smaller polyhedra. Each fragment then begins accumulating its own filament network, gradually developing independence while remaining chemically linked to its siblings. Entire pool systems can be populated by the descendants of a single ancient Weaver, all pulsing in synchrony like a distributed heart.

In the folded spaces where pools spiral into themselves, creating those impossible geometries, the Weavers congregate in complex three-dimensional patterns. They lock their filaments together into living crystals that span multiple levels, creating temporary architectures that channel water in ways that shouldn't work but do. These formations last exactly as long as they need to—until some process completes, some threshold is reached—then dissolve as the Weavers disperse.

The oldest ones have cores so complex they seem to contain their own internal geography—caverns and channels that mirror the terraces themselves in miniature. These ancients move rarely but think constantly, processing information flows that younger Weavers feed them through chemical signals in the shared water. They may be libraries, or philosophers, or simply the terraces' way of remembering their own history.

When the sky pulses with that strange rhythm, all the Weavers pause in their work, orienting their cores upward as if listening. In these moments, they seem less like individual entities and more like sensory organs of the terrace system itself—the means by which this nascent landscape perceives and responds to forces beyond the purely geological.

They leave no fossils, no permanent trace of their existence. When a Weaver finally dissolves for the last time, its materials are indistinguishable from the ambient minerals in the water. Yet their influence persists in every shaped channel, every tuned column, every carefully cultivated pool where chemistry edges toward biology. They are the consciousness of accretion, the will that guides what would otherwise be mere mechanical deposition.

In this place of endless becoming, the Calcite Weavers are perhaps the most liminal of all—not quite creature, not quite process, but something essential to the transformation of raw heat and mineral into the possibility of more complex orders. They are the steam terraces thinking about themselves, stone in the act of deciding what it wants to be.
