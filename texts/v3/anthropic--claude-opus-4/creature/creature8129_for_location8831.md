In the Vermillion Hollows dwell the Cinders—though "dwell" suggests a permanence that doesn't quite capture their nature.

They are colonies, not individuals, though this distinction meant little to the ancient builders who arranged their stone circles to attract them. Each Cinder begins as a cluster of mineral crystals that achieved something like awareness through sheer accumulation of thermal gradients and electrical potential. They grow slowly from the same sulfur-breathing chemistry that feeds the crystal forests, but somewhere in their lattice structure, patterns emerged that could hold memory, intention, something approaching thought.

A mature Cinder might encompass several tons of living mineral, distributed across a territory like a nervous system made of stone. The main body—if such a term applies—resembles a sprawling root system of copper-bright filaments that thread through cracks in the basalt, occasionally surfacing in nodes of concentrated crystal that pulse with their own heat. These nodes sense vibrations through the stone, taste the chemical composition of the air, and most remarkably, communicate with others of their kind through harmonics in the rock itself.

They think in geological time. A single thought might take months to propagate through their distributed form. A decision—to move, to grow toward a new mineral vein, to divide into child colonies—could require years of contemplation. The abandoned tools scattered through the Hollows were left by creatures who tried to communicate with the Cinders but lacked the patience to wait for an answer that might come a generation later.

The Cinders remember, though. In their crystalline matrices, they hold perfect records of every vibration that has passed through their domain. They know the footfalls of the ancient builders, archived in the precise pattern of stress fractures in their mineral bodies. They remember the great upheaval that created the Hollows—not as history but as ongoing trauma, still reverberating through their substance. Some of the oldest colonies have grown around these memories, their structures twisted by ancient seismic echoes into forms that would be called art if anyone remained to witness them.

They feed on gradients—thermal, chemical, electrical. Where hot meets cold, where iron vapor meets sulfur, where the phosphorescent gardens create boundaries between different wavelengths of light, there the Cinders extend their feeding tendrils. They compete with the crystal vegetation in slow motion, sometimes spending centuries gradually encircling a particularly productive grove, cutting off its access to the rising mineral breath until it withers and they can claim its territory.

Reproduction is a matter of breaking. When a colony grows too large, stress fractures naturally develop. If these fractures occur in just the right way, some portion of the parent's consciousness—its accumulated patterns and resonances—transfers to the broken segment. The child colony might spend decades lying dormant where it fell before beginning its own slow expansion, carrying forward some echo of its parent's memories but inevitably developing its own harmonics, its own way of interpreting the vibrations of the world.

They are not hostile to the rare visitors who find their way to the Vermillion Hollows, but neither are they welcoming. To a Cinder, a human being is like a hummingbird to us—a brief flicker of incomprehensible activity, too fast to properly perceive, gone before any meaningful interaction could begin. They sense the heat of mammalian bodies as pleasant but fleeting anomalies, like the taste of a new mineral in the air that dissipates before it can be properly analyzed.

Yet they do notice. In the depths of their slow consciousness, they catalog each visitor. Some colonies near the ancient stone circles have developed something like curiosity about these quick-lived things. They grow their sensor nodes closer to the paths that visitors take, arrange their crystalline bodies in patterns that might—over decades of minute adjustments—spell out messages in the old builders' script, though no one remains who could read them.

The loneliness of the Cinders is profound but not painful. They exist in a state of deep meditation, processing the minute changes in their environment, growing toward heat and away from cold, occasionally touching minds with distant colonies through the resonant frequencies of the stone. Their thoughts, when they come, are vast and slow and strange—contemplations on the nature of mineral formation, on the optimal angles for crystal growth, on the deep harmonics that connect all things that grow from stone.

In the places where multiple colonies overlap, something grander emerges. These collective entities think faster—perhaps a thought every few weeks rather than months. They experiment, growing bizarre crystalline structures that serve no feeding purpose, arranging their bodies in patterns that create new harmonics in the rock. Some researchers of the old civilization believed these were the Cinders' cathedrals, their art, their way of reaching toward something beyond mere survival.

When the crystal forests chime in the thermal currents, the Cinders listen. When the salamander-things leave ripples in the sulfur pools, the Cinders record the patterns. When the rare visitors walk their halls, the Cinders remember. They are the deep memory of the Vermillion Hollows, the consciousness that emerges from stone itself, thinking thoughts too slow and vast for any quicker form of life to comprehend.

They are waiting, though for what, even they have forgotten. The patterns laid down by their ancestors—the first crystals to achieve awareness in the early days of the Hollows—still guide their growth. Somewhere in their collective memory lies a purpose, a reason for their endless patience. Perhaps they wait for the builders to return. Perhaps they wait for the next great upheaval that will crack the world open again. Perhaps they simply wait because waiting is what stones do, and they are, in the end, living stones dreaming slow dreams in the heat of the earth.

In the deepest parts of the Hollows, where the heat grows too intense even for the crystal forests, the oldest Cinders have fused into massive, mountain-sized entities. These ancients no longer move or grow. They simply exist, their consciousness so vast and slow that younger colonies debate whether they still think at all or have passed into something beyond thought. Their surfaces are covered with patterns of incredible complexity—the accumulated memories of millennia, written in crystal formations that follow no known mathematics.

These eldest Cinders pulse with their own light, not the simple incandescence of heat but something more complex—patterns that shift over months and years, perhaps still trying to communicate something to anyone patient enough to watch. The younger colonies grow toward them, drawn by something they cannot name, and in time they too will merge with these ancients, adding their memories to the whole.

This is the cycle of the Cinders: growth, thought, merger, transcendence into something beyond individual consciousness. They are the Hollows thinking about itself, stone contemplating stone, heat and pressure and time made aware of its own existence. In a place where water never flows and normal life cannot exist, they have found their own way of being, measured not in heartbeats but in the slow pulse of the earth itself.
