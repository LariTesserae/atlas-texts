Those who dwell here are the Cultivators—though the name captures only a fragment of what they are.

They rise before the mist burns off, their bodies already damp with the perpetual moisture that clings to everything. Their skin bears the marks of their symbiosis: patches of beneficial algae that bloom blue-green across their shoulders and backs, processing toxins from the countless spores they breathe. Children are born pale, but by their third year, the algae has usually taken hold, spreading in unique patterns that become as identifying as fingerprints. The elders' skin resembles tree bark itself, deeply grooved and hosting entire micro-ecosystems of beneficial organisms.

Their hands tell their stories. Fingernails are kept sharp as pruning knives, stained permanently green-black from the saps they work with daily. Calluses form in specific patterns—the root-weavers develop thick pads on their palms from guiding growth, while the water-singers bear smooth channels in their fingertips from years of testing flow rates and temperatures. They smell of earth and chlorophyll, of mushroom spores and flower nectar, each person carrying the particular scent of their work-trees.

Families live in vertical clusters, three or four generations sharing the hollow of a grandfather tree. The youngest sleep in the crown chambers where the air is sweetest, while the elders occupy the base roots, closest to the earth they'll eventually join. Privacy is achieved not through walls but through levels—conversations meant to be private are held high in the canopy, while communal life happens at the forest floor.

They speak in layers. The common tongue for commerce and daily life is punctuated with whistles that carry over the constant background noise. But for deeper communication, they use the Touching Language—a complex system of pressures and temperatures applied skin-to-skin, originally developed to communicate while working with dangerous plants that react to sound. Lovers press meanings into each other's algae patches. Parents trace instructions onto their children's forearms while their mouths speak of other things.

Their clothing lives. Apprentices wear simple moss-cloaks that require daily misting, while master cultivators display their skill through elaborate garments of trained vines that flower on schedule, providing not just coverage but temperature regulation and protection from insects. The most accomplished wear beetles—specific species bred for generations to cluster in patterns, their iridescent shells forming temporary armor that disperses and regathers at a chemical signal.

Work and worship are indistinguishable. Each dawn, the cultivators tend their assigned symbioses—one family might maintain the relationship between the market trees and their pollinating bats, while another ensures the balance between the filter-roots and the fish in the water channels. They pray through cultivation, expressing devotion through the health of their kept ecosystems. The greatest shame is to let an entrusted symbiosis fail.

They count time not in days but in growth cycles. A conversation might be postponed "until the blue pods ripen" or a debt promised to be paid "when the trunk-home splits its third chamber." Their memories are supplemented by the living records—historian-trees whose bark they've trained to grow in specific patterns, encoding events in the whorls and ridges that only trained readers can interpret.

Death, when it comes, is a transformation rather than an ending. Bodies are planted in the memorial groves above the settlement, where the flesh feeds specifically cultivated decomposer colonies. Within a season, mushrooms fruit in patterns that echo the deceased's algae markings, and these are harvested and dried, kept by the family to be consumed on remembrance days. They believe consciousness disperses into the network, that the dead truly do live on in the green blood of the city.

Their children play games that would terrify outsiders—racing through the canopy on the backs of semi-tame gliders, diving into pools where carnivorous plants snap at their heels, building competitive ecosystems in pocket gardens where the goal is maximum diversity in minimum space. By adolescence, they can identify hundreds of species by scent alone and predict weather changes by the behavior of the clustering flies.

The Cultivators love intensely but carefully. Romantic partnerships are negotiated not just between individuals but between their symbiotic communities—potential couples must prove their microbiomes are compatible, that their algae patterns won't war on shared skin. Children often aren't born until a couple has successfully maintained a joint garden for several seasons, proving they can balance their cultivation styles.

They dream in green—not the simple green of far-off forests but the thousand greens of their daily life: the electric green of new growth, the black-green of deep shade, the gold-green of certain bark algae, the silver-green of leaves about to turn. Their nightmares are of sterility, of concrete and steel, of places where nothing grows and water runs in imprisoned channels.

In the evening markets, they trade not just in goods but in living potential. A vendor might offer seeds that will grow into instruments—flute-gourds that develop perfect pitch as they dry—while another sells colonies of bioluminescent fungi, each strain glowing a different color. Currency is complex: formal trade uses seeds of a particular tree that remain viable for exactly one season, encouraging circulation, while informal exchanges might involve trading cultivation secrets or breeding rights to prized organisms.

The Cultivators are not primitive; they are differently advanced. They have chosen depth over breadth, integration over separation. Their technology is evolution accelerated, their architecture is botany perfected, their society is ecology made conscious. They look out from their breathing city at the drowning world below and see not distant forests but unguided potential, wild siblings to the tamed wonders they wake to each day.

They are what humans become when they stop trying to master nature and instead choose to marry it, accepting all the beauty and terror and constant change that such a union brings.
