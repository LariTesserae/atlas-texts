The Pale Gardeners tend these depths. They are a brotherhood—though women work among them—bound not by vows but by the peculiar demands of their labor. You know them by their eyes: permanently dilated from lives spent in furnace-glow, the irises bleached to amber or grey. They see better in the ruddy half-light than in the sun.

They wear the rubber suits only when protocol demands. Otherwise, they strip to the waist, their skin glistening with the same perpetual sheen that coats everything here. Years of exposure have changed them. Their perspiration no longer salts—their bodies have learned to hoard minerals, recycling them internally. They drink constantly but rarely eat, subsisting on nutrient broths that match the mineral content of their strange environment.

The eldest among them, Yem, has worked the Furnace Quarter for twenty-three years. Her hair has gone the same translucent white as the root systems she tends. She claims—and others confirm—that she can taste the chemical composition of steam, can diagnose a failing filter by the flavor of the air around it. Her fingers are permanently stained by the compounds she works with, nails gone soft and flexible as cartilage. She hasn't been to the surface in four years.

They speak in hand signals as often as words, a language evolved from the necessity of communicating through steam and across the acoustic dead zones. A flutter of fingers might mean "pressure building in sector seven" or "time for shift change" or simply "I see you." They know each other by gait, by the rhythm of footfalls on metal grating, by the way each person's body disrupts the air currents.

The younger workers still dream of promotion to the surface crews, but most who stay past their third year never leave. They develop a sensitivity to the machinery that borders on supernatural—hearing harmonics in the pump-thrum that predict breakdowns days in advance, seeing patterns in the condensation that reveal blockages three levels down. They navigate the maze-like chambers without thought, their bodies remembering every turn.

Some have made homes in the dead spaces between chambers, hanging hammocks in the tangle of pipes where the heat is most even. They cultivate the pale plants, not clearing them but guiding their growth, training root systems to reinforce weakening joints, encouraging the translucent leaves to form living filters across ventilation grates. Officially, this is forbidden. Practically, it's understood that the Gardeners know their realm better than any surface engineer.

They trade with the maintenance crews—information for surface goods, diagnoses for real food, their peculiar expertise for small luxuries. A Gardener might trace a system failure to its source in minutes, following trails invisible to outsiders: the way moisture beads differently on a pipe under stress, the subtle change in air pressure that signals a developing crack, the shift in the electromagnetic fields around failing pumps that makes their teeth ache.

Children are born here sometimes, though rarely. The ones who survive their first year develop the dilated eyes within months, their bodies adapting with disturbing speed. They learn to read the steam patterns before they learn letters, can navigate the chambers by touch and sound before they can properly walk. The other Gardeners watch them with a mixture of pride and sorrow, knowing these children will never truly belong anywhere else.

The Gardeners have their own rituals. They leave offerings at particularly temperamental machines—drops of their own mineral-rich blood to "season" new filters, fragments of the pale plants wound around pipes that tend to corrode. They believe the Quarter has moods, that the machinery responds to respect or neglect in ways that transcend mere mechanical cause and effect. They're not entirely wrong.

At shift changes, they gather in the largest chambers, sharing the nutrient broths and fermented condensate they call "steam wine." They sing—wordless harmonies that resonate with the machinery's frequencies, creating standing waves in the mist. The songs are functional as much as social, a way of reading the acoustic properties of the space, checking for changes that might signal problems.

They are changing, generation by generation. The newest apprentices show signs their trainers find disturbing—eyes that reflect light like nocturnal animals, skin that seems to photosynthesize in the furnace glow, children born with webbed fingers better suited to gripping wet metal. The Gardeners don't speak of this to outsiders, but among themselves they wonder: are they still human, or have they become something new, a species shaped by steam and stone and the endless cycle of transformation?

The city above depends on them utterly but thinks of them rarely. Down in the dripping heat, tending their pale gardens and ancient-new machinery, the Gardeners persist: part engineer, part mystic, part evolution in progress, keeping the water flowing through the thousand arteries that sustain the city's life.
