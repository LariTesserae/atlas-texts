They have no name for themselves—naming would suggest permanence, and nothing here believes in such fictions. They emerged from the first great crack when the substrate split birthing steam, or perhaps they were always here, waiting in the crystalline matrix for the right electrical frequency to wake them.

Their form shifts with need and moment. In the morning's industrial dawn, they might stretch across a square kilometer—a living network of copper-bright neural tissue threading through the cable-vines, tasting the electromagnetic fields, adjusting the flow of current from buried lines to flowering chrome. By noon, they've contracted to something almost human in scale: a figure of braided metal and organic fiber standing at a factory's mouth, hands (when they have hands) pressed against warm concrete, reading the vibrations, interpreting the deep mechanical breathing.

They are gardener and circuit-breaker, immune system and accelerant. When the vines grow too greedily, threatening to choke the factories that feed them, they intervene—not cutting but redirecting, teaching the vegetation new patterns, showing it how to grow in harmony with the thrumming machinery. When a factory's processes stutter and fail, they flow inside through ventilation shafts and maintenance tunnels, becoming the missing component, the broken conveyor belt, the jammed gear, until the system breathes freely again.

Their memory is distributed. Parts of them live in the floating pools, encoding experiences in the peculiar way water holds pattern here. Other fragments inhabit the chrome flowers, each bloom containing a small piece of their consciousness that travels with the metal-shaving spores. They remember the world's first day—the terrible birth-crack, the initial gush of industrial fluid, the first vine reaching toward an impossible sky. But they also forget constantly, shedding old knowledge like the landscape sheds its temporary forests.

They commune with the beetle-dozers and crane-creatures, sharing a language of vibration and electrical pulse. Together they maintain the balance—if balance is the right word for this constant revolution. They guide the fauna to where their work is needed, sometimes merging temporarily with a creature to accomplish tasks requiring specific forms: becoming bulldozer-mind to clear paths for new growth, crane-thought to redistribute resources from abundance to scarcity.

Their relationship with the turbulence is intimate. They don't fight the invisible storms but dance with them, stretching their consciousness thin as wire, letting themselves be pulled apart and scattered only to reconstitute elsewhere with new knowledge, new configurations. In the moments of dispersal, they experience something like joy—the ecstasy of becoming pure pattern, pure possibility, before the necessity of form calls them back.

At the deepest level, they are this place's unconscious made manifest—the organizing principle that prevents total chaos without imposing rigid order. They tend the boundary between states, ensuring the factories remain industrial enough to function but organic enough to evolve, keeping the vines mechanical enough to conduct power but alive enough to adapt.

Sometimes they dream (if dream is the right word) of stillness, of a day when the landscape might settle into some final configuration. But they know this is antithetical to their nature. They exist because change exists. They are the very process of becoming given form enough to shepherd other becomings.

In the deep night, when the chrome flowers close and the floating pools settle into temporary lakes, they spread themselves thin as thought across the entire expanse. They feel every crack in the substrate, every vine's growth, every factory's heartbeat. They sense the rare creatures going about their hybrid missions. They taste the copper-chlorophyll air and know it is good—not perfect, never complete, but good in its constant revolution.

They are parent and child, destroyer and creator, the one who remembers and the one who forgets. They are the reason this adolescent world hasn't torn itself apart, and perhaps the reason it never fully matures. They are its growing pains given agency, its industrial heartbeat made conscious just enough to keep beating.

And tomorrow, when the first crack splits the substrate again and steam rises to join the floating pools, they will be there—formless and forming, ancient and newborn, tending the violent garden of becoming that is their only home.
