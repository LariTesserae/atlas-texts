The Condensation Keepers move through their domain with practiced blindness, navigating more by sound and steam-sense than sight. They've been here long enough that their skin has taken on a peculiar quality—not quite scaled, but covered in a fine layer of water-repellent cells that bead moisture like duck feathers. Their eyes, when you can see them through the mist, have developed nictitating membranes that blink horizontally, clearing the constant film of condensation.

Old Tam has been here longest, forty-three years by her count, though she's stopped counting. Her body has adapted in ways that would disturb outsiders: elongated fingers for gripping slick pipes, joints that bend at angles suited for navigating the cramped maintenance tunnels, lungs that process the thick air with an efficiency that makes normal atmosphere feel thin and unsatisfying. She sleeps standing up in her shanty, water running down her oilcloth walls in streams she's memorized like a lullaby.

The younger keepers—though 'younger' here means anyone with less than a decade in the steam—still remember what dry clothes feel like. They cluster in the break stations, sharing stories and the potent fungal tea that grows only on the underside of the primary condensers. The fungus is another adaptation; they cultivate it carefully, knowing which strains help their bodies process the mineral-heavy air and which will send them into fever dreams where they believe they're drowning in reverse, water leaving their bodies to join the eternal mist.

Then there are the Pipe Children—not truly children anymore, some in their thirties, but they earned the name by being born here, raised in the elevated shanties. They know no other world. Their play as youngsters involved racing the pale crabs through the maze of pipes, learning to read the pressure gauges before they could read letters. As adults, they're the most valued workers, able to sense a developing leak by the way the harmonics shift in the constant industrial song. They communicate in a pidgin of whistles that carries through the steam better than voices, a language that incorporates the rhythm of the pumps as punctuation.

The Collectors are a different breed—entrepreneurs who harvest the district's bounty. They tend the moss farms that grow in the eternal twilight beneath the walkways, thick carpets of growth that filter toxins from the runoff and can be processed into everything from medicine to industrial sealant. They know where the eyeless fish congregate, how to read the bacterial mats for signs of mineral deposits worth extracting. Some have built elaborate still-suits that collect and purify their own perspiration, selling the mineral residue to alchemists in the dry districts who prize the concentrated essence of the steam district's unique chemistry.

In the deepest parts, where even the regular keepers rarely venture, live the Forgotten. They've been here so long they've become almost amphibious, their movements in the flooded lower levels more swimming than walking. Their hair grows in thick, water-resistant dreadlocks that serve as sensory organs, detecting vibrations through the water. They maintain the oldest machinery, the foundation systems that even the city planners have lost the blueprints for. Some say they no longer need air the way others do, that they can extract oxygen from the humid atmosphere or even from the water itself. They surface rarely, and when they do, they speak in hydraulic metaphors that few can parse.

The shanty neighborhoods have their own hierarchies. Premium spots sit high enough to avoid the worst flooding but low enough to easily tap the cleaner condensation before it picks up too much machinery residue. These spots are inherited, fought over, sometimes purchased with years of saved wages. The homes themselves are marvels of adaptation—walls that channel water into collection systems, floors that drain into personal purification setups, ceilings that encourage the right kind of moss growth for additional income.

The elder matriarch, Douse Mary, rules the central platform. She's been here sixty years, maybe seventy—time blurs in the constant cycle of condensation and evaporation. Her shanty is the largest, built up over decades into a multi-level structure of scavenged materials. She knows everyone, settles disputes, determines who can stay and who must leave. Her body has changed the most dramatically: her skin has developed a kind of symbiosis with the beneficial bacteria in the air, giving her a faint phosphorescent quality in the darker corners. She hasn't left the district in twenty years, claims the dry air would kill her now.

The children who grow up here develop differently. By age five, they can navigate in zero visibility. By ten, they can identify problems in the machinery by sound alone. Their games involve controlling the flow of condensation, creating temporary waterfalls and rivers in the industrial landscape. They keep the pale crabs as pets, training them to run messages through the pipe systems. Some develop the ability to hold their breath for extraordinary lengths, diving into the deeper pools to retrieve tools or clear blockages.

But it's not just humans who truly inhabit this place. The Pipe Moths have evolved a complex social structure, their colonies pulsing in patterns that the workers have learned to read as indicators of pressure changes. The crabs have their own territories, their own hierarchies, defending prime spots where the mineral deposits are richest. In the deepest pools, things that might once have been fish have become something else—blind, sensing through lateral lines so sensitive they can map the entire district through vibrations in the water.

The true masters of the steam district might be the symbiotic organisms—the bacteria, the fungi, the algae that form living films on every surface. They process the minerals, break down the industrial waste, create the very foundation that allows larger life to exist here. The workers have learned to read these films like texts, knowing that a shift in color or texture might herald a change in the system's chemistry that could cascade through the entire ecosystem.

Late at night, when the industrial lights dim and the only illumination comes from phosphorescent organisms and the occasional spark from failing equipment, the district reveals its strangest inhabitants. Things that might be human, might be something else, moving through the mist with purposes unknown even to the eldest keepers. They maintain machinery that no one else remembers, speaking to the pipes in frequencies that register on the equipment but not in human ears. Some say they're the original builders, changed beyond recognition by decades of exposure. Others believe they're what the Forgotten become when they stay down too long.

The steam district doesn't just house its inhabitants—it transforms them, incorporates them into its cycles of humidity and heat, makes them essential components in its great respiratory system. Everyone who stays long enough becomes part of the machine, their biological rhythms syncing with the industrial ones until the boundary between keeper and kept dissolves entirely in the eternal mist.
