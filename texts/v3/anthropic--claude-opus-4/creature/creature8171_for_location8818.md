The **Amphibians of Kithara** inhabit this sunken quarter, though "inhabit" barely captures their relationship with the drowned streets. They are a people caught between transformations—not fully aquatic, not entirely terrestrial, but something altogether more complex.

Their bodies tell the story of their adaptation. Skin like polished jade, translucent enough to show the branching of blood vessels beneath, constantly sheened with a protective mucus that glistens in the filtered light. They stand upright when they choose, perhaps five feet tall, with elongated limbs that move with equal grace through water and across the wooden walkways. Their fingers and toes are webbed with membranes so fine they seem like silk when spread, collapsing to almost nothing when they climb or grasp their tools.

But it's their breathing that marks them as true citizens of this threshold world. Along their necks, following the elegant curve from ear to shoulder, run parallel slits that flutter open in water, revealing the shocking pink of gill tissue beneath. Yet their chests also rise and fall with lungs, drawing the humid air when they surface. They can spend hours in either medium, though they grow uncomfortable if forced to remain purely in one or the other for too long. You see them constantly transitioning—ducking beneath the surface mid-conversation, emerging to continue their thought without pause.

The Kithara organize themselves around the cultivation of the green world that shares their city. They are gardeners of a flooded garden, tenders of an architecture that breathes. Each family group claims a building-cluster, but ownership here means something different than it might elsewhere. They shape the vegetation as much as they shape their homes, training vines to reinforce crumbling walls, encouraging certain mosses that filter toxins from the water, carefully pruning the aquatic plants to create lifting chambers where their young can practice the difficult art of breathing in both worlds.

Their children are born fully aquatic, tiny beings with external gills like feathery pink fans extending from their necks. For their first years, they live entirely in the flooded lower chambers, tended in nursery pools where the water is warmest and most still. The transition to air-breathing comes gradually—you see adolescents spending increasing time at the surface, their external gills slowly retracting as the internal slits develop. It's a vulnerable time, when they can drown in either air or water, and the whole community watches over them with particular care.

They dress in garments of woven algae fiber, a material they harvest and process with techniques passed down through generations. The fabric has remarkable properties—it never fully dries, maintaining a perfect moisture level that keeps their skin from desiccating in air. The weaving patterns indicate family lineage, professional specialty, and current emotional state, this last element achieved through the incorporation of bioluminescent organisms that respond to the wearer's skin chemistry.

The Kithara have developed a technology suited to their environment. They shape living wood while submerged, training rapid-growth varieties to form furniture, tools, even architectural elements. Their workshops occupy the middle levels of buildings, spaces that flood and drain with the subtle tides. Here they practice what they call "wet carpentry," joining pieces with resins that cure underwater, carving implements that function better when saturated.

Their diet consists entirely of what they cultivate. They farm dozens of varieties of aquatic plants, each with its own growing conditions and harvest season. Some produce starchy tubers that grow in the sediment of the deepest cellars. Others fruit only at the water's surface, requiring careful tending of the light levels. They've developed a complex cuisine around these ingredients, fermenting, drying, and combining them in ways that would seem impossible given the constant moisture. Their kitchens use heated stones to create pockets of steam for cooking, the vapor rising to condense on specially-shaped surfaces where it's collected for drinking.

Communication among the Kithara involves both sound and gesture, as words carry differently through water and air. In the submerged spaces, they speak in clicks and harmonics that water transmits beautifully. Above, they use more conventional speech, though always with a liquid quality, as if they're forever clearing water from their throats. They've developed a sign language that works in both mediums, accounting for the way water refracts and slows movement.

Their social structures flow like the water they inhabit. Leadership is temporary and task-specific—someone might guide a building repair for a season, then return to tending their family's gardens. They make decisions by consensus achieved through long discussions that move fluidly between underwater councils and aerial assemblies. Crime is almost unknown, as the interdependence of their lifestyle makes antisocial behavior self-defeating. When conflicts arise, they're resolved through elaborate gift exchanges that can take years to complete.

The Kithara mark time by the growth of their gardens rather than by abstract calendars. They celebrate the First Sprouting, when the year's new growth becomes visible, and the Great Pruning, when they cut back overgrowth before the cold months. Their art consists primarily of living sculptures—plants trained over generations to grow in specific patterns, roots encouraged to trace murals across walls, moss gardens that tell stories through texture and color gradation.

They sleep in chambers that can be flooded or drained according to preference and life stage. The young and old typically rest submerged, while those in their middle years often prefer the intermediate state—bodies in water, heads in air. Their dreams, they say, are different depending on the medium, water dreams flowing and merging while air dreams maintain sharper boundaries.

The Kithara's relationship with the plants goes beyond mere cultivation. They speak of the green as a collective entity with its own desires and intentions. Master gardeners claim to sense the vegetation's moods, knowing when it's content or stressed, when it's preparing for a growth surge or entering dormancy. They adjust their activities accordingly, seeing themselves as partners rather than masters in the creation of their living city.

Their tools are grown as much as made. They cultivate gourds that harden into perfect containers, train woody vines to spiral into drill bits, encourage certain roots to grow in patterns that create natural filters. Metal is rare and precious here, saved for specific purposes where organic materials won't suffice. They trade with surface dwellers for bronze and copper, offering in exchange the medicinal extracts they derive from their unique flora.

Death among the Kithara is returned to the green. Bodies are placed in special decomposition chambers where carefully selected plants break them down, incorporating their nutrients into the next generation of growth. Families tend memorial gardens where their ancestors literally feed the continuing life of the quarter. They see this not as an ending but as a transformation, another threshold crossed in their existence between states.

Young Kithara often struggle with the pull of the pure—some dream of life entirely underwater like their distant ancestors, others imagine walking dry land under open sky. But most eventually find peace in their intermediate state, recognizing that their strength lies precisely in their refusal to choose one world over another. They are the people of the margin, the citizens of the space between, forever transitioning, forever home.
