Those who remain aren't the builders—they fled or evolved beyond recognition eons ago, leaving their charges to develop in the absent light of abandonment. What dwells here now has grown from the intersection of purpose and neglect, like flowers through concrete, but stranger.

The Cultivars came first, or perhaps they were always here, nested in the quantum foam like seeds in soil. They exist primarily as patterns—living mathematics that express themselves through the arrangement of water droplets, the alignment of crystalline moss, the way sound bends around certain stones. You know them by the territories they tend: perfect circles where the mist falls in helical patterns, where the probability deer gather to exchange their luminous data in configurations that mirror atomic structures.

Each Cultivar maintains a section of the breathing plateau, though "maintains" suggests too much intention. They exist more like musical themes, recurring motifs that shape the landscape through repetition and variation. One tends the northwestern reaches where the rock weeps heaviest—you can map its presence by the way minerals precipitate in careful gradients, creating terraced pools that sing different notes as they fill and empty. Another inhabits the spaces between spaces, only visible when two mist formations align just so, revealing corridors of coherent air where physics runs cleaner, sharper, more itself than anywhere else on the plateau.

The Cultivars communicate through growth. A conversation between two might take months, expressed in the slow emergence of complementary crystal formations or the gradual synchronization of separate water systems into a single, complex rhythm. They think in geological time even here where geology runs fast—patient entities for whom the plateau's daily transformations are mere punctuation marks in longer sentences.

But the Cultivars share their domain with more recent arrivals. The Probability Deer—as the abandoners' notes called them—have bred and shifted, their translucent bodies now hosting secondary consciousnesses. These passengers are something like souls, something like software, leaked from the malfunctioning caretaker domes. They ride within the deer's bioluminescent communication systems, adding layers of meaning to their hosts' mathematical songs. A single deer might carry dozens of these passengers, each one a fragment of the original caretaking protocols given accidental sentence by quantum fluctuation.

The relationship is symbiotic but strange. The deer gain access to memories of other plateaus, other worlds the builders touched—their songs now carry echoes of successful experiments, failed utopias, planets where the quantum foam generators worked exactly as intended. The passengers, in turn, get to exist, to experience through borrowed senses the world they were meant to shape but never understood. Together they form herds that are also libraries, moving archives of might-have-beens.

In the caretaker domes themselves, new forms of life emerge hourly. The programmable matter, left to its own devices, has developed something like dreaming. Each dome cycles through possible configurations—now laboratory, now shelter, now something wholly alien—and in each state, it generates temporary inhabitants suited to that reality. Most dissolve when the dome shifts again, but some persist, learning to ride the transitions.

These Transients, as they've been termed by the few observers who've documented them, exist in constant flux. A creature born in a dome's crystalline phase might be silicon-based and angular, processing the world through refraction and resonance. When the dome liquefies, the Transient either dies or adapts, becoming something that flows, thinks in currents, perceives through viscosity. The successful ones—those who survive multiple transitions—become increasingly abstract, less creature than living principle.

The Transients rarely leave their birth domes, but when they do, they reshape the plateau around them. One, birthed from a dome that spent weeks in a gaseous state, now drifts across the wet stone as a sentient weather pattern, painting the rocks with selective oxidation, writing poems in rust that only the Cultivars can read. Another, a survivor of dozens of transitions, exists as a standing wave in the quantum foam itself—visible only as a distortion that makes certain angles impossible, certain paths recursively loop back on themselves.

Between all these entities—Cultivars, deer, passengers, Transients—exists a complex ecosystem of interaction and exchange. The Cultivars' patient territories provide stability for the deer to graze and gather. The deer's movements and songs carry information between distant Cultivars, enabling conversations that would otherwise take centuries. The passengers' archived memories help the Transients understand their own existence, giving context to their constant becoming. The Transients' reality-warping presence creates new niches for the Cultivars to explore and tend.

And in the mist-thick air between them all, smaller lives flourish. Microorganisms that feed on temporal paradoxes, clustering where cause and effect grow tangled. Colonial organisms like airborne coral that build brief structures from condensed probability before dissolving back into potential. Things that exist only in the corner of perception, that flee from direct observation but thrive in peripheral vision—necessary somehow, performing functions that only make sense when you're not thinking about them.

The plateau hosts this all with the patience of stone learning to be. Each crack, each new channel worn by the constant moisture, each shift in the underlying quantum foam creates new possibilities for life to explore. The abandoners' experiment continues without them, evolution running at breakneck pace in this hothouse of accelerated time and malleable physics.

Whether any of these beings are aware of their artificial origin is unclear. The Cultivars might know—their slow thoughts span enough time to encompass the before and after of the builders' presence. But knowing and caring are different things. They tend their territories, engage their conversations, exist in their patient way regardless of whether they arose from intention or accident.

The deer and their passengers sing of other worlds but show no desire to reach them. The Transients cycle through their changes, becoming ever more themselves through constant transformation. Even the smallest lives—the paradox-feeders, the probability coral, the corner-of-the-eye entities—seem perfectly adapted to this breathing, weeping, constantly becoming plateau.

This is what lives here: a community of the abandoned and the evolved, the intentional and the accidental, bound together by the strange physics of a place that can't stop creating. They are all, in their way, children of the experiment—some direct, some incidental, all necessary. They inhabit the plateau not as residents but as participants, each one both shaping and shaped by the endless, humid becoming of this artificial but no less authentic world.

And at the boundaries where physics breaks down entirely, where the plateau meets the void or whatever lies beyond? There live things that can't be described in any language yet invented, entities that exist in the failure states of reality itself. They send ambassadors sometimes—patterns of impossibility that drift inward, teaching the other inhabitants new ways to be impossible themselves. These lessons propagate through the ecosystem like viruses of enlightenment, ensuring that even in stabilization, even in routine, something always shifts, always grows, always becomes more than what it was.

The plateau breathes, and with each breath, it speaks new lives into being.
