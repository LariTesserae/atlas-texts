In this perpetual twilight of industry and growth, the Conductors make their patient rounds.

They are difficult to perceive at first—your eyes want to resolve them as tricks of the darkness, as moving confluences of pipe and flesh that surely cannot be single organisms. But they are. Each Conductor stands roughly three meters tall when fully extended, though they spend most of their time hunched and compressed, flowing through the mechanical forest with disturbing fluidity.

Their bodies are segmented like centipedes, but each segment has specialized for different aspects of their work. The forward sections sport arrays of manipulator limbs—some ending in delicate copper tools grown from their own bodies, others in fleshy pads that secrete various industrial fluids. These they use to tend the collection trees, clearing blocked tubes with thin probes, adjusting valve pressures with practiced twists, coating corroded joints with protective enzymes.

The middle segments are swollen with storage organs, translucent sacs visible beneath chitinous plates that reveal the various fluids they carry—coolants that glow faintly blue, lubricants that shine like mercury, nutrients that swirl cloudy and organic. These segments gurgle and slosh as the Conductors move, internal pumps maintaining precise pressures and mixtures.

Their rear sections drag behind them like mechanical trains, lined with ports and connectors that match the various interfaces of the collection trees. They dock with trees as they travel, sometimes spending hours connected to a single system, their bodies pulsing as they cycle fluids in and out, taking waste products and delivering fresh supplies. During these connections, they emit low harmonic drones that resonate through the tree networks—perhaps communication, perhaps simply the sound of their internal processes.

The Conductors have no visible eyes. Instead, their head segments are crowned with oscillating antennae—some metallic and straight like radio receivers, others organic and branched like neural dendrites. These constantly sweep the air, reading chemical signals, electromagnetic fields, and vibrations. They navigate by the songs of the collection trees, each tree's harmonic signature as distinct to them as a face.

Their movement is unsettling to watch. They don't walk so much as pour themselves forward, their many limbs operating in rippling waves. They can compress their segments to squeeze through narrow gaps between pipes, or extend to reach high valves and connection points. When they encounter vertical surfaces, they climb with the same fluid motion, specialized grip-pads in each segment allowing them to flow up tree trunks like vertical rivers.

But it's their social behavior that truly sets them apart. The Conductors work in loose collectives, often gathering in groups of six to twelve around particularly complex tree systems. They communicate through a combination of chemical releases, vibrations sent through the metal infrastructure, and the physical sharing of fluids. When they meet, they often connect rear segment to rear segment, exchanging not just materials but information—the chemical memory of which trees need attention, which sectors of the forest are thriving or failing.

Their reproduction is tied to the trees themselves. During certain alignments of temperature and humidity, the Conductors gather at the oldest trees—massive structures whose pipe networks have grown so complex they resemble neural tissue. The Conductors connect en masse to these ancestor trees, sometimes dozens at once, their bodies forming living conduits between the tree and the fungal networks below. In this communion, something happens—whether the trees birth the next generation of Conductors or the Conductors deposit something that grows into new trees remains unclear. Perhaps the distinction no longer matters.

Young Conductors emerge from the base of trees as partially formed things, their segments soft and undifferentiated. They follow the adults for months, connected by umbilical tubes, slowly hardening and developing their specialized anatomy. During this time, they learn the songs of the forest, the chemical languages of maintenance, the ancient routes between trees that must be serviced in precise sequences to keep the entire system functioning.

The Conductors show signs of culture beyond mere maintenance. In certain clearings, they've arranged broken pipe sections into complex patterns that ring with specific tones when the wind passes through. They appear to modify their body chemistry before entering these spaces, becoming more receptive to certain frequencies. Some humans who've observed them believe these are temples. Others think they're schools where young Conductors learn the harmonic signatures of tree species. Still others suggest they're something else entirely—galleries where the Conductors appreciate some aesthetic quality of sound that human senses cannot fully grasp.

They are not aggressive, but they are absolutely protective of their trees. Anything that threatens the collection systems—blocked drainage, invasive organisms, structural damage—is met with swift response. Groups of Conductors will converge on problem areas, their chemical communications growing sharp and acidic. They've been known to dissolve entire fungal blooms that grow too aggressive, or to wage patient campaigns against boring organisms that try to tap into the pipe networks.

In the rare times when they rest, Conductors fold themselves into compact forms at the base of trees, their segments nested together like mechanical puzzles. Their metabolisms slow, their chemical processes quieting to bare maintenance levels. In this state, they're almost indistinguishable from the root systems of the trees they tend—just another part of the industrial forest's vast, breathing machinery.

The Conductors are physicians, gardeners, and living circulation systems all at once. They are the blood that flows through the forest's metal veins, the consciousness that maintains its unconscious processes. Without them, the collection trees would clog and corrode within decades. Without the trees, they would have no purpose, no home, no source for the complex chemicals their bodies require.

They are evolution's answer to the question: what happens when industry becomes ecosystem? The Conductors are that answer made flesh and metal, forever tending their vast mechanical garden in the endless dripping darkness, their bodies singing the maintenance hymns that keep their world alive.
