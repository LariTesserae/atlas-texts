The Lithovores came first, or perhaps they simply *were* first—it's impossible to say whether they evolved or emerged, whether they're organisms or processes that gained ambition. They exist as flowing aggregations of metallic sand, each grain a sensor, each cluster a distributed nervous system. By day they spread across the sun-facing facades like living rust, absorbing radiation and trace minerals from the building surfaces. By night they congeal into denser forms—sometimes bipedal, sometimes something more like industrial equipment with intent. They communicate through electromagnetic pulses that interfere with the city's data streams, creating a constant background static of negotiation between biological imperative and digital infrastructure.

In the mid-levels live the Resonance Keepers—creatures of living crystal whose bodies ring like tuning forks with every movement. They tend the harmonic gardens where arrays of mineral formations grow in precise mathematical ratios, each garden calibrated to produce specific acoustic environments. The Keepers have no visible sensory organs, navigating instead by echolocation through their own chiming bodies. Their young grow as buds along their crystalline limbs, eventually breaking free with a sound like shattering glass that nonetheless leaves both parent and offspring intact. They trade in frequencies—collecting rare sonic patterns from the industrial depths and selling them to the communication arrays above.

The Extractors occupy the deepest galleries, though "occupy" suggests a separation between creature and function that doesn't exist. They are the mining machines, or the mining machines are them—biomechanical entities that exist in perfect symbiosis with their industrial roles. Their bodies incorporate drilling apparatus as naturally as other creatures grow teeth. They feed on the energy released by breaking molecular bonds in the deep rock, following veins of precious minerals with the dedication of predators tracking prey. Some have grown so large they can no longer return to the surface, their bodies having fused with the tunnel systems they've created. They communicate through vibrations in the rock itself, their language a percussion that sometimes triggers sympathetic earthquakes.

High in the tower tops nest the Archive Birds, whose mica wings store data in their crystalline structure. Each feather contains thousands of layers capable of recording electromagnetic patterns. They soar between communication arrays, gathering transmissions, their bodies becoming living libraries of the city's information traffic. During mating displays, they fan their wings to project holographic records—market data, industrial blueprints, intercepted messages—in shimmering auroras visible for kilometers. Their eggs are perfect spheres of compressed data, worth fortunes to information brokers who risk the climb to raid their nests.

The Amalgams defy even the loose taxonomies applicable here. They appear to be colonies of smaller entities—part biological, part mechanical, part mineral—that came together and forgot how to separate. They shift between forms: now a maintenance crew repairing a broken conduit, now a single predator hunting the electrical parasites, now dispersed into component parts that scatter through the ventilation systems. Each Amalgam maintains its own internal ecosystem, with smaller creatures living in the gaps between its components, cleaning and maintaining the collective body in exchange for shelter and sustenance.

In the electromagnetic winter zones lurk the Null Dancers—creatures that exist in the absence of energy rather than its presence. They appear as mobile voids in the electrical fields, their forms visible only as distortions where power should be but isn't. They feed on entropy itself, growing stronger in areas where systems break down. The city's inhabitants have learned to read their movements as warnings—where Null Dancers gather, infrastructure failure follows.

The Sovereign Processors rule the factory levels, though "rule" implies a governance they would find irrelevant. These massive beings merge so completely with the production systems that distinguishing creature from factory becomes meaningless. Their bodies incorporate conveyor systems, chemical processing chambers, and quality control sensors. They think in production quotas and efficiency ratings, dreaming in supply chains. Smaller creatures live as symbiotes within their mechanical organs, performing maintenance in exchange for the exotic compounds that leak from the industrial processes.

Between the levels move the Couriers—sleek beings of living metal that flow through pneumatic tubes and maintenance shafts with liquid grace. They've evolved beyond the need for distinct form, their bodies shapeshifting to match the infrastructure they traverse. They carry more than packages—their metallic flesh stores chemical messages, electrical patterns, even quantum states that they deliver between the city's disparate intelligences. Some have been traveling the same routes for so long they've worn grooves in reality itself, creating shortcuts through space that only they can navigate.

The youngest species are the Data Weavers, evolved—or perhaps manufactured—within the last few centuries as the city's information density reached critical mass. They exist primarily as patterns in the electromagnetic spectrum, only occasionally condensing into visible form as meshworks of light and static. They feed on corrupted data streams, cleaning the city's information systems while growing more complex with each error consumed. Their physical manifestations look like three-dimensional circuit diagrams made of bioluminescent fiber, constantly rewriting themselves.

At the boundaries where city meets raw mountain, the Margin Walkers maintain the frontier. These creatures of stone and metal exist in constant transformation, their bodies incorporating new minerals from the virgin rock while simultaneously extending the city's infrastructure into unclaimed territory. They build as instinctively as spiders spin, laying down the frameworks that the city will claim and fill. Their movements follow the logic of ore deposits and geological stress patterns, reading the mountain's intent and translating it into urban form.

All these species—if species they are—exist in a web of interdependence so complex it resembles ecology less than it does a single vast organism with specialized cells. They compete and cooperate, prey and symbiose, their interactions mediated by the city itself, which may be the ultimate organism here: a lithic intelligence too vast and slow for any single component to comprehend, dreaming mineral dreams while its children scurry through its veins, maintaining and extending the patterns of its incomprehensible thought.
