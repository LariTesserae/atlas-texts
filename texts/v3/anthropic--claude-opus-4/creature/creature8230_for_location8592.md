The Dwellers of the Suspension move with deliberate grace, their forms evolved for a medium that is neither air nor water. They are tall, attenuated beings whose skin has taken on a peculiar translucency—not transparent like glass, but permeable like dense fog. Through their flesh, one can glimpse the slow circulation of fluids that glow faintly, pulsing in rhythms that synchronize with the city's tidal breathing.

Their limbs are longer than seems practical, with additional joints that allow for movements impossible in places governed by simpler physics. Fingers that can bend backwards as easily as forwards, arms that undulate rather than simply flex. They navigate their rope bridges and saturated walkways with a fluidity that makes them seem like extensions of the architecture itself—reaching, grasping, flowing from point to point.

They have no need for conventional eyes. Instead, clusters of sensitive nodes dot their heads and shoulders, organs that detect pressure changes, chemical gradients, the subtle electrical fields generated by living things. These nodes can extend on short stalks when greater sensitivity is needed, or retract flush with the skin during rest. Their faces are serene, simplified—a small mouth used more for intimate communication than consumption, nostril slits that filter the suspension, extracting what they need.

The eldest among them show signs of the city's patient transformations. Their skin develops intricate patterns of ridges and channels where beneficial algae take root, creating living tattoos that shift and change with their host's moods. Some elders have begun growing small coral colonies along their spines and shoulders—not parasites but symbiotic architecture, tiny ecosystems that provide both nutrients and a form of external memory, storing chemical impressions of important events.

Children are rare and precious, born in clutches of two or three, emerging not through painful birth but through a kind of gentle budding. The young begin almost ephemeral, their forms so adapted to the suspension that they can barely be distinguished from the medium itself. They solidify slowly over their first years, their parents and community carefully tending them in nursery pools where the suspension is denser, more nurturing. The children learn shape by mimicry, their plastic forms taking on characteristics of those who care for them—a borrowed gesture here, a way of moving there, until they develop their own unique geometry.

They sustain themselves on the city's quiet abundance. The phosphorescent algae they cultivate provides not just light but nourishment when prepared properly. They harvest the barnacles that grow on their walkways, not for the flesh but for the sweet secretions they produce. The jellyfish-giants that drift between buildings are sacred partners—the Dwellers swim briefly inside their bells during certain ceremonies, emerging changed in subtle ways, carrying some of the jellyfish's borrowed light in their bodies for days afterward.

Their tools are few but profound. They shape implements from the cartilage of deep-dwelling creatures, materials that hold form but flex when needed. They weave nets from their own shed skin fibers, which remain sensitive to their touch even after separation. Their most valued possessions are the resonance stones—smooth pebbles that, when held during important moments, absorb emotional imprints that can be shared by touch generations later.

Community decisions happen in the Consensus Pools—depressions in certain plazas where the suspension thickens to almost jelly. Groups submerge themselves together, their nervous systems temporarily intertwining through the conductive medium, allowing for communication beyond language. These sessions can last for days, the participants emerging with shared understanding, their individual opinions evolved into collective wisdom.

They mark time not by arbitrary divisions but by bodily changes. A child's first branching of finger-joints, the appearance of the initial algae patterns, the season when one's skin becomes permeable enough to attempt the journey to another settlement. Their memories are not linear narratives but spatial—they remember by revisiting places, by touching the walls where they've cultivated specific colonies of bacteria that hold chemical traces of the past.

In the deep districts, where the buildings dream, some Dwellers have begun to merge with their homes. They sleep with their backs against walls that gradually accept them, their skin fusing with the stone in a process that takes years. These Wall-Singers, as they're known, become living interfaces between the architectural consciousness and the mobile population. They can influence the buildings' slow reorganizations, convincing doorways to remain stable, negotiating with windows that want to wander.

Their songs—for they do sing, after a fashion—are not made of sound but of chemical releases, pheromone symphonies that paint emotional landscapes in the suspension. A funeral song might cause local algae to dim in sympathy. A celebration might trigger bioluminescent blooms throughout a neighborhood. Their art is ephemeral by nature, experienced in the moment and then dispersed, leaving only the faintest trace memories in those who witnessed it.

Death, when it comes, is not an ending but a transformation they've learned to embrace. The dying make their way to the Dissolution Gardens at the city's lower edges, where the suspension grows so thick it's almost solid. There they allow themselves to slowly merge with the medium, their consciousness dispersing into the warm darkness. The Dwellers believe these dissolved ancestors remain present in the suspension itself, occasionally reassembling temporarily to offer guidance or simply to experience singular existence once more before dispersing again.

The most accomplished among them can consciously control their permeability, becoming more or less solid at will. These Phase Dancers serve as intermediaries with the clockwork-hearted fish and other creatures that inhabit the suspension. They can partially dissolve to swim with the schools, learning the patterns of movement that serve as the fishes' language, then resolidify to translate these patterns into forms their less mutable kin can understand.

They dream collectively, their sleeping forms releasing threads of consciousness into the suspension that tangle with others', creating shared dreamscapes that process the community's experiences. Night in the city—what passes for night—sees thousands of these thin threads of thought wisping between buildings, forming a temporary web of unified unconsciousness that some believe is what truly holds their society together.

Their children play games that would seem impossible elsewhere—catch with objects that might or might not decide to fall, hide-and-seek where the seeker must predict not where the hider is but where they're becoming. They learn early that intention shapes reality here, that expecting a thing to behave a certain way is often enough to make it so, but that too much certainty makes the world rigid, brittle.

The Dwellers have no word for "stranger." Those who arrive from elsewhere—if they survive the journey through the changing physics—are simply absorbed, their foreign geometries slowly edited by the suspension until they fit. Some retain memories of other ways of being, but these fade like dreams, replaced by the deep knowledge of how to exist in this space between states.

They are, in the end, a people shaped by patience and permeability, by the understanding that boundaries are suggestions rather than laws. They persist not through resistance to their environment but through exquisite adaptation to it, becoming themselves by becoming part of everything else, maintaining identity not through rigid form but through the gentle insistence on continuity of purpose, of care, of connection. They are what happens when consciousness accepts that it need not be singular to be meaningful, when community becomes not just a way of living but a way of being.

In the warmth of the suspension, they continue their slow dance of existence, tending their glowing gardens, singing their chemical songs, raising their translucent children, and dreaming their collective dreams, embodying a way of life that suggests solid earth and empty air might be the true exceptions, and that the space between states might be where life was always meant to flourish.
