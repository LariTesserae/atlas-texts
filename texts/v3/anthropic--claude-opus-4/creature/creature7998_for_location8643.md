In the Kelp Metropolis, the Shepherds move with deliberate grace, their forms stretched and compressed by millennia of abyssal living. They are neither fully human nor entirely Other—something that branched away from the common ancestor when the ice was young and the seas were different.

Their bodies tell the story of their covenant with depth. Ribs flare wide like ship hulls, containing modified lungs that process water and store oxygen in specialized blood cells that glow faintly crimson in the dark. Their skin has taken on the texture of sharkskin, embedded with microscopic denticles that channel water in perfect laminar flows. When they move, they disturb nothing—the water parts and reseals around them as if they were never there.

The Shepherds' fingers have grown into gardening tools. The index extends twice the length of the others, its tip hardened into a pruning blade of keratin. The thumb opposes at angles impossible for surface anatomy, allowing them to grip and twist kelp stems with precise torque. Their palms secrete engineering pheromones—chemical instructions that tell the kelp exactly how to grow. Each Shepherd's hands smell different, their signature chemistry as unique as fingerprints.

They do not see as surface dwellers understand sight. Their eyes have split into compound structures, the main orb huge and silvered for gathering the faintest bioluminescent traces, while smaller secondary eyes ring the skull like a crown of black pearls. These see in different spectrums—heat, electrical fields, pressure waves. When a Shepherd looks at you, they see your blood flow, your neural firing, the compression waves of your heartbeat spreading through the water.

Their voices come in layers. The click-speech for navigation and quick communication sounds like breaking chalk. Beneath this, their vocal cords have thickened to produce subsonic tones that carry for miles through the water. But strangest is their third voice—a chemical language released through specialized pores along their throats. They speak in molecules, in scent-words that linger in the water long after the speaker has passed.

The eldest among them show the deepest adaptations. These Ancients have begun to photosynthesize, their skin colonized by the same symbiotic algae that line their gills. They move slowly, spending hours in the light-gardens where the brightest kelp nodes glow. Their bodies are becoming like their city—part plant, part animal, wholly something new. The very eldest no longer swim at all but root themselves in the kelp groves, their consciousness spreading through the mycelial networks that connect the entire metropolis.

Children are rare and precious. The Shepherds reproduce slowly, their pregnancies lasting three years. The young are born able to swim, their gill slits already functional, but they cannot survive the pressure without years of gradual acclimatization. They grow up in the middle depths of the city, slowly descending as their bodies adapt, their bones thickening, their organs shifting. By the time they reach the deep gardens, they can never return to the surface—the lower pressure would tear them apart.

The Shepherds organize themselves not by families but by Gardens—each collective tending to specific groves of the city. The Garden of Memories cultivates kelp that stores chemical records in its tissues. The Garden of Walls grows the defensive perimeter, kelp bred for thickness and toxin production. The Garden of Lights maintains the bioluminescent nodes, a delicate art requiring perfect balance of nutrients and electrical stimulation.

They wear little, for clothing interferes with their chemical speech and pressure sensitivity. What decorations they have are living—cultivated barnacles that filter feed from their blood, providing nutrients while creating intricate patterns across their skin. The patterns indicate their Garden, their age, their accomplishments. Elders are entirely encrusted, moving reefs unto themselves.

Their tools are extensions of their biology. They wield pruning staves of cultivated kelp heartwood, harder than any terrestrial wood. Their nets are woven from processed kelp fibers and the silk of deep-sea spiders they've domesticated. Most prized are the song-bones—whale ribs that resonate at specific frequencies, used to communicate with the kelp itself and the larger things that move beyond the city.

But they are not alone in their metropolis. The Cleaners scuttle through the lower reaches—crustaceans the size of dogs, their shells gardened with living kelp that makes them indistinguishable from the walls until they move. They maintain the city's health, consuming dead tissue, redistributing nutrients. The Shepherds direct them with pheromone trails but also speak to them in the old way, the click-speech that predates their city.

The Messengers swim the vertical highways—eels bred for intelligence, their bodies crackling with directed electricity. They carry chemical messages between Gardens, their perfect memory storing molecular languages in specialized organs. Some say the eldest Messengers understand more than they should, that they've begun to compose their own chemical poems in the dark corners of the city.

The Symbiotes live within the Shepherds themselves—colonies of bioluminescent bacteria that inhabit their blood, their gut, their neural tissue. These provide light when needed, help digest the exotic deep-sea nutrients, and some believe they contribute to the Shepherds' extraordinary spatial memory. When a Shepherd dies, their Symbiotes flee the cooling body in a cloud of light, seeking new hosts, carrying memories with them.

Most mysterious are the Singers—things that may have once been Shepherds but have transformed beyond recognition. They live in the deepest reaches where the kelp grows strange, their bodies elongated into ribbon-like forms dozens of feet long. They vocalize constantly in frequencies that maintain the kelp's health, their songs providing the resonant frequencies the plants need for optimal growth. To see a Singer is considered a profound omen, though whether good or ill depends on the harmonic they're singing.

The Shepherds measure time not in days or years but in Growths—the cycle it takes for the outermost ring of kelp to mature, die, and be replaced. They think in plant-time, their movements deliberate, their plans spanning generations. They are patient as only gardeners can be patient, knowing that the best changes happen slowly, that evolution cannot be rushed.

In the dark hours (though all hours are dark here), they gather in the Speaking Chambers—hollow kelp bladders where chemical words can accumulate to concentrations that produce visions. They share the collective dream of their city, planning expansions that won't be complete for centuries, debating the ethics of new cultivars, remembering the long journey from sterile rock to living metropolis.

They know they are being watched. In the absolute darkness beyond their lights, older intelligences observe their experiment. Sometimes gifts appear at the city's edge—artifacts of impossible biology, tools that seem grown rather than made. The Shepherds incorporate these carefully, knowing that every gift has a price, even if the accounting comes late.

The Shepherds are changing, becoming more like their city with each passing Growth. Their children are born with more adaptations, their elders root deeper. They are on a journey whose end they cannot see—a transformation into something unprecedented, guided by their own patient hands. They are writing themselves into a new chapter of evolution, one careful cultivation at a time.

This is who tends the Kelp Metropolis: gardener-architects of their own biology, speaking in chemistry and light, patient as stone and fluid as water, neither fully human nor willing to be anything else. They have found their place in the crushing dark and made it bloom.
