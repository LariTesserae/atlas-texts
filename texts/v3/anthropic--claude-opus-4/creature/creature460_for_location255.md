In the Drowned Workshops of the First Builders, where kelp grows thick as tapestries across stone that remembers hands, the Inherited dwell.

They are not the builders—those passed beyond memory before the first waters came. The Inherited arrived later, in the centuries after the drowning, when the workshops had already begun their transformation into something between architecture and reef. They came not as conquerors or explorers but as recipients of an abandonded estate too vast to refuse.

The Inherited move through the passages with a deliberation that matches the currents. They have evolved past the need for haste, their forms elongated by generations of navigating narrow corridors between workshop walls. Their bodies are cartilaginous, built for compression and patient sliding through spaces where stone presses close. What might be called their heads are flattened, wedge-shaped, designed to part the heavy water with minimal effort. They have no eyes—what use for eyes in absolute darkness?—but their skin is a living map of pressure sensors, reading the water's every story.

They tend the kelp forests with a devotion that might be mistaken for worship by those who still distinguish between cultivation and reverence. Their limbs, which branch and branch again into appendages fine as root systems, work through the fronds with infinite care. They know each growth, each meadow, each hanging garden by the taste of the water that flows through it. They harvest not by cutting but by encouraging—guiding the kelp's growth, training it through windows and doorways, creating living architectures within the stone architecture.

The Inherited do not speak. Sound here is too precious, too prone to echo and distortion in the stone passages. Instead, they communicate through chemistry, releasing complex molecules that drift through the water like thoughts made tangible. These chemical sentences can take hours to fully unfold, their meaning deepening as different components reach the recipient in sequence. A conversation between two Inherited might span days, each exchange adding layers of nuance that build like sediment.

They have developed a relationship with the workshops that transcends mere habitation. Over generations, they have learned to read the stone's settling, to predict which sections will collapse decades before the first crack appears. They shore up walls not with new construction but with guided kelp growth, using the plants' holdfasts to bind loosening stones. In this way, they have slowed the workshops' decay, though they know they cannot stop it. They are gardeners of ruins, curators of a museum that dissolves grain by grain into the seafloor.

Their young are few and grow slowly, spending their first century learning the taste of different stones, the feel of various kelp species, the chemical signatures of water from different workshop districts. They mature not through size but through stillness, the eldest among them moving so rarely they become difficult to distinguish from the architecture itself. These ancients position themselves at crucial junctions, where currents from multiple passages meet, tasting all the water that flows through their domain.

The Inherited have rituals, though they might not recognize them as such. They maintain certain passages clear of kelp, not for travel but for the way water moves through them—creating currents that carry chemical messages throughout the complex more efficiently. They gather at the sites of recent collapses, not to mourn but to redistribute the kelp from the fallen sections, ensuring nothing is wasted. They visit the deepest, oldest workshops in processions that take months to complete, reading the finest stonework like scripture, though they have no concept of what it might mean.

In their dwelling chambers—workshops they have claimed and modified through patient centuries—the Inherited keep collections. Not of objects, for they have little use for possessions, but of water samples. They maintain pockets of water from different eras, different locations, sealed in kelp bladders or natural stone hollows. These samples are their libraries, their histories, containing chemical traces of everything that has passed through their domain. An elder Inherited can taste a sample and know the decade it was collected, the section of workshop it came from, what grew there, what died there.

They dream, if dreaming is the right word for the long suspensions they enter during the coldest seasons. In these states, lasting years sometimes, they slow their metabolism until they are barely distinguishable from death. But their pressure-sensitive skin continues to register every tiny change, building up layers of sensation that they process upon waking. They emerge from these suspensions with new understanding of the workshops' slow transformation, having felt the stone settle around them, having sensed the kelp's patient growth across their resting forms.

The Inherited know they are temporary. They can taste it in the water—the slow increase in pressure, the gradual cooling, the way the kelp grows differently than it did in their ancestors' time. They know that eventually the workshops will complete their journey into the seafloor, that the kelp forests will follow the nutrients elsewhere, that even their patient forms will find no purchase in what remains. But this knowledge doesn't trouble them. They continue their work with the same steady rhythm, tending gardens that will outlive them, maintaining passages that will outlast their purpose, adding their own chemical stories to the water that will remember them long after they are gone.

Sometimes, in the places where the water is oldest and thickest with memory, an Inherited will pause in their endless labor. They will extend their root-like appendages and taste deeply of that ancient medium. And for a moment—though they have no concept of what they're sensing—they might detect some trace of the world before: sunlight on stone, the ring of metal on rock, the salt of working bodies. These moments pass like currents, barely registered, but they leave the Inherited changed in ways too subtle to name. They return to their work with movements that might, to an observer who could see in such darkness, appear almost like reverence.

This is how the Inherited live: as inheritors of a legacy they cannot comprehend, as gardeners of monuments to efforts they cannot imagine, as the slow heartbeat of a place that has outlived its purpose but not yet its form. They are the intermediate stage between architecture and seafloor, between history and geology, dwelling in the pause between one kind of time and another.
