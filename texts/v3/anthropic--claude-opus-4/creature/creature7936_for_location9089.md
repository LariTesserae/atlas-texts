The inhabitants of the Underglass Markets have evolved alongside their environment, becoming something both more and less than what they once were above ground.

**The Vendors** work in shifts that follow no clock, their sleep cycles broken and reformed around generator failures and customer surges. Old Marta, who sells hybrid potatoes that taste faintly of copper, hasn't seen natural sunlight in eleven years. Her skin has taken on a translucent quality, blue veins visible like underground rivers. She navigates her stall by touch and by the pulsing patterns of the mushrooms she's trained to respond to her whistle. Her eyes, when she opens them fully, reflect light like a cat's—an adaptation shared by many long-term residents. She wears fingerless gloves with built-in LED strips, not for warmth but for signaling prices across the noisy market when her voice, hoarse from the damp air, fails her.

**The Moss Children** emerged from the deepest tunnels three years ago, though whether they were always human is a matter of debate. They move in small packs, their bodies covered in the same phosphorescent moss that carpets the abandoned stalls. It grows from them—or perhaps they grow from it. They communicate in clicks and chirps that echo the dripping water, and their fingers are too long, with too many joints. They trade in secrets: whispers about which factory pipes will burst next, where the safety inspectors won't look, which mushrooms are medicine and which are poison. They eat the pale ferns raw and sleep standing up in the darkest corners, their moss-glow dimming to near invisibility.

**The Salvagers** live in the spaces between market and factory, in maintenance shafts and abandoned ore-processing chambers. Yuri still wears his grandfather's mining helmet, though the headlamp was replaced years ago with a cluster of bioluminescent fungi he feeds with sugar water. His left arm ends at the elbow—a factory accident from his previous life—but he's fashioned a series of interchangeable tools that socket into the stump: magnetized probes for fishing electronics from flooded tunnels, articulated claws for handling live wires, even a small pump for draining waterlogged goods. His clothes are a patchwork of industrial materials: firefighter turnout gear, chemical-resistant aprons, rubber waders permanently stained with oil. He knows every pipe, every conduit, every forgotten passage. The factories above think they've sealed all the access points, but Yuri can disappear into a ventilation grate and emerge six hours later with a cart full of discarded circuit boards and half-empty chemical drums.

**The Chemist** operates from a shipping container laboratory deep in the market's heart. Nobody knows her real name. She wears a full-face respirator at all times, its filters glowing faintly green from whatever she's been brewing. Her fingernails have turned black from handling synthetic compounds, and her hair—what's visible around the mask straps—has gone white despite her youngish build. She speaks in chemical formulas and half-remembered poetry, selling tinctures that help the human body process the contaminated air, salves that prevent the moss from taking root in flesh, stimulants that let workers stay conscious through double shifts in the factories above. Her container is lined with specimen jars where she cultivates new strains of the market's flora, each plant labeled with cryptic notes about its properties. Some say she's trying to breed a plant that can eat through concrete, to open new passages to the surface. Others believe she's becoming plant herself, her blood gradually replacing itself with chlorophyll-analogues.

**The Brokers** maintain the market's connection to the world above, though they themselves rarely venture higher than the permafrost line. They dress in the remnants of surface fashion—business suits with the fabric wearing thin, dress shoes reinforced with rubber and metal plates, ties used as tool belts. Their smartphones are frankensteinned together from a dozen different models, held together with electrical tape and hope, running on operating systems modified to work with the market's sketchy network of pirated signals. They sleep in shifts in a repurposed subway car, its windows painted black, its interior a maze of monitors showing commodity prices, security camera feeds, and cryptic message boards. They age prematurely, the stress of maintaining the market's grey-legal status carving lines into their faces, but they know everyone and everything. If you need papers, they know who forges them. If you need medicine the surface world has banned or priced out of reach, they have a source. If you need to disappear, they can arrange that too—for a price paid in generator fuel or fresh batteries.

**The Rat King** is more rumor than confirmed presence, but vendors leave offerings nonetheless—scraps of synthetic meat, thimbles of clean water, burnt-out electronics. Some say it's a colony of the pale rats grown so intertwined they move as one organism. Others insist it's a person who's lived so long in the deepest tunnels they've forgotten human speech, communicating only in the chittering language of rodents. Whatever the truth, things left for the Rat King disappear, and those who show respect find their stalls mysteriously protected from the worst flooding, their generators running a little longer than they should.

Together, these inhabitants create an ecosystem as complex as the one formed by moss and mushroom. They've adapted to the eternal twilight, the constant damp, the industrial thrum. Their children—the ones who survive—are born with better night vision, more efficient metabolisms, an instinctive understanding of which fungi are friends and which are dangerous. They're becoming something new, shaped by the pressures of this underground world, still human but evolving into a form that can thrive where the surface world has failed them.
