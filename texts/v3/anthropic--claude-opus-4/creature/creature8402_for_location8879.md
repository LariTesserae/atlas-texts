The Cling-folk move through their vertical world with an unsettling grace, their bodies evolved for this life between stone and sky. They are compact people, rarely exceeding five and a half feet, with overdeveloped shoulders and forearms from a lifetime of climbing. Their hands are their most distinctive feature—broad as shovels with thick, articulated fingers that can find purchase on wet stone where others would slip to their deaths. The palms are deeply lined, almost grooved, channeling water away from their grip.

Their skin has a peculiar quality, somewhere between human and amphibian. It's thick and slightly waxy, beading water rather than absorbing it, with a faint iridescent sheen in certain lights. This adaptation extends to their hair, which grows in thick, rope-like strands that naturally mat together, forming water-shedding curtains. Most wear it long, braided with strips of treated leather into functional arrangements that keep rain from running into their eyes. The elderly develop patches of symbiotic moss on their skin—a sign of honor, as it only grows on those who've spent decades in the mist.

Children learn to climb before they properly walk. Toddlers are fitted with soft leather harnesses tethered to their parents, and by age four, they scramble up cliff faces with disturbing confidence. Their games involve racing up vertical surfaces, leaping between platforms with no safety lines, and a peculiar form of tag played across three dimensions. Adolescents prove themselves by solo climbs to the gardens above the cloud layer, returning with sun-fruit as proof of their journey.

The social structure mirrors the vertical nature of their world. Families don't live side by side but stacked atop one another in multi-generational towers carved into the cliff. The eldest occupy the lowest levels—partly from respect for their decreased mobility, but mainly because the deepest caves hold the family shrines and water-sources. Each generation builds upward, adding their own chambers and platforms, creating architectural genealogies readable in stone and wood.

Water-readers form the closest thing to a priesthood, though their role is intensely practical. These individuals can predict weather changes by the taste of mist, locate new springs by pressing their ears to stone, and most crucially, sense when the mountain is preparing to shift. They undergo years of training in the deepest caves, learning to interpret the mountain's humid whispers. Their pronouncements determine when to evacuate sections of the city, when to plant, and when to prepare for the rare dry spells that threaten their way of life.

The vertically-grazing goat-creatures, called cloudhooks by outsiders but known locally as "wet-brothers," have been so thoroughly domesticated they're arguably no longer the same species as their wild ancestors. Each family line breeds for specific traits—some for milk that won't curdle in the constant damp, others for wool that grows in naturally water-repellent spirals, and a few elite bloodlines for the ability to carry messages between city levels. The bond between herder and flock goes beyond the practical; herders learn to interpret their animals' movements as weather signs and use their bleating as a form of long-distance communication across the fog.

Artisans occupy a special niche, literally and figuratively. The rope-weavers work in teams, their workshops spanning multiple levels as they create cables hundreds of feet long. Master weavers can produce rope that remains flexible when frozen and actually grows stronger when wet. Wood-singers treat lumber with a combination of pressure, smoke, and fungal cultures, creating planks that flex without breaking and resist rot for decades. Stone-shapers read the cliff face like a text, identifying where to carve that works with the mountain's grain rather than against it.

The memorial practices reveal much about their relationship with mortality. The dead are not buried but given to the mountain through a process called "root-weaving." Bodies are placed in specially prepared crevices along with seeds of the carnivorous vines that help protect the city from climbing predators. As the vines grow, they incorporate the deceased into their structure, creating living memorials that defend the community. Families tend their ancestor-vines for generations, trimming and training them, reading omens in their growth patterns.

Their diet consists largely of what they call "shade-food"—plants adapted to the eternal twilight. Terraced gardens produce pale tubers with flesh like crystallized fog, mushrooms that grow as large as shields, and a peculiar grain that thrives in near-darkness. Protein comes from the cloudhooks, the bat-fish (caught in nets strung between platforms during their feeding flights), and large grubs cultivated in rotting log chambers. Everything is prepared with an eye toward preservation—smoking, salting, pickling, and a unique fermentation process using cave-molds that produces foods edible for months in the damp conditions.

The true rulers of the cliff city, however, may not be human at all. In the deepest occupied caves live the Listeners—individuals who've spent so long underground they've become something other. Their skin grows translucent, revealing the blue veins beneath. Their eyes cloud over, useless in the eternal dark, but their hearing becomes supernatural. They claim to hear the mountain's thoughts, to understand the language of dripping water and shifting stone. Whether they're mystics, madmen, or something altogether stranger, their prophecies guide major decisions. They survive on offerings brought by those seeking wisdom, never emerging into even the dim twilight of the city proper.

Children born during the mountain's shifts—when passages close and new ones open—are considered blessed and cursed in equal measure. Called stone-touched, they often develop unusual sensitivities. Some can find water by touch alone. Others seem to always know which routes through the city will be fastest, as if they feel the mountain's intentions. A few claim to dream the mountain's dreams, speaking of vast slow thoughts that span centuries. These children often become water-readers or descend to join the Listeners, bridging the human and geological worlds.

The cliff city's inhabitants are not merely adapted to their environment—they've become part of it, as integral to the mountain's ecosystem as the mist and moss. They think in verticals, dream in the sound of falling water, and measure time by the mountain's slow breathing. To leave the cliffs for flat ground induces a kind of vertigo that goes beyond the physical—a spiritual displacement from the only orientation that makes sense. They are, in the truest sense, a people defined by their place, shaped by stone and mist into something that could exist nowhere else.
