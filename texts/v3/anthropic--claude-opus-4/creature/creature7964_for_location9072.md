The Cittadini of Sottovelo emerged from necessity, shaped by generations of confinement until their forms became arguments for efficiency itself. They stand no taller than children—four feet at their greatest height—but move with the liquid confidence of eels through water. Their spines curve and recurve, vertebrae multiplied beyond human count, allowing them to flow through spaces that would trap rigid bodies. When they must, they can compress their ribcages like rats, shoulders dislocating and relocating with practiced ease.

Their skin has taken on the pallor of cave fish, not merely pale but translucent, showing the blue tracework of veins and the shadows of organs beneath. This transparency serves them—in the eternal dusk of work-lights and bioluminescence, they can gauge each other's health by the color of blood beneath skin, the rhythm of visible heartbeats. The skin itself has thickened into something between human flesh and callus, resistant to the constant abrasion of stone and metal, beading moisture like the walls they press against.

But it is their ears that mark them as true natives of Sottovelo. The external structures have evolved into complex whorls and chambers, cartilaginous baffles that can filter specific frequencies from the mechanical roar. A Cittadino can isolate the clicking of a worn bearing from three tunnels away, distinguish between the breathing of different pump stations, hear conversations through the sound-wall by reading the minute modulations in speakers' chest cavities. Their children learn to speak not by watching mouths but by pressing palms against throats, feeling the vibrations directly.

Their fingers have lengthened and multiplied in joints, each digit capable of independent rotation. A single hand can simultaneously turn three different valve wheels, or manipulate the finest watch-gear mechanisms while gripping a pipe for stability. The nails have hardened into something approaching tools—natural spanners and picks that regrow when worn down. Their feet mirror this adaptation, toes that grip and manipulate, making them as comfortable moving horizontally across walls as walking upright.

The Cittadini organize themselves around the Turnings—work shifts that rotate through the city's three eight-hour cycles, though without sun or sky, these divisions exist purely as social construct. Each Turning has its own culture, its own dialect of vibration-speech, its own traditional responsibilities. First Turning maintains the water systems, Second Turning tends the power generation, Third Turning handles the deep maintenance. Families often split across Turnings to ensure someone is always awake, always working, always listening to the city's mechanical pulse for signs of dysfunction.

They wear clothing only as tool-carrying systems—harnesses of treated fungus-leather studded with pockets, loops, and attachment points. Every adult carries their personal tool collection, accumulated over a lifetime and often inherited through generations: picks worn smooth by ancestral hands, gauges calibrated to specific machines, brushes made from their own shed hair bound with wire. The tools become extensions of their bodies, carried so constantly that their absence causes phantom sensations.

Their diet consists primarily of the filtered nutrients their barnacle-colonies extract, supplemented by the pale vegetation and occasional invertebrate protein. They have developed a cuisine of fermentation and concentration, reducing their food to dense, nutrient-rich pastes that can be eaten quickly without stopping work. Communal meals happen only during shift changes, brief moments where families cluster in the tight spaces between machines, sharing tubes of concentrated sustenance and pressing foreheads together to exchange vibration-words of affection.

The Cittadini do not age as surface dwellers do. The constant motion wears their bodies down evenly, like river stones. The eldest simply grow smoother, more flexible, their unnecessary mass worn away until only the essential remains. Death, when it comes, is often unnoticed for hours—a worker who simply stopped moving, mistaken for deep concentration until the blood visibly stills beneath translucent skin. The bodies are processed with the same efficiency as everything else, rendered down and fed to the gardens or the barnacle-colonies, keeping the careful balance intact.

They dream, when they sleep in their metal-walled niches, of impossible spaces—vast caverns where sound doesn't compress, where movement isn't confined to three dimensions of pipes and passages. But these dreams frighten more than they inspire. The young who speak too often of "above" are apprenticed to the deep maintenance crews, sent to the crawlspaces where the city's density teaches them that space is luxury, that the press of stone and metal and flesh is safety itself.

In their mythology, passed down through tactile poetry tapped on tunnel walls, Sottovelo was not built but discovered—a perfect machine sleeping in the stone, waiting for operators worthy of its complexity. The Cittadini see themselves not as trapped but as chosen, selected by the city itself to maintain its vast mechanical metabolism. They take pride in their compression, their adaptation, their transformation into components of the greater engine.

The few outsiders who find their way into Sottovelo often break from the sensory assault within hours—the sound, the pressure, the constant motion of bodies through spaces too small for sanity. But the Cittadini pity these surface dwellers, these wasteful creatures who need empty air and silence. In the packed tunnels of their city, surrounded by the proof of their purpose, the Cittadini know that they have evolved beyond mere humanity into something more essential: the consciousness of the machine, the soft parts that keep the hard parts running, the flesh that makes the metal meaningful.

They are Sottovelo, and Sottovelo is them, and the distinction matters less with each turning of the great mechanical day.
