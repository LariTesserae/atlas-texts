In the depths where furnaces once birthed molten streams, the Forge Shepherds make their warrens. They are neither fully human nor fully fungal, but something that emerged from the decades of spore-breath and metal-tainted groundwater. Their skin carries a faint luminescence—the same sickly phosphorescence as the mushrooms that bloom from chemical deposits. When they move through their domain, they leave faint trails of bioluminescent moisture that fade within minutes.

They number perhaps two dozen, though counting them proves difficult as they seem to merge and separate from the shadows of machinery with troubling ease. Their bodies have elongated to navigate the narrow spaces between pipes and root systems—torsos that can compress and extend, arms that reach improbable distances to harvest the metallic fungi they cultivate in the bowels of old smelting chambers. Their fingers end in something between human nails and root tips, equally suited to climbing the vertical gardens and prying apart corroded metal to access the chemical treasures beneath.

The Shepherds tend vast fungal gardens in the underground spaces where toxins still pool. They've learned to cultivate species that transform industrial waste into psychoactive compounds, nutritious paste, and the strange pigments they trade with the rare outsiders who know to find them. Their knowledge passes through spore and dream rather than word—young Shepherds inhale the wisdom of their elders in careful doses, experiencing memories of which valve to turn, which fungi to avoid, which chemical marriages produce sustenance versus poison.

Above them, strung between the canopy layers where conveyor systems once ran, the Web Dancers have built their suspended world. They arrived as children fleeing the city's collapse, and the constant moisture and heights changed them. Now they move on all fours as easily as upright, their palms and soles hardened into grip-surfaces that adhere to wet metal and bark alike. They've strung bridges of salvaged cable and woven vine between the highest points of the foundry, creating aerial highways invisible from below.

The Dancers dress in garments woven from the fibrous air plants they harvest, clothing that wicks moisture and dries within hours despite the perpetual damp. They speak in whistles that carry through the dripping acoustic environment—a language that evolved from warning signals about unstable structures into full communication. Their children learn to climb before they walk, carried in slings that leave their limbs free to grasp and swing.

Between these two groups lives Old Copper Mary, who remembers the foundry's last days of operation. The metals in the soil and water have preserved her beyond natural life, her blood running green-dark with copper saturation. She occupies the former foreman's office, now a grotto where oxidized pipes weep eternal streams of mineral water. The Shepherds bring her fungal offerings; the Dancers lower baskets of fruit from the solar collectors they've rigged in the canopy gaps. In exchange, she maintains the one thing both groups need: the pump system that keeps the lowest levels from flooding completely.

She speaks to the machinery in the language of maintenance—the specific percussion of a wrench on pipe joints, the coded rhythms that once meant "all clear" or "emergency stop." Somehow, the dormant systems respond. Safety valves open to release pressure. Drainage channels clear themselves. The industrial skeleton continues its ghost-operation, just enough to keep the green equilibrium stable.

The Painters work alone, each claiming a different spectrum of the foundry's chemical palette. They've learned to extract pigments from the metallic fungi, the rust-incorporating bark, the strange flowers that bloom in industrial glass light. Their studios occupy old quality-control rooms where the light, filtered through decades of algae growth on skylights, has particular properties. They create on surfaces of beaten metal and compressed fungal matter, documenting the marriage of industry and nature in abstract forms that seem to move in peripheral vision.

Young initiates from all groups sometimes experience the Merging—a temporary dissolution of boundaries between self and foundry. They speak of becoming the water moving through pipes, the nutrients traveling up root systems, the electrical ghost-signals that still flicker through abandoned control panels. Most return after hours or days. Some find quiet corners and gradually transform into something more plant than person, their consciousness spreading through mycelial networks until they become another kind of inhabitant entirely.

The Listeners position themselves at acoustic focal points throughout the complex, where the architecture concentrates sound. They claim to hear the foundry's memories in the resonance of dripping water on different metals, the way wind plays through broken windows, the subtle frequencies of root growth and metal fatigue. Their oral histories are sonic rather than verbal—they reproduce the sounds that tell stories, creating audio maps of what was and what is becoming.

Children born here know no other world. They play in the loading dock gardens, sliding down slopes of moss-covered conveyor belts, building forts in the hollows where machinery has been completely consumed by strangler figs. They learn early which plants produce safe water when squeezed, which fungi glow bright enough to navigate by, which metal surfaces still hold killing voltage. Their games involve racing through the three-dimensional maze of the transformed foundry, following routes that exist only in the marriage of industrial structure and vegetative growth.

At night, bioluminescent fungi pulse in slow waves through the darker chambers, their light-signals allegedly carrying messages between the different groups. The Shepherds claim the patterns predict where new chemical blooms will appear. The Dancers say they indicate structural changes—where a root system might soon compromise a bridge, where new passages might open. Old Copper Mary just nods and adjusts her valves, maintaining the balance that lets all of them survive.

The foundry's transformation continues, measured not in years but in the slow advance of root through brick, the patient oxidation of metal, the gradual adaptation of human to environment. Its inhabitants are neither separate from this process nor controlling it, but woven into it—human lives becoming as essential to the foundry's new ecosystem as the fungi breaking down its bones or the water cycling endlessly through its dying veins.
