The Inheritors of Keth-Maral exist as living equations, their bodies the solution to an industrial-biological formula written across generations. They call themselves the Siphon-Born, though outsiders—when they rarely come—see only their elongated forms moving through the murky water like vertical fish, their torsos stretched to accommodate modified lungs that can process both air and the mineral-rich liquid of their world.

The eldest among them, the Sphere-Wardens, have been so transformed by their environment that they no longer fully leave the water. Their skin has developed a subcutaneous network of copper filaments that conduct the sphere's electrical currents, allowing them to sense pump failures and pressure changes through their nervous systems. They float in the habitation sphere's center, eyes closed, finger-webbing spread wide, reading the city's vital signs through their bodies. When they speak—which is seldom—their voices carry harmonics that match the industrial thrum, as if they've tuned themselves to the city's frequency.

The Harvest-Swimmers form the majority of the working population. Their bodies show the most obvious adaptations: elongated ribcages that compress efficiently for deep dives, secondary eyelids that filter the industrial murk, and hands where the webbing between fingers has evolved tool-attachment points—small metal sockets grafted into the flesh where they can lock in wrenches, scrapers, and mineral extractors. They move in choreographed teams through the processing spheres, their work somewhere between dance and industrial maintenance. Each team develops its own rhythm, its own language of gesture and vibration, becoming a single organism with multiple bodies.

Children—called Bracklings—spend their first years in the nursery spheres, smaller chambers where the water is filtered cleaner and kept at precise temperatures for optimal development. They learn to swim before they walk, developing the distinctive Siphon-Born gait that serves them both in water and on the narrow catwalks—a rolling, undulating motion that looks awkward in air but flows like silk in their liquid medium. By age five, the mechanical symbiosis begins: they're introduced to their first companion mechanimal, usually a small copper fish whose clockwork heart they must wind daily, teaching them the rhythm of mechanical care that defines their culture.

The Pipe-Runners represent a newer adaptation, a caste that emerged only in the last three generations. Thinner and more flexible than their kin, they navigate the veins of the city—the thousands of tubes and channels that connect the spheres. Their joints bend in ways that would horrify standard human anatomy, and their skin secretes a protective oil that lets them slide through even the narrowest maintenance shafts. They've developed echolocation abilities, clicking and whistling to navigate the dark arterial networks, and some claim they can understand the language of the evolved eels that share their cramped domain.

Among all castes, the Filter-Priests command unique reverence. These individuals demonstrate a rare genetic mutation: their kidneys and liver have adapted to process the industrial water directly, making them living purification systems. They float in designated chambers, the water cycling through their bodies, emerging cleaner. The process is voluntary but taxing—Filter-Priests rarely live past forty, their organs crystallizing from the inside out, leaving behind beautiful mineral formations that their families preserve as sacred relics. They're marked by the blue-white tracery that spreads across their skin as they age, mineral deposits forming fractal patterns that are considered holy.

The Siphon-Born mate in the water, their courtship rituals involving complex swimming patterns that mirror the city's pipe configurations. Pregnancy occurs in specialized maternity spheres where the pressure can be carefully controlled. Their children are born able to hold their breath for up to twenty minutes, a time that extends as they age. By adulthood, most can remain submerged for over an hour, though the Sphere-Wardens have been known to stay under indefinitely, their modified metabolisms switching to something approaching suspended animation.

They dream collectively, sharing visions through the medium of their city's water. The mineral content and electrical activity create a kind of liquid consciousness that they tap into during sleep, experiencing shared memories of sphere maintenance, inherited knowledge of pressure tolerances and flow rates. Some claim to dream the memories of the mechanimals, seeing through compound camera eyes or feeling the tick of clockwork hearts.

Their greatest fear is the Dry Death—not drowning, but the absence of water. Those who must work in the air pockets suffer from a kind of reverse decompression, their bodies struggling without the familiar pressure. They wear weighted vests and breathe humidified air through specialized masks, counting the minutes until they can return to their liquid element.

In the frontier spheres, where evolution runs wild, some Siphon-Born have begun exhibiting unprecedented changes. Reports speak of individuals whose skin has developed bioluminescent patches that pulse in response to the aberrant water patterns, of children born with gill slits that actually function, of workers who've formed symbiotic relationships with the evolved mechanimals—their bloodstreams hosting tiny mechanical parasites that enhance their ability to process toxins and extend their dive times beyond anything previously recorded.

The Siphon-Born know they are no longer quite human, but they've forgotten what that word once meant. They are the Inheritors, the Suspended Ones, the Pressure-Adapted. They are what the city requires them to be: living components in an vast industrial organism, their hearts beating in time with the pumps, their breath synchronized with the filters, their dreams flowing through pipes and channels older than memory. They maintain their hanging world not out of duty but out of necessity—they have become so integrated with their environment that the city's death would mean their own, and perhaps that is exactly what the Deep Builders intended.
