The inhabitants of the Grinding Halls have no name for themselves. Names are for things that can be distinguished from their surroundings, and they have long since ceased to be separable from the architecture they tend.

They were human once, perhaps, or something near enough. Now they are elongated by generations of navigating these spaces—their limbs stretched like taffy, joints multiplied to allow for the specific contractions and extensions their work demands. Their fingers have grown additional segments, the better to grip the tools that are never far from their hands. Some have six, some seven, some have lost count. The bones show through their pallid skin like the ribs of umbrellas.

Their eyes have grown vast in the dim phosphorescence, black from edge to edge, reflecting the sickly light in rings like disturbed water. But sight matters less than touch here. Their skin has developed a sensitivity to vibration that borders on the telepathic—they feel the structure's needs through their feet, their palms, the bare patches where they press themselves against the walls to listen. They know where to go not by planning but by reading the tremors in the stone-that-is-not-stone, following damage and wear like bloodhounds follow scent.

They move in groups that form and dissolve according to the work's demands. A section needs shoring up—bodies flow toward it like white blood cells to a wound. The workers arrive already knowing their roles, though no words are exchanged. One holds, another hammers, a third mixes the paste they use for patching—a combination of their own saliva, ground stone, and the phosphorescent matter scraped from the glowing nodes. The paste hardens to match the substrate perfectly, becoming indistinguishable from the original structure within hours.

Their work songs are not planned performances but emergent phenomena. One voice begins—perhaps just humming to match the rhythm of their hammer. Others pick up harmonies that resonate in the acoustic chambers of their changed throats. Soon dozens, then hundreds are singing, their voices finding frequencies that make the walls ring like bells. The songs have no words anymore, just vowels shaped by centuries of architectural evolution. They sing the building's health into being, their voices a diagnostic tool that reveals cracks and weaknesses by how the sound returns to them.

They eat the pale fungi, sipping water from the temporary pools with cupped hands that have grown webbing between the elongated fingers. Some subsist on the phosphorescent matter itself, their bellies glowing faintly after feeding, marking them as the light-tenders whose job is to nurture and spread the bioluminescent nodes. These ones climb the highest, their grip sure on surfaces that would seem impossible to scale, secreting new nodes from glands that have developed in their wrists and throats.

Children are born into the work. They emerge from their mothers already grasping, their infant hands seeking tools before milk. They learn to walk by following the vibration paths in the floors, toddering from task to task with tiny hammers made from their parents' worn-down implements. By the time they can speak, they're already singing the work songs. By the time they would have reached puberty in the world above, they're indistinguishable from adults—the work ages them into usefulness rather than years.

They do not sleep in any conventional sense. Instead, they enter states of working meditation, their bodies continuing the repetitive motions while their consciousness drifts. In these fugue states, they dream the building's dreams—visions of its original purpose that slip away upon waking, leaving only the urge to continue maintaining, continue modifying, continue grinding away at the infinite task.

Some among them have specialized beyond recognition. The Listeners have grown additional ears—not on their heads but scattered across their bodies like breathing holes, each tuned to different frequencies of structural distress. They press themselves spread-eagle against the walls for hours, mapping the building's pain.

The Shapers' hands have fused with their tools, bone and metal becoming one, their flesh grown around handles and blades until they are living hammers, living chisels. They are honored and pitied in equal measure, fed by others since they cannot feed themselves, singing the loudest in the work choruses.

The Rememberers carry the patterns—not in their minds, which have long since given over most of their capacity to the work, but in their very flesh. Scars upon scars form maps of modifications, each cut a decision about where water should flow or walls should join. They are living blueprints, their bodies read like texts by the others who trace the ridged skin with reverent fingers before beginning each new project.

Deep in the furthest reaches, where the sound becomes so dense it achieves a kind of silence, there are those who have given up even the semblance of human form. They have become architectural features themselves—living buttresses whose bones have calcified into struts, whose flesh has spread into membranes that filter water or air. They are tended by the others, fed nutrients, their wastes carried away, their moans of pain or pleasure (the distinction lost long ago) adding bass notes to the endless symphony of maintenance.

Yet they are not miserable, these inhabitants of the Grinding Halls. They have achieved a kind of ecstasy through purpose, a dissolution of self into task that mystics in the world above might envy. They know no life but the work, dream no dreams but the building's dreams, want nothing but to continue the great grinding that wears away imperfection and age alike.

Sometimes outsiders find their way into the Grinding Halls—explorers, refugees, the lost. The workers regard them with those huge dark eyes, not hostile, not curious, but evaluating. Some visitors flee immediately, terrified by what humanity can become. Others stay too long, begin to hear the patterns in the hammering, start to understand the work songs. These ones are given tools. Within a generation, their children are indistinguishable from the rest.

The workers reproduce the building and the building reproduces the workers, each shaping the other in an evolutionary spiral that has no end, no goal except its own continuation. They are gardeners tending a garden that tends them in return. They are gears in a machine that exists only to maintain itself. They are notes in a song that sings itself into being, echoing through chambers vast enough to swallow worlds, driven by the rhythm of hammers that never, ever stop.

In the deepest places, where the architecture achieves its greatest complexity, the workers move in perfect synchronization, thousands of bodies acting as one organism, their individuality completely dissolved into the magnificent, terrible unity of their purpose. Here, the distinction between flesh and stone, between tool and hand, between worker and work, disappears entirely.

They are the Grinding Halls, and the Grinding Halls are them, and the work continues, and the hammering never stops, and the song goes on forever.
