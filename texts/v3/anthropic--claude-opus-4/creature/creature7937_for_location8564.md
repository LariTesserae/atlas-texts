The Khora-Vethans are a people carved by their environment into something between flesh and mineral. They stand shorter than most humans, their bodies compressed and dense, with skin that has taken on the texture and coloration of volcanic glass—smooth in some places, rough and pitted in others, ranging from obsidian black to ash gray with veins of metallic sheen running beneath the surface. Their eyes have adapted to the eternal twilight, enlarged and multifaceted like those of deep-cave dwellers, capable of detecting the slightest variations in heat and light. These eyes never fully close—instead, a nictitating membrane of translucent stone-dust flickers across them for protection.

Their hands tell the story of their existence: broad and powerful, with naturally thickened skin at the palms and fingers that lets them handle hot stone without tools. Their nails have evolved into something like organic obsidian, sharp and durable, perfect for scraping mineral deposits and testing rock stability. Each individual's hands bear unique patterns of scars and calluses that serve as a kind of identity—no two Khora-Vethans work the stone in quite the same way.

They dress in layered wrappings of a strange fiber spun from the heat-resistant threads certain crystal-crabs produce. These garments start pale but gradually take on the colors of their environment—sulfur yellow, iron red, copper blue—creating a living record of where each person has traveled and worked. The wealthy among them (if wealth can be said to exist in such a fluid society) wear additional plates of polished mineral secured with the same resinous paste used in their buildings, creating a kind of armor that clicks and whispers as they move.

Their social structure mirrors their geology—constantly shifting, reforming around new vents of opportunity or catastrophe. They organize themselves into what they call "breath-bonds," groups of five to seven individuals who have learned to work in perfect synchronization, reading each other's movements in the dangerous dance of construction and demolition. These bonds form and dissolve based on the needs of the moment, though some particularly successful combinations might last for years, their members developing an almost telepathic understanding of each other's intentions.

The Khora-Vethans communicate through a combination of spoken language and mineral sign. Their vocal cords have adapted to produce sounds that carry well through the dense air—low thrums and high, cutting whistles that can pierce the constant background hiss of steam. But equally important are the patterns they trace with their obsidian nails on nearby surfaces, leaving temporary marks that convey complex technical information about structural integrity, mineral quality, and seismic warnings.

Children are rare and precious, raised communally in the most stable structures the city can maintain. From the moment they can walk, they learn to read the stone's moods, to feel the subtle vibrations that precede a quake, to taste the air for changes in mineral content. By the age of seven, most can identify dozens of stone types by touch alone and predict within minutes when a new vent will open.

Their diet consists primarily of the fast-growing vent-coral, which they process through elaborate grinding and fermentation to break down its mineral-heavy structure into something digestible. They supplement this with the rare organic matter that arrives on the plateau—seeds carried by the strange winds, the occasional hardy thornbrake fruit, and most prized of all, the fatty deposits found in certain crystal-crab shells. They have developed a cuisine of extreme concentration, where flavors are distilled down to singular, intense notes that explode on the tongue.

The Khora-Vethans worship, if it can be called worship, through the act of building itself. They believe that each structure they raise is a argument posed to the earth, and the earth's response—whether it stands or falls—contains profound truth. Their priests, called Stone Speakers, are those who have survived the most collapses and rebuildings, their bodies bearing the scars of countless negotiations with the volcanic forces below. These individuals develop an almost prescient ability to predict the plateau's behavior, leading their communities in the endless migration across the mineral shelf.

They mark time not by the sun—useless in their eternal twilight—but by the great eruptions that periodically reshape entire districts. Generations are named for the color of the ash that fell during their birth-eruption: the Gray Children, the Yellow Inheritance, the Rare Blues. Their oral histories are vast catalogues of structural engineering passed down in rhythmic chants that encode building techniques, material properties, and the locations of reliable water-condensation points.

In their few moments of rest, they practice a form of art that involves arranging small pieces of colored stone in patterns that shift based on viewing angle, creating images that can only be fully seen while in motion—fitting for a people who never stay still. Their music consists of resonant tones produced by striking different grades of volcanic glass, creating haunting harmonies that seem to rise from the earth itself.

The eldest among them gradually take on more and more mineral characteristics, their skin hardening, their movements slowing, until they become almost indistinguishable from the stone statues they sometimes carve. It is considered the highest honor to finally merge with the plateau itself, to become part of the foundation upon which future generations will build.

Despite their harsh existence, or perhaps because of it, the Khora-Vethans maintain a dark humor about their circumstances. They have a hundred different words for types of collapse, each more elaborate and ridiculous than the last. They place small wagers on which buildings will survive the night, and their children play games that involve stacking blocks while their friends shake the ground beneath them.

They are a people of profound practicality wedded to an almost mystical understanding of geological forces. In their eyes burns a fierce pride—not in what they have built, for all things fall, but in their ability to build again, to read the earth's violent language and respond with stubborn creativity. They are philosophers of impermanence, finding meaning not in monuments but in the act of construction itself, in the brief moment when fitted stone stands against the sky before the earth reclaims it once again.

To watch them work is to see a form of collective consciousness in action, each individual moving in response to vibrations and shifts that outsiders cannot perceive, their rope bridges swaying in anticipation of tremors yet to come, their hands already reaching for the next stone before the current one has settled into place. They waste nothing—not motion, not material, not moisture—and in their careful economy lies a form of beauty as refined and compressed as the mineral deposits they mine.

The crystal-crabs they have half-tamed serve as both livestock and early warning system, their behavior patterns indicating deep geological changes hours or even days before human senses can detect them. The Khora-Vethans have learned to read the migrations of these creatures like a living map of the plateau's intentions, following their trails to new deposits of workable stone or away from areas about to undergo catastrophic upheaval.

In the end, the Khora-Vethans are not so much inhabitants of their city as they are participants in a vast, slow dialogue between consciousness and geology, their entire civilization a kind of performed argument about the possibility of stability in an unstable world. They build not because they believe their buildings will last, but because the building itself is what makes them human, what separates them from the crystal-crabs and the living stone. In each block they place, they assert their existence against a universe of flux, and in each inevitable collapse, they find not defeat but the opportunity to assert it again, differently, better informed by the earth's latest lesson.

They are a people of ash and effort, of careful water-hoarding and explosive creativity, forever dancing on the knife's edge between destruction and construction, finding in that precarious balance a way of life that has endured far longer than any of their individual works. In Khora-Veth, nothing is permanent except change itself, and the Khora-Vethans have made themselves into living embodiments of this truth, as fluid and resilient as the magma that flows beneath their feet, as structured and purposeful as the stone they shape with their obsidian hands.
