The inhabitants have evolved alongside their city, becoming something between gardener and garden themselves.

Their skin carries a faint greenish undertone—not from any pigment, but from the symbiotic algae that lives in their pores, processing toxins from the humid air and supplementing their diet with traces of sugar. Children are born pale and gradually green as the algae colonizes them in their first years. The elderly grow darker, mottled where decades of symbiosis has created complex patterns unique to each individual.

They move with deliberate slowness, not from the heat alone but from a deeper rhythm. Their metabolism has slowed to match the city's pace. They think in seasons rather than days, plan in decades rather than years. A conversation might stretch across weeks, participants adding thoughts when they pass each other on the branch-paths, building ideas like coral. Their language itself has adapted—rich in long vowels that carry through the sound-dampening foliage, punctuated by clicking consonants that pierce the constant whisper of leaves.

Physical modifications mark their adaptation. Their feet are wide and gripping, toes that spread and clutch, soles thick with calluses that nonetheless remain sensitive enough to read the health of a branch by its vibration. Their hands too have changed—fingers slightly elongated, with tough pads that resist thorns and bark-scrape, but fingertips so sensitive they can feel the pulse of sap beneath bark, the first signs of disease in a leaf.

They dress in living clothes. Vines trained from birth to grow in specific patterns, their leaves providing coverage and cooling. The wealthy wear cultivated moss that changes color with the wearer's mood—a slow blush of red when angry, deep green when content. Working clothes are tougher affairs of woven bark and spider silk, but even these are alive, self-repairing when torn. Jewelry grows from the body—implanted seeds that sprout into delicate flowering vines, symbiotic orchids that root in hair and bloom once a year on the wearer's birthday.

Their diet blurs the line between agriculture and architecture. They eat the city itself—fruits from the building-trees, leaves from the window-vines, mushrooms from the basement-roots. But this is carefully managed. Each building has its edible sectors, marked by traditional patterns of growth. To overharvest is taboo; to take without tending is theft not just from the community but from the building itself. They supplement with insects—grubs raised in the walls, honeypot ants that store nectar in specialized chambers, beetles whose shells are candy-sweet.

Water defines their daily routines. They wake with the tide, descending to the root-streets when the water is low to tend the aquatic gardens, harvest shellfish that cling to the building foundations, check the health of the root systems. As the tide rises, they climb, their workday ascending with the water. By high tide, they're in the canopy, tending the aerial gardens, maintaining the bridge-roots, training new growth.

Social structure mirrors the city's vertical zones. The root-workers are a distinct class—those who spend their lives in the tidal zones, as comfortable swimming as walking. Their lungs have adapted to hold breath for long minutes, their eyes protected by transparent membranes. They speak a dialect rich in underwater gestures, hand-signs that communicate through the murky flood. Above them, the trunk-dwellers maintain the city's bones—the architects who speak to the trees, coaxing growth, healing disease, opening new chambers. At the top, the canopy-folk tend the connections between buildings, maintaining the aerial infrastructure, managing the complex negotiations when districts merge or divide.

Children grow slowly into their roles. For their first years, they live communally in nursery-chambers—hollow trees specially grown with many small alcoves where young ones can climb safely. They learn by tending—first mosses and simple vines, then more complex plants, finally apprenticing to whichever vertical zone calls to them. Education is tactile; they learn the hundred types of bark by touch, the thousand shades of green that indicate health or disease, the subtle signs that predict growth patterns years in advance.

Their medicine is the city's medicine. Healers diagnose by reading the sympathetic changes in their patients' symbiotic flora—a rash might appear first as discoloration in their moss-clothes, fever as wilting of their vine-jewelry. Treatment involves adjusting the balance—introducing new beneficial organisms, pruning overgrowths, sometimes simply relocating the patient to a different microclimate where their body's ecosystem can rebalance.

They mark time not by sun and moon but by the flowering of the city. The great building-trees bloom on different cycles—some annually, some once a decade, a few once a century. Religious ceremonies align with these bloomings. When the Cathedral-Tree flowers once every twelve years, the entire district celebrates for the week of its blooming, the air thick with hallucinogenic pollen that brings visions. Funeral rites return the dead to the city directly—bodies wrapped in quick-growing vine shrouds and placed in specially cultivated hollow chambers where they decompose into the wood itself, their nutrients absorbed, their carbon becoming part of the structure their descendants will inhabit.

Memory, for them, is spatial and botanical. They remember through growth patterns—that conversation happened when this branch was only a twig, that celebration occurred in the season these flowers first bloomed. The oldest can read decades of history in the growth rings of floor-branches, centuries in the twist of major trunks. Their art is living art—sculptures that are shaped trees, paintings that are gardens viewed from specific angles, music that incorporates the natural sounds of growth and decay.

They dream of roots and reach. In sleep, they say, their consciousness extends into the fungal networks that connect the building-trees, experiencing the city's slow thoughts. Whether this is metaphor or some deeper truth born of generations of symbiosis, none can say. But all agree that to sleep in the city is to dream its dreams—of water and light, of the push of growth and the patient dissolution of death into new life.

This is who they are: not builders but cultivators, not residents but participants in the city's long, slow life. They have given up the sharp edges of purely human existence for something softer, slower, more enduring. In growing their city, they have grown themselves into something new—still human in form but vegetative in patience, architectural in purpose, as much a part of the city's ecosystem as the beetles in the walls or the fish in the flooded streets.

They are the ones who tend the breathing city, and in return, it tends them.
