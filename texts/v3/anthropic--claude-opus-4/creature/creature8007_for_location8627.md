The inhabitants of the Vertical City of Moths have evolved alongside their strange symbiosis, becoming something between human and machine, though they remain fundamentally organic. They call themselves the Tenders.

Their bodies have adapted over countless generations. Limbs have lengthened subtly, fingers grown more articulate with an extra joint that locks in place when gripping the bridge cables. Their feet are broader, toes prehensile, able to grip through the soft-soled coverings they wear. But it's their internal changes that mark them most profoundly: lungs that process thin air with impossible efficiency, hearts that beat slower but stronger, pushing thickened blood through arteries that branch and rebranch like the city's own infrastructure.

They age differently than humans elsewhere—maturing slowly through decades-long childhoods, reaching their peak operational years around sixty, maintaining that prime for perhaps another century before the gradual decline begins. The eldest among them are uncertain of their own ages, having seen so many production cycles they've lost count.

The Tenders work in shifts that last months. A single maintenance rotation might see a worker stationed at one turbine array for an entire season, making minute adjustments to blade angles, monitoring the almost-imperceptible accumulation of harvested material. They sleep in small pods built into the factory walls, spaces just large enough to lie down in, where the constant vibration of the machinery creates a white noise that they cannot sleep without.

Their communication has evolved beyond spoken language. In the thin air, voices carry unpredictably—sometimes lost entirely, sometimes echoing between structures in confusing cacophonies. Instead, they speak through touches, through the precise angles of bodies on bridges, through patterns tapped on cables that carry vibrations between platforms. They have signs for technical concepts that exist nowhere else: the particular quality of wind that indicates optimal harvesting conditions, the seventeen distinct types of turbine hesitation, the names for different ages of moths.

Each Tender develops a personal moth-cloud. It begins in childhood—certain moths drawn to certain children, following them across bridges, clustering on their quarters during rest periods. By adulthood, each Tender moves within a constant constellation of perhaps thirty or forty moths that have learned their patterns, their scent, their electromagnetic signature. The moths serve as extensions of their senses, reacting to pressure changes, chemical leaks, structural instabilities long before human senses could detect them. A Tender reading their moth-cloud can diagnose problems in machinery they haven't even approached yet.

The relationship goes deeper. The moths feed not just on the factories' exhaust but on the Tenders themselves—not blood or flesh, but something more subtle. A kind of bioelectric field that healthy human bodies produce, which the moths can tap into without harm. In return, the moths' presence does something to the Tenders' metabolism, allowing them to process the harvested material that supplements their carefully managed diet. Without their moth-clouds, Tenders sicken within weeks, their bodies unable to properly metabolize. Without Tenders, the moths lose their navigational abilities, flying in confused spirals until exhaustion takes them.

There are specialists among them. The Bridge-Singers who maintain the cable networks, testing each strand through vibrations they create with tuning forks evolved into complex instruments. They can hear microscopic fractures in metal, feel the fatigue of materials through their feet. The Harvest-Monitors who read the invisible currents, understanding through senses both trained and inherited where the flows concentrate, where they thin, how to angle the turbines for optimal collection. The Deep Mechanics who descend into the oldest parts of the factories, where even the moths rarely go, maintaining machinery so integral to the city's function that no one fully remembers what it does—only that stopping it would mean death.

Children are rare, precious, planned carefully around production cycles. A pregnant Tender carries for thirteen months, their moth-cloud growing denser, more protective during this time. The children born here emerge with eyes already adapted to the eternal twilight, with that extra finger joint already beginning to form. They spend their first years in the crèche-platforms at the city's stable center, watching the work, learning the touch-language before they speak, developing their first tentative bonds with young moths.

The Tenders have their rituals, though they would not call them that. The Changing of the Filters, when entire sections of the city shut down for maintenance, and hundreds of workers move in choreographed patterns across the structures, replacing components in precise sequence. The Moths' Mating Season, when the air fills with pheromones that make the Tenders drowsy and euphoric, and work slows to essential maintenance only. The Accounting, when the harvested material is measured, processed, and a portion set aside—though for what purpose, only the eldest remember, and they communicate it through touches so complex younger Tenders can only partially understand.

They dream collectively. Not every night, but often enough that they've developed a rich vocabulary of touch-signs for the shared visions. Dreams of falling that last for hours of subjective time but end in soft landings. Dreams of the mountain speaking in frequencies below sound. Dreams of other cities, glimpsed impossibly far above or below, their lights flickering in patterns that might be language. Dreams of the time before the city, though none can say whether these are memories or fantasies—visions of the empty cliff face, of the first turbine's installation, of the first moths arriving like a gray snow.

Some Tenders develop the ability to hear the mountain itself. They speak of vast movements in the stone, pressures building and releasing over centuries, the slow breath of the living rock. These Deep Listeners often relocate to the furthest extremities of the city, where structure gives way to raw cliff face. They maintain the anchor points, but more importantly, they serve as early warning systems for the geological shifts that could, if undetected, shear away entire districts.

The dead are processed like everything else in the city. Bodies are wrapped in specialized cocoons and placed in chambers where the moths concentrate in their millions. Over the course of a year, the moths consume not flesh but something else—memories perhaps, or the accumulated bioelectric patterns of a lifetime. What remains is a crystalline structure, light as pumice, which is ground to powder and fed into the factories' intake systems. The Tenders believe this returns something essential to the city, that their ancestors literally become part of the infrastructure, their essence cycling through the turbines and processors forever.

There are those who leave—young adults who take the long climb up or down, seeking rumored other settlements. Most return within a decade, unable to adapt to thicker air, to stable ground, to life without their moth-cloud. Those who don't return are mourned with a specific pattern of bridge vibrations, a chord that carries through the entire city, felt in the feet of every Tender. Their moths, released from their bond, join the general population, carrying within their genetic memory the patterns of their lost partner.

The Tenders measure time not in days or years but in production cycles, in the gradual wear of turbine blades, in the generations of moths. They speak of the future in terms of structural maintenance, planning repairs that won't be needed for centuries, training children for jobs that won't exist until those children are elderly. Their culture is one of profound patience, of movements so slow and deliberate they approach the geological.

Yet they are not without joy. They find pleasure in the perfect synchronization of a maintenance dance, in the subtle variations of moth-wing patterns, in the rare moments when the wind carries sounds from impossibly far away—other cities perhaps, or something stranger. They compose vibration-songs that play through the bridge cables, melodies that can only be felt through feet and hands. They form deep bonds, partnerships that might last two centuries, marked not by ceremonies but by the gradual interweaving of their moth-clouds, until it becomes impossible to say which insects belonged to whom originally.

In their most private touch-conversations, they share theories about what they're actually doing. The harvested material—what is it? The energy they process—where does it go? Some believe they're feeding something vast sleeping in the mountain. Others think they're part of a machine so large its purpose can't be understood from within. A few whisper through careful touches about the times they've seen things in the deep factories that shouldn't exist—rooms larger inside than outside, moths that appear to be made of living metal, turbines that run backward but still produce.

But these are mysteries for the quiet hours, for the spaces between work. Most of the time, they simply tend. They maintain. They process. They traverse their bridges with measured steps, moths swirling around them like personal atmospheres, and keep the ancient machinery running for reasons that perhaps no longer matter, if they ever did. They are the Tenders, and tending is what they do, have always done, will always do, as long as the mountain stands and the wind carries its invisible harvest up the cliff face to their waiting turbines.
