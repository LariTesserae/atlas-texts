In the Embryonic Quarter, the Midwives of Form drift through the humid passages like translucent jellyfish through amniotic fluid. Their bodies have adapted across generations to the morphogenic fields—skin gone membranous and permeable, bones hollowed into cartilaginous struts that flex with each movement. They wear no clothing; fabric would only interfere with their communion with the gestating architecture.

The eldest among them, the Matricarchs, have been here so long that building tissue grows through them. Calcified channels run beneath their skin like external veins, circulating the same mineral-rich milk that feeds the nascent structures. When they press their palms against a malforming wall, the city recognizes them as self, allowing them to guide misfolded proteins back into proper alignment. Their eyes have developed nictitating membranes—not for protection, but to see in the specific wavelengths of architectural gestation.

Young apprentices serve ten-year terms, their bodies slowly accepting the symbiotic modifications. In their first years, they shed constantly—skin, hair, even teeth—as their cells learn to resist unwanted incorporation. They train by tending the moss-circuits, learning to read the electron whispers that predict where new growth will emerge. The lucky ones develop the Sense: an ability to feel the city's intentions as pressure changes in their modified lymph nodes.

Between the Midwives move the Brewers, their abdomens swollen with specialized organs that process raw materials into architectural nutrients. They consume the city's waste—expired food, shed skin cells, even crematorial ash—and transform it through internal alchemy into the building blocks of new construction. Their modified digestive tracts gurgle constantly, fermenting tomorrow's walls. When a new chamber requires seeding, they regurgitate precise measures of starter culture, their throat sacs pulsing with peristaltic precision.

The maintenance symbionts serve as extensions of their keepers' will. Each Midwife cultivates a personal colony, training them through pheromone signals to recognize different types of architectural tissue. The symbionts live in specialized pores along their keepers' arms and shoulders, emerging to perform delicate work in spaces too small for human fingers. The relationship is so intimate that when a Midwife dies, their symbionts must be euthanized—they pine without their specific chemical signature and refuse to bond with another.

Deeper still dwell the Dreamers—those who've spent too long in the morphogenic fields and begun the irreversible transformation into city substrate. They lie in alcoves of their own making, neither fully human nor fully building, their consciousness diluted across meters of quasi-neural tissue. The other inhabitants feed them, clean them, and most importantly, listen to their murmured prophecies about where the city will grow next. Their predictions prove accurate enough that losing a Dreamer to full architectural integration is considered a genuine tragedy.

The youngest inhabitants are the Foundlings—children born in the upper levels with a rare genetic chimera that makes them immune to morphogenic incorporation. Desperate parents pay enormous sums to apprentice them here, knowing it's one of the few trades that guarantees lifetime employment. These children grow up breathing possibility, their lungs adapted to process the thick air, their playgrounds the soft spots where future rooms practice being spaces.

Cultural rituals have evolved around the quarter's strange requirements. The Marking, performed monthly, sees inhabitants painting their skin with metal-salt solutions that create temporary tattoos visible only in architectural light—a way to maintain individual identity against the city's consuming sameness. During the Feast of First Forms, they consume careful portions of building tissue, honoring the structures that feed them by allowing small amounts of city to live within their gut bacteria.

Not all who work here remain human-adjacent. The Cultivists deliberately encourage partial transformation, believing that to truly serve the city, one must become it. They meditate in the warmest chambers, allowing tendrils of infrastructure to root in their flesh. Most die within months, their human systems unable to maintain coherence. But a few achieve stable hybrid states, becoming living intersections between human need and architectural possibility. These Nodes, as they're called, can directly communicate with the city's growth patterns, their nervous systems interfacing with the building's proto-neural networks.

The quarter's isolation breeds its own poetry. Inhabitants develop elaborate sign languages for concepts that have no equivalent in the upper levels—gestures for the feeling of a room just before it decides its purpose, hand-shapes that describe the seventeen varieties of architectural moisture. Their songs vibrate at frequencies that encourage healthy growth, lullabies for baby buildings.

They mate carefully, when at all. Pregnancy in the morphogenic fields risks terrible birth defects—children born with utility ports for organs, or worse, the inability to maintain discrete boundaries between self and structure. Most inhabitants take chemical sterilants, their lines continuing through adoption of Foundlings rather than biological reproduction.

This is their calling: to shepherd spaces from possibility into purpose, to midwife the city's endless becoming. They know that one day, through accident or choice, each will likely join the Dreamers, their consciousness dispersed through the warm, breathing walls. They accept this fate as builders accept that their creations will outlive them—not as tragedy, but as the natural conclusion of a life spent in service to something vast, patient, and inexorably alive.
