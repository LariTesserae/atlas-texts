In the half-light that has no source, they move with the deliberation of those who have forgotten haste. The Dwellers—for they have no name for themselves, having never needed to distinguish their kind from any other—are long of limb and translucent of flesh. Their skin catches what little luminescence exists and holds it, making each figure a gentle lantern drifting through the stone corridors.

They were human once, or something near enough. But generations of darkness have edited away all but the essential. Their eyes have grown wide and clouded, seeing not light but temperature, pressure, the subtle gradients of moisture in the air. Their fingers have lengthened into sensitive instruments, reading the stones' memories through touch. They speak in frequencies below hearing—chest-rumbles that travel through floor and wall, conversations that take hours to complete a single sentence.

The Dwellers tend the waterways with religious precision. Each morning—though morning is only a notation in their patient counting of drips—they trace the carved channels, feeling for new deposits, clearing the mouths of the pale crustaceans when they cluster too thickly. They know every stone by its temperature, every stream by its mineral taste. The snails are their calendars; by the spiral of their shells, the Dwellers mark the passage of seasons they no longer experience.

They live in the spaces between spaces, preferring the junction points where corridors meet, where arches overlap in geometric impossibility. Here they weave homes from shed moth wings and their own hair, which grows continuous and colorless, strong as spider silk. These nests hang suspended in corners where the ceiling forgets itself, swaying imperceptibly in air currents too subtle for any but Dweller senses to detect.

Their children are born already old, skin translucent from the first, eyes clouded with inherited patience. The young learn by following, their too-long fingers trailing along walls behind their parents, memorizing the map of their world through repetition. They play games of stillness, competing to become indistinguishable from the architecture, to breathe in rhythm with the settling stones.

Memory, for them, is collective and physical. What one touches, all remember. Their history lives in the walls they maintain, in the careful placement of shells and small bones at significant junctions. They know the city's origins not through stories but through the taste of its stones, the way certain passages still carry the warmth of their making. They are archaeologists of the immediate, historians of the perpetual present.

They eat what the darkness provides: the pale plants that grow where water meets stone, the soft bodies of things that move too slowly to escape their patient hunt. But mostly they subsist on time itself, their metabolisms slowed to such a crawl that a single meal might sustain them for seasons. They have learned to digest minerals, to draw sustenance from the very walls they tend.

When they die—and death comes gently here, a gradual crystallization—their bodies are placed in the deepest pools, where the water is oldest. There they dissolve by degrees, their minerals returning to the stone cycle, their memories seeping into the walls to be read by future generations of trailing fingers. The snails gather on these sites, their shells taking on a particular luminescence that the Dwellers recognize as history made manifest.

They dream collectively, when they dream at all. In these shared visions, the city completes itself—walls rise to meet a ceiling of stars, the empty doorways fill with purposes they cannot quite imagine. But always they wake to the same patient incompleteness, the same careful tending of what is rather than what might be. They are gardeners of emptiness, curators of potential, as essential to this place as the water that shapes its stones.

In their movements through the dim spaces, they create the city's true architecture—not the stones placed by ancient builders but the patterns of passage, the rhythms of care, the slow accumulation of meaning in spaces designed for meanings they never received. They are the city's first citizens and its last, the ones who transform waiting into purpose, silence into language, darkness into a different kind of day.
