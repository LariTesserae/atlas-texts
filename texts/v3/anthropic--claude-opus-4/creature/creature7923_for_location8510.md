The inhabitants of this vertical city move with an economy of motion born from necessity. Their bodies have adapted—barrel chests that work harder for each breath, legs corded with unusual muscle from constant climbing, hands that instinctively grip any available surface. They dress in layers that never fully come off: thermal undersuits beneath coveralls, fleece-lined canvas jackets, knit caps pulled low. Everyone owns the same type of boot—steel-toed, thick-soled, with aggressive tread that grips wet metal grating.

The miners arrive on the first morning funicular, their headlamps still glowing from the pre-dawn descent. They're a quiet bunch, communicating mainly through nods and hand signals developed in the noisy depths. Their faces carry a permanent squint from transitioning between the dark of the tunnels and the harsh mountain light. At shift's end, they gather in the station's steam rooms—concrete chambers built around geothermal pipes where the heat loosens joints stiffened by hours of repetitive motion. They sit in towels, passing shared bottles of something home-distilled that burns away the taste of rock dust.

The cable car operators know every resident by their gait—how much the car will sway when someone boards, where they'll stand, whether they'll grip the rails or ride loose-kneed through the crosswinds. These operators live in small apartments built into the cable stations themselves, their windows overlooking the void. They keep cats that never go outside, fed on birds that fly too close to the warm station lights. During ice storms, when the cables shut down, they're the ones who venture out with steam hoses to clear the lines, moving hand-over-hand in harnesses while the city waits.

Children here grow up fearless of heights but respectful of edges. They attend school in reinforced buildings near the center of each ridge, their playgrounds enclosed in chain-link that rattles in the wind. They learn knots before they learn cursive, practice evacuation drills monthly, carry emergency whistles on lanyards. Their games involve racing up maintenance ladders and daring each other to walk the narrower catwalks. By adolescence, most can free-climb between platforms faster than adults can navigate the official walkways.

The elderly don't leave. Those who survive to old age have lungs like leather and a lifetime's intuition about which handholds will bear weight. They tend the few growing spaces—greenhouse shelves bolted to south-facing walls where hardy vegetables struggle in improvised soil. They run the city's small shops, selling necessities shipped up from below: replacement parts, dried goods, medicines for altitude sickness. They're the keepers of institutional memory, remembering which sections of the city have collapsed before, which foundations show signs of stress, where the rock wept water during the last warm spell.

The city's engineers live in a constant state of vigilance, their offices filled with seismographs and strain gauges, their radios crackling with reports from inspection crews. They sleep in shifts, someone always monitoring the instruments that measure the mountain's mood. They've developed an entire vocabulary for types of settling, for the sounds that precede a rockfall, for the way ice formation changes the resonance of the cables. Their social hierarchy is based on who has successfully predicted and prevented catastrophe.

There's a subculture of young adults who work the night shifts at the smelters, their social lives inverted to match the industrial rhythm. They gather after work in windowless bars carved into the rock, where the music has to be loud enough to overcome the constant industrial hum. They drink heavily, love recklessly, live with an intensity that matches the precariousness of their environment. Their apartments are cramped studios in the least stable buildings, the ones that sway noticeably even in calm weather, where rent is cheap because evacuation probability is high.

The medical workers here are generalists by necessity—trained to handle everything from altitude-induced edemas to injuries from falling rock. The clinic anchors the middle ridge, its helicopter pad the only flat surface for miles. They maintain hyperbaric chambers for the worst cases of altitude sickness, surgery suites that can handle trauma, a small morgue carved into the cold rock. The doctors and nurses rotate in from below on two-year contracts, though some find themselves staying longer, addicted to the intensity of practicing medicine at the edge of the world.

Between all these human inhabitants move the scavengers—both animal and those humans who exist at the margins. The goat-creatures have learned that human activity means occasional food scraps, and they've become bold, their sure-footed forms appearing suddenly on platforms, their strange rectangular pupils watching for opportunity. The flying creatures nest in the industrial superstructure but also in abandoned sections of the city, their chittering colonies creating micro-ecosystems of guano and insect life that some humans harvest for fertilizer.

And there are always transients—those passing through on the way to even more remote mining claims, fugitives who think the city's precarious nature means less oversight, researchers studying the young mountains, thrill-seekers drawn to the engineering marvel. They stay in the city's few boarding houses, structures that creak and pop as they settle, where hot water is rationed and the walls are thin enough to hear every neighbor's cough.

The city's spiritual needs are met by a handful of practitioners who've adapted their faiths to the vertical life. Services happen in repurposed industrial spaces, the congregation seated on metal folding chairs, hymns competing with the sound of ore cars passing outside. The cemetery is a series of niches carved into a north-facing cliff, bodies burned because the ground is too hard and unstable for burial. Some scatter ashes into the void between ridges, watching them disperse in the updrafts.

At night, when the smelter glow turns everyone into silhouettes and the mountain groans with thermal contraction, the city's life continues. Shift workers navigate by headlamp, their breath visible in the cold. Couples meet in the few warm corners, pressed against steam pipes. Children sleep fitfully in beds that sometimes shudder when the rock shifts. The animals hunt in the darkness—the flying creatures swooping between platforms, the goat-like browsers picking their way along paths only they can see.

This is a population that has learned to live with constant vertigo, to find comfort in the industrial thrum that means the city still functions, to build communities in spaces that hang above the abyss. They share a gallows humor about their chosen home, a pride in their ability to inhabit the uninhabitable. They mark time not by seasons but by periods between evacuations, between cable replacements, between the geological events that remind them they're living on a mountain that's still deciding what it wants to be.

In the morning, they'll wake to ice on the inside of windows, dress in yesterday's clothes with fresh thermal layers, check the weather reports and seismic readings, and step out onto platforms that sway slightly in the wind. They'll nod to neighbors, grip the railings, and move through their vertical world with the practiced grace of those who've chosen to build their lives in defiance of gravity itself.
