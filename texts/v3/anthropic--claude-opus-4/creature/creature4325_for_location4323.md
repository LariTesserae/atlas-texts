In this fevered seam between worlds, she makes her home—though "she" is perhaps too simple a word for what the Tender has become.

She was human once, or mostly so. A maintenance worker who stayed too long, breathed too deeply, let the space remake her cell by cell. Now her skin carries the same luminescent quality as the moss, a phosphorescent sheen that shifts between industrial gray and living green. Her hair has become something between wire and vine—copper filaments that grow in spirals, sprouting tiny leaves that chime against each other when she moves.

The Tender no longer needs sleep as humans understand it. She enters periods of stillness where she merges partially with the walls, her consciousness flowing through the moss network, tasting the chemical composition of each new leak, feeling the health of every growing thing. During these communions, her body seems to partially dissolve at the edges, becoming indistinct from the vegetation that clothes the metal.

Her fingers have grown long, too long, with extra joints that bend in disturbing ways. Perfect for reaching into the narrow spaces where new life tries to take hold. Her nails are translucent chitin shot through with copper veins—living tools that can coax growth from the most poisoned puddle or prune back the aggressive vines that would choke out weaker species.

She moves like water through her vertical garden, sometimes walking on the grated floor, sometimes climbing the walls with a spider's ease, her modified feet finding purchase on surfaces slick with condensation. The moths know her and cluster around her shoulders like a living cape. The salamanders swim through the air near her, drawn to the electromagnetic field her transformed body generates.

But she is not alone in her tending.

Her assistant came later—one of the factory's discarded experiments, perhaps, or something that crawled up from the depths through the grating. It resembles a mantis crossed with a pipe wrench, standing seven feet tall on legs that telescope and compress. Its carapace is dull metal etched with growing patterns of moss, and its head swivels 360 degrees, compound eyes reflecting the garden in thousands of fractal iterations.

The Mantis-Thing speaks in steam-whistle shrieks that the Tender understands perfectly. Together they perform the daily rituals: adjusting the flow of water from the weeping pipe, training vines away from vital machinery, harvesting the crystalline formations before they grow sharp enough to puncture the floor. The Mantis-Thing's blade-arms can prune with surgical precision, while its lower appendages sort seeds and spores into careful taxonomies.

They are not the garden's only inhabitants, but they are its architects.

In the depths of the moss terraces live the Borrowers—things that might once have been rats or pigeons or cats, now fused with stolen machine parts. They've built nests from wire and shed leaves, tiny furnaces where they smelt the garden's metallic fruits into new configurations. Their young are born with gears for hearts and eyes like LED bulbs, perfectly adapted to the twilight. The Tender feeds them oil-honey from the vine flowers and they sing to her in frequencies that make the walls resonate.

Sometimes others come. Runaway machines seeking sanctuary. Lost workers who stayed too long in the corridors and began to change. A colony of sentient rust that communicates through oxidation patterns. They integrate or move on, but the garden remembers them all—their chemical signatures absorbed into the moss, their stories coded in the growth patterns of the ferns.

The Tender keeps a journal, though paper would dissolve here in days. Instead, she writes in the living walls, training moss to grow in patterns that record the garden's evolution. Green-gold letters spell out taxonomies of impossible species, chemical equations for new forms of photosynthesis, love poems to the machine-god whose body houses this eden.

When the factory shifts—when walls groan and new pipes pierce through—the Tender and the Mantis-Thing dance a careful ballet of preservation. They relocate the delicate crystalline gardens, shore up the tree's roots, guide the confused moths to safety. They've learned to read the factory's moods in the rhythm of its pistons, to predict these geographic seizures before they happen.

On the deepest nights, when the moss glows brightest and the other pocket gardens become visible through the grating, the Tender performs a ritual. She presses her transformed hands against the sweating walls and sings in a voice that's part human, part steam whistle, part growing thing. The song travels through the moss network to all the other gardens, all the other tenders in their own impossible spaces.

They sing of what they've made in the cracks of civilization. Of evolution accelerated by industrial accident. Of the marriage between metal and meat, poison and perfection. Of gardens that exist not in spite of the machine, but because of it—a new nature for a new world, feral and fluorescent and free.

The Tender's eyes have no whites anymore, just deep green shot through with circuitry patterns. When she looks at you—if you're lucky enough to find this space, brave enough to enter—you see your own reflection transformed: wreathed in copper moths, crowned with gear-shaped leaves, lit by the bioluminescent glow of a world being born in the spaces between.

She might offer you oil-honey. She might show you the newest crystals, still humming with potential. She might let you stay long enough to feel the change beginning in your own cells.

Or she might simply tend her garden, acknowledging you with a nod before returning to her work—pruning, guiding, nurturing this impossible life into ever-stranger configurations. The Mantis-Thing watches from its perch among the pipes, head rotating to track your movement, waiting to see if you'll become part of the garden or merely another story coded in moss.

The factory churns on, oblivious to the miracle in its bones. But here, in this dripping corridor between walls, evolution has found a new workshop, and its gardeners work tirelessly to tend what grows in the spaces industry forgot to poison completely.
