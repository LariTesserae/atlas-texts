Those who dwell in Yith-Maral are the **Veil-Workers**, and they move through their mist-world like thoughts through a dreaming mind.

They are a tall people, averaging seven feet, but you rarely see their full height. The Veil-Workers walk always in a practiced stoop, their spines curved like question marks, heads tilted to catch the mountain's breathing-songs through the elongated, fluted cartilage of their ears. These ears—more like the sound-gathering organs of deep sea creatures than anything terrestrial—can swivel independently, cupping and channeling the harmonics that rise from the volcanic tubes. Each ear bears delicate scarification that supposedly helps them parse specific tones, patterns earned through decades of listening.

Their skin drinks the mist. Quite literally—it's semi-permeable, a blue-grey that shifts to deeper purple in the areas most exposed to moisture. Along their arms and shoulders run channels like river deltas, natural grooves in their skin that guide condensation to collection points where tiny, specialized pores can absorb what they need. They wear little clothing, just wraps of beaten bark cloth around their waists and elaborate glass-beaded moisture veils that hang from wooden frameworks around their shoulders. The veils serve both practical and social functions—catching extra water, yes, but also displaying status through the complexity of their beadwork and the rarity of the beetle-shell dyes used to color them.

But it's their hands that mark them as the shapers of this breathing city. Each finger extends to nearly twice human length, with an extra joint that allows them to curve back on themselves like a closing fern frond. The fingertips end in soft, paddle-like pads that secrete a mild adhesive—useful for handling wet glass tools without slipping, essential for their work with the aerial roots. When they work, their hands move like underwater plants in a current, flowing more than grasping, coaxing rather than forcing.

The Veil-Workers organize themselves around the principle of *sh'vel*—a concept that translates roughly as "productive loneliness." Each adult maintains their own workshop dome, always built at least fifty paces from any other. They work in isolation for days at a time, developing their particular craft—whether that's tuning the glass pipes that sing in the wind, breeding specific strains of phosphorescent moss, or maintaining the delicate ecosystem of a crown-toad's back-garden. But this solitude is punctuated by elaborate gatherings at the communal pools, where they share their work through a complex gift economy that values innovation over accumulation.

Their children are another matter entirely. Born with their skin still fully permeable, young Veil-Workers spend their first years submerged to their necks in the nursery pools, tended by the eldest members of the community who have grown too stiff to properly curve their spines for listening. The children's early years are spent learning to control their moisture absorption, to close and open their pores at will. You can identify a child's age by how much time they spend out of the water—newborns never leave, toddlers venture out for minutes at a time, and by adolescence they can manage whole days in the mist, though they still sleep in shallow pools until their majority.

The Veil-Workers practice a form of craft that borders on cultivation. They don't so much make things as encourage things to become. A master root-weaver will spend decades training aerial roots to grow in specific patterns, singing to them in subsonic tones that vibrate through the volcanic glass. Glass-tuners will carefully fracture the terrace stone along its natural stress points, then spend years playing the resulting tubes until they achieve just the right harmonic convergence. Even their tools are cultivated—they feed specific minerals to the crown-toads, whose back-gardens then produce thorns of precisely the hardness needed for different tasks.

They speak in two languages simultaneously. The first is verbal but nearly whispered, a susurrus of sibilants and soft vowels that barely rises above the ambient sound of falling water. The second is gestural, using those long, curved fingers to draw moisture patterns in the air that linger just long enough to be read before dissipating. Complex ideas require both channels, the words providing context while the gestures carry emotional weight and subjective meaning. Arguments—rare but memorable—look like elaborate dances, participants circling each other while pulling water from the air in increasingly agitated patterns.

Their relationship with the settlement's giant flowers is one of wary reciprocity. Selected individuals, known as Petal-Wardens, undergo a years-long preparation involving dietary restrictions and meditation in the highest pools, gradually altering their body chemistry until their sweat takes on a particular sweetness that the flowers seem to recognize. These Wardens alone can approach the blooms during their brief opening periods, collecting the strange star-seeds-eyes in baskets woven from their own shed hair (which grows increasingly translucent as they age, eventually becoming like fiber optic cable). What they do with these collections, they don't say, but the oldest buildings in Yith-Maral have walls studded with what might be seeds that seem to watch, seem to pulse with their own faint light when the mist grows thickest.

At night, when the phosphorescent moss glows brightest and the crown-toads begin their low thrumming courtship calls, the Veil-Workers gather at the pool edges—but not in them. They sit just far enough away that the water-light plays across their skin without fully illuminating them, and they practice *mol'var*, the art of productive silence. For hours they'll sit, breathing in unison until their exhalations match the rhythm of steam rising from the pools, becoming part of the settlement's living percussion. Young Veil-Workers often mistake this for meditation, but the elders know better—they're listening for the mountain's settling, feeling for new cracks, new breathing holes, new places where the roots and stone might collaborate on something unprecedented.

They mark time not by days or seasons but by settlings—the major shifts when whole terraces drop an inch or two, when new fumaroles open, when sections of the root-bridge network must be regrown. Each settling gets a name, usually after whatever new pattern appears in the breathing-songs. The current period is known as the Settling of Brass Reeds, after a peculiar harmonic that emerged three years ago from the newest terrace. Some of the younger Veil-Workers have taken to incorporating this tone into their work-songs, though the elders warn against becoming too attached to any particular sound. The mountain, they say, is still young. Its voice will change many times before it finds its true pitch.

When Veil-Workers die, their bodies are given to the aerial roots. Wrapped in sheets of crown-toad leather and suspended high in the fern-trees, they slowly merge with the root systems that will, in time, become new bridges, new homes, new instruments for the mountain's ever-evolving song. The eldest among them claim they can identify which roots contain which ancestors by the way they vibrate in the wind, though younger generations suspect this might be more poetry than fact.

Still, in a place where breath becomes mist becomes rain becomes the very blood in their moisture-drinking veins, perhaps the boundary between poetry and fact is as permeable as everything else in Yith-Maral. The Veil-Workers would appreciate the ambiguity. They have seventeen different words for "maybe," each one a different pattern drawn in mist, each one as true as the speaker's skill in shaping water and air and meaning from the perpetual fog of their volcanic home.
