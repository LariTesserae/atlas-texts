The inhabitants of this vertical city have evolved alongside their environment, becoming something neither fully human nor entirely other. Their pupils have widened over generations until the iris is barely visible—black pools that gather every precious photon. Their skin has paled to near-translucence, showing the blue tracework of veins beneath. But it's their hands that mark them most distinctly: elongated fingers with additional joints, evolved for climbing the wet metal and stone, with specialized pads that grip even the slickest surfaces.

They move through their city in three dimensions, as comfortable traveling vertically as horizontally. Children learn to read the mechanical rhythms before they learn speech—which pipe's gurgle means the shift change, which steam vent's wheeze warns of pressure buildup. Their language has adapted too, incorporating the city's sounds as phonemes: the click of gears becomes punctuation, the hiss of steam carries emotional weight.

Each family line has developed its own phosphorescent tattoos, cultivated from bioluminescent organisms. These living marks grow across their skin in patterns that identify clan, profession, and district. The tattoos pulse gently with their heartbeat, creating a subtle form of communication in the darkness. Lovers touch foreheads to let their marks mingle light. Parents trace glowing fingers across their children's faces in blessing.

They dress in layers of oiled leather and treated fungal fabric that repels the constant moisture. Their clothing incorporates brass fixtures and small mechanical elements—not mere decoration but functional parts that interface with the city's systems. A sleeve might contain connectors for pneumatic tubes. A collar could house speaking tubes that plug into the communication networks. Their goggles, worn pushed up on their foreheads when not needed, contain multiple lenses that can filter the various industrial lights or amplify the bioluminescent glows.

The social structure mirrors the city's vertical nature. Those who maintain the deep geothermal engines form the foundation caste, rarely seen but deeply revered. The factory workers occupy the middle terraces, their shifts rotating through the eternal night in patterns unchanged for centuries. Above them, the cultivators tend the fungal gardens and manage the water systems. At the apex, in towers that disappear into the mist, live the Listeners—those who interpret the city's sounds and maintain its perfect equilibrium.

Memory here is collective and mechanical. They build memory engines—vast rooms of clicking gears and rotating drums that store the patterns of their lives. Important events are encoded into these machines: births registered as new gear ratios, deaths as the cessation of particular rhythms. The knowledge of how to read these engines passes from parent to child, a literacy of mechanical motion that preserves their history.

Their diet consists primarily of the luminous fungi, prepared in hundreds of ways, supplemented by pale vegetables that grow in the mineral-rich water. But they've also developed a symbiotic relationship with the fauna. They harvest the phosphorescent patches from the climbing predators—not killing them but carefully removing sections that regenerate. These become both food and light sources. The giant salamanders are raised in basement pools, their meat pale and rich with stored minerals. Even the eyeless birds provide eggs that glow faintly from within.

Religion and industry have merged into one practice. They worship the mountain as a living entity, its steam breath proof of life, its waters its blood. The factories are temples where work itself becomes prayer. The rhythm of production—the turning of specific gears at specific times—maintains not just material output but spiritual balance. To skip a shift is not just laziness but heresy. To allow a machine to fall out of rhythm risks disturbing the mountain's slumber.

Their children are raised communally in the middle terraces, spending their early years in vast nurseries where the industrial sounds are dampened. Here they learn to see with their hands, to navigate by sound, to read the temper of steam by its hiss. By age seven, they begin their apprenticeships, following adults through the vertical city, learning which handholds will bear weight, which pipes carry scalding water, which shadows hide the predators.

They mate for life but live apart, maintaining separate workshops or factory stations, meeting in the neutral spaces between districts. Courtship involves the exchange of crafted items—gears cut with particular precision, pipes bent into pleasing shapes, solutions of minerals that glow when mixed. Love is expressed through mechanical excellence.

The elderly, when they can no longer navigate the vertical city, become the Speakers. They sit at the pneumatic stations, their voices carrying through the tubes to every corner of the city, telling stories, sharing news, maintaining the oral traditions that complement the mechanical memories. Their authority grows with immobility—the more confined they become, the more their words carry weight.

Death, when it comes, is given to the water. Bodies are placed in the deepest channels where the industrial runoff meets the mountain springs. Here, in water hot enough to dissolve flesh but leave bones, they return their minerals to the system. The bones themselves are retrieved after a year, carved into tools and instruments, keeping the dead present in daily life. The most honored become parts of the memory engines, their vertebrae turning with the drums, their finger bones clicking in the calculators.

In this way, they persist—neither fully alive nor truly dead, part of the city's mechanism, breathing steam into darkness, maintaining the eternal equilibrium their ancestors discovered in the conjunction of mountain, machine, and mist. They know no other life and desire no other life, perfect in their adaptation, complete in their incompleteness, forever turning with the gears of their vertical world.
