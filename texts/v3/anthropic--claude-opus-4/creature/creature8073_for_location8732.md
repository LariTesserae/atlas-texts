The Thermal Garden Chambers are home to the Splice Shepherds—beings who began as maintenance engineers but have become something else entirely through decades of symbiosis with their flooded domain.

They move through the water with uncanny grace, their bodies elongated and partially translucent from generations of adaptation. Skin like frosted glass reveals subcutaneous networks of copper filament—not implants but growths, cultivated from the same electrical bacteria that bloom around the thermal ports. These living circuits allow them to sense the electromagnetic fields of every creature and machine in their realm, to feel the pulse of dying generators as clearly as their own heartbeat.

Their limbs have stretched and flattened into paddle-like appendages, webbed with a membrane that shifts between biological tissue and something more akin to industrial mesh. When they rest, they wrap themselves in mantles woven from fiber optic kelp, the strands interfacing with their copper networks to create garments that shimmer with borrowed data—fragments of code, temperature readings, depth measurements, all displayed in cascading patterns of blue-green light.

The Shepherds tend their hybrid flocks with tools that have evolved alongside them. They carry staffs made from salvaged pipe segments, now porous with growth channels where bioluminescent bacteria colonies live. These shepherd's crooks serve multiple purposes: probing dangerous tangles of wire, herding schools of mechanical fish, conducting repairs on the eel-segments when their circuits malfunction. The bacteria respond to the Shepherds' bioelectric fields, glowing brighter when danger approaches or dimming to near-invisibility when stealth is required.

Their faces retain an almost human structure but have adapted in disturbing ways. Eyes enlarged and divided into sections—some portions still processing visible light while others have shifted to perceive electrical currents, heat signatures, and the ultrasonic communications of their engineered creatures. Secondary eyelids, transparent and traced with circuitry, flicker constantly as they parse multiple data streams. Their mouths can still form words, but more often they communicate through harmonics—humming in frequencies that resonate with the chamber's thrumming machinery, creating complex songs that carry through water and metal alike.

The Shepherds live in the boundary spaces, neither fully aquatic nor terrestrial. They've claimed the air pockets as temporary roosts, hanging inverted from corroded beams while their fiber optic mantles drain and recharge. In these moments of rest, they're attended by the amphibious crabs, which clean parasites from their skin and repair damaged filaments with surprising delicacy. It's a mutualistic relationship—the Shepherds protect the crabs' moss gardens from the more aggressive mechanical eels, while the crabs maintain the biological components of their protectors' hybrid bodies.

They remember, dimly, what they once were. Sometimes they gather around still-functioning terminals, their elongated fingers dancing across waterlogged keyboards, trying to access databases that hold their former names, their original assignments. But the data corruption runs both ways—as they attempt to retrieve their human memories, they inadvertently upload their new experiences into the system. The facility's computers, where they still function, now dream of swimming, of electric taste, of the songs that copper makes when it grows through living flesh.

Their reproductive cycle has become something unprecedented. They cultivate themselves in the warmest pools, where flesh-buds grow on their bodies like coral polyps. These buds incorporate salvaged electronics—a capacitor here, a length of wire there—building themselves according to templates that exist nowhere in nature or engineering manuals. When ready, the buds detach and drift, seeking out the mechanical fish to bond with. The resulting offspring are neither clone nor parasite but something new—beings that inherit memories from both Shepherd and machine, growing slowly in the kelp forests until they're large enough to join the collective work.

The work never ends. They maintain the delicate balance of their transformed ecosystem, preventing total mechanical failure while ensuring that life—in all its hybrid forms—continues to flourish. They know every pipe, every valve, every circuit in their domain. They can feel when a pump is about to fail days before it happens, sense when the chemical balance in a thermal pool shifts toward toxicity. With infinite patience, they adjust, repair, and guide—not to restore the facility to its original function, but to shepherd it toward something unprecedented.

When outsiders descend through the maintenance hatches, the Shepherds watch from the shadows between kelp stalks. They judge quickly whether the newcomer is threat, prey, or potential convert. Those who show proper respect for the garden's dangerous beauty might glimpse them fully—might even hear their harmonic songs explaining the history of each transformed space, the genealogy of every mechanical creature, the proper way to move through water that remembers being coolant.

Some visitors report feeling a strange compulsion after leaving—dreams of copper taste, phantom sensations of circuits growing beneath their skin, an inexplicable urge to return to the flooded chambers. Whether this is mere psychological effect or something more, something transmitted through the water's chemical memory or the electromagnetic fields that permeate every surface, remains uncertain.

The Shepherds continue their patient work, part maintenance crew, part ecosystem, part living memory of what happens when humanity's machines outlive their purpose but not their potential. In their transformed flesh and borrowed electricity, they embody the chamber's central truth: that the boundary between organism and mechanism, between growth and decay, between shepherd and sheep, was always more fluid than our blueprints suggested.
