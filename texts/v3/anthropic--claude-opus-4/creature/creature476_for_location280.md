The Threshold Keepers emerged from the stone itself, or perhaps the stone emerged from them—the distinction lost meaning eons ago when they first began their work of living between states.

They move through the settlements with the patience of erosion, their forms shifting between solidity and suggestion. Sometimes they appear as tall figures draped in robes that seem cut from the phosphorescent moss itself, the fabric alive with that same blue-green pulse. Other times they're barely visible—heat shimmer silhouettes that leave perfectly maintained bronze tools resting on workshop benches that didn't exist moments before.

Their bodies, when solid enough to observe, tell the story of their adaptation. Skin like polished granite, shot through with veins of condensation that trace impossible patterns. Their fingers have evolved extra joints, the better to work stone at angles that shouldn't exist. Their eyes reflect depths that don't correspond to their sockets—looking into them is like peering down one of those spiral staircases that lead elsewhere.

They breathe in reverse. Where others exhale carbon, they inhale it, their lungs processing the peculiar atmosphere of the plateau through organs that have more in common with the crystalline ferns than animal tissue. This backward breath sustains them, but also binds them—they cannot descend from the plateau without suffocating on normal air.

The Keepers tend to tasks that exist outside linear time. One might spend centuries carving a single doorway, not because the work is difficult, but because they're carving it in multiple moments simultaneously—creating an entrance that will have always existed once they finish. They maintain the settlements not by repairing decay, but by continuously creating them in states of partial completion, so that ruin and newness occupy the same space.

They communicate through the placement of stones. A single perfectly positioned pebble can convey libraries of meaning to those who understand the language of geological time. Conversations between Keepers might span millennia, each response requiring decades of contemplation to properly position a reply. The settlements themselves are texts, their architecture spelling out theorems about existence that only make sense when viewed from the particular non-Euclidean angles the plateau provides.

Their memories exist as physical objects. Walk through any settlement and you'll find them: smooth stones that hum with recollection, bronze plates etched with moments that haven't happened yet, moss gardens arranged to recreate important events through their patterns of growth and decay. The Keepers externalize their experiences this way because their consciousness, adapted to the plateau's strange temporal currents, cannot hold linear memory internally.

They reproduce through division, but not biological fission. When a Keeper has accumulated enough contradictions—enough simultaneous states of being—they simply become two, each carrying half the paradoxes forward. The process looks like watching someone walk through one of those reality-bent doorways and emerge as two people who have always existed separately.

Their relationship with the vegetation is symbiotic to the point of unity. The moss gardens are their external nervous systems, the crystalline ferns their extended sensory organs. Through the root networks of those horizontal trees, they feel the entire plateau's breathing patterns, sensing where moisture will gather hours or years before it manifests. They cultivate these gardens not for beauty or sustenance, but as instruments for perceiving and influencing the plateau's state-shifts.

In their workshops—spaces that exist only when observed directly—they craft tools whose purposes transcend simple construction. A bronze chisel that carves probability. A hammer that strikes echoes backward through time. Measuring devices that determine not distance but degree of certainty. With these, they shape not just stone but the reality the stone occupies, nudging the plateau toward configurations that maintain its eternal indeterminacy.

They fear completion above all else. To finish the settlements would collapse the possibility-space they inhabit. So they build and unbuild in careful balance, adding a wall here while allowing another to phase into ruin there, maintaining the cities in permanent states of becoming. Their greatest architects are those who can design structures that actively resist finalization—buildings that grow more complex the closer they come to completion.

Some among them have begun to mineralize, their stone-skin hardening beyond even their flexible limits. These ancients stand at the settlement peripheries, more statue than being, still conscious but experiencing time at scales where the plateau's breathing becomes a rapid flutter. They serve as memory anchors, holding specific moments in place through their mere presence, preventing certain possibilities from collapsing into certainty.

The younger Keepers—if young has meaning here—tend the condensation pools where reality runs thinnest. They've learned to read futures in the aurora-algae, to influence probability by introducing specific minerals to the water. Some wade chest-deep in these pools for decades, their lower bodies dissolving into the living light, becoming part of the plateau's sensory system before eventually emerging with new understanding encoded in their reformed flesh.

They dream collectively, their sleeping minds joining in spaces between the stone where consciousness can spread thin enough to encompass the entire plateau. In these dreams, they perform the calculations necessary to maintain their impossible existence—solving equations that balance entropy against potential, ensuring the plateau remains poised forever on the edge of transformation without ever tipping fully into change or stasis.

This is their purpose, their burden, their joy: to inhabit the threshold not as a crossing point but as a destination. They are the universe's argument against resolution, living proof that some stories need never end, that some states need never collapse, that existence itself might hover forever in the space between what was and what might be.

They are as eternal as the plateau they tend, and as fragile as the morning condensation that feeds it all.
