In the Living Weave dwell the **Threadkeepers**—though to call them a people suggests a unity their scattered existence defies. They are humans, or were once, before generations of negotiation with the Weave wrote new terms into their flesh.

Their bodies have adapted in unsettling ways. Fingernails grow thick and curved like hooks, perfect for catching hold when the floor suddenly tilts vertical. Their skin secretes a faintly bitter oil that certain aggressive vines find distasteful. Most disturbing are their eyes—pupils dilated permanently wide from lives spent in near-darkness, irises shot through with branching patterns that mirror the vessel-tubes of the Weave itself. Some say these patterns shift with the growth around them, a kind of living map written in the body.

They dress in cultivated skins—not leather in any traditional sense, but sheets of induced bark-growth, panels of compressed fungal matter, cloaks woven from the silk of web-spinning root systems. These garments grow even as they're worn, requiring constant pruning to prevent them from fusing with their wearer's skin. More than one Threadkeeper has been found transformed into a garden statue, their clothing having finally won its slow victory.

The Threadkeepers organize themselves around water-nodes, but these organizations dissolve and reform as fluidly as the Weave itself. A mother-node might support thirty souls one day, three the next, as inhabitants follow rumors of stable growth or flee an aggressive flowering. They leave marker-scents for each other—complex chemical messages painted on growth-points, readable for perhaps an hour before the Weave absorbs and redistributes them.

Their tools are living things: **grove-beetles** the size of housecats, selectively bred for their tunneling patterns; **whistle-worms** that emit specific frequencies to encourage or discourage growth; pouches of **architect-mold** that can convince wood to grow in spirals rather than straight lines. The most prized possession is a **memory-vine**, a parasitic plant that, when wrapped around the skull during sleep, can store and transfer knowledge of safe paths through the Weave. These vines are traded for dearly, passed down through loose lineages, and worn until they merge so completely with their host that removal would mean death.

Children born in the Weave rarely survive their first year—not from predation or disease, but from the Weave's eager incorporation. Infants left unattended for mere minutes might be found with tiny rootlets exploring their fontanelles, or wrapped in cocoons of helpful moss that slowly digest what they embrace. Those who survive childhood develop an uncanny ability to predict the Weave's intentions, moving away from walls moments before they sprout thorns, waking just as their sleeping platform begins its nightly migration.

The Threadkeepers have no written language—ink is food for mold, carved marks heal within hours. Instead, they maintain their culture through **growth-songs**, melodies that encode instructions for shaping useful spaces. A lullaby might contain the pattern for a nursery-pod that rocks itself. A work song could teach the precise angle to cut a water-vine without killing the entire system. These songs mutate as they pass between singers, accumulating new verses that describe new discoveries, losing outdated stanzas to the constant forgetting that survival demands.

They worship no gods but acknowledge the moods of the Weave. They leave offerings at particularly ancient growth-nodes—not to placate any consciousness, but as contributions to the ongoing experiment of existence. A handful of processed bone meal here might yield a grove of fruiting bodies there. A copper coin pushed into soft bark could redirect an entire sector's growth pattern. They read these patterns like auguries, though whether they're divining the future or creating it remains unclear.

Most unsettling are the **Deep Dwellers**, those Threadkeepers who've ventured so far into the Weave's interior that they've begun to change more dramatically. They return to the settlement-zones rarely, and when they do, they move wrong—too fluid, pausing mid-step as if listening to something others can't hear. Their skin shows patches of bark-like texture. Their hair grows in patterns that mirror their favorite travel-routes. They speak less and less, communicating instead through touch, through scent, through the subtle adjustment of growth patterns that other Threadkeepers learn to read or die.

The Deep Dwellers claim there are chambers in the Weave's heart where time moves differently, where a heartbeat lasts hours and growth can be observed like weather. They speak of the **Consensus Zones**, places where plant, animal, and human consciousness merge into something new—not hive minds but jazz ensembles, improvising reality in real-time. They bring back impossible things: fruits that sing when bitten, water that flows upward, beetles that build tiny copies of the Weave in miniature, recursive and perfect.

But most Threadkeepers stay in the middle zones, that endless space between surface and depth where life is merely difficult rather than transformative. They tend their temporary homes, teach their children the seventeen different screams of hunting vines, follow the water and the seasons of growth. They bury their dead by feeding them to the Weave—within days, a new garden blooms where the body was planted, carrying something of the deceased's movement patterns in the way its branches reach for light.

They are not the Weave's masters or its prisoners, but its collaborators in the ongoing experiment of existence—junior partners in an architecture that dreams new rooms into being with each breath of its vast, dispersed body.
