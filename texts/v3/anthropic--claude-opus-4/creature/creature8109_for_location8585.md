The inhabitants of the Canopy Cities have evolved alongside their environment into something new—neither fully human nor entirely other.

Their skin has taken on a faint jade translucence, subcutaneous chlorophyll supplementing their diet with processed sunlight. The adaptation runs deeper than pigmentation: their metabolisms have slowed to match the tree-time of their city. They live for centuries now, aging like the bark they tend—slowly, imperceptibly, adding rings of experience rather than withering. Their fingers have lengthened and developed additional joints, better for navigating the breathing pores and cultivation chambers, for coaxing growth from reluctant saplings.

They dress in living fabric—moth-silk garments that repair themselves, cloaks of engineered moss that process their waste heat into useful warmth. The industrial workers wear thick aprons of fungal leather that resist the caustic secretions of the fermentation engines. Status shows not in jewelry but in symbiotic partnerships: the wealthy cultivate rare luminescent orchids that root in their hair and shoulders, drawing nutrients from specially modified sweat glands. The factory overseers carry command beetles on their wrists, insects whose pheromone emissions can direct entire colonies of manufacturing invertebrates.

Their movements have adapted to this vertical world. They climb as easily as they walk, their bare feet gripping bark with specialized pads, their motion through the city combining elements of arboreal apes and patient gardeners. They think in three dimensions naturally, their language rich with positional concepts that ground-dwellers would struggle to parse.

The bio-engineers form the city's essential caste, though caste is too rigid a word for these fluid hierarchies. They wear robes of responsive lichen that display their current projects in patterns of bioluminescence—growth charts written in living light across their backs. Their laboratories are indistinguishable from gardens, their tools are modified organisms, their experiments take decades to yield results. They speak to trees in chemical whispers, adjusting pH with a touch, redirecting growth with hormone-laced saliva applied to the right junction.

Children are rare and precious. Pregnancy takes eighteen months now, the fetus developing in synchrony with the city's slower rhythms. The young are born with their eyes sealed, adapting to the eternal green twilight gradually over their first year. They learn to read the city's chemical language before they master speech, todddling through the vine-bridges following scent trails their parents have laid down for safety.

The workers of the industrial districts have their own adaptations. Their lungs filter spores and production gases. Their gut bacteria, inherited through generations, can digest cellulose directly, allowing them to subsist on wood pulp during lean seasons. They operate the fermentation engines through touch and smell rather than sight, their fingertips sensitive to minute temperature variations that indicate the health of their biological machinery.

Social gatherings happen in the orchards and along the vine-bridges. They share meals of processed fungus and fruit, the food as much medicine as sustenance—each bite carefully composed to maintain the delicate bacterial balance that allows them to digest their city's produce. They sing, but their music would sound alien to ground-dweller ears: long, droning harmonics that resonate with the wood, punctuated by chemical releases that add olfactory notes to the melody. Their instruments are grown rather than built—hollow gourds with internal structures that create haunting, organic tones.

Romance follows the patterns of the forest. Courtship is slow, involving the gradual exchange of pheromones, the cultivation of shared gardens, the intertwining of personal flora. Partners might spend years growing their homes together before committing to the final biochemical bonding that joins their symbiotic ecosystems permanently. 

Death, when it comes after centuries, is not burial but incorporation. The deceased are laid in the decomposition chambers deep in the trunk-towers, their bodies feeding the trees that fed them. Their personal symbionts—the lichens, the messenger birds, the cultivation beetles—are inherited by their descendants or released to find new partners. They believe consciousness disperses rather than ends, joining the vast, slow thoughts of the trees themselves.

The eldest among them are almost indistinguishable from their environment. Bark-skin and root-fingers, they sit in the growing chambers and guide the city's expansion through decades of patient work. They communicate mainly through chemical emissions now, having little need for speech. The young seek them out for their deep knowledge of growth patterns, their ability to predict where the city will shift over the coming century.

These are people who have chosen integration over dominion, who have allowed their city to reshape them as thoroughly as they reshape it. They dream in the language of growth and decay, think in cycles rather than lines, and measure success not in accumulation but in the health of the vast organism they inhabit and compose. They are gardeners and machines, wild and cultivated, human and other—a new branch on the evolutionary tree that sprouted when the first refugees climbed above the mist and decided to stay.
