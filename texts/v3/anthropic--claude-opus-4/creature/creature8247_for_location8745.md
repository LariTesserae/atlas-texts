The builders themselves have lost any memory of eyes. Their ancestors' ocular cavities have long since been repurposed—transformed into resonance chambers that help them navigate the eternal twilight of their copper cathedral. Where sight once lived, now a specialized organ thrums with each hammer strike, reading the water's vibrations like sheet music written in pressure waves.

Their bodies tell the story of their evolution. Each builder's dorsal shell has flattened and hardened into a working surface, ridged with geometric patterns that form naturally as they age—spirals and hexagons emerging from years of repeated impacts. The youngest among them still show hints of their ancestral forms: shells merely dimpled rather than perfectly flat, claws still bearing traces of joints meant for grasping rather than striking. But by their fifth molting, the transformation is complete. Their hammer-claws fuse into solid clubs of calcified tissue, dense as stone, ringing with their own internal harmonics.

They breathe the metallic water through modified gills that have learned to filter sustenance from dissolved minerals. Their digestive systems are furnaces in miniature—processing the bronze kelp into new metals that seep from specialized pores along their undersides. This excretion hardens instantly in the cooler water near the walls, forming the raw material for their endless construction.

The builders move in three-dimensional patterns that would seem random to eyes that could see them, but follow deep mathematical principles encoded in their nervous systems. They never collide. Even in the vastness of their chamber, they maintain precise distances from one another, their movements choreographed by instincts that predate their blindness. When one builder begins to hammer, others adjust their positions subtly, unconsciously, maintaining optimal acoustic spacing.

Their language is percussion. Simple concepts—food, danger, mating readiness—are communicated through rhythmic codes tapped against their own shells. But their true eloquence emerges in the work songs, the complex polyrhythmic symphonies that arise when multiple builders find themselves in acoustic alignment. These songs can last for days, each builder adding their voice to the composition, creating harmonics that make the entire chamber ring like a struck bell.

The eldest builders show signs of their eventual fate. Their shells grow ever flatter, ever harder, until movement becomes difficult, then impossible. These ancients eventually fuse to the walls themselves, becoming permanent fixtures—living anvils that younger builders use as working surfaces. They do not die in any conventional sense. Their hearts still beat, slow and deep. They still sing, their voices now pure tone, no longer modulated by movement. The youngest builders bring them food, pressing bronze kelp against what remains of their mouths, honoring them as both tools and ancestors.

Mating occurs in moments of perfect acoustic convergence. When the harmonics align precisely—perhaps once in a hundred cycles of work—two builders will recognize compatible frequencies in each other's songs. The courtship is a duet of increasing complexity, each partner adding new rhythmic patterns until their combined music causes the walls themselves to vibrate in sympathy. The act itself is brief, almost anticlimactic after such elaborate prelude. The female deposits eggs in the seams between copper plates, where they develop in the warm crevices, fed by the same mineral-rich water that sustains all life here.

The larvae emerge as tiny, translucent versions of their parents, already bearing the first hints of flattened shells. They feed on microscopic flakes of metal that constantly shed from the walls, growing slowly in the eternal twilight. Many are lost to the thermal currents, swept into the center of the chamber where the water grows too hot even for their adapted physiologies. Only those who quickly learn to read the water's movements survive to join the eternal work.

In their dreams—if creatures so transformed can be said to dream—they remember water that moved differently. Ancestral memories of tides and currents, of spaces with boundaries defined by light and darkness rather than metal and heat. But these memories fade with each generation, replaced by the deeper satisfaction of the work itself. The ringing of hammer on shell, the slow growth of new metal over old, the perfect moment when a fresh plate fits exactly into the space prepared for it.

They are no longer whatever they once were. The Drowning Forge has remade them as surely as they remake it, a perfect closed system of creation and creator, each defining the other in endless reciprocity. They persist because the chamber persists. The chamber persists because they persist. In the hot darkness, there is no other truth than this: the work continues, and the builders build, forever.
