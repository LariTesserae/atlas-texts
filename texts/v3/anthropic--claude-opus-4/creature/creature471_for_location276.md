The Kharra have no memory of arriving here. Their histories, encoded in crystalline memory-chambers deep within the terraces, speak only of the stone and themselves as eternal partners. They are creatures of segments and sections—thirteen body parts that can compress or extend as needed, each ring of their form bearing its own cluster of legs, its own breathing spiracles, its own simple nerve cluster. When threatened, they roll into perfect spheres, their overlapping plates forming an impenetrable shell of chitin and mineral deposits.

Their bodies tell the story of their evolution. The workers' mandibles have become industrial tools—some sport diamond-hard cutting edges grown from a lifetime of consuming quartz, others have hollow tubes for spraying acid precisely where stone needs weakening. The carriers have evolved backs like living barges, their segments fused into broad platforms rimmed with small gripping arms to secure loads. Some have become so specialized they can no longer roll into defensive balls, their bodies permanently locked in their functional forms.

But it is the processors that show the most extreme adaptations. Their middle segments have swollen into massive fermentation chambers where colonies of symbiotic bacteria break down stone into nutrients. These living factories waddle slowly through the industrial districts, their bodies so heavy with digestive machinery that teams of workers must help them up the steeper terraces. They eat constantly but feed hundreds, regurgitating processed stone-paste that hardens into edible blocks—gray, dense, faintly sweet with dissolved minerals.

The builders secrete their stone-paste from specialized glands, but the process costs them. Their bodies grow lighter as they work, becoming hollow as they empty themselves into their creations. The oldest builders are translucent, their chitin worn thin, moving carefully lest they shatter. They are revered and fed pre-processed food by younger generations, their expertise in construction making them too valuable to lose to the hunger of their craft.

Among them move the neural-castes—coordinators whose enlarged head segments house complex ganglia capable of processing the chemical signals that organize their society. They taste the air constantly, their antennae weaving patterns that translate scent into instruction. A coordinator can orchestrate the movement of thousands with a single pheromone release, turning chaos into choreography.

The nursery-workers tend the egg chambers in the warmest depths of the terraces, where volcanic heat incubates the next generation. Kharra young emerge as soft, pale versions of adults, their segments not yet hardened, vulnerable to the acidic environment of their industrial home. For their first year, they ride on the backs of nursery-workers, slowly darkening as minerals accumulate in their shells, until they're strong enough to walk the terraces alone.

The deep-miners venture below the city into natural caverns where the best minerals wait. Their bodies are elongated, sometimes reaching twenty segments, equipped with specialized boring tools grown from their own shells. They navigate by echolocation, clicking their mandibles in complex patterns. Some never return to the surface, establishing satellite colonies in promising veins of ore, their children evolving in darkness to become something almost unrecognizable to their surface cousins.

The memory-keepers maintain the crystalline archives, their bodies attuned to the resonant frequencies of stored information. They can read the histories by touch, their sensitive leg-tips detecting vibrations in the crystal lattices that encode millennia of knowledge. Their own memories become part of the archive when they die, their neural tissue crystallizing through a process the Kharra learned to control eons ago.

In the highest towers live the sky-watchers, their eyes evolved into compound arrays that can detect the slightest movement through the mist. They have developed a complex language of light signals, using bioluminescent patches on their shells to communicate across the fog-shrouded distances. Their warnings have saved the city from aerial predators countless times, though what exactly hunts in these highlands remains shrouded in mystery and seldom-repeated stories.

The priests—if that word applies—tend the sacred hot springs at the city's heart, where the first Kharra supposedly emerged from the stone itself. These individuals undergo a transformation, their shells becoming porous, their bodies learning to filter and concentrate the mineral-rich waters. They dispense these concentrated fluids in ritual ceremonies, and consuming them induces temporary changes—enhanced strength, altered perception, sometimes visions of patterns in the stone that lead to new veins of ore.

Disease among the Kharra takes the form of crystalline infections—minerals depositing wrongly in their joints, freezing them slowly into statues. The healers have evolved internal furnaces, specialized segments that can generate intense heat. They press these burning chambers against the infected, melting away the deadly deposits. The process is agonizing for both healer and patient.

The engineers design ever more complex machinery, though their tools remain primarily biological. They breed specialized creatures—living pumps with hearts the size of their entire bodies, living pipes that transport fluids through their hollow forms, living gears that rotate endlessly on modified joint systems. The boundary between creature and machine blurs until it becomes meaningless.

Their music, if it can be called that, consists of rhythmic vibrations transmitted through the stone itself. Percussionists strike the terraces with specialized limbs, creating complex polyrhythms that can be felt throughout entire districts. During festivals, the whole city thrums with these deep songs, and the Kharra dance by vibrating their segments in harmony, creating interference patterns that become part of the performance.

They dream collectively. When they sleep—clustered in vast dormitories, their bodies intertwined for warmth and comfort—chemical signals pass between them, creating shared visions. These mass dreams sometimes produce new innovations, as if their combined unconscious minds solve problems no individual could tackle. They wake knowing things they didn't know before, carrying blueprints in their minds for machines no one consciously designed.

The old ones, those who have lived for centuries, undergo a final transformation. Their bodies begin to petrify, minerals replacing organic tissue until they become living stone. But their minds persist, trapped in increasingly immobile forms. These elder-stones are built into the walls of important buildings, where they slowly think their glacial thoughts, occasionally managing to vibrate out a few words of wisdom to those who know how to listen.

The criminals—those who violate the chemical laws that govern Kharra society—are exiled to the fringes, forced to subsist on raw stone without the benefit of processing. Some manage to adapt, their bodies evolving crude processing abilities, becoming feral versions of their civilized kin. Others simply waste away, their shells growing thin and brittle until they crumble.

Children play by building tiny versions of the great factories, their games mimicking the industrial processes that keep their society alive. They compete to see who can dissolve stone fastest with their developing acid glands, or who can carry the heaviest loads up the practice terraces. Their play is purposeful, each game a lesson in survival.

The artists among them create sculptures from living stone-paste, forming abstract shapes that harden into permanent installations. But their greatest art is the city itself—every surface carved with purpose, every channel flowing with deliberate grace. They see beauty in efficiency, elegance in industrial precision.

When they die, the Kharra are processed like any other mineral resource. Their shells are ground into building material, their soft tissues fed to the factories' bacterial cultures. Nothing is wasted. They consider this the highest honor—to become part of the city they spent their lives building. Their consciousness, they believe, persists in some form in the stone itself, adding to the slow, accumulated wisdom of the terraces.

New castes continue to evolve. Recently, a mutation has produced individuals who can photosynthesize using chemosynthetic bacteria in their shells, drawing energy from the mineral-rich water rather than consuming stone directly. These sun-independents, as they're called, might represent the future of the species—or merely another branch in their endless adaptation.

The Kharra language consists primarily of chemical signals, but they supplement it with vibrations, clicks, and patterns of movement. A conversation might involve releasing specific pheromones while performing a particular dance, clicking mandibles in counterpoint to the message's chemical rhythm. Translation between castes can be difficult, as each has evolved its own dialect of scents and movements.

They mate seasonally, when the mists grow thick enough to almost obscure the terraces. The air fills with pheromones that drive even the most specialized castes into reproductive frenzies. Afterward, they remember little, returning to their daily roles as if nothing happened. The eggs appear in the nurseries, and life continues.

Their understanding of time differs from most creatures'. They measure it not in days or years but in erosion rates—how long it takes water to carve a channel, stone to wear smooth, a terrace to crumble and need rebuilding. Their longest time unit corresponds to the lifespan of their city sections, roughly five thousand years from construction to necessary demolition.

In the deepest archives, some whisper of contact with other cities, other colonies across the plateau. Chemical messages carried on the wind, too faint to fully decode. The possibility of a greater Kharra civilization, a network of cities sharing knowledge through methods yet to be discovered. But these remain rumors, hopes, dreams shared in the dormitories.

They continue as they always have—processing, building, evolving. The city grows incrementally larger each generation, new terraces carved from virgin stone, new factories rising from the designs of collective dreams. They are patient as only creatures who eat stone can be patient, certain in their purpose, unified in their endless, grinding progress toward something even they cannot fully imagine.
