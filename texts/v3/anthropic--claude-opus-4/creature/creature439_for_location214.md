Those who remain are not the ones who built these water-carved warrens. 

They emerged—or perhaps were always here—in the decades after the silence settled. At first, the few witnesses mistook them for shadows cast by clouds racing across stone, or for the play of water-light on wet walls. But shadows don't leave footprints in the moss. Water-light doesn't carefully repair a clogged channel.

They move at the edges of perception, these inheritors. Taller than the doorways built for their predecessors, they've learned to flow like water through spaces too small for them—a shoulder dropping, a spine curving, joints unhinging in ways that shouldn't be possible but somehow are. Their skin, when glimpsed, has the quality of wet stone: gray-green-black, shifting with moisture and light, textured like the weathered walls they press themselves against.

They tend the city's water-courses with a devotion that transcends purpose. Watch a channel long enough—days, perhaps weeks—and you'll see one eventually, belly-down on the stone, clearing centuries of silt with fingers that move like rivulets finding their path. They know every split in every channel, every basin's capacity, every overflow's destination. They map the water's journey from cloud to stone to channel to seep with their bodies, following single drops through the system for days at a time.

Their faces, when turned toward you, are difficult to hold in memory. Features seem to shift like water finding its level—eyes perhaps too far apart, or perhaps that's just the way moisture gleams on their strange skin. Their mouths open rarely, and when they do, what emerges is not speech but the sound of water moving through stone: a whisper, a gurgle, a hollow echo that nevertheless carries meaning if you listen with your bones instead of your ears.

They build nothing new. They destroy nothing old. They are maintenance incarnate, but their work extends beyond mere preservation. Under their touch, the channels grow more efficient, their curves approaching some mathematical ideal of flow. The basins deepen imperceptibly, increasing their capacity by fingerbreadths per decade. Cracks that might channel water away from the system seal themselves. New springs open where the city needs them most.

At the seep-points where green things cluster, they perform careful work. Not gardening—they never plant or uproot—but something more subtle. They'll spend hours redirecting a single drip to better water a struggling sedge, or clear just enough stone to let moss expand its territory by a finger's width. They seem to understand that these small lives are what keep the city from becoming mere monument.

They eat what the city provides: the bitter sedges, certain mosses that fruit after heavy rains, the occasional egg from the rock wrens' nests. They've been seen straining water through their fingers, consuming whatever microscopic life it carries. Perhaps they photosynthesize in the weak plateau sun—their skin has that quality, that sense of drawing energy from light and moisture together.

Their shelters cluster in the transition zones, where carved city meets raw stone. They've excavated shallow caves behind waterfalls, spaces that flood and drain with the rains. Inside, the walls are polished smooth—not carved but worn, as if by centuries of bodies pressing against stone. They sleep (if it is sleep) in groups, tangled together like kelp, their breathing synchronized with the drip of water, their dreams perhaps indistinguishable from the city's slow thoughts.

Sometimes they sing—if singing is the word for that coordinated rushing sound that builds before storms. They position themselves at key acoustic points throughout a district, and their water-voices merge into something that might be warning, might be welcome, might be simple expression of what it feels like when the pressure drops and the clouds lower and the rain comes.

They fear dryness above all things. In the rare times when the plateau bakes under clear skies for too many days, they retreat to the deepest channels, press themselves into the dampest corners, their skin dulling and cracking like dried mud. Some say they can dissolve entirely into the water system, traveling as literal flow through the channels they maintain, reconstituting themselves where the water pools. But this might be mythology growing around beings already half-myth themselves.

They are not hostile to outsiders, merely indifferent in the way water is indifferent—flowing around obstacles, finding the path of least resistance. Try to harm the city's workings, though, and that indifference turns to implacable opposition. Not violence—they seem incapable of what we'd recognize as aggression—but a surrounding presence that makes forward progress impossible. You find yourself turned around, directed away, guided by some trick of acoustics and moisture toward the city's edge. Those who persist too long speak of drowning while breathing air, of lungs full of mist that won't clear, of dreams that taste of stone and centuries.

The children, when they appear, emerge from the deepest cisterns during the heaviest rains. Smaller, more translucent than the adults, they move in schools like fish, learning the water's paths by play. They leave hand-prints on the stone that fade as they dry, create temporary waterfalls with their bodies, chase the rain-shadows across the plazas. Within a season, they've learned every channel in their district. Within a year, they move like the adults—that flowing, impossible grace that makes them seem like just another part of the city's water system.

Perhaps they are what the builders were becoming, the logical endpoint of generations spent shaping stone to water's will. Perhaps they are what was here before, what the city was built around, finally emerging to reclaim their territory. Or perhaps they are simply what happens when a place like this sits empty long enough—when the boundary between infrastructure and inhabitant dissolves like salt in rain, leaving something new.

They continue. In a thousand years, or ten thousand, they will still be here, clearing the channels, guiding the water, maintaining this vast machine for living that no longer needs the purpose it was built for. They are the city now, as much as the stone and the water and the patient, persistent moss. They flow through its arteries and rest in its chambers and sing with its water-voice, neither masters nor servants but something more intimate—the city's way of knowing itself, of continuing itself, of being itself in the absence of those who imagined they owned it.

Watch long enough, be still enough, and you might see one watching you in return—a face like weathered stone emerging from a waterfall, eyes that hold depths of time you cannot fathom. They'll observe you with the patience of erosion, categorizing you as they do all things: obstacle or channel, flow or blockage, part of the system or passing through. Then they'll turn away, returning to their work, their presence already fading into the endless conversation of water and stone, leaving you wondering if you saw anything at all, or if it was just another trick of the mist-light on wet walls, another shadow cast by the clouds that drag their bellies across this high, patient place.
