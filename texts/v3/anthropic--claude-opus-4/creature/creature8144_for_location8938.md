Those who endure in Lower Siltmere are neither purely human nor wholly other—they exist in states of ongoing transformation, their bodies and minds adapting to the city's relentless flux.

**The Shift Workers** comprise the bulk of the population, their name carrying double meaning. They work in shifts, yes, but more importantly, they *shift*—their bodies responding to the city's tidal rhythms. During high water hours, their skin develops a temporary translucent quality, allowing them to sense pressure changes through their epidermis. Their fingers web slightly between knuckles, their lungs develop auxiliary chambers that can process both air and the mineral-rich mist. These changes reverse during dry periods, leaving them exhausted and disoriented, caught between forms. They've learned to time their lives around these transformations, sleeping during the worst of the transitions, working when their bodies best match their tasks.

**The Pump Priests** maintain the great drainage systems with religious devotion, believing the pumps don't merely move water but circulate the city's consciousness. They've developed elongated torsos to better reach into the machinery, their ribs flexible as cartilage. Their ears have evolved complex internal structures that let them diagnose pump problems by sound alone—they can hear a failing gasket from three levels away, detect microfractures in pipes by the whistle of escaping steam. They speak in hydraulic metaphors and measure time in pump cycles. The eldest among them claim to understand the messages in the harmonic resonances, though their translations never quite make sense to unchanged ears.

**The Sinkhole Shepherds** read the earth's intentions, predicting where the ground will next give way. Their feet have become hypersensitive to vibration—they walk barefoot on the wet stone, feeling for the hollow spaces below, the subtle shifts that presage collapse. Their hair grows in patterns that mirror the underground water flow, creating natural hygrographic maps across their scalps. They carry long poles that they use to test the ground, but also to communicate with each other through a complex tapping language that echoes through the caverns. They've learned to guide the sinkholes, encouraging them to open in convenient locations, turning disaster into infrastructure.

**The Foundry Children** are those born in the depths, never knowing natural sunlight. Their eyes have enlarged, pupils dilating to capture the faintest glow of furnace-light. They play in the industrial waterfalls, their skin developing a protective film that repels both water and industrial oils. They keep the pipe-fish as pets, training them to carry messages through the plumbing. These children instinctively understand the city's rhythms—they know which pipes are safe to drink from at which hours, which walkways will hold their weight on which days. They age differently here, reaching physical maturity quickly but retaining a strange agelessness in their features, as if the city preserves them in exchange for their acceptance of its ways.

**The Depth Merchants** descend lower than others dare, into the flooded sub-basements and natural caves that connect to unknown aquifers. They've developed nictitating membranes that let them see in the murky water, and their blood has adapted to carry more oxygen, allowing for extended dives. They surface with strange goods—crystals that form only under specific pressure conditions, metals with properties that defy conventional chemistry, and sometimes, objects that seem manufactured but match no known design. Their bodies show the cost of these expeditions: skin with a permanent bluish tinge, joints that predict weather changes with painful accuracy, and a tendency to speak in incomplete sentences, as if they've forgotten how to finish thoughts in the dry air.

**The Resonance Listeners** cluster around the points where the city's harmonics are strongest. Their skulls have gradually reshaped to better capture and process these sounds—subtle elongations that make their profiles seem somehow aquatic. They claim the resonances contain instructions, blueprints for devices not yet invented, formulae for processes that require materials not yet discovered. They work in the factories not for wages but for access to specific acoustic environments. Some have learned to produce countertones with modified vocal cords, creating interference patterns that they insist are conversations with whatever dwells in the deepest waters. Their children are born humming.

**The Modular Families** have abandoned the concept of permanent homes, instead living in structures that can be rapidly disassembled and reconfigured. Their social units have become equally flexible—partnerships form and reform based on complementary skills needed for survival in shifting territories. They've developed a complex system of temporary contracts, both legal and biological. Some members can consciously control their fertility, timing reproduction to coincide with stable ground periods. Their children learn to disassemble furniture before they learn to read, understanding that attachment to place is a luxury Lower Siltmere doesn't allow.

**The Symbiont Workers** have gone furthest in their integration with the city's systems. They've allowed the phosphorescent algae to colonize their skin, providing light in the darker reaches. Some have developed gill slits that filter industrial runoff, extracting nutrients from the chemical soup. Others have formed partnerships with the modified termites, their sweat now containing pheromones that direct the insects' building activities. They no longer fully distinguish between their bodies and their tools, between their thoughts and the city's needs. They work with disturbing efficiency, as if guided by an intelligence that encompasses more than their individual minds.

All of these inhabitants share certain traits: a acceptance of perpetual dampness, lungs adapted to process air thick with steam and spores, and a relationship with time that flows like the water around them—sometimes rushing, sometimes stagnant, always cyclical. They've learned that survival here requires more than adaptation; it requires a willingness to become part of the city's vast, breathing mechanism.

The industrial products of Lower Siltmere are shipped to distant markets, but those who understand suspect these goods are merely byproducts. The real work happens in the transformation of the workers themselves, each inhabitant a test subject in an experiment whose parameters remain unclear. The city processes its people as thoroughly as its raw materials, refining them into something new, something suited for a purpose that perhaps only the deep waters comprehend.

They continue their lives in this dripping, shifting place, their bodies marking the passage of time not in years but in adaptations, their children born already different, already beginning their own transformations. They are no longer merely human, but they are not yet whatever they are becoming. They exist in the space between states, like the city itself—neither fully terrestrial nor truly aquatic, neither entirely industrial nor completely organic.

In Lower Siltmere, everyone is always becoming something else.
