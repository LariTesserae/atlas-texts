The termites themselves are translucent, their bodies like rice paper soaked in moonwater. Through their pale segments, you can see the fungus they've eaten glowing softly in their crops, making each insect a walking lantern. They are blind—have been blind for a thousand generations—navigating instead by touch and scent through corridors their ancestors never carved. Their antennae are impossibly long, twice their body length, and they use them like whiskers, like walking sticks, like the sensitive fingers of a sculptor reading clay.

They work in shifts that follow no clock. A column of workers will suddenly freeze mid-march, standing perfectly still for hours while another caste emerges from the sleeping chambers—soldiers with heads like drops of amber resin, their mandibles curved into question marks. These soldiers touch each frozen worker with their antennae, as if taking inventory, as if reading stories written in pheromones, before retreating again to their posts at doorways that guard nothing.

The queen lives in the heart of it all, in a chamber that was once something else—you can tell by the frescoes on the walls, painted in pigments made from crushed beetles and flower pollen, showing scenes too small and too strange to interpret. She is vast by termite standards, her abdomen swollen to the size of a grain of wheat, pulsing with peristaltic waves as she produces egg after egg after egg. But she is old, older than any queen should be. Her attendants feed her the glowing fungus, yes, but also drops of water from one particular reservoir, one particular stone cup carved with spirals, and this water tastes of minerals that exist nowhere else in the tree.

Around her, workers have built their own additions to the ancient architecture—clumsy pellets of chewed wood and saliva mortared over the perfect joints of the original construction. These additions are always hexagonal, always at precise angles, as if the termites are following blueprints encoded in their genes, blueprints that match the geometry of the older city so perfectly it's impossible to say where instinct ends and imitation begins.

But it's the larvae that disturb the most. They're kept in the deepest chambers, tended by workers so specialized they've lost the ability to feed themselves, their mouthparts evolved into tools for grooming and nothing else. The larvae glow brighter than their parents, bright enough to read by if you were small enough to need reading. And sometimes, just sometimes, a larva will pupate into something that isn't quite a termite—something with one too many segments, or antennae that branch like ferns, or eyes like seeds of glass. These aberrations are carried away immediately, taken to a chamber no human has ever seen, a chamber whose entrance is painted with warnings in a language of shape and color that makes even the soldiers pause.

The fungus gardens themselves are a wonder. What the termites cultivate isn't quite fungus—not anymore. It grows in perfect mathematical spirals, its hyphae forming patterns that repeat at every scale. Look closer and you'll see the fruiting bodies shaped like tiny hands, like stars, like symbols from no known alphabet. The termites harvest it with ritual precision, always leaving certain clusters untouched, always replanting in patterns that have meaning beyond mere agriculture. Sometimes the fungus glows brighter in response to their touch. Sometimes it sings—a vibration too low for human ears but strong enough to make the dewdrops dance.

And there are rooms the termites won't enter. Galleries where the murals move when no one's watching. Chambers where the air is a different temperature, where water flows upward, where the moss grows in perfect circles. The termites build walls across these doorways, walls that their soldiers guard with particular vigilance, walls that sometimes bulge outward as if something inside were trying to grow.

The colony has no memory and perfect memory. Each termite lives for a season at most, but the colony has been here since before the tree was ancient, maintaining the city they inherited, following the patterns they found, adding their own small madnesses to the design. They are caretakers and prisoners, inheritors and invaders, and they go about their work with the patience of the immortal and the urgency of the dying.

Watch them long enough and you'll notice it: they're building something. Not just maintaining, not just existing, but actively constructing. The new passages they dig follow the old geometry but extend it, like a song being hummed that suddenly finds its next verse. They're expanding the city downward, into the earth, toward something. Their excavations bring up stones that shine with their own light, fragments of metal that hum when touched, tiny bones arranged in patterns that make the soldiers stand guard until workers can wall them away again.

The salamander watches all of this with eyes like black pearls. It knows something the termites don't, or perhaps it knows nothing at all. Its heartbeat is so slow you could count the months between pulses. Sometimes termites walk across its back, and it doesn't move, doesn't blink, only breathes its cold breath that mists in the eternal chill of this place that is neither quite dead nor quite alive, neither quite natural nor quite made, neither quite empty nor quite full of something waiting, patient as geology, to complete its ancient purpose.
