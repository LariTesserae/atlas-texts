The Keepers know water's weight by how their shoulders slope, by the particular ache that settles into the third vertebra after carrying collection buckets up spiral stairs for forty years. They are a recognizable breed in any crowd: permanently damp at the cuffs and collar, hair gone prematurely white from the constant moisture, fingers stained blue-black from handling wet volcanic stone.

Most are born here, in the windowless warrens tucked between boiler rooms where the walls sweat warm condensation year-round. The midwife Ora keeps a special cistern just for birthing—its water tastes of iron and moss, and she swears children born in it never leave the Quarter. She might be right. The young ones grow up playing hide-and-seek in the pipe forests, learning to read the sound of water like other children learn their letters. By five, they can tell a healthy pump from a dying one by its rhythm. By ten, they're scrambling up condensation towers to clear clogged collectors, their small fingers perfect for the work.

The Keepers dress in layers of waxed canvas and oiled wool, everything treated to shed water. Their boots are tall, rubber-soled things that squelch with every step. Around their necks hang the tools of their trade: wooden listening rods for detecting leaks, copper tubes for tasting water quality, levels and wrenches sized for every valve in the Quarter. The veterans wear their tools like jewelry, each piece polished by use, each carrying stories—this wrench turned the valve during the Great Drought of '89, this rod found the leak that nearly drained Reservoir Seven.

They speak in water-words, a dialect thick with technical terms and onomatopoeia. A leak isn't just a leak—it might be a whistler, a weeper, a gusher, or a seeper, each requiring different approaches. They measure time not in hours but in fill-rates and cycle-counts. Their curses invoke dry pipes and burst seals.

The work shapes them. Their hands grow webbed with scars from sharp volcanic stone. Their hearing adjusts to the constant water-noise until silence makes them anxious. Many develop what they call "the dropper's ear"—an ability to isolate individual drips in the cacophony, to track a single water-thread through the maze of pipes by sound alone. Their sense of smell sharpens too; they can detect mineral changes in the mist, predict weather shifts by the scent of the condensation.

The families inhabit converted pump houses and maintenance chambers, spaces never designed for living but claimed through squatter's rights and stubbornness. The Vasquez clan controls the main eastern collector array from a home built into Pump Station Three—their sitting room vibrates with the thrum of machinery, their kitchen window opens directly onto a primary collection tank. Children sleep in hammocks strung between warm pipes. Furniture legs stand in dishes of water to keep the beetles from climbing up.

They eat what thrives in perpetual damp: mushrooms cultivated in darker corners, watercress from the runoff channels, newt eggs gathered in season and preserved in brine. The tea shops tucked between towers serve a local brew made from cloud-pine needles and condensation, supposed to prevent the lung-rot that claims those who breathe too much mist. Every family keeps a few tunnel-weaver spiders as pets—partly for their beauty, partly because their webs make excellent wound dressings when boiled.

The old Keepers retire reluctantly, usually only when their joints seize too badly to turn valves. They sit in the tea shops, listening to the water music, offering advice no one asked for about pressure readings and flow rates. When they die, their families pour a measure of collected water into the volcanic stone—a return of what was gathered, a completion of the cycle.

The young ones who try to leave find the dry world harsh and strange. They can't sleep without the sound of dripping water. The sun feels too bright, the air too thin and empty. Most return within a year, taking up their inherited positions at pump stations and collection points, sliding back into the humid embrace of the Quarter like newts returning to their pools.

They mark their calendar by maintenance cycles and weather patterns. The Festival of First Steam celebrates the Quarter's founding. During the Dry Week—a period of ritual fasting from bathing—they demonstrate their water-wealth by letting the ornamental fountains run wild. Courtships happen on the catwalks between collectors, proposals offered with gifts of rare rain—water collected from the brief storms, bottled in crystal, worth more than gold in the Quarter.

The Keepers dream of drowning and wake grateful. They read omens in the patterns of rust on pipes, in the way newts cluster, in the color of morning mist. They are priests of a practical religion, their cathedral a maze of iron and steam, their prayer the endless turning of valves, the constant vigilance against the drought that waits beyond their artificial wetland.

This is who they are: the Keepers of the Cistern Quarter, more water than blood in their veins, guardians of the city's most precious resource, living proof that humans, like beetles and newts, can adapt to any niche—even one they built themselves.
