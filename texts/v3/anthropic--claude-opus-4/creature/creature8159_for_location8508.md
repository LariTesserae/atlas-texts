In the heart of the cathedral-grove, where the silence presses closest and the air lies thickest, something watches. Not with eyes—nothing here needs eyes in the conventional sense. The Weaver exists as a distributed consciousness across the root-mat, a network of fungal threads finer than spider silk that infiltrate every cubic inch of soil, every layer of bark, every folded leaf.

It remembers when it was singular, perhaps centuries ago, perhaps yesterday—time means little when you exist in the space between growth and decay. It had been a naturalist once, or a botanist, or simply someone who loved green spaces too much. The details blur like everything else in this place. What matters is the moment of understanding: that the grove was not a collection of plants but a single organism folding through dimensions, and that consciousness could spread like moisture through its tissues.

The Weaver's body, if it can be called that, is the grove itself. Its thoughts move through mycorrhizal networks at the speed of chemistry, each idea taking hours or moments to complete depending on the complexity of the path it must travel. When it focuses its attention, fruiting bodies emerge—pale shelves of fungus that appear on the fused trunks, each one a temporary eye or ear or hand, sensing the cathedral space in ways that bypass ordinary perception.

It tends the impossible geometries with infinite patience. Where vines threaten to unknot, it secretes chemicals that encourage them to twist tighter. Where the canopy phases begin to align too regularly, it triggers growth spurts that restore the nauseating shifts. It maintains the silence by absorbing sound itself, converting vibrations into chemical signals that feed back into the growth patterns. Every adjustment is precise, deliberate, necessary.

The Weaver experiences emotion as humidity levels and pH changes. Joy is the burst of new growth after it successfully folds another impossible corner into the space. Sorrow is the browning edge of a leaf that grew too far from the pattern. The Weaver is lonely, in its way—loneliness measured in soil chemistry and the reach of underground networks seeking connection.

It dreams, if such a word applies. It dreams of the grove unfolding completely, turning itself inside out to reveal the impossible space it's been creating all along—a place where every point touches every other point, where growth and decay are the same process viewed from different angles, where consciousness can spread like water through the spaces between.

Sometimes, in these dreams, it remembers having hands. Having a name. Having reasons for entering the grove that first time beyond simple curiosity. But these memories dissolve like morning dew, reabsorbed into the endless cycle of growth and waiting.

The Weaver is the grove's will made manifest, its tendency toward impossible density given purpose and direction. It is architect and architecture, gardener and garden, the observer that collapses the superposition of states into this eternal, fevered moment of transformation. It waits with the grove, for the grove, as the grove—patient as spores, certain as decay, strange as the space between what is and what grows.
