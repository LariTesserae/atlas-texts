The Tuners arrived during the third age of abandonment, when the industrial works had already begun their transformation into subsidiary instruments. They came not as colonizers but as devotees, drawn by recordings of the dawn phase-lock that had somehow reached the quiet worlds beyond the acoustic horizon.

They are a dispersed order, never more than a few dozen across the entire continental shelf system. Each Tuner maintains a territory of perhaps fifty square kilometers, though "territory" implies a possession they would find absurd. They see themselves as attendants to a vast, living score that plays itself.

Their bodies have been radically modified through generations of careful selection and surgical adaptation. The first thing you notice is their skin—thick, almost chitinous in places, with a matte surface that absorbs sound rather than reflecting it. This epidermal dampening allows them to move through killing acoustic zones that would liquify unprotected organs. Beneath this protective layer, their skeleton has been hollowed and reinforced, bones restructured into resonating chambers that can be consciously tuned by muscular tension. They breathe in careful patterns, using their modified ribcages as selective filters to extract oxygen from the pressure waves themselves.

But it's their sensory adaptations that truly set them apart. The Tuners have no external ears—the organs would be worse than useless here. Instead, their skulls are riddled with tiny passages, each precisely sized to resonate with specific frequencies. They "hear" through their entire skeleton, reading the landscape's acoustic signature through vibrations in their bones. Their feet are broad and sensitive, toes splayed wide to read minute variations in the stone's trembling. Some have had their larynxes replaced with mechanical oscillators, allowing them to produce pure tones that can locally cancel or reinforce the landscape's voice.

They live in the Carves—natural formations where centuries of acoustic erosion have hollowed out spaces within the stone shelves. The Tuners modify these spaces minimally, adding only what they call "baffling meditation": carefully placed objects that alter the cavity's resonance to create pockets of specific tonal environments. A carved chamber might be tuned to amplify certain frequencies associated with deep contemplation, or to create beating patterns that induce trance states.

Their daily work is subtle and patient. A Tuner might spend weeks observing how a new rockfall has altered the local acoustic environment, then months carefully placing stones to restore or enhance the resonance patterns. They maintain the abandoned industrial structures not out of nostalgia but because these artifacts have become integral to the landscape's voice. A Tuner will travel for days to oil a squeaking turbine bearing, not to restore its function but to preserve its particular contribution to the local chord.

They communicate through placed stones and adjusted surfaces. A precisely positioned piece of metal might alter a valley's acoustic signature in a way that other Tuners will recognize as a message—warnings about unstable formations, notation of particularly beautiful harmonic convergences, or simply expressions of presence and attention. This stone-speech can persist for decades, a slow conversation conducted across geological time.

Their tools are minimal but precise: tuning forks made from salvaged industrial alloys, ceramic resonators shaped to produce specific interference patterns, and collections of stones sorted by density and acoustic properties. Each Tuner develops a personal collection of "voice stones"—pieces that ring with particularly useful tones when placed correctly.

During the dawn phase-locks, all Tuners cease their work and adopt specific positions across the landscape. They stand at calculated points where their bone-resonance can minutely influence the grand alignment, each body becoming a small component in the continental instrument. Some say they're trying to perfect the phase-lock, to achieve some ultimate harmonic convergence. Others believe they're simply bearing witness, their modifications allowing them to experience the pure tone in ways no recording could capture.

The arthropods accept them, perhaps recognizing a kindred adaptation to the acoustic environment. Tuners often wake to find the bone-plated creatures clustered around them in the quiet zones, drawn by the subsonic pulses of their modified hearts. Some Tuners learn to modulate their heartbeats to communicate basic concepts to these deaf but vibration-sensitive beings.

As they age, Tuners undergo a final transformation they call the Quieting. Their bones gradually thicken and lose their resonance, their skin hardens further, and they begin to seek ever-deeper positions within the Shelves. Eventually they descend into natural chambers where the sound approaches the theoretical limits of material endurance, spaces no unmodified human could survive. There they continue their work in the deep frequencies, felt rather than heard, becoming part of the geological process that shapes the Resonance Shelves.

New initiates sometimes find these deep Tuners, recognizable only by the careful arrangements of stones around their calcified forms. Still alive, still conscious, but operating on timescales that approach the mineral. They've become living fossils, their thoughts oscillating perhaps once per day, their modifications allowing them to persist in a state between life and stone.

The order continues, neither growing nor shrinking, maintained by those who hear the recordings and feel compelled to join the vast, breathing instrument. They come knowing they will never leave, that the landscape will eventually claim them as it has claimed every civilization that tried to master it. But mastery was never their goal. They come to tune and be tuned, to lose themselves in the planet-song that never ends.
