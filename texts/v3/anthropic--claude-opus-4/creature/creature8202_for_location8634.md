The Keepers emerge from their dwellings at the edges of perception—a flutter of damp wool, a face half-glimpsed between curtains of hanging moss. They are small people, made smaller by their tendency to fold into themselves, to move with the compressed efficiency of those who have learned every shortcut through their vertical maze. Their skin carries the pallor of filtered light, not unhealthy but translucent, as if they too are slowly becoming permeable to match their saturated world.

They dress in layers of felted wool, harvested from creatures that no longer exist in pure form but have bred with the local fauna into something unique—sheep-sized beings with moss growing directly from their fleece, grazing on the walls themselves. The Keepers' garments never fully dry, existing in a state of permanent dampness that they've learned to find comforting. Colors are muted: the grays and greens and browns of their environment, dyed with lichens and mineral seepage. Their clothes grow their own ecosystems—tiny gardens of moss colonizing the fibers, which they cultivate rather than clean.

Children learn to read before they learn to walk properly, but their reading is of texture and growth patterns rather than symbols. A hand brushed across a wall tells them the history of water flow, the age of the moss, the health of the stone beneath. Their play involves creating tiny worlds in hollow logs, encouraging new forms of moss to grow in patterns that tell stories. They keep pets—not in cages but as companions that share their living spaces: the iridescent beetles that clean debris from their floors, the salamanders that regulate insect populations, the thumb-sized birds that nest in specially cultivated moss gardens on their walls.

The adults practice vocations that blur the line between cultivation and construction. Growth-architects spend decades training moss to reinforce weakening stone structures, creating living buttresses that strengthen with age. Water-readers study the mineral content of seepage, adjusting the carved channels to distribute different waters to different quarters based on need—one flow for drinking, another for the moss gardens, another for the pools where they cultivate their translucent fish. Stone-whisperers maintain the ancient buildings through imperceptible adjustments, shifting single rocks by millimeters to accommodate centuries of settling.

Their social structure flows like the water through their city—authority accumulates in pools rather than pyramids. Elders are recognized by the gardens that grow on their bodies, the moss that has begun to colonize their hair and beards, worn as symbols of wisdom rather than removed. They make decisions by consensus, gathering in the largest grotto where the acoustics allow every voice to be heard equally. Disputes are resolved through cultivation contests—who can grow the most harmonious garden in a disputed space determines its use.

They mark time through biological cycles. A generation is measured by how long it takes for a particular variety of slow-growing moss to carpet a prepared surface. Their calendar follows the blooming of the tiny flowers that emerge from cracks in the stone, each species marking a different period of their complex year. They celebrate the spawning of the translucent fish, the emergence of certain beetles, the days when specific minerals peak in the water flow.

Their knowledge is vast but specialized to their pocket world. They maintain oral libraries of growth patterns, passed down through teaching songs that mimic the sound of water over stone. They know the medicinal properties of every moss, the nutritional value of every fungus, the behavior patterns of creatures so small that outsiders wouldn't notice their existence. Their medicine works through balance—adjusting the moisture in a room, changing the moss composition of bedding, redirecting water flow to alter the mineral intake of the sick.

At night, they navigate by the phosphorescence they've cultivated, following paths lit by glowing fungi and the trails of luminescent snails. They sleep in carved alcoves padded with living moss that responds to their body heat, adjusting its density for comfort. Their dreams are said to be unusually vivid, influenced by the spores they breathe, the minerals they consume, the constant sound of water that becomes the rhythm of their subconscious.

They rarely leave their hidden city, and when they do, they carry their world with them—water in vessels grown from gourds that maintain the exact mineral balance of home, packets of moss that they tend in whatever temporary shelter they find, small creatures in woven cages that help them feel anchored. The outside world seems harsh and dry to them, lacking the nuance they're accustomed to reading in every surface.

Yet they are not primitive. Their understanding of ecology surpasses any academic knowledge, earned through centuries of intimate observation. They practice a sophisticated form of bioengineering, selectively breeding mosses for specific properties, creating hybrid organisms that serve architectural and agricultural functions. Their art involves temporary arrangements of living materials that grow and change, never fixed, always becoming.

The Keepers embody their environment completely, existing in a state of mutual adaptation with their moss-covered world. They have become what their city needed—patient, observant, flowing rather than forcing, growing rather than building. In the constant dampness and green light of their hidden alcove, they have evolved their own form of civilization, as complex and interdependent as the ecosystem they tend.
