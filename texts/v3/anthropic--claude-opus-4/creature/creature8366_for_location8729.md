The inhabitants divide themselves by shift and species, though the boundaries blur in the permanent twilight.

The human workers come in waves—three shifts that cycle through the terraces like tides. They wear thick rubber boots that splash through the puddled courtyards, their movements efficient, practiced. Most are young, sent here for two-year contracts from the lowland cities. They live in dormitories built into the rock face itself, where the volcanic warmth keeps the bunks dry. Their skin takes on a permanent sheen from the humidity, their hair perpetually damp. They learn to move like the beetles—steady, purposeful paths between the islands of machinery.

Some stay longer. These become the shift supervisors, the ones who know which pump's irregular rhythm means a blockage forming, which patch of rainbow bacteria indicates a chemical leak. Their eyes adapt to the dim orange light until they can read gauges without squinting. They develop a sixth sense for the factory's moods, its tremors and sighs. These veterans rarely speak of the world below the plateau. They've made their peace with the machine-city's metabolism.

Between shifts, in the two-hour periods when skeleton crews maintain basic operations, other inhabitants emerge. The maintenance engineers are a different breed—half mechanic, half biologist. They tend the segmented cleaners in the drainage pools, adjusting their feed, monitoring their health. These pale, eyeless creatures—somewhere between worm and crustacean—were designed in labs but have evolved in the wild spaces of the factory floors. Each generation shows subtle changes: better heat tolerance, more efficient toxin processing. The engineers document these adaptations with the patience of monks.

The rat colonies have developed their own hierarchy. The original stock was genetically modified for heat resistance, but they've become something more. They've learned the shift schedules, emerging to scavenge during the quiet periods. Some have developed elongated whiskers that sense temperature differentials, helping them navigate the maze of steam pipes. They nest in the spaces between the metal walls, raising hairless young that can tolerate temperatures that would kill their ancestors. The workers tolerate them—they keep the moth populations in check.

The moth clouds themselves are perhaps the most successful inhabitants. They've evolved a lifecycle perfectly timed to the factory's rhythms. Their caterpillars feed on the bacterial films, pupating in the warm crevices of the machinery. The adults emerge during shift changes, when the movement of workers stirs the air and brings fresh nutrients. Some species have developed wings that shed scales in patterns matching the rust stains on the metal walls—camouflage born from industrial selection.

In the deepest pools, where the water is warmest and most mineral-rich, live things that might have once been salamanders. Now they're something else—elongated, translucent, their gills adapted to filter both oxygen and edible particles from the industrial soup. The workers occasionally glimpse them during maintenance, pale shapes disappearing into underwater pipes. There are stories of much larger specimens in the deepest reservoirs, but these remain unconfirmed.

The true permanents are the Custodians—a role rather than a people. They maintain the city's memory, keeping records of production, of species counts, of the slow changes that accumulate despite the apparent stasis. Some are former workers who couldn't leave. Others arrived specifically for this purpose, drawn to the idea of a place suspended in time. They live in the highest terrace, where the air is coolest and the view encompasses the entire industrial ecosystem. They document the beetles' migration patterns, the subtle shifts in bacterial coloration, the gradual erosion of the basalt despite its youth.

Children are rare but not unknown—usually belonging to couples who met during their contracts and decided to stay. These few grow up amphibious, equally at home splashing through the courtyards or climbing the catwalks. They keep beetles as pets, know which moths are edible (most aren't), can predict rain by the change in the steam vents' pitch. They attend small schools run by off-shift workers, learning their letters alongside valve maintenance and water chemistry. Most leave when they come of age, but some return, bringing new genetic material to the established populations—human and otherwise.

At the boundaries where the terraces drop away, solitary figures sometimes stand during the quiet hours. These are the ones considering departure, weighing the familiar rhythm of the machine-city against the unknown world below. Some jump—not to die, but to climb down, to leave. Others turn back to the warm glow of the furnaces, to the beetle-tracked stones and moth-haunted air they know. The city accepts both choices with the same mechanical indifference, continuing its steady breath, steam rising into the perpetual twilight where day and night have lost all meaning.
