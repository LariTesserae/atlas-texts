The inhabitants of the Breathing Plateau exist in states of perpetual becoming, their forms as mutable as the city-forest they call home.

**The Cultivators** move through the vertical maze with a patience that borders on geological. Their bodies have stretched and thinned over generations, joints hyperextended to accommodate the constant climbing. Bark-like calluses pattern their skin in whorls and ridges that match the trees they tend, though whether this is adaptation or adornment remains unclear. They wear living garments—moss cloaks that feed on their perspiration, vine belts that tighten and loosen with the humidity, lichen patches that serve as both decoration and emergency food source.

They speak in chemicals rather than words, communicating through scent-marks left on the bark-walls: warnings of unstable passages, notifications of fruiting chambers, love letters that bloom into flowers when the intended recipient passes by. Their children are raised communally in nursery-groves, spending their first years more plant than human, rooted in place by umbilical vines that feed them the city's nutrition directly. Only when they prove able to resist the urge to permanent rootedness are they severed and taught to walk.

**The Architects** have gone further in their symbiosis. They no longer build—they dream, and the city-forest responds. These beings have allowed fungal networks to colonize their nervous systems, creating hybrid consciousnesses that think in mycelial time. They sit for days in meditation chambers deep within the mother trees, their thoughts branching through the wood-wide web, suggesting new configurations that the city-forest may or may not adopt.

Their bodies fruit with ideas—small growths that emerge from their skin like shelf fungi, containing blueprints for new districts encoded in spore-patterns. Other inhabitants harvest these architectural proposals, planting them in promising locations to see what structures might emerge. The most successful Architects eventually merge entirely with their chambers, becoming node-beings in the city's neural network, their human consciousness dispersed but somehow preserved in the endless conversation between roots.

**The Harvesters** maintain the closest thing to traditional human form, though they've developed specialized sensory organs—fingertips that can taste the readiness of fruit through bark, eyes that see in the fungal spectrum, ears attuned to the ultrasonic cries of plants under stress. They navigate the ever-changing passages by memory-scent, following pheromone trails they've laid down over years of practice.

They know which wall-fruits are meant for eating and which for building, can identify the medicinal saps by the sound of their flow, understand the seasonal cycles of architectural decay and renewal. Their knowledge passes between generations not through teaching but through ingestion—elder Harvesters' memories preserved in special gourds that young apprentices consume, gaining decades of experience in a single, hallucinogenic meal.

**The Symbionts** have abandoned human form almost entirely. They exist as colonial organisms, their original bodies serving as mobile hosts for gardens of specialized plants and fungi. Orchids grow from their shoulders, providing extra sensory organs. Vines wind through their hair and beard, extending their reach and perception. Their skin hosts complex ecosystems of beneficial microorganisms that process the thick air into more refined nutrients.

They serve as mobile pollinators and seed-dispersers, their movements essential to the city-forest's reproduction. In return, they're fed directly by their symbiotic partners, no longer needing to consume in any traditional sense. Some have rooted themselves semi-permanently in favorable locations, becoming meeting-places where others gather to exchange information through shared spores and crosswind conversations.

**The Dreaming Children** are those who never fully separated from the nursery-groves. They exist in twilight states between sleep and waking, their consciousness merged with the city-forest's slow thoughts. They prophesy through growth patterns, reading the future in the arrangement of leaves and the direction of new branches. Their bodies remain child-sized but ancient, preserved by the same flows that keep the mother trees alive for millennia.

Others come to them for guidance, though their pronouncements come in the form of seeds that must be planted and tended, sometimes for years, before their meaning unfolds. They feed on dreams—their own and others'—processing the collective unconscious of the Breathing Plateau into new forms of growth.

**The Witnesses** are the newest adaptation, emerging only in the last few generations. They've developed transparent skin through which their chlorophyll-tinted blood is visible, allowing them to photosynthesize directly. They position themselves at the highest points of the city-forest, above even the canopy, serving as living sensors for the organism below. Their role is to experience and remember the hard light that the city-forest cannot bear to face directly, processing it into gentler forms of information that filter down through the layers.

All of these forms are fluid, unfixed. A Harvester might take root and become an Architect, a Cultivator might accept enough symbiotic partners to transform into a Symbiont. The city-forest doesn't distinguish between its human and non-human inhabitants—all are part of its body, all contribute to its endless becoming. Even death here is merely another form of transformation, bodies gratefully absorbed by the root networks, consciousness preserved in the chemical memories of the soil.

They measure time not in days or years but in growth cycles, in the rise and fall of architectural generations, in the slow breath of the mother trees. Their art is indistinguishable from their agriculture, their religion from their urban planning. They worship through cultivation, pray through pruning, celebrate through the encouragement of beneficial growth.

When outsiders occasionally find their way to the Breathing Plateau, the inhabitants observe them with the patient curiosity of gardeners studying a new species. Some visitors flee immediately, unable to tolerate the claustrophobic intimacy of a city that touches everything, knows everything, incorporates everything. Others find themselves seduced by the promise of belonging, of becoming part of something larger than individual existence. These few who stay begin their transformation the moment they take their first breath of the spore-thick air, the city-forest already beginning to dream them into new shapes.

The inhabitants continue their work—if it can be called work, this collaboration with growth itself. They tend and are tended, shape and are shaped, in an endless reciprocity that makes questions of individual agency irrelevant. They are the city-forest thinking about itself, mobile nodes in a vast biological computer calculating the optimal configurations of existence. They are gardeners and gardens both, citizens of a metropolis that builds itself from their dreams and their decomposition alike, forever becoming what they always were: part of the breathing, growing, endless whole.
