She came here first as a child, thin-limbed and fever-bright, following the scent of water during a drought year when the public fountains ran dry. Now, forty years later, she still folds herself through the gap between the stones each dawn, her joints protesting what her muscle memory accepts as necessary.

The botanist—though she would laugh at the title, having no formal education beyond what the plants themselves have taught—maintains her laboratory in the maintenance alcove. Her tools are few: glass jars salvaged from the city above, a knife worn thin by sharpening, bundles of cloth for straining and filtering. She knows each fern by its particular curl, has named the distinct colonies of moss that advance and retreat across the walls with the seasons.

She harvests carefully, sustainably. The watercress from the grating for her morning meal. Specific mushrooms at specific stages of growth, dried and ground into powders she trades to the herbalists in the market—though they don't know where she sources such potent specimens. The moss, when prepared correctly, stanches bleeding. The fern spores, mixed with spring water and left to ferment in the darkness, create a tincture that eases the chest-cough that plagues the canal workers.

Her hair has gone white as the rootlets that penetrate the ceiling, and like them, she seems to draw sustenance from the very air. She speaks rarely when above ground, but here she maintains long conversations with the chamber—commenting on new growth, scolding the mushrooms for their enthusiasm, greeting the water as it finds new paths through the stone.

She has mapped the chamber's moods through decades. Knows that the east wall weeps more heavily before storms, that the mushrooms in the northwest corner fruit in response to the moon's cycle, that the particular fern near the entrance only releases spores when the temperature above ground drops below freezing. Her knowledge is written nowhere but in her movements through the space—the automatic duck beneath the hanging root, the precise step over the spot where water pools, the gentle touch that tests the moss's moisture without disturbing its growth.

Sometimes she brings others—a child with wounds that won't heal, an old woman whose lungs rattle with each breath. They descend into the green darkness and she administers her treatments, applying poultices of moss and fungus, having them breathe the spore-thick air, drink water filtered through specific combinations of plants. The city's physicians would scoff, but her patients climb back through the gap renewed.

She sleeps above ground, in a rented corner of a shared room, but she *lives* here in the dripping sanctuary. Her few possessions migrate gradually underground—a wooden cup worn smooth by use, a wool blanket that smells perpetually of earth, books whose pages have swollen with humidity but whose plant diagrams she traces by candlelight.

Recently, she's noticed changes. New species appearing—seeds carried in by the cats or birds, or perhaps dormant in the soil for centuries, waiting for the right conditions. She documents these arrivals in her way, learning their properties through careful observation and cautious experimentation. The chamber is teaching her still, revealing new secrets as her own body grows closer to the earth it will eventually feed.

She has trained no apprentice, trusts no one with the location. But sometimes she sees a sharp-eyed child watching her in the market, notes which youth linger near the old walls, observes who stops to listen when water whispers through the stones. When the time comes—and she'll know, just as the mushrooms know when to fruit—she'll take a small hand and guide it through the gap, introducing a new guardian to the breathing sanctuary beneath the city's weight.

For now, she tends and harvests, heals and observes, a human element in the chamber's ecosystem as essential as the water's patient work—part gardener, part guardian, part growth herself, rooted in the space between civilization and wildness.
