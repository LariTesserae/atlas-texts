In this fevered cradle between states, the Cultivator tends to their garden of impossibilities.

They move through the steam like a figure painted in watercolors—their form running and reforming with each step, edges bleeding into the thick air before reconstituting. Their body appears to be woven from the same principles that govern this place: partially solid, partially suggestion, always in conversation with the heat shimmer that makes everything here uncertain.

The Cultivator's skin, if it can be called that, resembles the surface of the pools—iridescent, catching light that shouldn't exist, reflecting spectrums that hurt to name. Along their limbs run channels like irrigation ditches, carrying fluids that glow with the same phosphorescence as the lichen. These channels pulse in rhythm with the pools' mathematical bubbling—three seconds dormant, seven seconds flowing—as if their circulatory system has synchronized with the caldera's heartbeat.

Their face shifts between expressions that might be features or might be the play of shadow and condensation across a surface too alien for human pattern recognition to parse. Sometimes, when they lean over a pool to harvest the bacterial mats, observers might glimpse what could be eyes—or perhaps apertures that serve a similar function—dilating and contracting to perceive wavelengths that correspond to temperature differentials and magnetic fluctuations rather than visible light.

The Cultivator wears—or perhaps extrudes—garments that seem cultivated rather than crafted. Fungal weaves drape across their form, growing and dying in accelerated cycles, fed by their body heat and the mineral-rich perspiration that beads constantly on every surface here. These living clothes shift color and texture in response to their tasks: hardening into protective shells when they work near the hottest vents, becoming gossamer and permeable when they wade into the pools to tend the things that grow there.

Their work follows patterns older than the pylons but somehow incorporated into their rhythm. They move between the crystalline structures with the confidence of someone who has learned to read their hum like sheet music, adjusting their position to create harmonics that encourage certain growths and discourage others. With tools that appear to be extensions of their own biology—fingers that elongate into pruning shears, palms that secrete cultivation mediums—they coax the aggressive vegetation into forms that serve purposes unclear to any observer but themselves.

The Cultivator speaks, though not in sounds. Their communication manifests as temperature fluctuations in the air, as changes in the mineral content of nearby pools, as alterations in the bioluminescent patterns of the succulents. The heat-shimmer sprites respond to these environmental utterances, clustering around certain pylons or avoiding others according to instructions encoded in thermal gradients.

They maintain the observation post not out of nostalgia but as part of their garden. Inside, they've encouraged the bio-ceramic walls to continue their dissolution, guiding the process so that the structure transforms rather than simply collapses. The surfaces that still respond to proximity now display patterns of their own design—growth charts for organisms that don't fit into any taxonomic system, calculations that track the interplay between the pylons' emanations and the rate of volcanic glass transformation, poetry written in pH fluctuations.

When the avian form phases overhead, the Cultivator extends something that might be an arm skyward, releasing spores or signals that briefly make the creature fully visible—revealing it to be not one organism but a colonial intelligence, a flying garden of interdependent systems that the Cultivator has been training to harvest energy from the spatial distortions around the pylons.

Their age remains impossible to determine. They could have arrived yesterday or could have been here since the first water touched the first fire. Their body shows signs of adaptation that suggest deep time—structures that mirror the volcanic glass, internal chemistry that has found equilibrium with the impossible physics of this place. Yet they also display the vigor of something newly sprouted, constantly growing new appendages or organs in response to the caldera's shifting needs.

The Cultivator rests, when they rest, by partially dissolving into the larger pools, their consciousness diffusing through the bacterial mats and mineral matrices. In these moments, the distinction between gardener and garden blurs entirely. The pools pulse with intent, the vegetation grows in patterns too deliberate for chaos, and the pylons' hum modulates in ways that suggest response rather than mere mechanical function.

They are cultivating something here, in this space between states. Not just the obvious organisms that carpet the black glass and fill the pools, but the very conditions that make such organisms possible. Each day they refine the balance, adjust the flows, encourage new impossibilities to take root in the superheated soil of this volcanic eden.

The Cultivator is patient. They understand that what grows here cannot be rushed, cannot be forced. It must emerge from the intersection of extremes, from the negotiations between incompatible states of being. They tend their garden with the dedication of someone who knows they are growing not just new forms of life, but new forms of existence itself—organisms and systems that will eventually teach the universe new ways to be.

In the amber light filtered through perpetual steam, the Cultivator continues their work, a figure both alien and perfectly adapted, as essential to this impossible ecosystem as the heat that drives it and the water that shapes it. They are the caldera's first child and its patient parent, forever cycling between states like everything else in this place where the rules themselves remain fluid, waiting to crystallize into new forms of truth.
