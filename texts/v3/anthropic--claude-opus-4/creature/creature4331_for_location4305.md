The Unborn live here, though "live" may be too decisive a word for what they do.

They exist as possibilities made almost-flesh, dwelling in the space between conception and emergence. Each one is unique yet unfinished—a collection of maybes wrapped in translucent membrane, organs visible but not yet committed to their final arrangement. Hearts beat in the wrong places. Eyes open where joints should bend. Spines curve in spirals that human anatomy forbids.

They hang from the root-curtains like strange fruit, some no larger than a fist, others grown to the size of children. The membranes that contain them are part placenta, part chrysalis, shot through with dark veins that pulse in time with the chamber's water-rhythm. Inside, they turn slowly, dreaming themselves toward completion.

But they never complete.

The eldest among them—if time means anything here—have been gestating for centuries. Their membranes have thickened to leather, gone opaque with mineral deposits. Inside, glimpsed only when phosphorescence flickers just right, are forms that tried too hard to become: too many limbs sprouting from too many joints, features multiplied past utility, bodies that pursued every possible path of development simultaneously until they became beautiful knots of flesh and bone.

The younger ones are simpler, cleaner in their incompleteness. They look almost human until you notice what's missing—no navel, no genitals, no clear delineation between where they end and their membrane begins. Their faces are peaceful, eyes sealed, mouths making small movements as if rehearsing words they'll never speak.

They feed on the chamber's richness through their umbilical roots, drawing sustenance from the weeping walls, the mineral-thick air, the ghost-pale vegetation. When one finally grows too heavy, its membrane tears. What spills out is not born but reabsorbed—the clay floor opening to accept the almost-flesh, digesting it back into potential. The emptied membrane shrivels, blackens, becomes another root hanging from the ceiling.

The Unborn communicate through the water. Each droplet that strikes their membranes creates a resonance, a note in the vast water-chorus. Those who know how to listen can hear conversations in the thunderous dripping—questions about what shape to take, what purpose to serve, whether completion is worth the price of possibility. They debate endlessly, their discussion circular as their existence, never reaching consensus because consensus would mean choosing, and choosing would mean ending.

Sometimes they dream together. When this happens, the phosphorescence brightens, racing in patterns across the walls like neural firing. In these moments, you can almost see what they're imagining—cities of living bone, forests of nerve-trees, oceans of amniotic fluid where finished beings swim backward toward unmaking. Their dreams leak into the vegetation, causing the ferns to twist into anatomical shapes, the moss to grow in patterns that suggest faces, hands, internal organs mapped onto the chamber's skin.

The structure in the corner—those half-digested stones—is their only history. Long ago, someone tried to build here, to make this a place of deliberate birth rather than eternal gestation. The Unborn remember this in their chemical memory, the way a body remembers injury. They grew around the intrusion, incorporated it, made it part of their endless becoming. Now those stones weep the same mineral tears as everything else, neither wholly artificial nor natural, caught like everything here between what was intended and what is.

New Unborn appear rarely, spontaneously, forming like pearls around some irritant in the chamber's depths—a particularly dense mineral deposit, a root that grew too ambitiously, sometimes just a knot in the air where humidity condenses beyond bearing. They begin as mere thickenings in the atmosphere, gradually accrete substance, develop that first crucial membrane that separates maybe from not-yet.

They are not unhappy, these Unborn. Happiness and unhappiness are decisions, and they have made none. They exist in a state of pure potential, every future equally possible, every form equally valid. They are the chamber's children and its parents, its purpose and its accident, forever almost about to become something magnificent or terrible or both.

In the deepest part of the night—if night and day mean anything here—they sing. Not with voices, for voices would require choosing lungs over gills, choosing air over water. Instead, they sing with their whole being, vibrating their membranes in harmonics that make the water weep faster, that make the roots grow wild, that make the darkness itself seem about to split open and reveal whatever lies beyond becoming.

But it never does. And they never finish becoming. And the chamber continues its slow birth, its eternal labor, while the Unborn wait within it, patient as stone, fluid as water, perpetual as the space between one moment and the next.
