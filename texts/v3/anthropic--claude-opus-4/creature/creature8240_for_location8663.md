Those who live here are the Readers.

They came to this work from mining families, from generations who knew stone's hidden languages—the way quartz sings differently than granite, how coal speaks in whispers of ancient forests. But the Readers have evolved beyond their ancestry. Their bones have grown dense from the constant vibration, their skeletons becoming tuning forks for frequencies human ears cannot detect. When veteran Readers remove their boots, their feet show peculiar calluses—thick ridges that match the resonance patterns of emerging stone.

They live in shifts of seventeen days, a number that corresponds to some deeper rhythm in the mountain's growth. Their quarters are cramped modules bolted to the scaffolding, swaying with each tectonic adjustment. Inside, the walls are covered with charts—not of the mountain's topology, but of its temporality. They map time the way others map space: here, a vein of stone that will emerge in three years; there, a formation still deciding whether to become.

The Readers develop particular sensitivities. Their hands grow steady in ways that have nothing to do with muscle control—they learn to move in harmony with vibrations, finding the null spaces between wave patterns where precision work becomes possible. They read the gauge instruments, yes, but more importantly they read the stone itself through their feet, their teeth, the fluid in their inner ears. New Readers often vomit for their first weeks, their bodies rebelling against the constant motion. Those who stay learn to surf the mountain's frequencies like wave riders.

Their sign language has evolved far beyond standard industrial signals. They have signs for concepts like "stone-soon" (rock that will emerge within the hour) and "depth-warm" (areas where the mountain's heart comes close to surface). Gestures can indicate not just direction but temporal direction—pointing not where something is, but where it will be. The most experienced Readers barely need to sign at all; they move in unconscious synchronization, a choreography dictated by vibrations only they can parse.

They eat carefully. The constant shaking makes digestion difficult; their meals are dense pastes and gels that won't separate in their stomachs. They've learned that certain minerals help—iron supplements to match the mountain's own ferrous veins, calcium to maintain their resonant bones. They drink the condensation when they can, claiming it carries information about the stone's intentions. Whether this is superstition or wisdom, none can say.

The Readers age strangely. The constant vibration preserves them in some ways—their muscles never fully relax, maintaining a peculiar vitality. But it wears them in others. Their hair grays not with time but with frequency exposure, turning white in patterns that match the standing waves of their work zones. Their eyes develop a particular thousand-meter stare, focused on distances that exist in time rather than space.

Some Readers claim to dream the mountain's dreams. In their cramped bunks, shaking with the peak's emergence, they speak of visions: vast perspectives spanning millions of years, the slow dance of continents, the patient gathering of sediments that will become tomorrow's peaks. They wake knowing things they shouldn't—where to place the next turbine to catch a stone surge that won't happen for months, which rock face will calve before showing any visible signs.

Between them move the Tenders—a subset of Readers who maintain the mysterious machinery. The Tenders have their own adaptations: their fingers grow extra joints, or perhaps the joints simply become more flexible, allowing them to work in the narrow spaces between grinding gears. They oil the turbines with substances that aren't quite lubricants—thick, opalescent fluids that seem to respond to pressure in non-Newtonian ways. Some Tenders develop what they call "machine sight," the ability to diagnose mechanical problems through vibration alone.

The creatures that flow like mercury between the structures—the Readers call them "chronovores," time-eaters. They've learned these beings feed on discontinuity, thriving where human time meets mountain time. The Readers neither hunt nor fear them; they've achieved a kind of symbiosis. The chronovores clean up temporal paradoxes, smoothing the places where industrial acceleration threatens to tear the mountain's natural emergence. In return, the Readers leave offerings: small pools of the strange lubricants, bits of metal that have absorbed particular frequencies, fragments of their own shed hair and nails that carry human time-signatures.

Children are born here, occasionally. The mountain's vibrations usually prevent pregnancy, but sometimes—always during the calmest periods, when the stone briefly rests—conception occurs. These children grow up bilingual in speech and sign, their first toys made from resonant metals that teach them the frequencies. They play games that would terrify valley-dwellers: leaping between scaffolds in perfect timing with the mountain's pulse, placing their hands on raw stone to guess what will emerge. By adolescence, they can read the gauges better than engineers trained in universities below.

The eldest Readers, those who have spent decades in the emergence zone, undergo a final transformation. Their bones begin to mineralize, not with disease but with sympathy. They move more slowly but with perfect precision, never fighting the vibrations but flowing with them. Some say that the eldest eventually merge with the mountain itself—that scattered through the emerging stone are the remnants of Readers who chose to match their time to geological time, their consciousness spreading through crystal matrices and pressure systems.

They maintain libraries of a sort—not books, which would shake apart, but metal plates etched with data, stored in racks that ring like bells when the mountain shifts. Each plate records not just gauge readings but interpretations: what the Readers felt in their bones that day, what the stone was trying to become, how the machinery responded. This archive of emergence grows daily, a human record of a more-than-human process.

The Readers have their own cosmology, their own understanding of what they're doing here. They don't see themselves as mining or midwifing so much as translating—converting the mountain's slow speech into something humanity can use, though for what purpose remains unclear even to them. They speak of the emergence as a gift, or perhaps a teaching. The mountain is showing them something about time, about patience, about the deep rhythms that underlie all faster movements.

They are not heroes or villains, the Readers. They are people adapted to an impossible place, their bodies and minds shaped by frequencies that shouldn't exist at this intersection of geological and industrial time. They read the mountain's becoming and tend the machines that somehow make that reading possible. In their vibrating world between stone and steel, they've become something new—not quite human anymore, but not yet mineral. They exist in the space between, translating eternities into shift reports, parsing the mountain's vast utterance one gauge reading at a time.
