The people of Kharan-Vel move like water itself—flowing around each other in the narrow passages, their bodies adapted to the constant navigation of tight spaces. They are a small people, rarely growing tall, with narrow shoulders and a peculiar grace that allows them to slip through gaps that would stop outsiders. Their hands are their most distinctive feature: fingers elongated from generations of feeling for holds in the wet rock, palms hardened and slightly cupped from a lifetime of catching and redirecting water.

They dress in layers of netted fabric woven from the fiber of climbing vines—garments that dry quickly in the constant mist, that can be easily stripped off or added as they move between the warm thermal chambers and the cooler heights. The netting serves a dual purpose: modesty and utility, as tools, food, and water vessels can be hung from the body, leaving hands free for climbing. They wear no shoes—their feet are broad and grip like a gecko's, the soles thick with callus but sensitive enough to read the stone's texture in darkness.

The Singers—for that is what they call themselves—have evolved a complex social structure based entirely on acoustic territory. Each family group maintains a specific resonance chamber, and their standing in the community is determined by the beauty and complexity of the sounds their chamber contributes to the overall symphony. The most prestigious families control chambers near the gorge's heart, where the acoustics are purest. Lesser families make do with peripheral caves where the sound is muddled or harsh.

Children are tested early for their ability to hear and shape the water-music. Those with the gift become Tuners—a hereditary but not bloodline class who spend their lives making minute adjustments to the carved channels, adding a groove here, smoothing a surface there, constantly refining the city's vast instrument. The Tuners move through the city like ghosts, accessing passages too small for others, their bodies scarred from decades of squeezing through limestone tubes. They communicate through a language of taps and scrapes that blend seamlessly into the background music, inaudible to any but another Tuner.

Those who cannot hear the subtleties—about one in ten—become the Gardeners. They tend the vertical farms with a devotion that borders on religious, each plant positioned not just for sustenance but for its contribution to the overall composition. A passion fruit vine's leaves rustling in the updraft, bamboo clicking against stone, the splash of irrigation—all must be precisely placed. Gardeners develop an uncanny ability to predict growth patterns, pruning months in advance to ensure the music remains constant.

The third class, the Memory Keepers, are perhaps the strangest. In a city without written language—ink would run, carved words would alter the acoustics—all knowledge is held in human minds. But the constant sound makes normal speech difficult to distinguish, so the Memory Keepers have developed a form of communication that uses the water itself. They teach by directing streams onto different surfaces, creating temporary patterns of sound that carry meaning. An apprentice might spend years learning to distinguish the difference between water falling on wet limestone versus dry, smooth versus rough, thick streams versus thin.

Marriage in Kharan-Vel is not between individuals but between chambers. When two young people wish to unite, they must prove they can blend their family chambers' sounds harmoniously. This often requires months or years of careful acoustic adjustment, supervised by Tuners and blessed by Memory Keepers. The wedding ceremony itself is performed in silence—the only silence the city knows—when for exactly one hour, all water is diverted away from the gorge through ancient emergency channels. In that terrifying quiet, the couple speaks vows that only they will ever hear. Then the water returns, and if their chambers now sing in harmony, the marriage is sealed.

The Singers age differently than outsiders. The constant vibration affects their bones, making them denser but more brittle. Elderly Singers often cannot leave the gorge—the silence would stop their hearts, they say, though whether this is physical truth or cultural belief remains unknown. They grow smaller with age, as if the city slowly claims them, until the very elderly can access passages that have been sealed since childhood. It's said that the oldest Singers eventually find their way to chambers no living person has seen, deeper in the rock where the water's song is pure and unconscious of human intervention.

Death, when it comes, is a careful process. Bodies cannot be buried—there is no soil—or burned—smoke would damage the acoustic properties. Instead, the dead are taken to the Dissolution Chambers, natural formations where particularly aggressive water has carved smooth basins. Bodies are laid in these basins and allowed to slowly dissolve, their minerals joining the limestone, their matter becoming part of the eternal song. Families often claim they can still hear their ancestors' voices in the water that flows past their chambers.

Children grow up fearless of heights and darkness but terrified of silence. They play games that would horrify outsiders—racing through passages barely wider than their skulls, leaping between wet ledges over drops that vanish into mist. They learn to echolocate, not like bats but like dolphins, using clicks and whistles that cut through the water-sound. Their toys are water—cups and channels and tiny wheels that create new rhythms. They sing, but not as outsiders understand it. Their singing is a modulation of breathing that works with the background symphony rather than against it, creating interference patterns that carry meaning.

The Singers eat what they grow, supplemented by trade with the plateau peoples who bring dried goods in exchange for the city's only export: perfectly tuned stone instruments. These small ocarinas and water flutes are carved from the gorge's limestone and retain something of its acoustic properties. Musicians across the known world treasure them, though they say the instruments grow silent after a few years away from their birthplace, as if homesick.

There are no leaders in Kharan-Vel, no central authority. Decisions arise like the music itself—from the interaction of countless small choices. When the city needs to act as one, the Tuners adjust the water flow to create a new harmony, and the inhabitants instinctively understand what must be done. This wordless democracy baffles outsiders but works flawlessly for the Singers, who no more need debate than drops of water need to vote on which way to flow.

Some among them are born different—the Listeners. These rare individuals can hear patterns in the water-song that escape even the Tuners. They speak of messages from the mountain's heart, of prophecies spelled out in decades-long rhythms. The other Singers treat them with a mixture of reverence and unease. Listeners tend to live apart, in the highest chambers where the sound is thinnest, spending their lives transcribing the unhearable patterns into dance, into garden arrangements, into the raising of children who might, perhaps, hear even deeper.

Romance among the Singers is a delicate art of acoustic courtship. Lovers learn to modulate their breathing, their heartbeats, even their footfalls to create private harmonies only their intended can detect. They leave gifts of shaped water—ice sculptures that melt into particular notes, channels carved to sing only when water flows at the rate of tears. Sexual desire is expressed through rhythm, with the most passionate lovers creating polyrhythmic dialogues that echo through their chambers for hours.

The few Singers who leave—driven by silence-madness or the rare wanderlust—rarely return. The plateau peoples speak of finding them years later, standing at the edges of waterfalls or sitting by rushing streams, trying desperately to recreate something they can no longer fully hear. Some become famous musicians or water engineers, but they always seem hollow, as if part of them remains forever in the gorge.

In the depths of night, when the thermal currents shift and the water flows strongest, the entire city dreams as one. They say the water carries dream-fragments between chambers, that sleeping minds merge into a collective unconscious as deep and strange as the underground river that feeds them. In these shared dreams, they are not human but water itself, seeping through stone, carrying minerals and memories, forever falling, forever singing, forever home.

The Singers have no word for music. How could they, when they have no word for silence? They have a hundred terms for different qualities of water-sound but no general term for the phenomenon itself. To them, the rest of the world lives in music, while they alone dwell in reality—the constant, comforting, all-encompassing truth of water and stone locked in eternal conversation, with humanity as merely the most recent note in a song that began before life and will continue long after.
