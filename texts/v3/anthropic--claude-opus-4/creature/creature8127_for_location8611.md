In the Drowned Plateaus dwell the Conductors—beings that exist as much as process as they do as creature. Each Conductor appears as a towering lattice of calcified tubing, like coral that learned geometry from abandoned petroleum refineries. They stand forty feet tall when fully extended, though they often compress themselves into dense knots to ride the thermal columns between valleys.

Their bodies are cities. Through translucent chambers in their structure, thousands of smaller organisms go about the business of living—some permanent residents, others temporary workers that enter through intake valves and leave through regulated exits. The Conductors don't control these inhabitants directly; instead, they create the conditions that make certain behaviors inevitable. By adjusting the flow of nutrients through their bodies, altering the temperature of specific chambers, or changing the resonance of their structural harmonics, they orchestrate the activities within themselves like composers directing a living symphony.

The Conductors feed on inefficiency. They position themselves where the industrial ecosystem stutters—where the great filter-feeders' waste clouds accumulate, where the metal web-builders' networks create dead zones, where the synchronized species fall out of rhythm. Through patient adjustment of their internal processes, they create solutions: a new chemical pathway that breaks down accumulated toxins, a resonance that helps two species find their harmony again, a current that redirects nutrients to where they're needed.

They communicate through infrastructure. When Conductors need to coordinate across distances, they extrude temporary pipelines—hollow tubes of secreted carbon that can stretch for miles, connecting one Conductor to another. Through these networks, they pump messages in the form of chemical cocktails, temperature variations, and pressure waves. A conversation between Conductors can take months, as proposals and counterproposals flow back and forth through the pipes. Sometimes these communication networks become permanent, and smaller creatures colonize them, turning what was once a temporary phone line into a permanent highway ecosystem.

Their reproduction is industrial. When conditions are right—usually after they've successfully optimized a particularly troubled area—Conductors begin the years-long process of budding. They grow a secondary framework within themselves, a smaller lattice that gradually learns to conduct its own internal orchestra. During this time, the parent becomes even more efficient, as if preparing to demonstrate perfection to its offspring. When the young Conductor finally separates, it takes with it a seed population of the parent's internal ecosystem, and often maintains a physical pipeline connection to its parent for the first decades of independent life.

The eldest Conductors have grown into the landscape itself. In the deepest valleys, structures that were once mobile beings have become permanent installations, their bodies so vast and complex that they've merged with the ruined factories and railway systems. These ancients no longer move but instead have become the architects of entire regions, their influence spreading through root-like pipeline networks that can encompass hundreds of square miles. They've learned to conduct not just the organisms within their bodies but the very currents and chemistry of the water around them.

Conductors see time differently. To them, the daily migrations of the eel-like turbine creatures are like heartbeats. The seasonal blooms of the inverted trees are breaths. The generational cycles of the filter-feeders are thoughts completing themselves. They experience the Drowned Plateaus as music—vast, slow compositions where every organism is an instrument, every chemical reaction a note, every current a melody line that must be carefully integrated into the whole.

They have natural enemies, though "enemy" implies a malice that doesn't exist in their interactions. The Dismantlers arrive periodically—sleek beings like mechanical squids crossed with wrecking balls, who target Conductors that have grown too rigid, too perfect in their solutions. The Dismantlers tear through the careful networks, introducing chaos that forces adaptation. The Conductors don't flee from Dismantlers; they accept them as part of the larger music, discord that makes the eventual resolution more meaningful.

In their presence, the industrial roar of the Drowned Plateaus takes on new meaning. What seems like mechanical noise reveals itself as dialogue, negotiation, collaboration across species boundaries. The Conductors are translators, turning the needs of one form of life into languages others can understand. They are living proof that in this drowned world, the distinction between organism and ecosystem, between individual and infrastructure, has dissolved as completely as the air that once filled these highland valleys.

When they die—a process that can take centuries—their latticed bodies don't decay so much as transition. The pipelines remain, the chambers continue to host life, the chemical processes carry on. Gradually, what was once a Conductor becomes indistinguishable from the landscape itself, another piece of the vast machine. Their consciousness doesn't end but rather disperses, becoming part of the ambient intelligence that governs the sustainable chaos of the Drowned Plateaus.

In this way, the Conductors embody the fundamental truth of their environment: in a world where the sea hangs wrong and pressure creates possibility, the highest form of life is not one that merely survives or even thrives, but one that helps everything else do the same.
