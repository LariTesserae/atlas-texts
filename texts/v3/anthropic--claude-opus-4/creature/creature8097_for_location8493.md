The salt dwellers move with a patience that borders on mineral stillness. When they gather in the banded halls during the afternoon rains, you might mistake them for deposits left by ancient floods—until one shifts slightly, rearranging limbs that segment like crystal fractures.

The eldest among them have lived here so long that their bodies have become libraries of the plateau's history. Layers of different salts have built up across their backs in stratified bands: white halite from drought years, pink potassium deposits from seasons of strange storms, blue-grey sheets from times when the mountain glaciers far to the north sent mineral-rich meltwaters across the plateau. They wear their centuries like tree rings, growing broader and heavier with each passing decade.

These ancients move perhaps once or twice in a day, usually to shift into better position to catch the morning condensation. Their undersides are etched with intricate grooves—a branching network that channels moisture directly to specialized pores where they can absorb both water and dissolved minerals. The younger ones cluster around them during the rains, learning the subtle art of positioning: how to read the weather in the crystal formations, where the best flows will develop, which galleries will flood and which will merely dampen.

The scale-backed browsers are more active, though active is a relative term in the salt city. They possess a peculiar method of locomotion—rather than walking, they secrete a thin film of specialized saliva that briefly dissolves the crystal surface beneath them, allowing them to glide slowly along their chosen paths. Behind them, the dissolved minerals recrystallize within hours, often in slightly different configurations. Over generations, the browsers have carved smooth highways through the city, routes that spiral up the hollow towers and bridge between formations.

Their feeding is a careful art. They cannot simply graze at will—too much salt too quickly would desiccate them from within. Instead, they cultivate. In pockets where their saliva has pooled, specialized algae bloom, fed by the minerals and the browsers' own waste. These gardens appear as patches of deep green-black against the crystalline white, clinging to the salt substrate. The browsers return to them in cycles, harvesting just enough to sustain themselves while leaving enough to regenerate.

The translucent hunters are perhaps the strangest inhabitants. Their bodies contain specialized organs filled with saturated brine, denser than their surrounding tissue. By shifting these internal reservoirs, they can change their center of gravity, allowing them to flow up vertical crystal faces and even hang inverted from salt stalactites. They hunt by persistence rather than speed—once they identify prey, they simply follow, using their ability to navigate three-dimensional space to gradually corner their target.

But hunting and fleeing are slow affairs here. A chase might last days, predator and prey moving through the galleries in a careful dance, each trying to outmaneuver the other through passages too narrow for quick movement. The hunters' patience is legendary—they can maintain pursuit for weeks if necessary, sustained by the biofilms they absorb through their permeable skin as they move.

In the deepest chambers, where the bioluminescent bacteria paint everything in soft blue-green, live creatures that have given up on the sun entirely. They are pale as the salt itself, with enormous eyes adapted to the bacterial glow. These deep dwellers feed on the bacteria directly, grazing along the walls like terrestrial whales filtering plankton. Their movements disturb the bacterial colonies, causing waves of brighter luminescence to ripple across the chamber walls—a light show that serves as both feeding sign and communication.

Some have developed symbiotic relationships with the bacteria, cultivating colonies within specialized pouches in their skin. These living lanterns move through the darkest passages, their bodies glowing with patterns that shift according to their emotional state—bright pulses for alarm, steady glows for contentment, intricate spirals during their slow mating dances.

The reproductive cycles of the salt city's inhabitants are tied to the rains. When the seasonal storms arrive and the drainage channels fill with flowing brine, creatures throughout the city begin their migrations to the convergence points. The cathedral spaces become nurseries, with different species claiming different levels along the walls.

The browsers cement their eggs to the ceiling with a special secretion that hardens into salt crystal, positioning them where the condensation will keep them moist but the floods won't reach. The ancients release clouds of crystalline spores that drift through the flowing air, settling in cracks and crevices where new individuals will slowly take shape. The hunters birth live young in the shallow pools that form on ledges, the salinity helping the translucent newborns maintain their osmotic balance as they develop.

There are creatures here that blur the line between animal and mineral formation. Colony organisms that appear to be elaborate crystal gardens until feeding time, when thousands of tiny polyps emerge from what seemed to be solid salt. Mobile formations that might be creatures carrying mineral shells or minerals animated by some strange life force—the distinction becomes meaningless when observed over sufficient time.

The smallest inhabitants are perhaps the most crucial. Microscopic organisms live within the crystal matrix itself, feeding on trace impurities and facilitating the endless dissolution and recrystallization that keeps the city growing. They are the architects working at the smallest scale, their metabolic processes determining how crystals form, which minerals precipitate where, whether a formation will be solid or hollow.

On quiet nights, when the moon is full and the salt city glows like bone in the pale light, a careful observer might notice the faintest movement everywhere—the entire city breathing, expanding and contracting with the temperature changes, its inhabitants moving in rhythms so slow they border on geological. This is their pace, their way—life moving at the speed of stone, stone moving at the speed of life, until the boundaries dissolve like salt in the morning rain.
