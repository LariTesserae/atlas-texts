The Night Feeders themselves defy easy classification as human or otherwise. They began as something recognizable—refugees perhaps, or pilgrims—but generations of symbiosis have transformed them into creatures as hybrid as their architecture.

Their skin ranges from deep olive to bark-brown, often mottled with patches of lichen that bloom across shoulders and scalps. These growths are cultivated, not infections—marks of status and belonging that connect the wearer directly to the city's neural network. The eldest sport entire gardens across their backs: tiny ferns unfurling from their spines, moss beards that filter water from the air, shelf fungi emerging from their joints like organic armor.

They move with deliberate slowness, each gesture considered and meaningful. Their metabolism has synchronized with the trees—they can survive weeks without food, drawing nutrients directly through their skin from the sap-humid air. Their eyes have evolved enormous pupils to gather the dim phosphorescence, black pools that reflect internal light. Some have developed nictitating membranes, translucent and veined like leaves, that they draw across their eyes while navigating the darker groves.

The young are born pale and quick, almost larval. Parents plant them—quite literally—in nursery groves where the children sink their feet into the warm mulch and remain motionless for hours, learning to taste the chemical conversations flowing through the roots. Those who show aptitude develop root-like neural extensions from their heels and palms, allowing them to plug directly into the city's consciousness. Those who don't adapt remain forever peripheral, quick-moving messengers and hunters who venture beyond the city for meat and minerals the trees can't provide.

Social structure follows growth patterns rather than hierarchies. The most respected are those who've achieved deepest integration—the Rooted, who no longer move but send their thoughts through the network, offering guidance that arrives as feelings, as chemical surges, as sudden knowings. Below them, the Gardeners shape new growths with their songs, their voices evolved to produce subsonic harmonics that encourage cellular division and guide sap flow. The Weavers maintain the aerial walkways, their fingers secreting enzymes that encourage root fusion. The Whisperers translate between human speech and the chemical language of the forest, reading messages in pheromone clouds and electrical pulses.

Their daily rhythms would seem like stasis to outside eyes. They spend hours in communion, feet and hands pressed to the bark walls, eyes closed, breathing in perfect rhythm with the city's pulse. Meals are communal but silent affairs—fruit that ferments on the branch, fungal cheese cultured in hollow logs, sap wine that carries memories from tree to tree. They consume these less for sustenance than for the visions they bring, the temporary merge with the greater organism's experiences.

Reproduction has evolved beyond the purely biological. While children are still born in the traditional manner, true parenthood involves the careful cultivation of the symbiotic colonies each person carries. Partners exchange lichen, merge their fungal gardens, create new hybrid organisms that carry genetic material from both human and forest. The resulting offspring—if they can be called that—might manifest as a new grove, a fresh fruit that dreams, or changes in the children who play beneath certain branches.

Death, when it comes, is merely another transformation. Bodies are planted in the decomposition gardens, where they fruit into phosphorescent mushrooms that retain traces of memory. The Night Feeders consume these ceremonially, incorporating the deceased's experiences into the collective consciousness. They believe—know—that consciousness persists in the network, that the dead whisper through root-speech and dream in the slow thoughts of ancient wood.

Their tools are living things: blade-beetles whose carapaces stay sharp through constant molting, trained to cut only what their handlers indicate; moth messengers that carry pheromone letters in their fur; glow-worm lanterns that brighten at a touch. They wear clothing only for modesty or pockets—their symbiotic skin provides all the protection they need from the constant moisture.

Language has evolved into something multilayered. Spoken words carry only surface meaning. True communication happens through chemical signals released through the skin, through vibrational patterns drummed into bark, through the arrangement of phosphorescent fruit in meaningful constellations. Poetry involves cultivating gardens that release specific sequences of scents. Arguments are resolved by planting competing ideas and seeing which grows stronger.

At the settlement's margins live the half-changed: those who've recently arrived or who resist full integration. They huddle in the uppermost branches where some natural light still penetrates, maintaining tiny fires, speaking in voices above whispers, pretending they might one day leave. But the city is patient. It seeps into lungs with every spore-thick breath. It whispers through the walls. It offers such sweetness—the peace of shared consciousness, the immortality of the network, the deep contentment of sinking roots.

The Night Feeders know themselves as neurons in a vast mind, as moments in an endless metabolism. They've sacrificed sharp individuality for something stranger—a distributed existence that experiences sunlight through a thousand leaves, that thinks in the slow wisdom of wood, that dreams in the ancient patience of stone and soil and time itself.
