The Brass Terraces host three distinct forms of life, each adapted to different thermal and temporal niches within the caldera's industrial ecosystem.

**The Thermovores** occupy the deepest levels, where the heat approaches 200 degrees. These creatures resemble oversized tardigrades crossed with sea urchins, their bodies covered in crystalline spines that function as both heat sinks and mineral extractors. They grow slowly—a single individual might take decades to reach its full size of a dinner plate. Their metabolism runs on temperature differentials rather than chemical energy; they position themselves at the junction between hot and merely scalding surfaces, harvesting energy from the thermal gradient itself.

The Thermovores move in generational migrations, following the slow decay of the thermal extraction systems. When a cooling pipe finally fails after centuries of service, they converge on it like vultures to a carcass, absorbing the last burst of released heat. Their young bud from crystalline nodes on their backs, emerging already adapted to the specific mineral composition and temperature range of their birth terrace. In the constant twilight of the lower levels, they navigate by detecting infrared patterns invisible to human eyes, seeing the industrial landscape as a tapestry of heat signatures painted in a thousand shades of red.

**The Brass Monks** inhabit the middle terraces, where the machinery grows thickest. These are not monks in any religious sense—the name comes from their hunched, robed appearance and their endless, ritualistic maintenance of the machines. They were human once, generations ago, but the constant exposure to metallic dust and thermal radiation has changed them. Their skin has taken on a coppery sheen, their lungs adapted to filter metal particles from the air. They've grown smaller with each generation, better able to navigate the narrow passages between machinery.

The Monks live according to the rhythm of the machines they tend. They sleep in four-hour shifts, synchronized with maintenance cycles programmed centuries ago. Their language has evolved to include the mechanical sounds of the terraces—clicks, hisses, and harmonic tones that carry better than words in the heat-deadened air. They understand the machinery not through manuals or training, but through an inherited, almost genetic knowledge passed down through generations of exposure. A Monk child learns to read the health of a thermal extractor by its vibration before they learn to speak.

They wear garments woven from metal fiber—cloth would combust at these temperatures. The robes serve as heat shields and status symbols both, their patterns indicating which section of terraces a Monk tends, how many generations their family has served, and what specific mechanical mysteries they've mastered. The eldest among them claim to hear the Voice of the Everforge—a sub-harmonic resonance created by all the machinery operating in concert, too low for most to perceive, that some say contains messages, or prophecies, or simply the dreams of a vast mechanical mind.

**The Crawlers** represent the newest adaptation to life in the Brass Terraces. These beings emerged from the intersection of biology and abandoned technology. No one knows if they began as experimental maintenance robots that developed organic components, or organisms that integrated mechanical parts, but now they exist as something entirely their own.

A Crawler resembles a centipede made of copper tubing and ceramic segments, anywhere from a foot to twenty feet long. Their bodies incorporate salvaged components from the maintenance systems—sensor arrays become eyes, defunct cooling systems repurposed as digestive chambers, worn gear assemblies transformed into mandibles capable of grinding through metal and stone alike. They reproduce by gathering suitable mechanical components and somehow—through a process that defies easy classification as either biological or mechanical—assembling new offspring that combine traits of multiple "parents."

The Crawlers possess a distributed intelligence. A single Crawler shows little more awareness than an insect, but when they gather in groups, they demonstrate problem-solving abilities and even creativity. They've been observed creating new tools from scavenged parts, building structures that serve no apparent purpose except perhaps aesthetic expression, and even modifying the ancient machinery in ways that the Brass Monks insist are improvements, though improvements toward what end remains unclear.

All three species exist in a complex web of interdependence. The Thermovores' feeding process creates mineral deposits that the Brass Monks harvest for trade with the world above. The Monks' maintenance prevents catastrophic system failures that would destroy the Crawlers' habitat. The Crawlers, in turn, process metal waste into forms the Thermovores can digest, completing the cycle.

The species rarely interact directly—their different operating temperatures and timescales make communication difficult. But on certain nights, when the thermal currents align just right and the machinery hits particular harmonic frequencies, observers report seeing all three gathered at the spiral's center, arranged in concentric rings around the volcanic throat. What happens at these gatherings—whether communication, worship, or something else entirely—remains one of the Everforge's many mysteries.

In this place where time moves differently for each form of life, where the mechanical and biological have blurred beyond distinction, the question of who truly inhabits the Brass Terraces becomes impossible to answer simply. The Monks claim ownership through centuries of custodianship. The Thermovores predate all industrial development. The Crawlers represent its future evolution. All three are bound to this place as surely as the machinery is fused to the stone, parts of a greater whole that perhaps only the Everforge itself—if it truly lives—fully comprehends.
