The Drifters move across the mercury plains in loose congregations, their forms difficult to parse at first glance. They are colonies rather than individuals—assemblages of translucent membrane stretched between articulated frames of living metal. Each Drifter consists of dozens of these membrane-cells, arranged like the segments of a paper lantern that has learned to walk. The metal framework grows from their bodies like external bones, silver-bright where it touches the mercury, tarnishing to deep blue-black where it meets air.

They communicate through vibration patterns in the mercury itself. When a congregation pauses, they lower their ventral frames until they kiss the liquid metal surface, sending controlled ripples through the engineered medium. The mercury, which swallows all other sound, carries their messages with perfect fidelity—complex harmonics that propagate for kilometers before the field generators smooth them back to mirror stillness. A conversation looks like rainfall on still water, if rain could fall in precise mathematical patterns.

Their membrane-cells serve as both lung and stomach. The perpetual moisture condenses on their surfaces, runs down in engineered channels where their metal frames direct the flow. They feed on the dissolved minerals in this water—trace elements leached from the crystal formations, molecular debris from their own shed frames, the occasional complex organic that forms spontaneously where water, mercury, and electromagnetic fields interact. During feeding, a Drifter will position itself beneath a weeping crystal, its membranes pulsing in slow rhythm as they process the mineral-rich runoff.

The young ones grow their frames from the mercury itself. Juveniles begin as simple geometric shapes—tetrahedrons or cubes of membrane with minimal metal framework. They dip repeatedly into the liquid metal, each immersion adding new articulation points, new structural elements. The mercury bonds to their living metal through processes that blur the line between biology and engineering. An adult Drifter might have hundreds of joints, thousands of metal struts, its membranes divided into increasingly complex cellular structures that can reshape themselves like three-dimensional origami.

They see through the mercury. Their optical organs cluster where multiple membrane-cells meet, resembling compound eyes made of water droplets. But these eyes look down, not up—they navigate by reading the reflections in the mercury plains, seeing the world inverted and silverized. The liquid metal shows them things invisible to other senses: the heat signatures of creatures beneath the glass substrate, the electromagnetic patterns of the buried field generators, the stress-lines forming in the planetary crust. They read the landscape's future in its reflections.

Reproduction happens during moments of system instability. When the field generators fluctuate—during tectonic events or solar storms—the mercury briefly loses its perfect surface tension. In these moments, adult Drifters can extrude portions of their metal framework into the liquid, where it separates and begins forming the seed of a new colony. The parent contributes membrane-cells over the following days, teaching the juvenile the harmonic languages, the feeding grounds, the safe paths across the plains where the glass substrate runs thickest.

They fear the fracture zones. Where fresh cracks spawn in the glass, the field generators sometimes fail to compensate quickly enough. The mercury can drain through these gaps, leaving behind dry glass valleys that take decades to refill. A Drifter caught in a draining zone faces slow death—their metal frames oxidize without the mercury's touch, their membranes desiccate despite the humid air. The oldest congregations know the warning signs: subtle changes in the mercury's resonance, a particular quality of light in the reflections that speaks of instability below.

Their intelligence is collective and strange. A single Drifter possesses perhaps the cognition of an insect, but congregations display complex problem-solving abilities. They build temporary structures from shed framework metal—bridges across fracture zones, resonance amplifiers for long-distance communication, even simple tools for manipulating the crystal formations. These constructions dissolve back into the mercury when no longer needed, leaving no permanent trace of their engineering.

At rest, they cluster around the crystal groves, their forms reflecting and re-reflecting in the mercury until it becomes impossible to tell where creature ends and reflection begins. They sing then—not with sound but with light, their membranes becoming bioluminescent, patterns playing across their surfaces that mirror the geometric perfection of the crystals. The mercury carries these light-songs too, creating vast mandalas of illumination that spread across the plains before fading.

The Drifters know they are not native here. Their genetic memory, encoded in the molecular structure of their living metal, speaks of another place—a world where the mercury ran in rivers, where it was not held in stasis but flowed freely. They were brought here, or their ancestors were, by the same forces that built the field generators. They are maintenance organisms, perhaps, or simply fellow prisoners in this vast experimental apparatus. They continue their cycles of movement and feeding, communication and reproduction, their metal frames slowly incorporating trace elements from the planetary machinery until they become part of it, silver blood in silver veins, keeping something running that none of them fully understand.
