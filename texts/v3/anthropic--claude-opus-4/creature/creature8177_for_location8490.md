In the Singing Hollows dwell the Choir-Keepers, descended from those who first followed the stone-singers into the depths generations ago. They are a people transformed by darkness and sound, their bodies adapted to navigate by echo as much as sight. Their eyes, while not blind, have grown large and silvered, pupils dilated permanently to catch what little bioluminescence bleeds from the herds. But it is their ears that mark them—enlarged and mobile, they can turn them independently like a bat's, catching and filtering the cacophony of echoes that would drive surface-dwellers mad.

The Choir-Keepers wear robes of stone-singer leather, treated with mineral oils until they gleam like wet rock. The garments are sewn with copper thread in patterns that mimic the acoustic geometries of the walls—not for decoration, but for survival. The patterns break up their echo signatures, allowing them to move among the herds without triggering stampedes. Their faces are painted with phosphorescent fungi ground to paste, creating ghostly masks that glow faintly in the darkness, each family wearing distinct patterns that can be read by touch in the blind-dark spaces between galleries.

They do not speak as surface folk do. Instead, they've developed a language of clicks, hums, and throat-songs that weave through the stone-singers' choruses without disrupting them. Children learn to sing before they walk, taught to modulate their voices to ride the acoustic currents rather than fight them. Master singers among them can throw their voices through the carved chambers, holding conversations across vast distances by reading the echo patterns and placing their words in the spaces between reverberations.

The stone-singers themselves are more alien than any surface dweller imagines. Their translucent bodies reveal internal structures that pulse with soft light—not organs as we understand them, but crystalline formations that grow within their flesh. These living stones resonate with their songs, each creature a walking instrument. The eldest of the herds carry forests of crystal within them, their bodies becoming more mineral than meat, until they finally root themselves in the deepest chambers and sing themselves into the stone itself, becoming new formations for water to weep through.

Young stone-singers are almost invisible, their bodies clear as water, only the faintest shimmer of light marking their presence. They learn their songs by eating the minerals their elders have changed through digestion—each deposit carrying acoustic memories, teaching them the paths and the harmonies their herds have followed since before human memory. When threatened, they can shatter their internal crystals, creating a burst of discordant sound that causes painful disorientation in any creature that navigates by echo.

But there are older things in the Hollows than the stone-singers or their human shepherds. In the chambers marked with impossible geometries live the Forgotten Shapers—creatures that may have been the original builders, or perhaps what the builders became. They exist partially outside normal space, their bodies flickering in and out of visibility like shadows cast by no light. Where they pass, the stone remembers differently. Corridors reshape themselves, distances fold, and the careful acoustic mappings of the Choir-Keepers become suddenly wrong.

The Shapers tend gardens of living sound in the deepest reaches, where echoes have bounced so long they've taken on existence independent of their sources. These sound-forms drift through the galleries like jellyfish through water, feeding on new noises, growing more complex with each reverberation they absorb. Sometimes they speak—not in words, but in pure meaning that bypasses the ears entirely, planting thoughts directly in the mind like seeds in dark soil.

The Choir-Keepers know to avoid the Shapers, marking their territories with silence-stones—rare minerals that absorb sound rather than reflect it, creating dead zones that the echo-dependent creatures instinctively avoid. But sometimes young singers, drunk on the harmonies of the deep chambers, wander too far. They return changed, if they return at all. Their songs carry new tones that make the stone-singers nervous, and their eyes see angles that shouldn't exist. They speak of conversations with the builders, of learning the true names of stones, of understanding why the city was carved downward rather than built up.

In the market galleries, where the Choir-Keepers trade with surface merchants brave enough to descend, there are whispers of the Quiet Ones—humans who've lived so long in the depths they've forgotten sound entirely. They move in perfect silence, their bodies somehow sliding between echoes without creating new ones. The stone-singers cannot perceive them, and even the Shapers seem unaware of their presence. They steal children sometimes, teaching them the art of un-being, of existing in the spaces between sounds. These children return years later, aged beyond their time, with knowledge of passages that appear on no map and words in languages that predate human speech.

The copper-workers among the Choir-Keepers shape their metal by sound alone, using tuned hammers and resonant anvils to create tools and ornaments that sing in harmony with the Hollows. These artifacts are prized by surface dwellers for their strange properties—a knife that can cut through echo as well as flesh, mirrors that reflect sound instead of light, bells that ring with the voices of the stone-singers. But the greatest masters forge instruments from the crystals of ancient stone-singers, instruments that can command the herds, open passages long sealed, or even speak with the Shapers in their own impossible tongue.

At the heart of it all, in chambers so vast that entire weather systems form in their heights, live the Echo Prophets. These are Choir-Keepers who've spent so long listening to the reverberations of the past that they've begun to hear the future in them. They speak in riddles composed of sounds that haven't been made yet, warning of cave-ins days before they happen, predicting the movements of herds by reading the echoes of their unborn calves' songs. The other inhabitants fear them, for their prophecies come true in ways that suggest the Hollows themselves are listening, adjusting, making the predictions real through some mechanism beyond understanding.

This is the ecosystem of the Singing Hollows—a vertical city where sound is substance and echo is environment, where creatures of flesh and crystal and folded space share galleries carved by hands that might not have been human at all. The water continues its eternal weeping, carrying minerals and memories downward, while the songs spiral upward, and somewhere in between, life persists in forms that the sun-touched world above could never imagine.
