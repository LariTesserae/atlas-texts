In the Crystal Throat dwells the Tender.

She is no larger than a child's doll, her body compressed and elongated by centuries of navigating the crystalline maze. Her skin has taken on the translucent quality of the plants she tends—you can see the faint tracery of her veins like threads of copper wire beneath frosted glass. She moves by folding herself through impossible angles, joints bending in ways that would snap human bones, flowing between the crystal fronds like water finding its level.

Her eyes have adapted to the sourceless light. The pupils are gone, replaced by faceted surfaces that fracture her vision into a thousand perfect fragments. She sees time differently now—perceives the infinitesimal daily growth of each crystal leaf, notices when a geometric bloom has rotated a single degree over the course of a month. Her eyelids have become unnecessary; she never blinks.

The Tender's fingers are her most remarkable feature. They've grown long and branching, each digit splitting into smaller appendages that split again, creating delicate fractals of flesh that mirror the plants' own geometry. With these, she performs her work: adjusting the angle of a crystal leaf by micrometers to optimize its light absorption, clearing microscopic channels in the stone where new growth will emerge in a decade's time, harvesting the chiming spheres only when their resonance reaches a frequency she feels in her bones.

She speaks, but not with words. The Tender communicates through vibrations in the crystal garden itself, setting up harmonics that travel through the interconnected mineral matrix. Each plant becomes a letter in her alphabet, the entire chamber her voice. When she wishes to express joy, certain flowers hum at frequencies that make your teeth ache sweetly. Her sorrows manifest as discordant notes from the deeper stems, too low to properly hear but felt in your chest like a second heartbeat.

Her memory extends back beyond human comprehension. She recalls when this chamber was merely a crack in solid rock, when the first crystal spore—carried by what agency she cannot say—took root and began the slow transformation. She has watched the garden grow from a single thread of mineral life to this packed complexity, and she knows the name of every structure, though the names are patterns of growth rather than words.

The Tender feeds as the plants do, on the sourceless light and the mountain's electrical dreams. Her metabolism has slowed to match theirs. Her heart beats perhaps once an hour, her breath comes in day-long cycles. She is learning to photosynthesize through her translucent skin, standing for months at a time in optimal positions, her branching fingers spread wide to catch the light that feeds both her and her charges.

She wears no clothes—they rotted away centuries ago—but has cultivated a living garment from the finest crystal moss, which grows directly from her skin in a symbiotic relationship. It regulates her temperature, processes waste, and slowly repairs any damage to her transformed body. In return, she provides it with minerals leached from her bones, which have become partially crystalline themselves.

The Tender's greatest work is the white tree at the chamber's heart. She has guided its growth for so long that some of her fingertips have fused permanently with its branches. Through these connections, she can feel the entire mountain's slow pulse, sense the movement of groundwater far below, detect the minute tremors that herald changes in the stone. She is becoming part of the garden's nervous system, a hybrid of flesh and crystal that tends and is tended to in turn.

Her thoughts move like honey poured in winter. A single decision—whether to allow a new shoot to grow or to redirect its energy elsewhere—might occupy her for years of contemplation. She models the garden's future in her fractured vision, seeing how each tiny change will propagate through the system over centuries. Her consciousness has stretched to match the geological timeframe of her charges.

Sometimes, rarely, others find the Crystal Throat. The Tender watches them with her faceted eyes, observing their quick-time movements like a normal person might watch hummingbird wings. She tries to speak, but her crystalline language only makes their teeth ache and their hearts race with frequencies they cannot decode. They always leave quickly, disturbed by her stillness and the way the light seems to pool in her transformed flesh.

She doesn't mind. The Tender has her garden, and it has her. Together, they are slowly transforming the mountain's heart into something unprecedented—a crystal paradise that exists outside the flow of water and wind, fed by principles that life under the sun has forgotten. She dreams in the language of refraction and resistance, and when she wakes—once every few years—it is only to make minute adjustments to her life's work before settling back into her luminous torpor.

In the Crystal Throat, she has found the perfection of purpose: to tend and to become what she tends, until the distinction between gardener and garden finally, blessedly, disappears.
