The Tenders move through their vertical garden with movements that have become liturgy. They are small—no taller than a child of ten summers—with bodies evolved for this cramped eden. Their limbs are long relative to their compact torsos, with joints that bend in subtle ways that allow them to fold themselves through the dense vegetation without disturbing a single leaf. Their fingers, elongated and spatulate at the tips, can grip both the smooth reed scaffolding and the oil-slick plant stems with equal facility.

Their skin has taken on the quality of their surroundings—not quite scaled, not quite furred, but something between, with a surface that repels the plant oils while absorbing just enough to maintain a healthy sheen. In the phosphorescent light, they appear almost vegetative themselves, their coloration shifting between grey-green and amber-brown depending on the angle of observation.

They have no need for clothing in their controlled climate, but wear harnesses woven from plant fibers—practical garments festooned with tools. Bone scrapers hang from loops, their edges worn smooth from generations of use. Small gourds containing different grades of plant oil swing gently as they move. Bundles of dried reeds for quick repairs. Everything has its place, positioned for silent access.

Their eyes have grown large in the perpetual twilight, but not in the manner of cave creatures. These are eyes adapted for close work in dim conditions—able to distinguish the subtlest variations in plant health, to spot the beginning of a fungal infection or the first signs that a section of scaffolding needs reinforcement. When they blink, it is slowly, deliberately, their translucent inner eyelids cleaning the surface while allowing continuous vision.

They communicate in whispers and gestures, their language evolved for minimum acoustic disturbance. A flutter of fingers might indicate which section needs attention. A soft clicking of the tongue—barely audible—calls attention to a successful propagation. They name each plant cluster, each scaffold section, each cultivation level with sounds that mimic the environment: the rustle of leaves, the drip of collected oil, the settling of reeds.

Their daily work is endless and cyclical. They guide the growth of new shoots with gentle pressure, training them along predetermined paths. They harvest the oil-rich seeds at precisely the right moment, some for the creatures they husband, others to press for the oils that light their fungal lamps. They repair the reed infrastructure constantly, replacing aged sections before they fail, weaving new supports as the living weight above demands.

The creatures are not pets but partners in cultivation. The Tenders know each by the pattern of its shell, by its preferred routes, by the particular plants it favors. They adjust the barriers—small woven gates and guides—to direct creature traffic where it's needed, ensuring even distribution of seed consumption and fertilization. The soft-bodied crawlers are monitored just as carefully, their populations managed through selective placement of their favored food plants.

Memory lives in their bones rather than in written records. Each Tender carries the knowledge passed through touch and demonstration—how to read the oils' sheen for ripeness, how to sense when the air circulation needs adjustment, which reed patterns will hold the most weight. They think in plant-time, planning modifications that won't mature for years, tending inheritance gardens their grandchildren will harvest.

Their social structure mirrors their garden—dense, interdependent, every space utilized. Families cluster in small hollows carved into the chamber walls, spaces just large enough for sleeping. The young learn by following, their small hands guided by patient elders until the movements become instinct. There is no room for conflict in such close quarters; disputes are settled by subtle negotiation, by the shifting of responsibilities, by the quiet rearrangement of working territories.

They dream, when they sleep, of impossible things: of water falling from above, of spaces too vast to fill with growing things, of light that burns instead of glows. But these are nightmares. Their waking world is complete—a perfect circuit of growth and tending, of patient accumulation and careful harvest, of life shaped by hand and time into something that sustains itself through sheer accumulated wisdom.

The eldest among them remember stories of the first plantings, when their ancestors discovered the principles that would allow life without water. How they learned that certain combinations of oils could substitute for moisture, that the right fungal partnerships could break down matter in ways that released sustaining vapors, that creatures and plants and Tenders could form a closed system that needed nothing from the desert above except isolation and time.

They are gardeners of the impossible, their entire species evolved to tend this one miraculous space. In their careful movements, their whispered communications, their endless patient labor, they embody a different kind of humanity—one that has traded the wide world for perfect mastery of a single, precious chamber where life blooms in defiance of every natural law.
