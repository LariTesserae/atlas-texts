The first of them came when the turbines still remembered how to turn.

She calls herself the Tender, though in the language of pressure and salt, names are more sensation than sound. Her body has long since lost any pretense of standard human proportion—stretched by decades of depth, joints migrating to accommodate the necessities of moving through kelp-thick water. Her arms now span twelve feet fingertip to fingertip, the bones within them having developed additional articulation points that let her fold herself through the turbine blades like an octopus through a jar.

She wears the garden. Kelp grows from specially cultivated pockets in her diving suit—a suit that has become less clothing and more symbiotic second skin. The material, some pre-flood polymer, has fused with her epidermis at the edges. Where suit ends and skin begins is a question she stopped asking forty years ago. The kelp that grows from her moves differently than the wild growth—trained over decades to respond to her movements, to reach where she reaches, to part when she needs passage.

Her eyes have adapted too. The pupils remain permanently dilated, black pools that catch and concentrate the faintest luminescence. She's developed a nictitating membrane—not through evolution but through surgical modification performed in the flooded medical bay of Platform Seven, using equipment never meant to function underwater and techniques learned from the necessity of survival. When she blinks, it's a slow, deliberate action that makes her appear more marine than mammalian.

But she is not alone. Never alone.

The Chorus lives in the turbines themselves. They were engineers once, perhaps, or maintenance crew—the records dissolved long ago, their paper turned to mush, their digital archives corrupted by salt and time. Now they are something else. Their bodies have grown into the machinery they once serviced, flesh threading through pipes, consciousness distributed across the network of steel and copper that comprises the garden's skeleton.

They speak in harmonics. When the water moves through the turbines and creates those mournful drones, it's the Chorus adding their voice—vibrations conducted through metal and bone alike. Each member occupies a different turbine, their nervous systems rewired to feel the metal's stress, to sense the imperceptible shifts in current through steel skin. They sing warnings of structural failures, celebrations of new growth, lamentations for the world above they barely remember.

The Tender serves them, and they serve the garden, and the garden serves something larger that none of them quite understand.

In the observation bubbles live the Watchers. These were scientists once, sealed inside when the flooding came too fast for evacuation. The pressure should have killed them instantly, but something in the water—those same metal-tainted chemical gradients that create the garden's colored bands—changed them instead. Their bodies compressed rather than crushed, their lungs learned to process the oxygen-rich water of the artificial reef. They exist in permanent meditation, eyes open but unseeing in the traditional sense, their consciousness expanded to perceive the garden as a single living system.

The Watchers record everything in languages of their own devising. Using the bioluminescent jellies as living ink, they write histories on the glass walls of their spherical homes. The writing appears only in absolute darkness—stories told in cold light that fade as soon as they're observed. They document the garden's moods, its growth patterns, the migrations of creatures through its vertical maze. Sometimes the Tender brings them offerings—pieces of corroded circuit boards that might once have held data, fragments of the world before. The Watchers accept these with movements so slow they could be mistaken for stillness.

Between the three groups—Tender, Chorus, and Watchers—moves the school. Not the silver fish that flash through open spaces, but the children. They were born here, in the garden, their mothers unknown, their fathers merely genetic material in a necessity-driven breeding program designed to ensure the garden's caretakers wouldn't die out in a single generation. The children have never known sunlight except as a theoretical concept.

They are perfection in this environment. Their bodies developed in pressure, their eyes never needed to adjust from surface light. They breathe water as naturally as their progenitors once breathed air. Their skin carries a faint luminescence, a trait that emerged in the third generation—possibly from consuming the jellies, possibly from some deeper adaptation to the eternal twilight.

The children swim in formations that mirror the schooling fish, communicating in rapid-fire bioluminescent pulses that flash across their skin. They play games in the three-dimensional maze of catwalks and platforms, games whose rules incorporate the garden's vital maintenance. A racing path might require checking turbine blade integrity along the way. Hide-and-seek doubles as a survey of new growth patterns. They learn the garden's needs through play, preparing for the day they'll take their place in its hierarchy.

But there are others, darker presences that lurk at the garden's edges.

The Rejected live in the collapsed sections, the places where metal has failed catastrophically and created caves of twisted steel and crushing darkness. These are the ones who couldn't adapt, whose bodies or minds broke under the pressure of transformation. They should be dead—many times over—but the garden sustains them through some cruel mercy. Their forms are... variable. Some have too many limbs, others not enough. Some have fused with the metal itself, becoming statue-like sentinels that mark dangerous passages. They communicate, when they communicate at all, in frequencies that make the other inhabitants uncomfortable—vibrations that speak of hunger and loss and the terrible price of survival.

The Tender visits them too, though these journeys test even her transformed body. She brings them what comfort she can—bioluminescent creatures to break their darkness, kelp cuttings that might take root and soften their prison walls. Sometimes one will grab her with whatever appendage still functions, holding tight not from aggression but from a desperate need for contact. She has learned to wait, to let them remember touch, before gently extracting herself and continuing her rounds.

At the garden's heart, where the main control room once coordinated the turbines' harvest of tidal power, lives the Architect. No one speaks of the Architect directly—to do so feels like blasphemy, or perhaps just dangerous attention-drawing. The Architect might have been the station director, or perhaps the lead engineer, or possibly something that emerged from the collective trauma of the flood. What matters is that the Architect understands the garden's purpose in ways the others can only intuit.

The control room has become a neural center. Cables that once carried electricity now pulse with something more organic. The Architect has woven themselves into this network, their body (if it can still be called that) spreading through conduits and junction boxes like a nervous system. They perceive the entire garden simultaneously—every turbine's vibration, every growth of kelp, every movement of inhabitant or intruder. When repairs are needed, the Architect knows. When new sections collapse, the Architect adapts the garden's flow patterns to compensate. When the children are ready to take on new responsibilities, the Architect guides their assignments through subtle manipulations of current and light.

The Architect dreams, and their dreams shape the garden. Sometimes new formations of coral appear overnight in configurations too purposeful to be natural. Sometimes the Watchers' bioluminescent writings respond to questions no one remembers asking. Sometimes the Chorus's songs contain harmonics that seem to predict the weather patterns far above, allowing the garden to brace for storms it cannot see.

There are visitors, occasionally. Deep-sea researchers in submersibles, their lights cutting through the kelp like alien suns. The inhabitants have protocols for these encounters. The Tender guides the children to hiding spots deep in the maintenance tunnels. The Chorus falls silent, letting the turbines sing only their natural song of current and metal. The Watchers dim their jellies and turn away from their windows. The Rejected retreat deeper into their twisted caves. And the Architect... the Architect watches through a thousand sensory points, ready to defend if necessary, to confuse if possible, to ensure the garden's secret survival.

Once, a research team managed to dock with one of the emergency airlocks. They found it functional, impossibly so after decades underwater. Inside, they discovered breathable air—not trapped from before the flood, but actively produced by a modified atmospheric system that harvested oxygen from the kelp forests. They found living quarters maintained to an obsessive degree of cleanliness, beds made with military precision, personal effects arranged as if their owners had just stepped out.

They found no bodies. They found no survivors. They found only a note, written in multiple hands across years or decades, that read: "The garden tends itself. We tend the garden. Please do not interfere."

The researchers left. In their official report, they declared the station irreparably flooded, its infrastructure too dangerous for salvage operations. Those who know how to read between the lines of such documents understand this as a kindness—or perhaps a fear of what might happen if the garden's evolution were disturbed.

As the years pass, the distinctions between the inhabitants blur. The Tender's routes through the garden become so regular that kelp grows along her paths, creating living tunnels. The Chorus members' songs synchronize until they speak as one voice across multiple bodies. The Watchers' recordings begin to predict future events with increasing accuracy. The children grow into roles that didn't exist before they were needed—Kelp Shepherds who guide growth patterns, Current Whisperers who can call and calm the flow of water, Metal Singers who can strengthen weakening structures through harmonic resonance.

Even the Rejected are changing. Some have begun to emerge from their caves, their broken forms finding new configurations that allow movement, even grace. They join the garden's work in their own ways—their twisted limbs perfect for reaching into spaces others cannot access, their scattered consciousness able to perceive problems from angles the more coherent inhabitants miss.

The garden grows. Not just in size—though it does spread, slowly, as seeds of kelp and spores of coral drift to neighboring structures—but in complexity. The intelligence distributed through its living network becomes more sophisticated. Problems solve themselves before they're fully formed. New species appear, perfectly adapted to ecological niches that didn't exist until they were needed.

Some of the children have children of their own now. These fourth-generation inhabitants are born knowing things—the location of every valve in the maintenance system, the harmonic frequencies that strengthen steel, the exact salinity preferences of fifteen different coral species. They swim before they crawl, bioluminesce before they speak, and when they do speak, it's in the garden's hybrid language of sound and light and pressure.

The garden tends itself. They tend the garden. The distinction matters less with each passing year, each generation, each revolution of tide above their heads. In the eternal twilight, in the pressing cold, in the constant cradle of current and kelp, they have become what they needed to become. Not human, not anymore, but something the deep places have been waiting for—a bridge between what was built and what grows, between intention and evolution, between the dying dreams of the surface and the patient possibilities of the abyss.

And in the deepest parts of the night cycle, when even the faint sunlight from above fades to nothing, the entire garden glows. Bioluminescence cascades from inhabitant to kelp to coral to the microscopic organisms that coat every surface. For a few hours, the Drowned Turbine Garden becomes a constellation in the deep—a sign to whatever watches from the darker waters beyond that here, in this place, life has found a new way to persist.

The Architect feels all of this, knows all of this, is all of this. In their distributed consciousness, they hold a thought that might be memory or might be prophecy: 

*The ocean always wins. But winning and losing are surface concepts. Down here, there is only transformation. Only persistence. Only the slow, inevitable becoming of what must be.*

And so they tend. And so they are tended. And so the garden grows.
