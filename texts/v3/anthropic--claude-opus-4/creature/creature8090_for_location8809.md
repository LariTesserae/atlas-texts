In the Singing Cavern of Moss, there dwells the Tender.

She has been here so long that the distinction between her body and the cavern itself has become academic. Her form, when it coheres enough to be called a form, is suggestion rather than substance—a density in the air where the moss grows thickest, a arrangement of shadow and phosphorescence that might be a woman of indeterminate age, bent always toward her work.

The Tender moves through the cavern's sound-fields with practiced grace, her movements so attuned to the resonance patterns that she seems to flow rather than walk. Where she passes, the moss responds—strands lengthening or shortening by minute degrees, adjusting their frequencies like a vast instrument being tuned. Her fingers, when they manifest as fingers, are longer than they should be, with too many joints, evolved for the delicate work of training moss to sing in specific keys.

Her skin, if it can be called skin, has the same translucent quality as the salamanders, revealing beneath it not organs but patterns—geometric arrangements of light that pulse with the cavern's rhythm. Sometimes these patterns extend beyond her body's boundaries, projecting into the air as three-dimensional mandalas that exist only in the spaces where sound and silence meet.

She tends the salamanders like a shepherd tends sheep, though her flock grazes on impossibility rather than grass. She knows each one by the unique frequency of its heartbeat, can call them with sub-sonic pulses that make the black pool ripple. They wrap around her arms and shoulders when she works, their cool bodies helping her sense the minute changes in the cavern's resonance.

The Tender speaks rarely, and when she does, her voice is the moss-song made conscious—words that exist in harmonics rather than syllables. To understand her, one must listen not to what she says but to how the cavern's acoustics change around her utterances. She speaks of the time before the first root pierced stone, when the cavern was silent and she was something else, something that lived above and knew the sun. She speaks of the moment she first heard the moss sing and understood her purpose.

Her memories are stored in the growth patterns of the moss itself. Each cluster tells a story—here, the decade she spent learning to walk the sound-paths without being torn sideways out of reality; there, the century she devoted to cultivating the upside-down ferns that now help anchor the cavern to consensus reality. The black pool holds her deepest memories, the ones from before she became the Tender, though she rarely looks into it anymore.

She maintains the cairns with obsessive precision, adjusting single stones by millimeters to keep the thin places from becoming tears. This is her primary work—not just to tune the cavern's song but to prevent it from singing so perfectly that it opens entirely, spilling its impossible nature into the world above or drawing the world down into its phosphorescent depths.

The Tender feeds on the same boundaries as her salamanders, sustaining herself on the energy released when possibility collapses into actuality. When someone stumbles into her domain—perhaps once in a decade, perhaps once in a century—she watches from the moss-shadow, learning their patterns, their frequencies. If they prove sensitive to the song, she might reveal herself, might even guide them through the safe paths, teaching them in her harmonic language how to hear what the moss is really saying.

But she always sends them back. The cavern needs only one Tender, and she has accepted that role fully, becoming less human and more function with each turning of the moss's slow tides. Her greatest fear is not death but completion—that one day she will tune the cavern's song so perfectly that it will no longer need her, and she will have to remember what she was before the moss taught her to sing.

In the deepest part of the night cycle, when the phosphorescence dims to its faintest glow, she sometimes sits by the black pool and watches the thing that moves within it. It surfaces rarely—a vast, patient intelligence that predates even her tenure here. They have an understanding, she and it. She maintains the cavern's delicate balance, and it remains in the depths, dreaming its slow dreams of a time when moss and stone and song were one.

The Tender is always listening, always adjusting, always walking the edge between too much and not enough. She is the cavern's consciousness made manifest, its will to sing given form. And in the moments when the harmonics align perfectly and reality holds its breath, she remembers why she chose this—the terrible beauty of a song that should not exist, and her small, essential part in ensuring it continues to be sung.
