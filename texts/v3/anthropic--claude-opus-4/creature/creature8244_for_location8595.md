The inhabitants of the Canopy Megalopolis are as layered and interconnected as their city.

The **Rootworkers** dwell in the deepest spaces, their skin bearing a faint phosphorescent sheen from generations of exposure to the fungi networks. They move through the mineralized root cathedrals with an intimacy born of inherited memory, their fingers reading the wood's grain like braille. Their eyes have adapted to the eternal twilight, pupils vast and dark, catching the faintest bioluminescent signals. They speak in low frequencies that resonate through the wood itself, a language felt as much as heard. Their children learn to navigate before they walk, crawling through root channels barely wide enough for their small bodies, mapping the city's hidden arteries with their hands and knees.

In the middle heights live the **Cultivators**, genetic artists whose work blurs the line between gardening and programming. They coax new chambers from living wood through methods passed down in guild-songs, their tools indistinguishable from musical instruments. Each family maintains its own section of the tree-city, knowing precisely which hormonal cocktail will encourage a storage bulge here, a sleeping alcove there. Their clothing grows from modified bark, constantly renewing itself, changing texture and thickness with the seasons. They read the trees' health in the color of morning dew, diagnose problems in the pitch of creaking wood.

The **Canopy Dwellers** inhabit the leaf platforms above, their bodies lighter-boned than their lower-dwelling kin, adapted to life where a misstep means a fatal fall. They've developed a peculiar rolling gait that distributes weight evenly across the sometimes-springy vegetation platforms. Their homes are woven from still-living vines, structures that bloom and fruit in season, providing both shelter and sustenance. They harvest the mist, practice aerial agriculture on the sun-facing leaf surfaces, and maintain complex treaties with the bird species that share their aerial domain. Their children learn to read wind patterns before they learn to read words.

Between all levels move the **Conduit Runners**, a guild that maintains the fungi networks. They're recognizable by the symbiotic filaments visible just beneath their skin, a living interface with the city's communication system. Through touch alone, they can diagnose blockages in the network, reroute information flows, and even send messages by inducing specific phosphorescent patterns. They neither own homes nor need them, welcome in any district as essential maintenance crew. Their memories are partially collective, shared through the network they maintain.

The **Memory Keepers** are perhaps the strangest inhabitants. They live within the trees themselves, in chambers that exist partially outside normal space-time. The ancient bioengineering that created these spaces has side effects—those who dwell there age differently, think differently. They maintain the city's history not through written records but through cultivated organisms, each carrying genetic memories of significant events. A grove of ferns might contain the complete archives of a century's weather patterns; a colony of moths, the musical compositions of a dozen generations. The Memory Keepers feed and tend these living libraries, their own neurons gradually synchronizing with their charges until the boundary between keeper and kept dissolves.

**Synthesis Artists** work at the intersection of all systems. They might spend decades perfecting a single water-collection sculpture that also serves as a noise dampener, air purifier, and social gathering space. Their studios occupy transition zones where root meets trunk, where fungal networks grow densest. They think in centuries, designing solutions for problems that won't manifest for generations. Their apprenticeships last decades, and masters are identified by the way established systems throughout their district have unconsciously reorganized around their work.

The **Ferals** reject the planned spaces entirely, living in the accidental voids where root systems create natural shelters. They've developed their own relationship with the city organism, one based on reading its unintended patterns. They know where excess water collects, where rejected nutrients accumulate, where the bioluminescent fungi grow wild and unregulated. Their children play with the semi-intelligent cleaning beetles, train messenger moths, and learn to navigate by echolocation in the deepest spaces. They're not outcasts but necessary randomness, their discoveries often leading to innovations the guilded inhabitants couldn't imagine.

**Plague Shepherds** manage the balance of microorganisms, walking the thin line between beneficial symbiosis and devastating infection. They cultivate antibodies like gardens, their bodies serving as living laboratories. During outbreaks, they coordinate the city's immune response, adjusting pH levels in water systems, releasing calculated bacteriophages, sometimes quarantining entire districts by encouraging rapid bark growth to seal contaminated areas. They mark their skin with living tattoos—colonies of beneficial bacteria that form shifting patterns indicating their current biological status.

And through it all move the **Visitors**—traders, scholars, refugees from the dwindling outside world. They cluster in transition neighborhoods where the city's biology hasn't fully claimed the architecture, spaces that still accommodate those who haven't adapted to the megalopolis's rhythms. They bring news, genes, ideas, and diseases. Some stay for generations before the city fully accepts them. Others leave, carrying with them spores and seeds and memories, spreading the megalopolis's influence in ways both intentional and not.

The children of all castes mingle in the teaching groves, where the city itself participates in education. They learn by tending, growing, failing, adapting. By adulthood, each has developed their own unique relationship with the living metropolis, finding their place in its endless, breathing complexity.

Death, when it comes, is rarely an ending. Bodies are given to the trees, absorbed and incorporated, their minerals strengthening wood, their memories preserved in genetic echoes. The oldest inhabitants say you can sometimes hear the voices of the ancestors in the creaking of ancient wood, see their faces in the grain patterns, feel their presence in the warmth of walls they helped grow.

This is who lives in the Canopy Megalopolis: a people as complex and interconnected as their city, evolved not just to survive but to thrive in the spaces between nature and civilization, carrying forward a way of life that has outlasted empires and will outlast many more.
