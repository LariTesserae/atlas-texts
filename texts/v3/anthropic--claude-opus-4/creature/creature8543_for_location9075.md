The inhabitants of the canopy city are the Threadkeepers—a people as intertwined with their environment as the trees themselves.

They are a lean folk, with long fingers stained perpetually green-black from handling sap and bark. Their skin tends toward deep brown or copper tones, often mottled with patches of pale green—not disease, but a benign symbiosis with the same fungi that light their nights. The spores settle in the skin's oils during childhood and never quite leave. The oldest among them are more landscape than person, their skin rough as bark, that green mottle spreading until they seem half-tree themselves.

Water has shaped their bodies. Generations of life in the perpetual damp has given them wide, splayed feet with semi-webbed toes—better for gripping wet bark and moss-slick bridges. Their hair grows thick and rope-like, naturally forming into locks that shed water like the leaves above them. Most wear it long, braided with thin vines that are still alive, sprouting tiny leaves and air roots. Some cultivate specific vine species in their hair—morning glories that bloom at dawn, or the prized silver-thread vine whose flowers release a gentle phosphorescence at night.

They dress in layers of what they call "breathing cloth"—fabric woven from spider silk and processed inner bark that never quite dries but never rots either. The garments hang loose and flowing, designed to dry quickly during the brief periods between rain. Jewelry is minimal and organic: carved seedpods, polished segments of crystallized root, beetles' wings preserved in tree resin. Metal is saved for tools, too precious to wear.

The Threadkeepers organize themselves around the growth patterns of the trees. Families tend to specific trunk-homes, but these families are fluid—children often apprentice with aunts or uncles in different trees, and it's common for adults to maintain sleeping quarters in multiple locations as their work requires. They count kinship through teacher-lines as much as blood, tracing their lineages through chains of knowledge passed down: who taught whom to read the trees' chemical signals, to guide a branch's growth, to know when a trunk is preparing to split and spawn a daughter-tree.

Their governance is equally organic. Decisions flow upward from working groups to tree-councils to the Grove Moot that meets in the old growth at the city's heart. But true authority rests with the Readers—those who have learned to interpret the trees' own slow signals. When a Reader declares that the forest is "unready" for some proposed change, the matter is shelved for a season or a generation. They practice patience as a cultural virtue, teaching their children through mantras: "Fast as moss, slow as stone, patient as water, certain as growth."

The youngest children spend their days in the canopy crèches—platforms near the crown where the thin branches sway constantly. Here they develop the balance and tree-sense that will serve them all their lives. They play games of bridge-building with living vines, seeing who can coax a connection fastest. By adolescence, they know every pathway in their district, can travel tree to tree without touching the ground for hours.

Adults wake before dawn to check their catch-nets—webs strung between branches to collect the night's condensation, flavored by whatever flowers or fruits hang above them. Morning water has a dozen subtle varieties, from the tart citrus-drip beneath the acid-fruit trees to the honey-sweet collection from the nectar vines. They breakfast on shelf fungus grilled over volcanic vents, spread with fermented fruit paste and the protein-rich beetle grubs that live in certain bark crevices.

Work rhythms follow the trees' daily cycles. Morning, when the sap runs fastest, is for construction and repair—guiding new growth, reinforcing weakening joints, planning where next season's platforms will hang. The blooming hours of midday are for indoor crafts, everyone retreating to workshop chambers while the air fills with pollen. Late afternoon brings the rains, and with them communal time—sharing meals in the covered common halls, teaching children the subtle art of wood-speaking, tending to the thousand small negotiations that keep a vertical city functioning.

They mark time not by calendar but by tree-sign: the budding of the compass flowers that always face volcanic north, the migration of the pearl-shell snails up and down the trunks, the fruiting of fungi that follows predictable cycles. Years are counted in rings, read from core samples carefully extracted from designated chronology trees.

Their stories are sung rather than written, voices carrying better than paper in the damp. Epic poems tell of the First Coaxers who convinced the trees to accept them, of the Warring Groves when two districts grew toward each other too aggressively, of the Season of Falling when a volcanic surge killed a quarter of the forest and the survivors had to quickly reshape their remaining homes. They sing in harmonies that mimic the city's sounds—the constant drip, the whistle of steam, the crack of growing wood.

Death, to them, is a return. Bodies are placed in specially prepared hollows within the trees, where the forest slowly reclaims them. Bones become calcium for new growth, flesh feeds the decomposers that keep the wood healthy. The greatest honor is to have a tree grow around your remains, incorporating you into its heartwood. They point to certain ancient trunks and say: "That's where Grandmother Keepsthreads rests. You can see her in the way the grain spirals."

The Threadkeepers are not without their tensions. Young radicals sometimes advocate for faster growth, for metal tools and stone foundations, for independence from the trees' slow wisdom. But the forest has a way of teaching patience—rushed construction fails, forced growth weakens and breaks, and those who try to impose instead of negotiate find themselves gently but firmly excluded from the tree-sense that makes life here possible.

They dream collectively, some say—when the whole city sleeps, their exhalations joining the trees' night breathing. In these dreams they see the city as it might be in a hundred years, a thousand: grown vast as a mountain, roots deep as the volcanic chambers, crown touching the clouds. They wake with dirt under their fingernails and sap in their hair, ready for another day of coaxing miracles from the marriage of wood and stone, patience and growth, human need and forest time.

The Threadkeepers know they are temporary. The trees are not. This knowledge shapes everything—their patient governance, their fluid families, their acceptance of constant change. They are not building a city to last forever. They are tending a process that will outlive them all, and finding meaning in their brief moment as part of something larger, older, and more patient than any human ambition. In the canopy city that breathes with ten thousand leaves, they have learned the hardest lesson: how to be still enough to grow.
