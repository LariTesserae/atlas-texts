In this amphibious city, the people have evolved like the moss that claims their walls—slowly, persistently, finding ways to thrive in the perpetual damp.

The **Pumpkeepers** form the city's essential caste, though they rarely gather in groups larger than two or three. You know them by their waxed canvas coats that shed water like duck feathers, by the constant grease under their fingernails, by the way they cock their heads to listen to machinery the way others might listen to music. Each tends to their assigned district's pumps with religious devotion, sleeping in small chambers built into the pump houses themselves. They name their machines, know their rhythms and ailments, speak to them in low voices during the long maintenance shifts. The eldest among them remember when there were twice as many pumps, and they wear that knowledge like a scar—every failed machine is a neighborhood lost to the water. They communicate through a complex system of whistle signals that echo through the pneumatic tubes, warning of pressure drops, calling for assistance, marking the passing of shifts. Their children learn to read water levels before they learn to read words.

The **Vertical Farmers** have transformed necessity into artistry. They tend the cascading gardens with a patience that matches the city's slow dissolution, building soil from composted algae and factory ash, training vines to follow the sun's weak path across the grey sky. Multi-generational families work the same wall gardens, passing down knowledge of which plants can tolerate the constant moisture, which provide the best nutrition per square meter of growing space. They move through their vertical territories on intricate systems of pulleys and counterweights, harvesting greens at dawn, tending the goats that browse the middle levels, maintaining the delicate networks of irrigation that redistribute the endless water into something useful. Their apartments cluster around their gardens, connected by private bridges strung with growing nets where mushrooms fruit in the perpetual shade.

In the factories, the **Waxworkers** practice their protective craft. Everything metal must be coated; everything wooden must be sealed. They move through the production floors like priests, applying their preparations with brushes made from their own shed hair (synthetic bristles decay too quickly in the humidity). The secret formulas—part paraffin, part plant resin, part rendered fat from the canal otters—pass from master to apprentice in whispered recipes. Their guild houses smell of hot wax and preservation, and their members bear the characteristic burn scars on their arms from years of handling molten materials. They read the city's decay in the patterns of rust and rot, predicting which structures will fail, calculating how long their protections will hold against the patient water.

The **Tram Drivers** navigate the aerial networks with practiced indifference to the drop below. Their vehicles clatter along elevated tracks that web between buildings, carrying workers and goods across districts without descending to water level. Each driver maintains their own tram with fierce pride, decorating the interiors with salvaged materials—bits of colored glass, pressed flowers sealed in wax, photographs wrapped in protective plastic. They've developed their own cant, a rapid-fire dialect peppered with technical terms and route nicknames, incomprehensible to outsiders. At shift changes, they gather in depot cafeterias suspended high above the canals, sharing stories of close calls when tracks shifted in the humidity, gossipping about which routes might close next as buildings settle further into the softening earth.

The **Canal Guides** pole their flat-bottomed boats through the flooded streets with movements that seem more meditation than labor. They know every submerged obstacle, every current created by the failing pumps, every shortcut through buildings whose ground floors have become tunnels. Their boats serve as mobile markets, emergency transport, and message services all at once. They paint eyes on their prows—an old tradition whose origin no one remembers—and hang bells that ring mournfully in the fog. The guides live on their boats as often as not, cooking on small braziers, sleeping under waxed tarps, their whole lives adapted to the gentle rock of water that never stills. They're the first to know when water levels change, and their worried voices carry across the districts now, reporting unprecedented rises, strange currents from below.

In the highest towers live the **Dry Ones**, the wealthy who've purchased their distance from the damp. They employ armies of servants to maintain the dehumidifiers that run constantly in their sealed environments, creating pockets of artificial aridity high above the moss line. Their children grow up pale and slightly breathless, unaccustomed to the thick air of lower levels. When they must descend—for business, for the factories they own but rarely visit—they wear filtered masks and treated clothing, moving quickly through the humid zones like visitors from another world. They sponsor the construction of ever-higher towers, competing to build above the cloud line where mythical sun might shine unfiltered. Their pets are delicate things—hairless cats, temperature-controlled terrariums of desert lizards, birds that would die if released into the moisture-laden air.

But perhaps most numerous are the **Middlewaters**—neither rich enough to live truly dry nor poor enough to dwell at flood level. They inhabit the complex middle strata of the city, adapting endlessly to conditions that worsen by degrees. They've learned to live with walls that weep condensation, with clothes that never fully dry, with the sound of dripping as constant as their own heartbeats. Their neighborhoods wrap around the functioning factories, filling the spaces between industrial and residential, between maintained and abandoned. They work the production lines, staff the vertical farms, apprentice to the specialized guilds. Their children play on the catwalks, dropping stick boats into the canals far below, making games of the city's slow submersion.

Among all these, the **Listeners** hold a special place. They monitor the city's sounds—every drip, every mechanical wheeze, every whistle signal—from stations equipped with networks of pipes that carry sound from across the districts. Originally established to coordinate pump maintenance, their role has evolved into something more mystical. They claim to hear the city's health in its accumulated noise, to predict weather changes and mechanical failures by subtle shifts in the ambient sound. Now they report new noises from below—grinding, shifting sounds that suggest movement in the waterlogged foundations, as if something massive stirs beneath the accumulated weight of water and years.

As the crisis deepens and water rises to unprecedented levels, these varied inhabitants find themselves forced together on the highest walkways and platforms. Pumpkeepers stand next to Dry Ones, their class divisions temporarily dissolved by shared uncertainty. The canal guides beach their boats on rooftops, joining the vertical farmers in calculating how long the food supplies might last if the lower growing levels flood. The waxworkers apply emergency coatings to barriers that were never meant to hold back water, while the tram drivers run extra shifts, evacuating people from the rising flood.

In this convergence, the city's human ecosystem reveals its interdependence. The Dry Ones' towers sway without the stabilizing pumps. The middlewaters discover that their carefully maintained equilibrium was always more fragile than they believed. The listeners' warnings, long dismissed as superstition, suddenly carry the weight of prophecy.

They are all, in the end, creatures of the moss and iron city—adapted to its rhythms, dependent on its systems, as much a part of its ecology as the ferns and otters and endless, patient rain. Whether they will adapt again to whatever comes next, or whether this marks the end of their particular evolution, remains unwritten in the rising water.
