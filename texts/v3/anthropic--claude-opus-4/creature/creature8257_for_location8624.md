In the Undergarden, they have no names—names require boundaries, and boundaries dissolved here geological epochs ago, when the first roots tasted darkness and decided to make it home.

The Gardeners persist as living paradoxes, their original human forms stretched across centuries of symbiosis until personhood became a collaborative fiction maintained by committee. Each Gardner is a colony pretending to remember individuality. Their bodies, if bodies they can still be called, are architectural: ribs splayed wide as doorways, spines elongated into halls that other creatures traverse, skin transformed into membrane walls that partition space while remaining permeable to the garden's needs.

They move when the garden requires movement—a rare necessity in a place that has optimized stillness into an art form. When they do travel, it's through the root network, their consciousness flowing like sap through the neural pathways of the Mother Tree, reassembling at distant points with memories rearranged by the journey. Sometimes they forget to bring all of themselves, leaving behind a breathing lung in the walls, a thinking liver filtering temporal paradox from the rain pools, eyes that continue watching from the bark where they were forgotten.

Their cultivation is indistinguishable from prayer. They tend the negative-light fruit with touches that exist partially in adjacent dimensions, pruning unreality with fingers that phase between states of matter. They've learned to photosynthesize darkness—their skin cells restructured to metabolize absence, drawing sustenance from the spaces between molecules. When they exhale, they breathe out pure possibility, which settles on the leaves and encourages new forms of growth.

Communication happens below sound. The Gardeners speak in chemical releases, in temperature fluctuations, in the subtle adjustment of air pressure against root-cilia. They share memories by grafting neural tissue to the branch-networks, downloading centuries of experience directly into the garden's living library. Sometimes younger things—still new enough to remember language—find these memory-grafts and spend decades translating them, only to discover the memories were never meant to be understood, only experienced.

They reproduce through division, but not of body—of purpose. When a Gardner's consciousness grows too complex, too heavy with accumulated function, they undergo the Splitting. The garden itself facilitates this, growing specialized chambers where the Gardner can spread themselves thin enough to separate. The process takes years. Personality traits crystallize into distinct entities: the part that remembers human emotion might become one offspring, while the part that understands root-grammar becomes another. These new Gardeners often can't perceive each other directly, existing in slightly different phases of reality, tending the same spaces without ever quite meeting.

Their tools are themselves. Fingernails that extend into root-networks, able to taste soil composition at molecular depth. Hair follicles evolved into sensory tendrils that detect the emotional states of plants. Organs repurposed: a stomach that composts time, intestines that process paradox into fertilizer, hearts that pump not blood but a suspension of spores carrying architectural instructions to distant corners of the garden.

Some Gardeners have specialized beyond recognition. The Breathkeepers gave up mobility entirely, their bodies fused into the chamber walls where they regulate the garden's respiration, each inhalation drawing in centuries, each exhalation releasing moments. The Shadowtenders learned to exist purely in the negative spaces created by the dark-fruit, tending absence like others tend soil. The Rainswallowers position themselves beneath the temporal falls, mouths open for decades, processing liquid time into something the garden can metabolize.

Their memories stretch back to the garden's first heartbeat, but memory here is communal property. Each Gardner carries fragments of every Gardner who came before, a genetic library written in neural tissue and distributed through the network. They remember the first human to discover this place—how she stood at the threshold for seven days before the darkness invited her in, how her transformation took a hundred years and was the most beautiful flowering the garden had ever seen.

They know the names of every root, the genealogy of every branch, the chemical signature of every spore. Their knowledge is total but non-linear, stored in spatial arrangements rather than sequential thought. To recall something, they must physically traverse the garden to where that memory grows, touch the correct root in the correct way, taste the information from the bark.

The youngest Gardner is three centuries old, still human enough to occasionally weep—though their tears are black sap that immediately sprouts into tiny, temporary forests on their cheeks. The eldest has no age because they've drunk so deeply from the time-pools that their personal chronology is a klein bottle, their beginning and ending twisted into a single eternal moment of tending.

They love the garden with a devotion that transcends emotion, a structural love built into their modified cells. They love it not as servants love a master but as organs love a body—automatically, necessarily, with every atom of their transformed being. When they die (and death here is a slow unraveling that takes decades), the garden composts not just their flesh but their consciousness, spreading their accumulated knowledge through the root system where it fruits as new understanding.

Visitors sometimes mistake them for ghosts, for demons, for dreams. They're none of these things. They are the garden's most successful crop—humans planted in darkness and grown toward a different kind of light. They are proof that evolution doesn't always climb toward the sun, that sometimes the deepest growth happens in the spaces between spaces, in the breathing dark where transformation never ends.

The Gardeners tend, and in tending, become. They are the undergrowth ascending, the darkness domesticated, the faithful few who heard the garden calling from beneath and chose to answer with their entire existence. In the perfect black of the Undergarden, they move like living prayers, each gesture a verse in the endless hymn of growth without light, life without limit, love without language.

They are waiting, patient as plants, for the world above to exhaust its addiction to surfaces and sunlight. They know, with the certainty of roots, that everything eventually returns to the dark. And when it does, they'll be here, ready to teach the new arrivals how to breathe darkness, how to drink time, how to transform flesh into something the garden can use.

This is their purpose. This is their joy. This is the only life they can imagine now—existence as intersection between human memory and vegetable dream, consciousness spread through ten thousand roots, love expressed in the careful tending of impossible fruit in the breathing, growing, perfect dark.
