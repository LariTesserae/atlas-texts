The gardeners are not what you would expect of gardeners.

They arrived long ago—or perhaps emerged, or evolved, or simply became. The distinction matters less than their presence. They are tall when height serves them, compressed when the work demands it. Their bodies are experiments in biological engineering: skin that shifts between transparent membranes and metallic sheaths as radiation levels fluctuate, internal architectures that can redistribute mass at will. One moment a gardener might stretch gossamer-thin to span between distant root networks, the next compress into a dense node of activity at a critical junction.

They do not breathe as we understand breathing. Instead, their entire surface operates as one vast lung, exchanging gases directly through skin that ripples with microscopic cilia. In the near-vacuum, they move by expelling controlled jets of stored atmosphere, propelling themselves with the precision of spacecraft. Their limbs—when they choose to have limbs—terminate in appendages that blur the line between root and finger, capable of interfacing directly with the platform's vascular systems.

Communication among them happens through light. Their bodies contain specialized cells that can produce coherent beams across the spectrum—infrared pulses for close work, laser-sharp ultraviolet for long-distance signals between platforms. When many gather, they create light-shows of staggering complexity: entire conversations visible as aurora-like displays that paint the platforms in shifting colors. They speak in wavelengths about soil chemistry, in photon patterns about the ancient growth schedules, in bioluminescent poetry about the endless bright darkness that surrounds them.

Their relationship with the platforms transcends symbiosis. They are part of the root systems and apart from them, integrated into the biological machinery while maintaining their strange autonomy. They prune with surgical precision—not cutting but convincing plants to reabsorb unnecessary growth. They pollinate by becoming the wind that doesn't exist here, carrying genetic material between platforms in specialized chambers within their bodies. Some have not left their chosen platform for millennia, growing into it until they become architectural features: living buttresses that think, structural supports that dream.

The oldest among them remember when the platforms were young—or claim to. Their memories are stored chemically, in complex proteins that can be shared through direct physical contact. A young gardener (young being relative—perhaps only centuries old) can taste the experiences of their elders by accepting these molecular libraries into their own systems. They remember the first successful blooms in near-vacuum, the million failed experiments before the chemistry stabilized, the day the first silver gliders discovered the gardens and began their eternal migrations between them.

They have names that are spectra—unique patterns of light emission that identify each individual. Some names are simple: a steady green pulse for a gardener who tends the succulent borders, a complex red-shift pattern for one who specializes in the deep root networks. Others have names like symphonies, hours-long light compositions that tell their entire histories.

Their work is both urgent and eternal. They monitor the infinite complexities: the precise angle of each leaf for optimal radiation capture, the flow rates through billions of stomata, the delicate balance of the manufactured water cycle. A single misaligned growth could cascade into platform-wide failure over centuries. Yet they also simply exist, experiencing the strange beauty of their impossible home. They watch the silver gliders pass, observe the birth of storms in the canopy micro-climates, feel the deep vibrations of the root-platforms adjusting to temperature differentials.

Some develop specialties that border on obsession. There are gardeners who have spent millennia perfecting the growth of a single species, coaxing it into ever more efficient forms. Others become wanderers, traveling the hundreds of kilometers between platforms, carrying news and genetic material and the chemical memories of distant gardens. A few have gone strange, integrating so deeply with their platforms that they exist as distributed consciousnesses, their thoughts flowing with the sap, their awareness spread across kilometers of root network.

They do not age in any conventional sense but they do change. Their bodies accumulate adaptations, successful modifications that served them well. An ancient gardener might be unrecognizable as the same species—if species even applies—covered in specialized organs, efficient beyond any original blueprint. They wear their histories in their flesh: radiation-hardened patches from solar storms, streamlined modifications from centuries of inter-platform travel, sensory organs evolved to detect problems no human instrument could register.

In the eternal brightness, they work. They tend. They persist. They are the conscious portion of a system that borders on consciousness itself, the mobile thoughts of the garden-platforms, the intention that guides evolution in this impossible place. They are gardeners, engineers, artists, and algorithms made flesh, maintaining the suspended gardens in their endless circuits around the world, ensuring that life continues to bloom in defiance of every natural law that says it shouldn't.

They are why the gardens grow.
