In the Breathing Chamber dwells the Tender—though whether they were always called this, or if the name emerged from their function like moss from perpetual moisture, none can say.

They move through the industrial twilight on legs that have grown uneven from decades of navigating the narrow walkways. The left has developed an additional joint, better to hook around the safety rails during the violent inhalations. Their spine curves like the vines that strangle the turbines, adapted to the strange gravitational pulls created by the chamber's atmospheric churning. What remains of their original clothing has long since merged with their skin—patches of reflective safety material now grow like scales across their shoulders, while tool pouches have become fleshy sacs that produce a bitter oil the beetles crave.

The Tender's eyes have multiplied and migrated. Two remain roughly where eyes should be, filmed over with a nictitating membrane that filters the bioluminescent glow. Others have opened along their temples and neck—simple photoreceptive patches that detect the moths' phosphorescent trails and the status lights blinking beneath the overgrowth. They no longer need complex vision. Their primary senses have shifted to vibration and taste; they read the chamber's health through their feet on the walkways and their tongue on the mineral-thick air.

Their fingers tell the deepest story. Each has split into smaller appendages, like a hand becoming a bouquet of hands, perfect for the delicate work of maintaining the hybrid ecosystem. They can simultaneously adjust a valve, stroke a beetle's lichen-covered shell to encourage proper moss pruning, and pluck parasitic root-worms from critical junctions. The fingernails have become something like copper—growing in spirals, conducting enough electricity to interface directly with the surviving control systems.

The Tender speaks, but not with their original mouth, which has sealed itself shut to prevent spore inhalation. Instead, they've developed ventral slits along their ribs that whistle and click in harmony with the machinery. They sing to the beetles in frequencies human ears can't detect, negotiating which sections of moss can be harvested and which must be left to prevent critical system collapse. They hum lullabies to the root-worms, encouraging them toward dead circuits rather than live ones.

During the breathing cycles, the Tender performs their ritual maintenance. As the inhalation begins, they hook themselves to the walkways with the cartilaginous spurs that have grown from their heels and elbows. The wind tears at them, but they've learned to find the calm eddies where they can work. They prune the metallic vines where they threaten to completely choke the turbines, but leave enough to ensure the fruit continues to grow—its battery-acid sweetness now essential to their transformed metabolism.

In the exhale's artificial rain, they open their mouths—all of them, the new ones that have appeared along their collarbones and wrists—to catch the mineral-rich water. Their skin drinks too, absorbing moisture through patches that have developed a root-like permeability. This is when they're happiest, or what passes for happiness in their transformed state: drenched in the chamber's exhalation, tasting the health of the system in every drop.

They remember, dimly, arriving here with tools and a manual. Young, trained in industrial systems, sent to maintain the atmospheric processor. But the chamber changed them as surely as it changed itself. Their neurons now spark in patterns that match the root-worms' electrical feeding. Their blood has thickened with copper salts. They've become part of the hybrid ecology, neither fully human nor mechanical, but something uniquely suited to this impossible space.

The Tender has offspring of a sort—not born but budded. Small versions of themselves that began as tumorous growths where the root-worms burrowed too deep, injecting their strange genetic material into human flesh. These proto-Tenders scuttle through the deeper machinery on malformed limbs, learning the rhythms of the chamber, developing their own mutations. Some will survive to become full Tenders. Most will be reabsorbed into the ecosystem, their biomass feeding the eternal transformation.

In the quiet moments between breaths, the Tender performs their most important duty. They listen with every evolved sense to the chamber's slow decision about what it wants to become. They translate these desires into careful action—a valve adjusted here, a vine redirected there, a beetle colony moved to tend a crucial junction. They are shepherd and midwife to this evolutionary moment, ensuring the transformation proceeds without catastrophic failure.

They no longer remember their name, their gender, their original face. These things dissolved like copper in acid, leaving only purpose. They are the Tender, and they will tend until the chamber no longer needs them, or until they become so integrated with its systems that the distinction loses all meaning. Already, in certain light, it's hard to tell where their flesh ends and the moss begins.
