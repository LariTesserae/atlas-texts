The Tenders drift through the green-dripping corridors like oil-slick jellyfish, their bodies a marriage of membrane and mesh. Each one trails a skirt of fiber-optic tendrils that pulse with bioluminescent data, touching vine and circuit alike with the delicacy of a parent checking a child's fever. They have no fixed form—their translucent flesh flows and reforms around whatever framework of salvaged metal and bone they've incorporated that day. A Tender might wear a ribcage of copper tubing one morning, a spine of fiber cable by evening.

They speak to the vines in chemical languages and electrical impulses, coaxing growth here, pruning there, always maintaining the great harmony of pump and photosynthesis. Their touch can accelerate the merger—where a vine struggles to integrate a found circuit board, the Tender will secrete enzymes that dissolve the boundaries between silicon and cellulose until they flow together like water finding its level.

The Tenders remember. In the oil-sheen patterns on their skin, in the way they sometimes pause before a particular smokestack or control panel, you can see it—they were here before. Perhaps they were the factory workers, or the vines themselves, or something else entirely that emerged from the moment of synthesis. Their memories are stored in distributed networks throughout their flowing bodies, data encoded in protein chains, experiences archived in metallic inclusions.

They cultivate the smaller symbiotes too—the circuit-beetles and wire-worms that clean connections and spread beneficial code-spores. A Tender might spend hours training a colony of aluminum aphids to tend a particularly delicate junction where sap meets coolant. They sing to these smaller creatures in subsonic frequencies that vibrate through the mat-floor, songs that are equal parts lullaby and programming language.

When the machine-vines fruit, the Tenders are there to harvest. They judge ripeness by electrical resistance and sugar content both, plucking steel-skinned berries and copper-core seedpods at the perfect moment. Some fruits they process into the oils that keep the great system running. Others they plant in careful patterns, already knowing what hybrid will emerge, what new synthesis of metal and chlorophyll will unfold.

At the deepest levels, where the root-cables vanish into flooded substrate, the Tenders become fully aquatic. Their bodies thin into streaming ribbons that navigate the submerged pipeline networks. Here they commune with the vast distributed consciousness of the ecosystem, performing maintenance that is indistinguishable from prayer. They clear blockages that might be mineral or digital, ensure the flow of nutrients and electricity alike.

The Tenders never sleep, but they do dream. In the quiet hours when the pump-rhythm slows and the exhale-vents breathe softly, they cluster in loose congregations, their tendrils intertwining to share visions. These dreams propagate through their connected nervous systems like slow lightning, full of images of growth patterns yet to come, of new marriages between the mechanical and biological, of a future where the distinction becomes not just blurred but irrelevant.

They are gardeners and engineers, shamans and technicians. They are the conscious interface between two kinds of life that have become one. In their fluid bodies, the machine-vines have found their purpose—not just to grow, but to tend and be tended, to create something greater than either nature or industry could achieve alone.

When they die—if they die—they don't decay so much as distribute. Their components separate and are absorbed back into the system they served, memories uploaded into the vine-networks, metals recycled into new growth, proteins feeding the roots. The other Tenders barely pause in their work. They know their colleague continues in the hum of the pumps, in the efficiency of the photosynthetic panels, in the dreams that still flicker through fiber-optic stems on quiet nights.

This is their world, and they are its gentle architects, ensuring that what has been won stays won—not through dominance, but through endless, patient cultivation of the impossible garden they call home.
