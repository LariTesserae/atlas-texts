The inhabitants move through their vertical world with a particular economy of motion—never rushing, understanding that haste creates only more heat in bodies already adapted to conserving energy in the perpetual warmth. They are a compact people, limbs lean from climbing stairs and ladders, skin that gleams with a constant sheen of moisture that never quite evaporates. Their hair grows thick and is worn short or tightly bound, practical against the humidity that would otherwise turn it to a constant tangle.

They dress in layers that breathe—loose linens and treated cottons that wick moisture, dyed in the deep colors that show well in the diffuse light: burgundy, forest green, the dark blue of deep water. Metal jewelry would rust; instead they wear polished stone and carved bone, volcanic glass shaped into subtle ornaments. Everyone carries a cloth, tucked into belt or pocket, for the endless wiping of condensation from faces, hands, surfaces.

Their hands are their primary tools, fingers sensitive to temperature gradients, able to test the heat of a pipe or the readiness of a thermal vent by proximity alone. Generations of working with hot water systems have bred an intuitive understanding of pressure and flow. Children learn early to read the sounds of the city—which hiss means a valve needs attention, which rumble suggests a blockage in the depths.

In the greenhouses, the cultivators move like dancers among their vertical gardens, reaching through curtains of leaves with practiced precision. They wear leather aprons treated with plant oils to repel moisture, their tools hung from belts within easy reach. They speak to their plants in low voices, believing the vibrations travel better through the humid air. Each family tends specific sections, knowledge passed through generations: which varieties fruit in sequence, how to read the color of a stem for readiness, the precise angle to cut for regrowth.

The engineers who maintain the thermal systems work in shifts, descending into the mechanical levels where the heat is most intense. They wear minimal clothing there, bodies marked with the scars of steam burns that are considered signs of experience rather than carelessness. They communicate through a system of pipe-knocks when verbal communication is impossible over the roar of rushing water. Their most prized possession is their personal pressure gauge, worn smooth from handling.

Social life revolves around the communal baths. Here, the strict hierarchies of work dissolve in the democracy of shared water. Conversations flow as languidly as the thermal streams, punctuated by long silences that are comfortable rather than awkward. The elderly hold court in the hottest pools, their weathered skin immune to temperatures that would send younger residents retreating. Children splash in the cooler edges, their laughter one of the few sounds that pierces the muffling atmosphere.

At mealtimes, families gather in the communal kitchens, where enormous pots steam over thermal vents. The diet is primarily vegetarian—the greenhouse abundance of leafy greens, squashes, legumes bred for productivity. Protein comes from tanks of thermal-adapted fish, blind pale things that thrive in the hot water. Everything is cooked by steam, flavored with herbs that grow wild in the crevices where soil collects. Meals are taken slowly, bodies already sluggish from the heat.

They mark time not by the invisible sun but by the cultivation cycles, by the maintenance schedules of the great turbines, by the gradual accumulation of mineral deposits that must periodically be chipped away. Their calendar follows the greenhouse harvests—the week of the great melon cutting, the days when the tea leaves are ready for picking, the careful timing of the medicinal herbs.

In the workshops, artisans shape volcanic glass with water-cooled tools, creating implements both functional and beautiful. The work requires patience—too much haste and the glass shatters from thermal shock. Weavers work with fibers that must be kept damp to remain pliable, their looms strung in rooms where the humidity is carefully controlled. Metalworkers forge in the depths where the heat is free, their hammers ringing in rhythms that become part of the city's mechanical heartbeat.

The young learn by observation and gradual participation. There are no schools as such—children follow their elders through the daily routes, absorbing the knowledge of pipe networks, plant cycles, thermal dynamics through repetition and slowly increasing responsibility. By adolescence, they can navigate the city's vertical maze blindfolded, guided by temperature changes and the subtle variations in the steam's mineral scent.

Death, when it comes, is given to the mountain. Bodies are carried to the highest accessible peaks, where the cooler air preserves them until the volcanic stone claims them, making them part of the geological process that created their city. It is considered a good death to become stone, to join the terraces that support the next generation.

They dream of clear skies they've never seen, of horizons not bounded by steam. But they also dream of roots reaching through warm water, of pressure building and releasing in perfect cycles, of green things growing in impossible abundance. Their art reflects this—intricate patterns that could be pipe networks or root systems, paintings where the boundary between mechanical and biological becomes uncertain.

This is how they live: patient as their plants, steady as their thermal flows, building their lives grain by grain like mineral deposits. They are engineers and gardeners, maintainers of a delicate balance between the geological gift of heat and their own careful cultivation. They cannot imagine living any other way, in any place where the air doesn't taste of minerals and growth, where the warmth doesn't wrap around them like a blanket that never lifts.
