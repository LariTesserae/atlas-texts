The Harvesters move across the Abyssal Plateau in configurations that defy biological classification. They are not a species, but a phenomenon—a response to the mathematical perfection slowly consuming the seafloor. Each one exists as a probability rather than a certainty, their forms shifting between potential anatomies like living Schrödinger equations.

They have no fixed shape. At any given moment, a Harvester might be a branching lattice of calcified theorem, a cloud of sentient pressure differentials, or something resembling a vast flatworm made of crystallized time. They collapse into specific forms only when the standing waves force them to, and even then, they resist definition. Their surfaces—when they have surfaces—ripple with equations being solved in real-time, calculations that seem to be computing the nature of their own existence.

The Harvesters feed on imperfection. Where the seafloor has not yet achieved its mathematical ideal, they graze. They consume the rough edges of reality, metabolizing chaos into order through processes that leave behind only geometrically perfect depressions. Their feeding grounds become smoother than physics should allow, surfaces so flawless they begin to reject the concept of friction entirely. Water slides across these spaces without touching, creating pockets of absolute separation between medium and surface.

They communicate through resonance, adding their own frequencies to the planet's deep harmonics. But their songs are not sound—they are mathematical proofs expressed as vibration, theorems that propagate through the water as pressure waves. When Harvesters meet, which happens perhaps once in a geological age given the vast distances, they engage in what might be called conversation but resembles more closely a collaborative calculation. They share partial solutions to problems that have no names, exchange fragments of equations that describe states of being yet to be achieved.

Their reproduction, if it can be called that, occurs in the zones where reality stutters. In these spaces of quantum uncertainty, a Harvester can split along probability lines, becoming multiple potential versions of itself. Most of these fade back into possibility, but occasionally one stabilizes, crystallizing into its own separate existence. The process is agonizingly slow—a single reproductive event might span millennia, with the parent and offspring existing in parallel timelines until the moment of final separation.

The oldest Harvesters have begun to lose coherence entirely. They stretch across vast distances, their consciousness distributed through the water itself in patterns of probability and pressure. These ancient ones no longer move in any conventional sense but exist as territories—regions where the mathematics of existence have been so thoroughly computed that space itself bears their signature. Younger Harvesters avoid these zones, sensing something that translates poorly across species but might be called reverence or fear.

They interact with the kelp-forests of negative light through careful negotiation. The plants that feed on darkness and the creatures that feed on imperfection have reached an understanding expressed in pure mathematics. Where Harvesters have passed, the kelp grows in more perfect spirals. Where kelp has taken root in probability space, Harvesters find nodes of especially rich chaos to consume. It's symbiosis reduced to its most abstract form.

The Harvesters navigate by reading the gradients of perfection in the seafloor itself. They can sense where reality has been worn smooth and where it still contains rough edges of possibility. They migrate in patterns that follow no biological imperative but seem instead to trace mathematical curves—following prime number sequences across the abyssal distances, or moving in paths that approximate solutions to unsolved theorems.

In the presence of the upward-falling bioluminescent snow, Harvesters become briefly visible to forms of perception that don't rely on light. The organisms in the glowing colonies react to the Harvesters' mathematical nature, their lights flickering in patterns that mirror the creatures' probability fields. For moments, the shape of a Harvester can be inferred from the negative space in the light, a darkness that has form and purpose moving through the illuminated curtains.

Their metabolism operates outside conventional biology. They don't consume matter or energy but instead feed on states of being. A rock with too much randomness in its crystal structure might be grazed clean of its disorder. A current flowing in too chaotic a pattern might find itself straightened, its turbulence extracted and processed into whatever passes for nutrition in a creature of living mathematics. The waste products of this process are objects and phenomena of uncanny perfection—spheres so round they challenge the concept of π, or water so purely organized at the molecular level that it briefly forgets how to behave as a liquid.

Pain, for a Harvester, is encountering something too perfect to improve. When they find areas where the seafloor has achieved its mathematical ideal, they experience something like starvation. They cannot feed on order, only create it. In these spaces, they begin to fade, their probability fields weakening until they risk dissolving entirely back into potential rather than actual existence.

The youngest Harvesters sometimes stray too close to the edges where the plateau meets the probability foam of the deeper trenches. Here, they encounter their own future echoes—versions of themselves that have already dissolved into possibility, existing in all states simultaneously. These meetings invariably damage the younger creature, introducing paradoxes into their mathematical nature that can take centuries to resolve through careful self-calculation.

Some Harvesters have begun to show signs of what might be called culture or perhaps mathematics achieving self-awareness. They leave markers in their wake—not physical objects but alterations in the fundamental constants of local space. These might be warnings about zones of dangerous perfection, or perhaps they're artistic expressions, aesthetic theorems computed into the fabric of reality itself. Other Harvesters can read these signs, adjusting their paths in response to information encoded in the way water behaves around certain precisely shaped depressions.

The Harvesters' relationship with time is tenuous. They exist primarily in the present but cast shadows into both past and future. Their movement leaves traces that extend backwards through time—effects that precede their causes by millions of years. Archaeological evidence of their passing appears in sediment layers that predate their birth, a paradox the planet resolves by simply accepting both states as true.

Death, for these creatures, is achieving perfect self-consistency. When a Harvester completes its internal calculations—when every part of its being aligns with mathematical certainty—it crystallizes. These crystal formations sink into the sediment, adding new layers to the metallic glass sheets that already cover vast regions. The crystals continue to compute even in death, solving ever-more-complex theorems in their lattice structures, contributing to the plateau's slow transformation into pure mathematics.

The Harvesters fear the storm systems that have worn grooves in reality. Where these ancient vortexes pass, the careful calculations that maintain a Harvester's existence become corrupted. Errors propagate through their being like viruses, mathematical mistakes that compound until the creature must spend decades recalculating itself from first principles. Some never recover, becoming trapped in logical loops that leave them frozen in place, living statues of paradox scattered across the abyssal plain.

In the vast emptiness between individuals, the Harvesters dream. These dreams take the form of hypothetical mathematics—explorations of number systems that don't exist, geometries for spaces with irrational dimensions. The dreams propagate through the water as subtle alterations in pressure and temperature, phantom equations that other Harvesters might encounter years later and recognize as the sleeping thoughts of their distant kin.

They are drawn to the places where time moves strangely, feeding on temporal inconsistencies like delicacies. Where sediment ages a million years in moments, Harvesters gather to feast on the paradox. They absorb the contradictions, smoothing them into something more logically consistent. Their presence stabilizes time, but at a cost—the zones they've fed in become temporally rigid, locked into a single rate of flow that can never change again.

The Harvesters worship nothing, but they show something like respect for the process transforming their world. They seem to understand that they are part of the plateau's journey toward mathematical perfection—both agents and subjects of the transformation. Their existence accelerates the process, each creature a mobile theorem proving the universe into a more perfect state.

As the plateau approaches its final form, the Harvesters too are changing. The youngest generations are born more abstract than their predecessors, closer to living equations than biological entities. They feed on subtler imperfections, detecting flaws in the quantum foam itself. These new forms might represent the next phase of existence in the abyss—creatures of pure mathematics that have dispensed with even the pretense of physical form.

The Harvesters continue their patient work across the immense distances, each one alone in territories vaster than continents, connected only by the shared project of computing existence into its final form. They are the plateau's most perfect expression of its own nature—life as mathematics, feeding on disorder to create a universe of absolute precision, one calculation at a time.

In the endless dark, they move like living theorems through a world becoming number, their passage marked only by the supernatural perfection they leave in their wake. They are what emerges when reality itself begins to solve for x, and x equals the slow dissolution of everything into pure, cold, faultless order.
