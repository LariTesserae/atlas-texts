In the Rust Quarter's perpetual twilight, the Riveters make their homes in the spaces between spaces—those gaps where pipes converge but don't quite meet, where structural beams create unintended cavities, where decades of haphazard construction have left pockets of refuge from the mechanical storm.

They are a people transformed by their environment, though whether they chose this transformation or simply survived it remains unclear. Their skin bears a faint metallic sheen, not unlike the patina on old copper, and when they sweat—which is rare in this desiccated place—the droplets leave rust-colored stains. Their eyes have adapted to the constant spray of sparks and metal shavings; a transparent nictitating membrane flicks across their vision every few seconds, clearing away debris. Their lungs wheeze with a peculiar resonance, having developed additional chambers to filter the metallic dust from what passes for air.

The eldest among them, a woman known only as Thread, claims to remember when water last flowed through the quarter's pipes. Her fingers have worn down to nubs from decades of handling sharp metal, and she's replaced them with articulated copper extensions that she's trained to move with disturbing dexterity. She operates a loom of her own design in a cathedral of discarded boilers, weaving cable-silk harvested from the brass spider's endless web into cloth that won't tear on the quarter's jagged edges. The younger Riveters come to her not for her weaving, but for her maps—intricate diagrams etched on sheets of beaten tin that show which passages are safe during which hours, when the shakes will hit each sector, and where the automata patrol in their mindless routes.

The Riveters have learned to move like the gear-moths, timing their passages through the quarter to slip between the teeth of its dangers. They've developed a form of communication that bypasses sound—in the overwhelming noise, voices are useless. Instead, they tap rhythms on the pipes that run throughout the district, a code that propagates through the metal faster than sound through air. A trained Riveter can feel these vibrations through their feet, interpreting messages from across the quarter. Children learn to tap before they learn to speak.

Their dwellings cluster in the most stable sections, though "stable" is relative. Families claim territories around the massive support pillars that bear the quarter's weight, building nests from scavenged materials. They've learned to construct their homes with deliberate weak points—sections designed to collapse safely when the shakes hit, absorbing the violence without crushing the inhabitants. These homes are rebuilt constantly, their configurations changing like living things. A dwelling might have six rooms one week, three the next, twelve the week after, as walls are knocked down, recycled, reformed.

The Riveters have developed a complex relationship with the quarter's machines. They don't merely use them—they court them, appease them, navigate around their quirks and malfunctions like one might handle dangerous but necessary animals. Each family maintains at least one ancient automaton, kept functional through generations of careful maintenance. These mechanical servants are more than tools; they're inheritance, status symbols, and in their strange way, companions. Old Hendrik's family has a copper-plated loader that's been in their care for sixty years. It only responds to commands whistled in a specific pitch, and every third Thursday it must be fed a mixture of graphite and oil or it begins dismantling everything within reach.

Children born in the Rust Quarter rarely survive without modification. Those who do are changed from their first breath. Their bones grow denser to withstand the constant vibration. Their blood carries higher levels of iron. Some develop a magnetic sense, feeling the pull and flow of the quarter's massive electromagnetic fields. They play in the mechanical graveyards, building toys from broken gears and springs, learning engineering by instinct before they can name what they're doing.

The Riveters have their own healers—not doctors in any traditional sense, but specialists who understand how flesh and metal can coexist. When Thread's grandson lost his arm to a stamping press, the healer didn't simply bandage the wound. She installed a socket, a universal joint that could accept various attachments. Now the boy swaps between a grasping claw for climbing, a powered drill for work, and a delicate hand made of brass rings for daily tasks. The healing halls reek of motor oil and antiseptic, where surgical tools share space with welding torches.

Food in the Rust Quarter requires creativity. With no water and no soil, traditional agriculture is impossible. The Riveters cultivate the phosphorescent fungi, not just for light but for sustenance. They've learned to process it, pressing it into dense cakes that taste of minerals and electricity. They harvest the eggs of the rust-eating beetles—tiny, iron-rich spheres that must be cooked in acid to be digestible. From the wire-worms, they extract a protein paste that, when mixed with synthetic lubricants, forms a nutritious if unpalatable sludge. Some families maintain vats of bacterial cultures that feed on metal shavings, producing a yeast-like substance that can be baked into something resembling bread.

Their culture has evolved its own aesthetics. Beauty, to a Riveter, is found in efficient design, in the perfect curve of a cam, in the precise mesh of gears. They wear their scars proudly—burns from welding accidents, missing fingers from industrial mishaps, skin marked by years of metal dust. Jewelry is functional: necklaces of spare parts, rings that double as wrenches, hair ornaments that can strip wire or measure current.

They have their own festivals, timed to the quarter's rhythms. During the Convergence, when all sectors experience the shakes simultaneously, the Riveters retreat to the deep shelters—reinforced bunkers in the turbine halls where the bass note of the machinery drowns out the chaos above. Here they share stories, perform maintenance on their automata, and young couples exchange bearings as tokens of commitment. When the shakes subside, they emerge for the Harvest, scavenging the machinery that has inevitably broken during the chaos, a time of opportunity and renewal.

The Riveters don't dream of escape. This is their world, and they've made themselves part of it as surely as the pipes and pistons. They speak of the quarter as a living thing, with moods and needs and a terrible vitality. They've learned its language—the meaning in the pitch of a grinding gear, the warning in the color of exhaust smoke, the promise in the rhythm of a well-maintained engine.

But perhaps most remarkably, they've found joy here. Children shriek with laughter as they race through the catwalks, playing tag with the automata. Musicians create symphonies from the industrial noise, adding their own rhythms with hammers on pipes, transforming cacophony into complex percussion. Artists arrange rust patterns into mandalas, program welding robots to paint with sparks, build kinetic sculptures that dance with the district's vibrations.

In the depths of the quarter, where even the Riveters rarely venture, lives Old Molten—a figure of legend who may or may not have once been human. Those who've seen him describe a being more machine than flesh, his body a patchwork of salvaged parts animated by unknown means. He speaks in the language of grinding gears and venting steam, understood only by those who've spent their entire lives listening to machines. Some say he's the original architect of the quarter, kept alive by the same systems he designed. Others believe he's an emergent intelligence, born from the collective functioning of all the quarter's machinery. The Riveters leave offerings for him—fresh oil, rare alloys, perfectly machined components—at shrines built into junction boxes and maintenance alcoves.

The young Riveters, those born after the last water disappeared, have begun to change in more fundamental ways. Some can interface directly with the older machinery, their nervous systems adapted to process electrical signals. They stick their fingers into access ports and commune with the control systems, adjusting processes by thought alone. These "Conductors," as they're called, are both revered and feared. They navigate the quarter's digital architecture as easily as its physical one, but they pay a price—their humanity becoming ever more ambiguous as they merge deeper with the machines.

This is who lives in the Rust Quarter: a people who've discovered that adaptation is not surrender but transformation. They've taken a place abandoned by the rest of the city and made it not just habitable but home. In the endless noise and motion, in the oil-stained air and the metallic light, in the constant danger and the strange beauty of their mechanical ecosystem, the Riveters have found a way to be human that the water-rich districts could never understand. They are proof that life is not about the circumstances of its environment but about the ingenuity of its response.
