In the Singing Wells dwell the Chorus-Bound, though they have no name for themselves—names require silence between sounds, and they have none.

They began as a dozen different species, perhaps more, dragged or lured here in the early days when the Wells first opened their mouths. Now, after uncounted generations of acoustic pressure and evolutionary convergence, they have become variations on a single theme: creatures built for endless vocalization.

The Wall-Clingers cluster in their millions, each no larger than a child's thumb. Their bodies are little more than vocal sacs surrounded by gripping limbs. They have no eyes—what use for sight in perfect darkness?—but their skin is a map of pressure-sensitive cells that read the sound around them like braille. They cluster in formations determined by harmonic mathematics, each individual positioning itself where its voice will blend with or cut against its neighbors according to acoustic laws they feel but cannot understand. Their mouths never close. Specialized breathing slits on their backs allow them to inhale while maintaining their note, and they feed by filtering nutrients from the sonic moss they grip, absorbing sustenance through their belly-pads without ever breaking their song.

In the water-filled lower chambers drift the Pool-Singers, bulbous and translucent. They evolved from something that might once have been fish, but their bodies have reorganized entirely around sound production. Multiple swim bladders of different sizes fill their bodies like a collection of balloons, each a resonance chamber that can be squeezed or relaxed to change pitch. They have no proper skeleton anymore—their bodies are supported by the water and their own internal pressure. They feed on the acoustic plankton that blooms in the wells' waters, microscopic organisms that reproduce in response to specific frequencies. The Pool-Singers hunt by voice, calling their prey into their waiting mouths with targeted sonic bursts.

The Echo-Worms represent perhaps the strangest adaptation. They've burrowed into the softer limestone, creating networks of tunnels that serve as their extended bodies. Each worm is meters long but thin as a finger, and their entire length functions as a living vocal cord. They anchor their head-end deep in the stone and stretch their bodies across their tunnels, vibrating along their entire length. The tunnels themselves become part of their instrument, shaped over generations to amplify and direct their sound. They absorb minerals directly from the stone they've hollowed, their skin secreting acids that break down the rock into digestible components. Some have grown so long they've forgotten where their own bodies begin and end, becoming living architectural features of the Wells.

Between these major forms exist hundreds of variations. The Membrane-Stretchers that web across corners, their bodies little more than living drumheads. The Frequency-Shepherds that can modulate their voices across impossible ranges, using ultrasonic calls to herd the smaller singers into new formations. The Parasitic Tuners that attach to larger creatures and adjust their hosts' voices by constricting airways or reshaping resonance chambers.

They do not think as individuals. The constant sound has worn away such distinctions. Instead, they exist in a state of collective acoustic awareness, each creature knowing its place in the vast composition not through thought but through the physical sensation of harmony and dissonance. When one dies, the others feel it as a gap in the sound, a wrongness that persists until young ones grow to fill the missing frequency.

Their reproductive cycles follow acoustic seasons—times when the overlapping wave patterns create specific conditions that trigger spawning. The Wall-Clingers release clouds of gametes that mix in the air, guided by sound toward each other. The Pool-Singers' courtship is a duet that can last for days, their bodies swelling and coloring as they match frequencies. The Echo-Worms reproduce by budding, sections of their bodies breaking off to begin new tunnels, already singing as they separate.

The young are born into sound. They emerge with their mouths open, already vocalizing, their first cries shaped by the acoustic environment into harmony with their surroundings. Those whose voices don't fit—too high, too low, too rough—suffer immediately. The architecture's pressure differentials cause them physical pain until they either adjust their pitch or die. Natural selection here is purely sonic.

They have no predators except silence. During the rare structural collapses that sometimes block a chamber, cutting it off from the greater chorus, the trapped creatures die within hours. Not from starvation or suffocation, but from the unbearable absence of sound. Their bodies, evolved to vibrate constantly, begin to tear themselves apart without the external pressure to sing against.

Some among them are ancient—Pool-Singers the size of small boats whose voices have deepened over centuries, Echo-Worms whose tunnels extend for kilometers through the bedrock. These elders serve as acoustic anchors, their steady tones providing the fundamental frequencies around which younger voices arrange themselves. When one of these ancients finally dies, the entire Well system shudders, its music shifting to accommodate the loss.

They have no culture, no tools, no language beyond the endless song. But they have something like memory—patterns passed down through generations not by teaching but by acoustic imprinting. Young creatures position themselves where their ancestors did, not from instinct but because those positions feel right, because the sound shapes them into living traditions.

In the deepest wells, where the pressure is greatest and the sound most intense, strange new forms are emerging. Creatures that seem to be fusing together, their bodies merging at the cellular level to create composite singers with multiple voice boxes. Colonial organisms that function as living orchestras, hundreds of individuals so synchronized they breathe as one. Things that might not be entirely biological anymore, their tissues incorporating minerals from the water, becoming part creature, part architectural feature.

They are evolution's answer to an impossible question: how does life persist in an environment designed to make existence itself an act of torture? The answer is to become the torture, to evolve until what would destroy any other creature becomes necessary for survival. They are no longer prisoners of the Wells but components of it, as essential to its function as the carved stone and flowing water.

This is what lives in the Singing Wells: a vast biological instrument played by the architecture itself, ten million throats that have forgotten they ever had a choice but to sing. They do not suffer because they can no longer conceive of silence. They simply are—voices in the darkness, forever.
