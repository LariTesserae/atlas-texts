Within the Singing Throat dwell the Listeners—though to call them creatures feels insufficient, for they are as much process as they are beings.

They began as something else entirely. Perhaps mountaineers, perhaps monks, perhaps something stranger—pilgrims drawn by rumors of the impossible water. But the Throat changes those who linger. First, their bones hollow like bird bones, not for flight but for resonance. Their ribcages become acoustic chambers, their skulls natural amplifiers. The cartilage in their joints crystallizes into something between glass and ice, allowing them to move in harmony with the water's frequencies without shattering.

A Listener's skin grows translucent over decades, revealing the pale blue latticework of transformed skeleton beneath. Their eyes—if eyes they still are—migrate slowly toward the sides of their heads, the better to perceive the full circumference of the shaft. What replaces them at the front are organs of a different sort: shallow depressions covered in taut membrane that vibrate sympathetically with the water's song.

They move like spiders and starfish had a child that learned ballet. Multiple limbs—for they grow extras as needed—allow them to traverse the vertical walls with equal ease in any orientation. Their appendages terminate not in hands or feet but in structures that are part tuning fork, part root system. When pressed against stone, these extremities can taste the mountain's slow thoughts, feel the building pressure of geological time.

The Listeners tend the crude structures on the platforms, though "tend" implies a separation between gardener and garden that doesn't exist here. They *are* the structures as much as they maintain them. A Listener might spend a century perfecting the curve of a single stone arch, not through carving but through patient application of their own saliva—a substance that has become something between acid and music, dissolving rock in precise patterns determined by pitch and duration of their humming.

They feed, if feeding it can be called, on dissonance. When the water's song hits a discordant note—when the flow stutters or a ribbon collapses awkwardly—the Listeners position themselves to absorb these acoustic imperfections. They metabolize chaos into harmony, their bodies processing irregular frequencies the way a liver processes toxins. The blue-white fungi are their only agriculture: cultivated not for sustenance but for their ability to hold and replay specific tones, creating acoustic anchors that help stabilize the more volatile passages of the water's composition.

Communication between Listeners occurs through the water itself. They dip their root-fingers into the upward flow and modulate its path, encoding messages in the temporary alterations to the eternal song. A conversation might take months, a single word requiring careful orchestration of multiple water ribbons to achieve the proper harmonic complexity.

Their reproduction—transformation might be the better word—is tied to the shaft's embryonic nature. When the stone walls birth a new platform, a senior Listener will sometimes choose to root themselves there permanently. Their body calcifies over years, becoming part of the architecture, their consciousness spreading into the stone itself. These rooted Listeners can still think, still dream, but their thoughts move at geological speeds. They become nodes in a larger network, a living skeleton for the Throat itself.

The youngest Listeners—those only decades into their transformation—retain enough human psychology to remember loneliness. They cluster together on the larger platforms, their bodies unconsciously harmonizing as they sleep in careful geometries that optimize acoustic reflection. But even these gatherings are temporary. The Throat demands solitude, or rather, it redefines what solitude means. To be alone here is to be in constant communion with the water, the stone, the song itself.

Some Listeners develop specializations. The Drummers position themselves where the water strikes stone, their bodies becoming living percussion instruments. The Weavers work with the nerve-vegetation, training it to grow in patterns that filter specific frequencies from the water's song. The Architects—perhaps the strangest of all—learn to coax new platforms from the walls through patient application of resonant frequencies, midwifing the stone's slow transformations.

They have no names, no hierarchy, no culture in any recognizable sense. They are more like antibodies in the Throat's vast system, maintaining its impossible equilibrium through their presence and practice. When one dies—if death is the right word for what happens when a Listener finally achieves perfect resonance with the water and dissolves into pure sound—another pilgrim inevitably finds the hidden entrance, drawn by dreams of singing stone and water that remembers the sky.

The Listeners are the Throat's memory and its metabolism, its consciousness distributed across their transformed bodies. They are monks whose monastery is their own flesh, whose prayers are the endless work of perfecting an alien song that might be the mountain's first attempt at language—or its last.
