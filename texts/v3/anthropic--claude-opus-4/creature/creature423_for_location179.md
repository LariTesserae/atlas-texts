The Cultivators arrive in shifts, descending on cable-cars that shriek against rust-seized guides. They emerge already dressed for their work: bodies wrapped in living suits that blur the distinction between clothing and symbiont. The suits are colonies—fungal networks interwoven with synthetic fibers, breathing apparatus that filters air through modified gill-slits lined with petroleum-eating bacteria. The material shifts color like a mood ring, darkening where toxins are highest, blooming with pale fruiting bodies when the wearer has stood too long in one place.

Their faces, when glimpsed through the bio-masks, show the telltale signs of long adaptation. Pupils permanently dilated from years of working in the steam-hazed twilight. Skin with a greenish undertone—chlorophyll augmentation, they call it, though whether surgical or simply environmental no one remembers. Their finger bones have elongated over generations, joints become hypermobile for reaching into the deep tangles where machine and vine interlock. The oldest among them have bark-like growths along their forearms, a condition they wear with pride.

They communicate in the Foundry's own language—not just the hand signals necessitated by the din, but a complex system involving pheromone releases from their suits, bioluminescent flashes from cultivation tools, and the rhythmic striking of metal that carries through the platform's bones better than any voice. When they gather for shift changes, the air fills with chemical poetry, conversations that smell of cut grass and hot copper, warnings that taste of bitter almonds.

The children are stranger still. Born in the platform's nursery levels—a series of pods suspended beneath the main deck like fruit—they emerge pre-adapted. Their mothers work through pregnancy, the suits extending protective wombs around their bellies, feeding the developing young the same cocktail of nutrients and industrial byproducts that sustain the garden. These offspring crawl before they walk, navigating the vine networks with an instinct their parents had to learn. Their first words are often not spoken but signed, or expressed in the color-language of bioluminescent bacteria they cultivate in their palms like pets.

The Foundry's veterinarians—if that title still applies—tend to both the fauna and the workers with equal dispassion. They've long since stopped distinguishing between biological and mechanical injury. They set broken bones with grafts of copper pipe, treat infections with industrial solvents that would kill any purely biological system. Their medical bays are gardens of horror and healing: patients suspended in harnesses while medicinal vines are encouraged to grow through their wounds, machinery that monitors vital signs by tasting blood through root networks.

The elder gardener-engineers have evolved beyond easy categorization. Some have worked their entire lives on single tree-machines, their own growth patterns beginning to mirror their charges. They prune themselves as carefully as they shape the metal branches—removing fingers that have grown too rigid, replacing them with tools that sprout directly from the stumps. They speak of their trees in the first person, no longer clear where cultivation ends and identification begins.

The platform's security forces—necessary to protect against raiders who would harvest the valuable bio-metal—have adapted their own strain of symbiosis. They wear armor of cultivated beetle shells, the living plates shifting to deflect both blade and bullet. Their weapons are part grown, part forged: rifle barrels that began as bamboo, bullets of compressed seed pods that bloom into tangles of razor wire on impact. They patrol the edges where the morning glory variants have been trained into early warning systems, vines that constrict when unfamiliar pheromones approach.

But perhaps strangest are the Listeners—workers who have developed, through generations of exposure to the chasm's acoustic phenomena, the ability to hear patterns in the mechanical drone. They claim the Foundry speaks, that the resonance of metal and plant creates a kind of consciousness. They tend the tuning of the entire structure, adjusting tension cables and growth patterns to maintain harmonics they insist are essential to the platform's survival. Their quarters are filled with notation systems that look like musical scores crossed with botanical diagrams, chronicling songs that take years to complete.

The death rites here involve neither burial nor cremation but transformation. Bodies are fed to the Foundry's heart, where the marriage of metal and flesh is most complete. The deceased become part of the next generation of tree-machines, their consciousness—so the workers believe—dispersed through root networks that tap into memories like minerals. The oldest structures contain dozens of generations, their growth rings encoding the history of everyone who has tended them.

New workers arrive occasionally, refugees or specialists drawn by rumors of the Foundry's impossible ecosystem. They either adapt or leave within days—there is no middle ground. Those who stay undergo the changes slowly at first, then with increasing rapidity. Within a year, their lungs have modified to process the thick air. Within five, they can no longer tolerate the pure atmosphere of the world above. Within a decade, they have usually chosen their specialization and begun the deliberate modifications that will make them irreversibly part of the Foundry's living system.

They are no longer entirely human, these Cultivators, but they are not yet something else. They exist in the space between categories, much like the platform itself—suspended between states, growing into something unprecedented. They tend their impossible garden with the dedication of those who know they are creating not just new forms of life, but new ways of being alive.
