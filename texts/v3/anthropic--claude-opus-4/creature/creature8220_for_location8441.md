The Condensers move through these levels like memories of water given form. They have no fixed shape—their bodies shift between states of matter as easily as thoughts change direction. Sometimes they appear as vertical streams of living mercury, sometimes as clouds of vapor dense enough to cast shadows, sometimes as sheets of impossibly thin ice that slide across the wet obsidian without melting.

They are not many. Perhaps a dozen inhabit the entire expanse of terraces, though counting them proves impossible—they divide and merge according to principles that have nothing to do with individuality as organisms understand it. A Condenser might split into a thousand droplets to navigate the geometric channels, each drop carrying the full awareness of the whole, then recombine miles away in configurations that suggest they've exchanged essential parts with others of their kind along the way.

Their metabolism is temperature. They feed on the differential between the burning white sky and the cool depths of the obsidian city, existing in the thermal gradients like fish in currents. Where the bright air meets the wet stone, they grow dense and quick. In the deep chambers where colors sleep in the glass, they slow to near-stillness, spreading into films so thin they become indistinguishable from the perpetual moisture coating every surface.

The Condensers think in phase transitions. Their memories are stored in the patterns of their state changes—liquid to vapor to solid and back again, each transformation encoding experiences in crystalline structures that exist for microseconds before dissolving into new configurations. They communicate through resonance, sending vibrations through the water films that coat the city, creating standing waves in the drainage channels that carry meaning in their interference patterns.

They built nothing here. The city was already ancient when they arrived, drawn by the perfect thermal environment, the endless moisture, the geometric precision that amplifies their resonant communications across vast distances. They are not the builders but the inheritors, perhaps the purpose for which the city was constructed, perhaps merely the latest in an endless succession of inhabitants drawn to this perfect equilibrium.

Time means something different to them. A Condenser might spend a century as a single drop of water in a reservoir, contemplating the mathematics of evaporation, then suddenly explode into action, racing through the channels as superheated steam, experiencing decades of motion compressed into minutes. They have no lifespan in any biological sense—they simply persist, exchanging matter with their environment continuously, maintaining patterns of organization that might be millions of years old or might have formed yesterday.

Their art, if it can be called that, manifests in the water patterns they create. Subtle alterations to the flow, interference patterns in the falling curtains between terraces, standing waves in the plaza pools that persist for exactly calculated durations before dissolving. They compose symphonies in humidity, sculptures in condensation, epic poems in the formation and dissolution of ice crystals too small for any eye to see.

The transparent drifting things at the edge of vision are their scouts—portions of themselves attenuated to near-nothingness, sent out to taste the air, to map the thermal currents, to search for... something. Even they may not remember what. Their oldest members sometimes speak in resonances of a Purpose, a Calling, a Reason that brought them here, but the memory has degraded through too many phase transitions, leaving only the shape of intention without its substance.

They fear dryness above all things. Not death—they have no concept that maps cleanly onto death—but the loss of coherence that comes when there isn't enough water to maintain their patterns. In the deepest chambers, they've found places where the obsidian weeps more freely, where the stone itself seems almost alive with moisture. Here they retreat when the bright air grows too hungry, when the equilibrium threatens to tip toward evaporation.

The Condensers know every channel, every reservoir, every surface of their inherited city. They've mapped its acoustic properties in frequencies no solid ear could hear. They've tasted every variety of mineral that leaches in microscopic quantities from the obsidian. They've counted every drop that falls between the terraces, calculated the exact volume of water in constant circulation through the system.

Yet for all their intimate knowledge, they understand the city no better than its absent builders. They live in it like fish in a reef they had no hand in making, perfectly adapted to its environment but blind to its purpose. They are its ideal inhabitants and yet perhaps not its intended ones—unless the builders somehow foresaw them, designed the city as a perfect trap for exactly such beings, a geometric equation that could only be solved by creatures of phase and resonance.

In the moments when they gather—rare convergences where multiple Condensers flow together in the great plazas—they create something approaching weather. Local storms of meaning, where their combined mass generates its own microclimate, where thoughts precipitate as actual rain, where memories crystallize and fall as snow that sublimates before it can accumulate. These gatherings last hours or days, then disperse as suddenly as they formed, leaving only subtle changes in the water patterns to mark their occurrence.

They dream, perhaps. In the long quiet times when they exist as films on glass, as drops in reservoirs, as vapor in the bright air, something like consciousness persists. Not thought as humans would recognize it, but a kind of geometric meditation, a contemplation of angles and flows and the endless, perfect mathematics of water finding its level. In these dreams they might touch the minds of the builders, might glimpse the purpose of their crystalline city, might understand for a fleeting instant why the stone sweats and the sky burns and the emptiness waits.

But then they change phase again, and the memory scatters like mist in the morning, leaving only the eternal present of flow and form, of temperature and transition, of existence balanced forever on the edge between states of being.
