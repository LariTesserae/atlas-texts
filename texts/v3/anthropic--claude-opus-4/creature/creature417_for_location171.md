The people of Uth-Mara are called the Coral-Weavers in the lowland tongues, though they name themselves simply *those-who-swim-between*. They have dwelt here for seven hundred years, or perhaps seven thousand—their histories dissolve like salt in the perpetual moisture, recorded only in the growth-patterns of the living city itself.

Their bodies have transformed through generations of immersion. Skin like sharkskin, faintly scaled and iridescent, shifts between blue-grey and pearl as they move through different densities of water-air. Their fingers and toes are extensively webbed—not with simple membranes but with sensitive tissue riddled with chemoreceptors that taste the chemical conversations of coral, the health of kelp-trees, the emotional pheromones of their neighbors. When they spread their fingers, it is like opening a fan of tongues.

They are taller than their ancestors, stretched by this gravity-confused environment. Their bones are lighter, honeycombed like those of birds, filled with oil instead of marrow. This gives them neutral buoyancy in the thick atmosphere. Children learn to walk by swimming, pushing off surfaces and controlling their drift, developing an underwater grace that translates to their strange aerial ballet. The elderly, their bones even lighter, must wear weighted belts of cultivated limestone or risk floating away entirely.

Their hair grows in ropy strands like kelp, naturally secreting oils that repel excess moisture. They cultivate it long, braiding in small organisms—cleaning shrimp that feed on dead skin, luminescent bacteria that create halos in the darker districts, pilot fish that have shrunk to minnow size and dart between the strands eating parasites. A person's hair is a tiny ecosystem, a marker of status and care. Lovers groom each other's hair-gardens with specialized combs of whalebone, an intimacy as profound as any kiss.

The Coral-Weavers see poorly in air but excellently in water. Their eyes, huge and dark, are protected by nictitating membranes that constantly wash away the accumulating moisture. They navigate their city more by lateral line than sight—organs along their necks and arms detect the slightest pressure changes, building a three-dimensional map of movement around them. In the deepest pools, they echolocate with clicking sounds produced in specialized throat sacs, their language seamlessly incorporating these sonic pings.

They are simultaneous hermaphrodites, changing their reproductive role based on social dynamics and population needs. During the quarterly spawning seasons, the city fills with clouds of gametes released into the communal pools. Children are raised communally in nursery districts where the coral grows fastest, tended by whoever feels the parental calling that season. Parentage is traced not through blood but through teaching—a master gardener may have a hundred children-of-knowledge, while birthing perhaps only a dozen.

The Weavers eat constantly but lightly, filtering nutrition from their environment like baleen whales. They cultivate bacterial mats on their forearms that process nutrients from the water-air. Their true meals are ritual affairs—fermented kelp bulbs that induce prophectic dreams, air-bladder wine that makes them buoyant enough to float to the city's highest spires, paste made from ground coral polyps that their ancestors believed helped them think like the reef itself. They have no word for hunger, only different flavors of satiation.

Their clothing is minimal—harnesses woven from processed kelp fiber, toolbelts of shark leather, ceremonial cloaks of manta ray skin that billow like wings in the water-currents. But their true adornment is scarification. As children, they receive their first cuts, which are packed with coral spawn. These grow into living ridges of reef across their backs and shoulders, each pattern unique. The coral feeds on their blood, and they on its secretions—a symbiosis that marks them forever as citizens of the suspended gardens. Elders are landscapes unto themselves, their bodies hosting entire miniature reefs.

They do not sleep as humans do. Instead, they enter states of reduced metabolism, hanging neutrally buoyant in rest-pools while half their brain remains active. They dream collectively in these pools, their lateral lines picking up the micro-movements of others' dreams, creating a shared unconscious narrative that influences the next day's work. Architects dream buildings into being before growing them. Gardeners preview the season's growth. Children's dreams are carefully monitored—a child who dreams too deeply might sink and drown, or rise and dissipate into the uncertain heights.

The Coral-Weavers worship through cultivation. Their priest-engineers are distinguished by the complexity of their symbiotic reefs, some supporting dozens of species in careful balance. They believe the city itself is thinking—slowly, on geological timescales—and that they are its quick thoughts, its darting impulses. They speak of the Convergence, when city and citizens will become indistinguishable, when the coral will grow through them so thoroughly that they become architecture, eternal and dreaming.

Death among the Weavers is a gradual calcification. As they age, the coral symbionts slowly convert more and more tissue to limestone. The dying drift to the city's lower levels, where they root themselves in favorite spots and complete their transformation. They remain conscious until the end, their nervous systems converting to networks of bioluminescent channels through which thought travels ever more slowly. The city is built on the bones of ancestors who are not quite dead, not quite asleep, but something other—load-bearing dreamers whose slow thoughts influence the growth patterns of their living tomb.

In the grey-green twilight of their suspended world, the Coral-Weavers dance their careful balance between sea and sky, between plant and animal, between individual and city. They are a people becoming reef, a reef becoming people, suspended forever in their transforming gardens where water rises and nothing is ever quite what it seems.
