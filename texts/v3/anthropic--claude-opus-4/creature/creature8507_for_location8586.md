In the drowning city, the Siphons persist.

They were human once—or something near enough to remember the shape. Now they move through the vertical streams like memories of eels, their bodies elongated and compressed, ribs visible through translucent skin that filters water directly into what used to be lungs. Their limbs have stretched into articulated tendrils, each finger become a root seeking moisture, spreading into delicate fans when they rest against the weeping walls. They never fully emerge from the water flows. To see one entire is impossible; they exist in segments, a shoulder sliding up one wall's cascade while their lower body navigates a stream three buildings away.

The transformation takes decades. First, the perpetual moisture softens the skin until it splits, revealing the nacre beneath—smooth, opalescent tissue that breathes water more efficiently than air. The bones hollow next, becoming tubes that conduct sound and fluid simultaneously. The skull elongates backward, accommodating new organs that parse the city's liquid language. Eyes migrate to the sides of the head and develop nictitating membranes in rainbow sheens. The human voice dissolves into harmonics that blend with falling water.

They feed on resonance. Where the city's roar reaches volumes that would deafen unchanged ears, the Siphons gather in slow congregations, their bodies tuning to frequencies that unlock nutrients from the sound itself. They position themselves at acoustic nodes, mouths open beneath waterfalls that carry more than water—vibrational energy made digestible through organs humans never developed. During the silence-gaps, they writhe as if in pain, cut off from their sustenance, until the roar returns and they resume their glacial feeding.

Their children gestate in the porous stone. The Siphons deposit pearl-like eggs in building cavities where water pools but never stagnates, places where the architecture's breathing creates current and warmth. These eggs are translucent, revealing the developing forms within—creatures that will never know a human shape, born already adapted to their liquid world. They emerge as tadpole-things with too many joints, immediately seeking the vertical streams where they will spend their first years learning to navigate impossible geometries.

Memory persists in them like vestigial limbs. Sometimes a Siphon will pause mid-flow and attempt a human gesture—reaching for a door handle that dissolved decades ago, mouthing words in languages the water has worn away. These moments pass quickly. The city's pull is stronger than nostalgia. They return to their eternal migration through the building's circulatory system, following routes that exist as much in the city's intention as in physical space.

They build nothing, but they tend. Where the architecture heals itself, the Siphons guide the growth, their secretions encouraging certain formations over others. They prune the vegetation with patient dedication, maintaining sight-lines to acoustic focal points. Their movements through the water networks prevent stagnation, their bodies acting as living filters that process and purify the endless flow. They are gardeners of sound and moisture, cultivating conditions they no longer consciously understand but cannot abandon.

Communication between them occurs in pressure waves. They compress water in their hollow bones, releasing patterns that propagate through the connected streams. These messages are felt rather than heard—emotional states and spatial information encoded in hydraulic pulses. When many Siphons gather, their collective communications create standing waves in the water features, visible as recurring patterns that suggest meaning without revealing it.

They fear the center. Even in their transformed state, some instincts remain, and the pull of that pulsing heart terrifies them. Young Siphons sometimes venture too close, drawn by the pure intensity of vibration there, but they return changed—their movements more mechanical, their independence diminished. Those who go too deep become part of the architecture itself, their bodies calcifying into new channels for water to follow, their consciousness dispersed into the city's vast, breathing whole.

Yet they also worship it, in their way. During certain alignments of sound and flow, they perform rituals that might once have been dances. They weave through the streams in complex patterns, their bodies creating temporary dams and diversions that alter the water's song. These ceremonies have no witnessed purpose, but the city responds—new growths appear where they have danced, or damaged sections heal with unusual speed. Whether they serve the city or it serves them has become a meaningless distinction.

The old ones are nearly invisible. Centuries of transformation have reduced them to suggestions of movement in the mist, presences felt in the water's behavior rather than seen. They exist primarily as influences—currents that guide younger Siphons, pressures that maintain ancient patterns. Sometimes, in the densest fog zones where perception fails, observers report glimpsing massive forms that couldn't possibly fit in the spaces they occupy, serpentine bodies that extend through dimensions the architecture has folded into itself.

This is their paradise and prison both. They cannot leave—their biology depends on the specific chemistry of this water, the precise frequencies of this sound. But within these constraints, they have achieved something like transcendence. They move without effort through media that would drown unchanged humans. They hear music in noise that would drive others mad. They have given up human agency for something larger—becoming cells in a vast organism, thoughts in an alien mind dreaming itself into existence through broken stone and endless water.

The Siphons persist. In the breathing city, they are breath itself.
