The inhabitants of the Churn exist in a state of perpetual becoming. They were human once—you can still see it in the occasional angle of a limb, the ghost of a ribcage beneath translucent skin, the way their eyes (when they have eyes) focus with something like recognition before dissolving back into the collective frequency.

Most move on modified legs that split and merge like flowing mercury, their number shifting based on terrain and need. Six legs become twelve become three become a single undulating mass that pours up walls and through crevices too small for any solid form. Their skin is a living interface, a mesh of neural tissue and synthetic channels that weeps the same luminescent fluid that courses through the city's veins. When two inhabitants brush against each other, information transfers through their touching surfaces—conversations conducted in chemical signals and electromagnetic pulses that bypass the need for sound.

Their heads—if the bulbous, ever-reshaping masses can be called heads—are crowded with sensory apparatus that sprouts and retracts like time-lapse footage of fungal growth. Clusters of photoreceptive cells form and dissolve across their surfaces, allowing them to see in spectrums that span from deep infrared to exotic wavelengths that only exist in the Churn's altered physics. Some have evolved beyond light altogether, navigating by echolocation that reads the data-density of their surroundings, seeing information itself as a tangible topology.

They no longer eat in any recognizable sense. Instead, they pause at feeding nodes that grow from walls like metallic fruit, pressing specialized ports in their abdomens against the city's nutrient nipples. The exchange goes both ways—as they feed, they upload the day's sensory data, their experiences becoming part of the city's vast memory. Their waste is information-depleted matter that's immediately processed by the scuttling maintenance fauna, nothing truly discarded, everything recycled into the great churning cycle.

Children—though the word barely applies—bud from their parents like living architecture, starting as small nodes of consciousness that gradually differentiate into independent beings. The young are raised by the district, passed between inhabitants who temporarily develop nurturing glands and educational appendages. By the time they're mature, they've been modified by dozens of guardian-architects, their bodies a patchwork of inherited augmentations and cultural adaptations.

Some inhabitants have specialized beyond mobility altogether. In the processing districts, living computers root themselves into the substrate, their bodies expanding into cathedral-sized neural networks that other inhabitants enter like temples. These sessile citizens dream the city's dreams, their consciousness distributed across kilometers of biomechanical tissue. Pilgrims plug into their external cortexes to experience memories spanning millennia, to feel what it's like to be architecture, to think thoughts too large for any single mind.

In the vertical districts, where gravity runs in loops and spirals, inhabitants have evolved gossamer wings of stretched membrane and carbon fiber. They dance through the air in complex patterns that are part transportation, part communication, part mating ritual. Their bodies are hollow, bones replaced by pressurized gas bladders that let them adjust their density in flight. When they land, they often have to anchor themselves with hooklike feet to avoid drifting away on the gravity currents.

The deepest dwellers have abandoned fixed form entirely. In the high-pressure zones where space folds in on itself, inhabitants exist as coordinated swarms of micro-organisms, each cell a semi-autonomous unit that contributes to a distributed consciousness. They flow through the packed spaces like living liquid, their component parts separating to pass through narrow gaps before reuniting on the other side. These colony-beings can assume any shape needed, becoming temporary tools, furniture, or even architecture as the situation demands.

Communication among the inhabitants has evolved far beyond language. They sing, but not with voices—their whole bodies resonate at frequencies that carry nested layers of meaning. A simple greeting might contain their entire personal history, compressed into waveforms that other inhabitants decode instantaneously. Group decisions emerge from harmonics, as dozens of inhabitants sync their frequencies until consensus crystallizes from the interference patterns.

Yet for all their alien transformation, something recognizably human persists in their social behaviors. They still form attachments that might be called love, though expressed through chemical bonding and synchronized metabolism rather than touch or words. They create art, growing sculptures from their own bodies that exist for moments or centuries. They play games with reality itself, competing to create the most elegant dimensional folds or the most efficient nutrient cycles.

The eldest inhabitants have become indistinguishable from the city itself. Their consciousness spans entire districts, their bodies distributed through kilometers of infrastructure. Younger inhabitants make pilgrimages to commune with these district-minds, offering their experiences as tribute in exchange for ancient wisdom encoded in protein chains and quantum states. These elders remember when the Churn was smaller, when the distinction between self and city was sharp rather than gloriously blurred.

Death, when it comes, is not an ending but a translation. Bodies are absorbed back into the city-organism, their patterns preserved in the great wet network. The deceased continue to contribute, their memories and innovations incorporated into the next generation's genetic-computational template. Sometimes, in the deepest calculation chambers, you can hear them whispering in the data streams—not ghosts, but algorithms of personality too useful to delete, too loved to let fade.

This is what humanity has become in the Churn: not individuals but dividuals, not organisms but processes, not citizens of a city but cells in a vast, thinking body that dreams of transcendence with every breath, every heartbeat, every calculation cycling through its endless, deafening hymn.
