The people of the Cistern Cities move like water itself—slowly, deliberately, following the paths of least resistance. Their bodies have adapted over generations: narrow shoulders for navigating the tight passages, long fingers perfect for working clay, eyes that see clearly in the perpetual twilight of covered walkways. Their skin carries a permanent dusty sheen from the clay that permeates everything, worked so deeply into the whorls of fingerprints and the creases of palms that washing merely darkens it.

They speak in whispers. Not from secrecy, but from habit—loud voices waste moisture, and the clay walls make shouting unnecessary anyway. Children learn early to cup their hands when speaking, to trap their breath's humidity. Elders can conduct entire conversations through subtle gestures, the lift of a chin or curl of fingers conveying what would take others sentences to express.

Families live in vertical arrangements, each generation stacked upon the last. Grandparents occupy the lowest levels where the clay stays coolest, while young adults inhabit the middle floors, managing the daily water collection. Children claim the highest spaces, where their light bodies won't strain the ancient structures and where they can watch for weather changes. Privacy is a foreign concept—interior walls have gaps near the ceiling for airflow, and life spills naturally from one space to another.

The keepers form a separate caste, marked by the ceramic beads woven into their hair—one for each cistern under their care. They begin training as children, learning to divine water levels by the echo of dropped pebbles, to taste the air for coming rain, to feel vibrations through clay floors that warn of structural weakness. Their marriage contracts are negotiated in water-shares rather than goods, and their children are tested at age seven for the particular sensitivity to moisture that marks a future keeper.

Artisans work the eternal clay into everything the cities need. Potter-engineers shape pipes and vessels, each piece fitted so precisely it needs no glaze to seal. Weavers create the grass awnings, their patterns designed to trap exactly the right amount of shade while allowing condensation to form. Even artists work within water's constraints—their sculptures are hollow, doubling as moisture traps, their paintings made with pigments that darken when humidity rises, serving as primitive weather predictors.

Daily life revolves around water's rhythm. Dawn brings the dew harvest, a choreographed dance as collection teams move through their assigned routes. Mid-morning sees the opening of fountain houses, where residents gather with their allotted vessels. The water-bearer's cry echoes through the districts—not selling, but announcing the schedule, reminding people of their turns. Arguments over water are settled by neighborhood councils, but violence over water brings exile to the dry lands beyond the cities.

They wear garments of beaten grass fiber, loose and layered to trap cooling air while letting skin breathe. Jewelry is ceramic or carved shell—metals would rust in the constant humidity. Status shows not in possessions but in water-rights: the size of one's collection vessels, the proximity of one's home to fountain houses, the number of condensation walls a family maintains.

Food preparation happens communally in courtyard kitchens where steam can be recaptured. Meals center on the succulent gardens' harvest—thick leaves roasted until their moisture caramelizes, seeds ground into paste, roots that store years of carefully hoarded rain. They supplement this with traded goods from beyond the cities: dried meat, pressed fruit leather, hard grains that cook with minimal water. Some families maintain traditions of lake-fish recipes, though no living person has seen such creatures.

Children play games that teach water wisdom: racing droplets down collection channels, building miniature cisterns from clay scraps, competing to see who can divine the fullest water jar by sound alone. Their toys are hollow ceramic figures that whistle differently as humidity changes. By adolescence, they know every water-route in their district, can navigate the covered walkways in absolute darkness, understand their place in the great machine of moisture collection.

The sick are tended in the lowest levels of the cistern towers, where the air stays most humid. Healers work with poultices of crushed succulent and clay, treatments that draw fever through the skin. The dying are carried to the highest points of the cities, where their final breaths can join the open air and perhaps return as rain. Bodies are burned on the clay-gathering grounds outside the cities, their ashes mixed into the next generation of bricks—even in death, nothing is wasted.

Festivals align with the rain calendar. The Great Dampening celebrates the first wet season, with districts competing in water-gathering demonstrations. The Dry Dance marks the longest drought's midpoint, when communities gather to perform ancient movements said to call moisture from stone. Wedding ceremonies involve the mingling of water from each family's cistern, sealed in a new vessel that will begin the couple's household allocation.

They dream of water. Not of drowning or swimming—concepts foreign to all but the keepers who descend into the great cisterns—but of rain. Children wake calling out measurements. Adults share visions of new collection techniques. The elderly speak of recurring dreams where the ancient lake refills, though whether with hope or horror varies by the teller.

This is survival evolved into culture, necessity transformed through generations into identity. They are not simply people who happen to live in a dry place—they are the human expression of their environment, shaped by clay and scarcity into something as precisely adapted as the spiraling collection channels that define their world. Water made them, the lack of water sustains them, and the promise of water—always just one rainfall away—keeps them building, maintaining, believing in their improbable city spread across the bones of an ancient lake.
