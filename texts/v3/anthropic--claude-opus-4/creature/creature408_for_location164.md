The people who inhabit these vertical ribbons are themselves caught between states, their bodies having adapted to this world of perpetual condensation and industrial respiration. They call themselves the Sinters—not out of poetry, but because that is what they have become: deposits left by the slow precipitation of the mountain's vapors, calcified yet still dissolving and reforming with each breath of steam.

Their skin carries a faint mineral sheen, like wet limestone, and their movements have a particular quality—not quite flowing, not quite mechanical, but something between the two, as if they're constantly navigating different densities of air. They've developed additional joints in their limbs, subtle but crucial adaptations that allow them to pivot and fold in ways that make vertical travel as natural as horizontal. Watch a Sinter descend a cliff face and you'll see them use handholds with their feet as easily as their hands, their ankles rotating with an almost unsettling flexibility.

They work in generations that overlap like sedimentary layers. A young Sinter might begin their work-life operating the condensation channels, learning to read the mountain's exhalations by taste—each vapor carrying mineral signatures that predict what the stone will do next. Their elders gradually calcify into their roles, their repeated movements wearing grooves not just in the stone but in themselves, until the most senior workers seem to merge with their machinery, their breathing synchronized with the factory's rhythm, their heartbeats marking time with the engines.

The Sinters dress in fabrics that breathe with them—woven from the pale vines that thread through the machinery, treated with secretions from the bat-lemur colonies until the material becomes something between plant fiber and leather, constantly damp but never rotting. These garments grow while being worn, extending new fibers to patch tears, adjusting their weave to match the humidity. A Sinter's clothes record their history—thick growths where old injuries were protected, delicate lacework where the fabric learned to channel sweat away from frequently overheated skin.

They organize themselves in Deposit Families—groups bound not by blood but by shared accumulation. When Sinters work together in the same spaces for long enough, breathing the same vapors, touching the same stones, they begin to carry the same mineral traces in their skin. These deposits create a kind of chemical kinship, a recognition deeper than sight. Members of a Deposit Family can sense each other through the mist by the particular combination of metals and salts they've absorbed.

Their children are born pale and soft, almost translucent, and must be kept in the high nurseries where the mist is thickest until their skin develops its first mineral layer. The young ones play games that would terrify a horizontal-world human—dropping through gaps between platforms, catching themselves on vines, learning to read the density of mist to judge safe landing spaces. By adolescence, they can navigate the city by sound alone, reading the echoes of the bat-lemur calls and the machinery's rhythm to build a sonic map of their vertical world.

The Sinters speak in registers that carry through mist and over the sound of machinery. Their language includes harmonics that resonate with the stone itself—certain words can only be properly pronounced while touching the mountain, letting the rock provide the lower frequencies. They have forty-seven different terms for types of condensation, each describing not just the physical properties of the moisture but its industrial and geological significance.

Food comes from the vertical gardens they tend between the platforms—plots of adapted vegetation that grow in the mineral-rich runoff from the factories. They cultivate the giant ferns not just for their fronds but for their starchy cores, which must be processed through several cycles of steaming and pressing to become edible. The bat-lemur colonies provide eggs—or something analogous to eggs—laid in soft clusters that must be harvested at exactly the right stage of development, when they're firm enough to handle but haven't yet developed their metallic shell.

Their dead are not buried but dissolved. Bodies are placed in special chambers where the mountain's vapors are concentrated, allowing the flesh to break down and the minerals to precipitate out. These crystals are incorporated into the city's structure—pressed into bricks, dissolved into mortar, ensuring that every Sinter eventually becomes part of the vertical world they inhabited. The oldest buildings shimmer with generations of accumulated minerals, their walls containing the essence of thousands of lives.

The Sinters dream collectively when the mist is thickest. On nights when the mountain's exhalations reach their peak, the entire city shares visions—not identical dreams, but overlapping experiences that bleed between sleepers like the moisture that seeps between levels. They wake with shared memories of spaces that don't yet exist, of new platforms budding from the cliff face, of factories that will be built when the stone is ready.

They measure time not in days or years but in Accumulations—the cycles it takes for a new layer of minerals to form on exposed surfaces. A young adult might have lived through twenty Accumulations, while the eldest Sinters remember a hundred or more, their memories stretching back to when the city occupied different stone, different air, when the mountain itself had a different shape.

Some among them undergo the Deep Work—descending to the lowest levels where the mountain's unfinished business is most active. These deep workers return changed, if they return at all. Their skin carries new minerals, unknown in the upper reaches. They speak of spaces where the rock flows like thick liquid, of chambers where the mountain practices making cities before letting them dissolve back into potential. The deep workers are honored and feared in equal measure, necessary for the city's function but no longer entirely human in the way the other Sinters understand humanity.

The Sinters create art that changes with the viewing. They carve the soft stone in patterns that evolve as minerals leach and redeposit, ensuring that no sculpture ever holds its form for more than an Accumulation. Their music incorporates the factory sounds and the bat-lemur calls, compositions that require the city itself as an instrument. They paint with condensation, creating temporary murals on vertical surfaces that last only until the next vapor cycle, each viewing unique as the moisture shifts and flows.

In their governance, they follow the Principle of Deposition—decisions accumulate slowly, discussed and re-discussed until consensus precipitates out like minerals from solution. There are no sudden laws, no abrupt changes in policy. Everything accretes gradually, allowing the community to adapt as seamlessly as they adapt to their ever-changing geography.

The eldest among them, the Fully Deposited, have ceased to move in any observable way. They position themselves at crucial junctions—where bridges meet platforms, where vapor streams converge—and stand in place for Accumulations at a time. The younger Sinters maintain them, ensuring moisture flows around rather than through them, protecting the accumulated wisdom that crusts their forms. These living monuments provide guidance through subtle means—the way their mineralized skin channels runoff, the harmonics that resonate through their crystallized bones when the mountain breathes just right.

Children born during particular atmospheric conditions develop unique talents. Those born when the deep vapors rise carry an affinity for the mountain's geology, able to sense new stone forming before it emerges. Those born during the dry spells that occasionally interrupt the constant moisture become the city's memory-keepers, their minds crystallizing information the way others' bodies crystallize minerals.

The Sinters understand their city as a single vast organism of which they are merely mobile components. They speak of the factories' hunger, the bridges' patience, the platforms' dreams. This is not metaphor to them but lived reality. They feel the city's needs in their bones, respond to its rhythms in their breath. To be a Sinter is to be both individual and infrastructure, person and precipitate, forever cycling between states like the water that defines their vertical world.
