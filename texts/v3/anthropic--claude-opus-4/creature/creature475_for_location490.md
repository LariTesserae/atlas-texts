In the Canopy Sea dwell the Gardeners—though to call them singular beings misunderstands their nature. Each Gardener is a colony, a living braid of what might once have been separate organisms, now fused into something between plant and nervous system.

They move through the water like slow storms. From a distance, you might mistake one for a drifting mass of kelp or a loose school of seeds. Closer observation reveals the truth: hundreds of tendril-bodies woven together, each strand no thicker than a human arm but impossibly long, coiling through and around their companions in patterns that shift like thought made visible. Their skin—if skin it can be called—is translucent green-gold, photosynthetic, with darker veins that pulse with their peculiar metabolism.

The Gardeners have no fixed form. When at rest, they spread themselves thin as fog through the root-hair mazes, individual strands barely visible as they filter-feed on the plant-thick water. But when they move with purpose, they contract and consolidate, their tendrils braiding into temporary limbs, sense organs, manipulation appendages. They might fashion themselves into something like a massive jellyfish to navigate open water, or compress into dense knots to squeeze through tight passages between the trunk-pillars.

Their intelligence is distributed, non-local. Each tendril can act independently, tasting water chemistry, sensing vibrations, making simple decisions. But when they braid together, their thoughts compound. A full Gardener colony in tight configuration might achieve consciousness comparable to—though utterly unlike—a human's. They think in chemical gradients and pressure waves, in the taste of nitrogen and the angle of filtered light.

They are the architects of the air pockets, though their methods remain mysterious. Observers have watched them gather at the boundaries where water meets trapped air, their tendrils weaving complex cat's cradles that somehow convince the water to pull back, to hold its shape against all physics. They tend the inverted gardens within these spaces with infinite patience, training the growth of impossible plants, pruning fractals with delicate precision, adjusting the humidity with their own bodies as living irrigation systems.

The Gardeners communicate through the water itself. They release complex cocktails of chemicals—words in a language of molecules. A conversation between two Gardener colonies might take days, their chemical sentences diffusing slowly through the medium, creating gradients of meaning that other colonies taste and interpret and respond to in turn. The water around an gathering of Gardeners becomes a library, a forum, thick with the taste of discourse.

They reproduce—if that's the right word—by budding and exchanging. When two colonies meet, they might trade tendrils, each accepting strands from the other, incorporating new genetic material and memories into their collective. A single tendril, separated from its colony, can potentially grow into a full Gardener given centuries of time and suitable conditions. But this is rare—the Gardeners seem to prefer their braided existence, finding something like joy in their collective complexity.

Their relationship with the other inhabitants of the Canopy Sea defies easy categorization. They are gardeners in truth, cultivating the plant life, maintaining the delicate balance that keeps this impossible ecosystem stable. But they are also predators of a sort. They have been observed surrounding the ribbon-creatures, those darker streams of organized water, and absorbing them—not violently, but inevitably, like a plant absorbing nutrients. Whether this is feeding or something else entirely remains unknown.

The eldest Gardeners are vast. In the deepest parts of the Canopy Sea, where the light fails entirely and the water grows thick as syrup with accumulated plant matter, ancient colonies have spread themselves through cubic kilometers of space. These deep Gardeners no longer move as units. They have become part of the architecture, their tendrils so interwoven with the root systems that it becomes impossible to say where Gardener ends and tree begins. Perhaps they are the same thing. Perhaps they always were.

Younger Gardeners visit these depths on what might be pilgrimages. They braid themselves into the ancient colonies, sharing chemicals and vibrations in exchanges that last months. When they emerge, they carry something back with them—behavioral changes, new techniques for shaping air pockets, fragments of chemical songs older than any individual memory.

The Gardeners do not build tools or structures as humans understand them. Instead, they shape living things. They coax roots into forms that serve their purposes, train luminescent organisms to create light where they need it, cultivate specialized plants that filter specific chemicals from the water. Their technology is indistinguishable from ecology.

They seem aware of the world beyond the Canopy Sea. During the rain-times at the edges, where water falls upward into sky, Gardeners gather in great numbers. They form complex patterns at the surface, their braided bodies creating symbols or perhaps machinery made of flesh. Some observers believe they are reading the rain, tasting news from distant lands carried in the droplets. Others think they are sending messages, encoding chemical signals that will travel with the ascending water to fall as rain elsewhere.

What the Gardeners want, what drives them, remains opaque. They show curiosity about foreign objects—a metal tool dropped into the Canopy Sea will attract Gardeners who taste it, manipulate it, pass it between colonies like a fascinating toy. But they show no desire to keep such things. They inevitably abandon them to sink into the depths.

They may not experience desire as humans understand it. Their motivations seem to flow from something deeper—an imperative to maintain, to cultivate, to ensure the continuation of the complex patterns they have spent millions of years evolving. They are gardeners in the truest sense: not mere tenders of plants, but shepherds of an entire world's slow transformation into something unprecedented.

Sometimes, in the thick water between sleeping trunk-pillars, you might encounter a lone tendril separated from its colony. It will not flee. It will taste you with chemical senses, perhaps attempt to braid with you as if you were another Gardener strand. In that moment of contact, you might glimpse their experience: the vast patience, the distributed consciousness, the deep contentment of being part of something larger, older, and stranger than any single mind could comprehend.

Then it withdraws, rejoining its colony, leaving you with only the lingering taste of chlorophyll and honey and time.
