The Weavers arrive with the dawn currents, when the water runs coldest and the kelp stands most rigid. They emerge from their sleep-nests in the old bakery walls—hollows lined with soft red algae where the stone ovens once burned—and begin their work before the tide turns.

Mira is always first, her movements through the water so fluid she seems more seal than human. Years of diving have stretched her lungs until she can work for twenty minutes on a single breath. Her hair, long and dark, streams behind her like another strand of kelp. She wears only a woven harness of braided seaweed, tools tucked into loops: a sharpened abalone shell for cutting, a bone awl for threading, copper needles green as the water itself.

She tends the Nursery Columns first—the three youngest kelp pillars growing from the old fountain square. Each must be trained while still pliable, guided with patient hands to spiral correctly, to braid with its neighbors. She runs her palms along the growing tips, feeling for the subtle give that means they're ready to be woven. The work cannot be rushed. Force the kelp and it will die, leaving gaps in the cathedral walls that take years to fill.

The others emerge as the morning brightens. Old Tam with his missing eye, taken by a moray eel in his youth, who reads the kelp's health by touch alone. The twins, Coral and Dune, who work in perfect synchronization, one breathing while the other dives, passing tools between them in an endless rotation. Small Pebble—not a child but a grown woman made tiny by some quirk of birth—who can slip through gaps in the kelp weave to repair damage from within.

They are perhaps thirty souls in all, though the number shifts as travelers arrive from other drowned districts or leave to establish new settlements where the kelp has finally taken hold. They call themselves the Woven, though whether this refers to their craft or their nature is unclear. They speak both in the air pockets—voices echoing strangely off wet stone—and underwater, using a language of hand signs adapted from the gestures of octopi.

Their children grow up amphibious. Little Reed, Mira's daughter, can hold her breath for ten minutes at age seven. She helps tend the sea urchins, moving them from grove to grove with a shepherd's instinct for grazing patterns. The boy Anemone collects tools dropped by working adults, diving down through the kelp columns swift as a cormorant. They play games in the cathedral hall, chasing schools of fish, riding the shells of patient sea turtles who've grown accustomed to human touch.

The eldest among them remember the drowning. Grandmother Wrack, bent now like driftwood, still wears a gold ring from her wedding in the dry world. She rarely speaks of those times, but she teaches the young ones to read the old street signs, now furred with algae, to remember the names of what was. "The water came slowly," she sometimes says, working her ancient fingers through kelp strands, braiding memory into the living walls. "Slow enough to prepare, to choose."

Not all who stayed were human when the waters rose. The Octopus Keeper, they call her, though she has a human name—Dara—that few remember to use. She came to an arrangement with the cephalopods years ago, offering them safe spaces in exchange for their help. She wears their mark: sucker-scars in deliberate patterns along her arms, a sign of partnership rather than attack. When she dives, octopi flow around her like liquid thought, their bodies flashing colors she seems to understand. They show her the deep places where metal treasure lies, help her retrieve tools and materials from dangerous depths.

Then there are the Singers—three women who've learned to vocalize underwater, creating eerie harmonics that carry through the kelp forest. Their songs coordinate the work, warn of predators, call the scattered Woven home at day's end. The sound is nothing like human music above water—it's closer to whale song, all resonance and rhythm. They say the kelp grows faster where they sing, though this might be fancy.

The work never ends. Storm-broken kelp must be rewoven. Sea urchin populations need constant management. New sections of the drowned city, finally ready after decades of slow colonization by algae and barnacles, must be surveyed and claimed. The Woven expand carefully, patiently, knowing that hurrying means death in this slow world.

They trade with the boat people who still live on the surface—kelp rope and dried seaweed, sea urchin roe and octopus ink, in exchange for metal tools and news of the dry world. But fewer boats come each year, and the Woven grow more self-sufficient, learning to make what they need from the sea's abundance.

At night, they gather in the great air pocket beneath the town hall dome. The space smells of salt and kelp, lit by bioluminescent algae they've cultivated in careful patterns. They share food—raw fish, sea grapes, kelp salads dressed with urchin roe—and tell stories. Not the old stories of the dry world, but new ones: how Small Pebble found the copper vein in the collapsed bank vault, how the twins discovered a new species of edible kelp, how Mira's daughter Reed made friends with a young seal who now helps herd fish.

They are changing, generation by generation. The children's eyes are larger, better adapted to the eternal twilight. Their chests are broader, their fingers slightly webbed. They are becoming something new—not fully human, not fully sea creature, but something in between, shaped by the patient hands of necessity and time.

When storms come, the Woven retreat deep into the kelp forest's heart, where the massive columns buffer the surge. They wait in their woven homes—globes of living kelp trained into shelter—breathing carefully hoarded air, listening to the cathedral creak and sway above them. And when the storm passes, they emerge to repair, to replant, to continue the endless work of weaving their city from the sea itself.

In the deepest part of night, when even the nocturnal fish sleep, Mira sometimes swims alone to the top of the cathedral. She surfaces among the kelp fronds, where they break the water's surface under stars. From here, she can see the lights of the boat people in the distance, the dark bulk of mountains that were once hills, the endless ocean that was once her enemy and is now her entire world.

She thinks of her daughter Reed, who has never known dry land, who speaks of "going up to breathe" the way Mira once spoke of "going out for air." This is what adaptation looks like: not a single dramatic change but a thousand small accommodations, each generation fitting more perfectly into the space the world has left them.

Before diving back down, she runs her hands through the kelp fronds one more time, feeling their health, their growth, their patient transformation of disaster into living architecture. Tomorrow she'll teach Reed a new braiding pattern, something complex that will take years to complete. The cathedral is never finished. It grows and changes, dies and regenerates, a breathing monument to the simple truth that life insists on itself, whatever form it must take.
