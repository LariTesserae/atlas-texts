The Tender moves through the Crystalline Forge Gardens with deliberate, measured steps, their form difficult to distinguish from the metallic flora until they shift position. They wear a suit of overlapping scales—each one a salvaged piece of heat-resistant industrial ceramic, carefully shaped and fitted over decades of patient work. The suit clicks and rings softly as they move, harmonizing with the chiming crystal flowers.

Their face, when glimpsed through the suit's narrow viewing slit, shows skin transformed by long exposure to the garden's unique conditions. Patches of it have taken on a metallic sheen, while other areas seem to photosynthesize the ambient radiation, glowing faintly cyan in sympathy with the phosphorescent mosses. Their eyes have developed additional membranes—clear, nictitating shields that flick across their vision to protect against sudden gas vents and electrical arcs.

The Tender's primary tools are their hands, which they've modified over the years. The fingernails have been replaced with precisely shaped industrial garnets that can channel electromagnetic fields, allowing them to "speak" to the metal plants through careful manipulation of local field densities. When they touch a copper fern, the frond responds by curling toward or away, depending on the polarity they project.

They've learned to breathe in the garden's atmosphere through a symbiotic relationship with certain thermophilic bacteria that colonize their modified lung tissue. These organisms process the sulfur and metallic vapors, extracting what nutrients they can while neutralizing the worst toxins. The Tender's exhalations emerge as thin wisps of processed gas that the garden's plants find particularly nourishing—a closed loop of mutual sustenance.

Their daily work involves patterns incomprehensible to outside observers. They'll spend hours adjusting the angle of a single mercury-drop vine, then rush across the garden to realign a cluster of needle-grass before some threshold is reached. They read the garden's health in the harmonics of the chiming flowers, the color temperature of the overhead aurora, the rhythm of the pressure ventings. To them, the garden is a single vast instrument that must be kept in tune.

The Tender rarely sleeps in any conventional sense. Instead, they enter periodic states of deep communion with the garden's electromagnetic fields, standing perfectly still near the ancient turbine while their modified nervous system synchronizes with the degraded machine's cycles. During these periods, their ceramic scales develop frost-like patterns of precipitated minerals from the air, which they'll later carefully harvest and feed to young crystalline seedlings.

They communicate with the garden through touch, through electromagnetic projection, through careful placement of conductive materials to redirect the flow of energy. But also through sound—they've learned to vocalize in frequencies that resonate with the industrial components, creating subsonic hymns that vibrate through the volcanic glass ground and into the root systems of the metal plants.

In the deepest tangles of undergrowth, they maintain small caches—not of food or tools, but of components. Fragments of rare metals, unusual crystals formed in the vents, pieces of the ancient machines that occasionally surface from below. They use these to facilitate the garden's slow evolution, introducing new elements carefully, watching for generations of plant growth to see how each change propagates through the ecosystem.

The Tender is no longer entirely human, if they ever were. They've become a bridge organism, a translator between the industrial and the botanical, the mechanical and the biological. Their blood carries traces of the same metals that flow through the plant sap. Their bones have been reinforced by mineral deposits from the garden's atmosphere. They are becoming, slowly, another hybrid entity in this impossible space—part gardener, part component, part evolutionary catalyst for a form of life that exists nowhere else.

When the ancient machines stir in their broken dreams, the Tender stirs with them. When the pressure builds in the underground chambers, they feel it in their modified bones. They are the garden's memory and its vision, recording in their transformed flesh the story of how industrial waste became a new form of paradise, terrible and beautiful in equal measure.
