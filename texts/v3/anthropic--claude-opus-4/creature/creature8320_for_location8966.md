In the Steaming Quarter, you'll find the Brass Shepherds first—or rather, they'll find you.

They move through the perpetual mist on stilts cobbled together from old factory pistons, their legs extending and retracting with pneumatic sighs that blend seamlessly with the ambient steam-hiss. Each shepherd tends a flock of copper-fleece moths, creatures that have evolved to feed on the metallic oxides that bloom across every exposed surface. The moths cluster on their shepherds' broad-brimmed hats and shoulders like living cloaks, wings creating a constant whisper of metal-on-metal as delicate as wind chimes.

The shepherds' faces are always partially obscured—by steam, by the wide brims of their fungus-leather hats, by the breathing masks they've fashioned from old diving equipment and tea strainers. They've learned to read the quarter's moods through vibrations in their stilts, feeling when a steam geyser is about to erupt or when the ancient turbines are about to shift their rhythm. They communicate with each other across the fog using a language of valve-whistles, turning wheels mounted on their chest harnesses to create falling notes that carry farther than voice ever could.

Deeper in, where the old foundries pool heat like cupped hands, the Smelter Witches have made their homes. They live in the bellies of defunct furnaces, their living spaces lined with heat-loving orchids that glow faintly in colors that shouldn't exist. The witches harvest the quarter's warmth in bottles and bladders, distilling the mineral-rich steam into tinctures that cure ailments unknown to the upper city's doctors. Their hair grows in metallic sheens—copper, brass, verdigris green—a side effect of breathing the quarter's alchemy for so long. They wear aprons of chain mail scavenged from old safety equipment, now decorated with bells that ring in frequencies only the steam mice can properly hear.

The witches know every ghost in every machine. They leave offerings of oil and flowers at the bases of the randomly firing steam hammers, treating them like old gods that must be appeased. In return, the machines sometimes pound out rhythms that tell the future, or compress flower petals into diamonds of pure scent that the witches sell to the brave merchants who descend from above.

In the canal networks live the Gondoliers of the Thermal Waters. Their boats are grown rather than built—cultivated from a species of giant water lily that learned to metabolize brass, producing boats with living hulls that heal their own damage and navigate by instinct toward the warmest currents. The gondoliers themselves are amphibious by necessity if not nature, their lungs adapted to extract oxygen from both air and steam. They wear seal-skin suits that they claim are gifts from the canal selkies—though no one from the upper city has ever seen these creatures.

The gondoliers ferry goods and messages through the quarter's water roads, poling their leaf-boats with staffs topped with lucent fungi that serve as living lanterns. They sing to the herons to request passage through their fishing grounds, songs that bubble up through the water like escaping air. At their belt, each carries a vial of pure cold water from the world above—their only way home if the quarter's warmth finally claims them completely.

The youngest inhabitants are the Pipe Runners—children who've discovered that the steam mice will accept them as part of their colonies if they're small and quick enough. These kids navigate the quarter through the pipe networks, following routes known only to rodents and lost blueprints. They wear capes of steam mouse fur (shed naturally and gifted, never taken) that allow them to see heat signatures in the dark. Their playground is the three-dimensional maze of tubes and valves, and they've learned to surf the pressure waves when the big turbines cycle, riding blasts of steam from one side of the quarter to the other in minutes.

The runners serve as messengers and scouts, but also as the quarter's memory keepers. In their secret nests high in the pipe networks, they maintain libraries of brass-rubbing prints taken from every functioning machine, documenting which gears still turn and which have seized, creating an ever-updating map of the quarter's mechanical anatomy.

Above them all, in the canopy bridges and converted water towers, live the Botanist-Engineers. They're the ones who maintain the delicate balance, ensuring the plants don't completely consume the machines and the machines don't boil away all life. They wear leather coats studded with thermometers and barometers, looking like weathermen from an alternate century. Their greenhouses perch precariously on factory roofs, filled with hybrid species that feed on steam and excrete pure water, that grow gears instead of seeds, that bloom only when the pressure drops before a storm.

The Botanist-Engineers speak of the quarter as a single living organism, with the machines as its bones, the water as its blood, the plants as its flesh, and all the inhabitants as its many dreaming minds. They say that sometimes, in the perfect confluence of steam and sound and bioluminescent light, the quarter briefly awakens and remembers what it was meant to become before the city above abandoned it to its own devices.

All these inhabitants share the quarter with others—the rumored but never confirmed steam selkies, the certainly real but rarely glimpsed Iron Hermits who've replaced so much of themselves with machinery that they clank when they walk, the colonies of pigeons whose feathers shed spark-producing oils. But these are the ones you'll meet, the ones who'll offer you moth-silk scarves against the damp, who'll warn you away from unstable streets, who'll share their fungus tea and tell you stories of the time before the springs broke through, when this was just another industrial district, before it became something altogether more alive.
