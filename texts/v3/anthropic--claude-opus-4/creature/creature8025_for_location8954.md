**The Dwellers of the Drowning Works**

The Pipewalkers came first, or perhaps they were always here—it's impossible to say when maintenance crew became something else. They move through the mechanical marsh with a fluidity that defies their appearance: humanoid shapes wrapped in what might be rubber suits or might be skin that has learned to repel water like seal hide. Their faces hide behind masks that could be respirators or could be the calcified remains of brass filters that have fused with flesh. They tend to the machinery with movements both purposeful and incomprehensible, adjusting valves that control nothing, oiling gears that grind regardless, their work a ritual whose meaning has been lost to the constant din.

They communicate through vibrations—placing palms against pipes to send thrumming messages through the metal nervous system of the Works. When they gather, which is rare, they stand in circles around the largest pump housings, hands pressed to the metal, their bodies swaying in rhythm with the mechanical heartbeat. Whether they're performing maintenance or prayer is a distinction that no longer matters here.

The Pipewalkers share their territory uneasily with the Collectors—children, or child-sized things, that swarm through the upper reaches where the ferns grow thickest. They wear coats stitched from the wings of copper moths, creating a scales-like armor that whispers metallically as they move. Their fingers have grown long and delicate, perfect for plucking the glass tubes and brass fittings that fruit from certain hybrid plants—flora that has incorporated the Works' detritus into their very biology. 

The Collectors' eyes have adapted to pierce the constant mist, grown large and multifaceted like insects, each facet reflecting a different spectrum of the strange light that filters through steam and vegetation. They harvest their technical fruit in woven baskets that drip constantly, the sound adding another layer to the Works' symphony. What they do with their gleaning, no one knows—they vanish into the deepest tangles when their baskets are full, following paths only they can see through the mechanical undergrowth.

But the true power in the Drowning Works belongs to the Gauge Readers—solitary figures who have claimed the ruined control rooms as their domains. Each has merged with their chosen space in unique ways: one's hair has become indistinguishable from the root systems that snake through her chamber, allowing her to feel every tremor in the building's structure. Another's skin has developed a patina that matches the corroded brass around him, making him nearly invisible when he stands still among the pipes.

The Gauge Readers divine meaning from the twitching needles and fluctuating pressures, interpreting the Works' mechanical dreams. They speak prophecies that sound like steam leaks and maintenance codes, their words bubbling up through water-filled throats. The other inhabitants bring them offerings—choice beetles, peculiar moss formations, the shed skins of pneumatic eels—in exchange for readings of the deep currents, the patterns that determine where the next floor section will rise or sink, where the newest springs will burst through.

Between all these, in the spaces too small for even the Collectors, live the Rust Farmers. These are perhaps the most changed—their bodies have become ecosystems unto themselves. Colonies of the iron-eating beetles nest in their joints, and their blood has thickened to a ferrous soup that sustains both them and their symbiotic swarms. They cultivate patches of metal-digesting bacteria in the dampest corners, tending their corrosion gardens with the patience of those who measure time in oxidation rates.

The Rust Farmers move slowly, deliberately, leaving trails of orange and red wherever they pass. Their cultivation creates the soil that feeds the ferns, their work essential to the strange ecology of the Works. When they sleep—if they sleep—they curl into the largest pipe junctions, their beetle colonies spreading out in protective circles, the insects' movement creating a lullaby of chittering that somehow harmonizes with the greater mechanical song.

There are others, glimpsed only in peripheral vision: the Echo Fishers who cast lines of woven wire into the deepest sumps, pulling up things that might be fish or might be memories crystallized in the mineral-rich water. The Pressure Dancers who surf the pneumatic bursts, their bodies compressed and elongated by turns as they ride the waves of force through the system. The Whistle Speakers who have learned to voice the steam vents, creating conversations that span the entire Works in a language of hisses and shrieks.

All of them, in their way, have become part of the mechanism—not replacing it or conquering it, but joining the eternal cycle of breakdown and renewal that defines this place. They are neither fully human nor fully other, but something new, shaped by the constant presence of water and metal, growth and decay, sound and silence-that-is-never-silence.

In the Drowning Works, everyone is always becoming something else, and the transformation never ends—it only cycles, like water through pipes, like rust through iron, like life through the spaces between what was built and what grows.
