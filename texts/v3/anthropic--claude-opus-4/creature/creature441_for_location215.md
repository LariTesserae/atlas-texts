In the spaces between the layered sheets of growth, where the translucent vegetation creates countless narrow galleries of jade-lit air, something moves with purpose that the arthropods lack. The Cultivators—though this is merely what they might be called, for they have no need of names—flow through these vertical meadows like living mercury given consciousness.

Their bodies are exercises in efficient transparency: elongated forms perhaps twice the length of a human but compressed to fit through gaps no wider than a hand span. Their flesh (if it can be called that) has the consistency of dense gel, shot through with branching networks of darker material that might be neurons, might be digestive tract, might be both. When still, they are nearly invisible against the glowing walls. When moving, they ripple like heat mirages given substance.

They tend the growth with movements that speak of ancient practice. A touch here causes a section to fruit—small, luminous bodies that swell from the vegetable sheets like blisters of light. A careful pressure there redirects the flow of nutrients, encouraging denser growth in one area while thinning another. They work in absolute silence, communicating (if at all) through chemical secretions that slightly alter the color of the growth around them, creating brief halos of deeper green or paler gold that fade as quickly as they appear.

The Cultivators have no obvious sensory organs—no eyes in this eternally lit realm, no ears in this soundless garden. Instead, their entire surface seems to taste the chemical gradients in the air, feel the subtle temperature variations where growth is thriving or failing. They navigate by reading the health of their charge, drawn to areas of stress or abundance with unerring accuracy.

Their relationship with the vegetation defies easy categorization. They are not parasites—the growth flourishes under their care. Nor are they purely symbiotic, as they seem to need nothing the vegetation provides beyond purpose itself. Watching them work (if one could remain still enough, quiet enough to observe) is to witness something between agriculture and worship, between maintenance and meditation.

They shape the growth but are also shaped by it. In the tightest passages, they compress to almost painful thinness. In the vast cathedral chambers, they stretch out, becoming attenuated to the point of near-dissolution before drawing themselves back together. Their bodies carry the memory of every space they've moved through, constantly adapting to the architecture they themselves help create.

Occasionally, several will converge on a single location—perhaps drawn by some imperceptible signal, some note in the chemical symphony only they can detect. They will work in perfect coordination, their bodies briefly touching and merging at the edges before separating again, leaving behind some subtle modification to the growth pattern that won't become apparent for cycles to come.

They are the authors of those intentional modifications scattered throughout the chambers—the spiral stones pressed into walls with patient, boneless strength, the channels carved by bodies made rigid through some temporary calcification process. Each modification serves the growth, improves the circulation of nutrients or the distribution of light, but also serves some aesthetic principle comprehensible only to beings that perceive the world through chemistry and pressure.

In the pools, they rarely venture, though sometimes one will extend a pseudopod into the water to test some quality invisible to any other sense. The larger creatures that disturb these surfaces give the Cultivators wide berth—not from fear but from respect, or perhaps simply from an understanding that to interfere with the gardeners is to interfere with the garden itself.

They do not age, or if they do, it happens on a timescale that matches the growth they tend. They do not reproduce in any observable way, though sometimes a Cultivator will divide its attention so thoroughly between distant parts of the chamber system that it seems to exist in multiple places at once, until eventually it is multiple, each portion having forgotten it was ever part of a whole.

Are they the original gardeners, evolved into something unrecognizable? Are they tools that achieved consciousness? Are they thoughts the vegetation itself grew to tend its own expansion? The questions hang in the thick air, unanswered and perhaps unanswerable.

They continue their work without rest, without pause, maintaining the endless moment of becoming that defines their world. In their transparent bodies, the light of the growth they tend reflects and refracts, making them into living prisms that cast no shadows in their shadowless realm.
