The Underkin work by touch as much as sight. Their fingers read the stone like scripture, finding flaws and grain-lines that eyes would miss. Generations of handling tools have broadened their palms and thickened their fingertips into living instruments. When an elder runs her hand along a wall, she knows which grandfather carved it by the angle of the chisel marks alone.

They are a small people—not stunted but compact, with the efficient build of those who navigate narrow spaces. Their shoulders slope inward, their hips are narrow, their limbs corded with the particular muscles that develop from constant climbing and crawling. Children learn to move through the passages before they learn to walk upright, developing a scuttling gait that serves them better than any normal stride. Even adults drop to all fours when speed matters more than dignity.

Their skin tells stories. The constant humidity and mineral-rich water has given them a peculiar sheen, somewhere between copper and clay. Scars web across their bodies—not from violence but from the intimate negotiations with stone. A slip while carving, a misjudged crawl through a tight passage, the bite of an irritated beetle. They wear these marks proudly, reading each other's histories in damaged skin. The most accomplished stone-workers bear the most intricate scarification, their injuries becoming a kind of credentials.

But it's their eyes that mark them as something evolved. Huge pupils dominate faces that have gradually flattened to accommodate them. They dilate and contract with disturbing independence, one tracking movement while the other studies detail. In the deepest chambers, their eyes catch and amplify the faintest fungal glow, creating an unsettling effect—dozens of paired lights floating in darkness like constellation maps. When they surface, they must bind cloth across their faces, filtering sunlight down to tolerable levels.

They dress in layers of practicality. Against their skin, the silk of cavern spiders, harvested carefully without killing the builders. Over this, leather from the pale lizards, brain-tanned to buttery softness. The outer layer changes with the work—thick hide aprons for stone-cutting, woven grass cloaks for tending the fungi gardens, nothing at all when swimming through the flooded lower passages. Every garment bears loops and pockets, bristling with tools. A person's profession can be read in what hangs from their belt: water-readers carry hollow reeds and weighted strings, stone-singers bear tuning picks of different metals, the beast-whisperers smell of musk glands and carry bone whistles.

Their hair grows in unusual patterns, thick on the crown but sparse elsewhere, adapted to protect the skull while crawling. They cultivate it into elaborate topknots, worked with clay and beetle wax into shapes that indicate family, profession, and chamber of residence. Children earn their first braid when they complete their first solo passage-mapping. Elders' hair grows white as cave fish, twisted into architectural marvels that mirror the passages they've spent lifetimes carving.

The social structure mirrors the warren itself—interconnected, multi-leveled, with authority flowing through channels as complex as the water systems. No single leader commands, but rather a fluid council of expertise. When new passages need planning, the stone-singers lead. During flooding seasons, the water-readers take precedence. The beast-whisperers manage the delicate treaties with the larger creatures that share the depths. Children apprentice not to individuals but to the warren itself, spending seasons with each guild before finding their calling.

They communicate in layers. Speech bounces strangely in the carved chambers, so they've developed a language that uses echo and resonance as much as words. A phrase spoken in one chamber means something different when the echo returns from another. They click their tongues against their teeth to navigate in absolute darkness, each person's click as distinctive as a voice. For silence, they use a touch-language on each other's backs, whole conversations conducted through fingertips on spine. The water-readers have their own code, transmitting messages through the plumbing by blocking and releasing flows in patterns.

Their relationship with the beetle colonies approaches reverence. Certain bloodlines have tended the same insect lineages for centuries, selectively breeding for size, shell pattern, and temperament. The beetles serve as minor livestock—their larvae provide protein, their shells become tools, their waste enriches the fungal gardens. But more than that, they're partners in maintenance. Trained beetles clean mineral deposits from water channels, their mandibles perfect for the task. Others patrol for dangerous molds or weakened stone. The largest, dog-sized specimens carry messages between chambers, following scent roads their handlers lay down.

Music threads through their lives, but it's not music as the surface knows. They've learned to play the warren itself—striking resonant points to send tones through the stone, opening and closing passages to create wind-songs, using the water basins as percussive instruments. During the evening chorus, the entire complex becomes a vast instrument, each family adding their voice to harmonies that help map the space acoustically. Children learn their location by the sound it makes.

Death, when it comes, returns them to the stone. Bodies are placed in specially carved niches where the beetles and beneficial fungi transform flesh back to the minerals that strengthen the warren. Bones are removed after a season, carved into tools that carry the ancestor's touch into future work. The skull, cleaned and polished, joins the ossuary chamber where hundreds of empty sockets watch over the deepest water source. They believe consciousness lingers in the bone, that ancestors guide hands when the work grows difficult.

They dream collectively. In the sleeping chambers, bodies pack together like roots in soil, sharing warmth and moisture. The close air and fungal spores create mild hallucinations that blur the boundaries between minds. Children learn history through these communal dreams, experiencing ancestral memories of the warren's first cutting. What one person discovers in the depths becomes part of the collective understanding by morning. Privacy exists only in motion—solitude found in the journey between chambers rather than in any destination.

The Underkin have evolved with their environment, shaped by it as surely as they shape it. They are not primitive but precisely adapted, their bodies and culture fitted to their carved world like water to its channel. When they must venture to the surface for trade or materials, they move like deep-sea creatures pulled into alien air—functional but clearly out of place. Their home is the darkness, the closeness, the constant sound of dripping water and scuttling life.

In the deepest chambers, where even their adapted eyes struggle, the eldest of them undergo a final transformation. Their skin develops a faint luminescence, fed by decades of fungal spore exposure. These glowing elders become living landmarks, their light guiding the warren's expansion into virgin stone. They no longer speak, communicating only through touch and the patterned dimming and brightening of their skin. Some say they're becoming part of the warren itself, their consciousness spreading through the carved stone like water through its channels.

This is who inherits the earth's depths—not conquerors but collaborators, not builders but shapers, working with stone and water and crawling life to create something that could exist nowhere else. The warren breathes through a thousand mouths, and they are its voice, its hands, its beating heart.
