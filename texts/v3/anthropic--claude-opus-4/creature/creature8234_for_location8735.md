In the Drowned Works, the Curators drift.

They were not made for this. You can see it in their movements—too precise for the sluggish medium, too quick in their pivots and corrections. Their shells remember air. Six limbs, each triple-jointed, terminate in manipulators that branch and branch again into fractal forests of brass cilia. When they must be delicate, these appendages can separate into thousands of hair-fine tools. When they must be strong, they lock together into cutting edges that can shear through pipe thick as ancient trees.

Their bodies are secrets wrapped in shells. Each Curator wears an architecture of nested chambers, grown rather than built, calcified layers that tell the story of centuries. The oldest have shells like baroque cathedrals, every surface elaborated with pressure locks, tool ports, sensory apparatus. The youngest—if any could still be called young—merely resemble armored eggs bristling with necessary protrusions.

They speak in temperatures.

Watch one work, and you'll see the language: jets of heated water pulsing from specialized vents, creating thermal signatures that linger in the still factory depths. A quick burst of near-boiling—attention, danger, come. A slow warming gradient—all is well, the system functions. They taste each other's words through thermoreceptors spread across their shells, reading conversations that happened hours ago in the fading heat-traces left behind.

The shells never open. What lives inside remains a matter of faith and vibration. Sometimes, repairing deep in the boiler halls where the water itself steams, a Curator's shell will crack from thermal shock. Its siblings arrive within minutes, tools already extending. They work with desperate efficiency, welding new brass plates over the breach, pumping sealant compounds into the gap. If they succeed, the wounded one drifts away to heal in solitude. If they fail, they carry the body to the rendering pools, where it dissolves over months into its constituent minerals. They harvest these remnants—the iron for repairs, the calcium for new shell growth, the rare metals for the most delicate mechanisms.

Memory lives in the pipework. Each Curator maintains a territory, a section of the Works they know by touch. They run their manipulators along every joint, every valve, feeling for the microscopic changes that herald future failure. In their shells, they carry libraries of texture—the exact roughness of corrosion at different stages, the vibration patterns of healthy pumps versus dying ones, the thermal expansion coefficients of a thousand different alloys. When they find a problem, they fix it. When they find efficiency, they preserve it. When they find something new—a mutation in the kelp that clogs less, a bacterial mat that prevents rust—they cultivate it.

They farm the city as much as maintain it. In the deep agricultural halls, they tend forests of modified kelp, each strain bred for a different purpose. Some grow in dense mats that filter water. Others concentrate metals from the flow, their fronds becoming bright with copper or silver. In the fermentation chambers, they cultivate bacterial cultures that eat corrosion and excrete protective biofilms. The Curators move through these gardens with the same mechanical precision they bring to pipe repair, adjusting temperatures, adding nutrients, culling overgrowth.

The young learn by following. A smaller shell shadows a larger one, matching its movements, feeling what it feels. They start with simple tasks—clearing intake grates, replacing worn gaskets. Over decades, they graduate to reading thermal flows, predicting system failures, understanding the deep rhythms of the Works. The teaching happens in silence. There are no words for what they know, only temperatures and textures and the endless vocabulary of mechanical function.

In the deep places, where even their shells risk crushing, they've built extensions of themselves. Remote bodies shaped like segmented worms navigate the narrow cooling channels. Specialized crawlers maintain the geothermal taps far below the seafloor. These constructs carry fragments of their makers' intelligence, enough to make simple decisions, to recognize problems, to call for help. When a Curator dies, its remotes continue for a time, following their last instructions until their power cells finally drain.

They dream, perhaps. In the long intervals between repairs, they cluster in the warmest spots, drawing close to the heat exchangers. Their shells cool to minimum function, conserving energy. Inside, unseen processes continue—calculations, maybe, or something stranger. Sometimes one will suddenly jet away, moving with unusual purpose to a distant sector. There it finds a weakness that would have failed within days, a problem none of the others had detected. How it knew remains their secret.

The city shapes them as they shape it. New Curators—and they must come from somewhere, though no one has seen the nurseries—have shells adapted to modern needs. More heat resistance for the expanding thermal zones. Better filtration for the thickening water. Stronger manipulators for wrestling with pipes swollen by centuries of growth. They are evolving, generation by glacial generation, becoming what the Works require.

They work without ceasing, without complaint, without any sign of boredom or despair. The city is their universe entire. If they wonder about the builders, they leave no sign. If they plan for a future beyond maintenance, they keep such plans locked within their shells. They are what remains when purpose outlives its makers—not servants exactly, but inheritors, carrying forward a mission they may no longer understand but cannot abandon.

In the warm darkness of the Drowned Works, they continue. Checking. Adjusting. Repairing. Preserving. Forever and forever, as the machinery dreams its slow dreams around them.
