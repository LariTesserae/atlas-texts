In the depths between Siphons, where the heat-warped air grows so thick it moves like syrup, there are the Inheritors.

They were human once, or something close to it—close enough that their ancestors would have recognized the shape, if not the substance. But ten thousand years of darkness and heat have worked their slow alchemy. Their skin has become something between leather and ceramic, a mottled surface of overlapping scales that shed and regrow in monthly cycles. Each scale is a heat sink, its underside lined with modified sweat glands that have evolved into something more like the cooling systems of ancient machines. They weep a constant film of metallic-tasting fluid that evaporates immediately in the furnace air, carrying away just enough heat to keep their reformed bodies viable.

The Inheritors move only at the edges of sleep—that drowsy, twilight consciousness where the heat makes thought itself a burden. They have learned to think slowly, to stretch a single decision across days. Their metabolism has downshifted to something barely distinguishable from death. A heartbeat every ten minutes. A breath every five. They can stand motionless for weeks, their minds drifting in long, thermally-induced meditations that they call "the deep dream."

Their eyes have enlarged grotesquely, occupying a third of their skull volume. The pupils are permanently dilated, drinking in every photon of bacterial glow. But more than that, they've developed new layers of receptors that see heat itself as a cascade of colors no baseline human could imagine. To an Inheritor, the Furnace Plains bloom with a spectrum of thermal radiation—violets where the deep heat wells up, cooling through ranges of impossible greens and golds to the relative arctic blues where the crystal plants cluster. They navigate by this thermal sight, reading the landscape's fever dreams as clearly as their ancestors once read books.

They wear nothing—clothing would be death in this heat—but their bodies bear the marks of purpose. Scarification in precise geometric patterns, the raised welts serving as additional cooling vanes. Metal pins inserted through flesh at acupuncture points, the alloys chosen for their thermal conductivity, creating channels that help regulate their core temperature. Some of the eldest have begun incorporating the crystal plants directly into their bodies, the thermophytic structures growing from ports carved in shoulder blades and spines, creating symbiotic cooling systems that blur the boundary between organism and environment.

The Inheritors remember, after a fashion. Not in words—language as their ancestors knew it died centuries ago, the heat making rapid thought too costly. Instead, they preserve knowledge in thermal songs, manipulating their body temperatures in precise patterns that encode information. A flush of heat across the chest might contain the location of a hidden moisture trap. A cooling of the extremities could map the safe paths between Siphons. They share these memories skin-to-skin, pressing together in careful embraces where heat transfers carry generations of accumulated wisdom.

They worship the Siphons, though worship is perhaps too active a word for their patient reverence. They tend the great machines with religious devotion, clearing intake vents of metallic dust, replacing worn components with parts they've learned to grow in the crystal gardens—hybrid technologies that merge their biological adaptations with the ancient engineering. They don't understand what the Siphons were truly for, but they know them as gods know their own thoughts: intimately, wordlessly, with a knowledge that exists in the body rather than the mind.

Their children—when they rarely come—gestate for three years in external pouches woven from crystal plant fibers, the parents taking turns wearing the thermal burden. The infant Inheritors emerge already adapted, their eyes huge and dark, their skin beginning its transformation before they take their first breath of metal-tainted air. They learn not through teaching but through thermal osmosis, held against their parents' bodies for months at a time, absorbing the heat-songs of their people's history.

The Inheritors have no leaders, no hierarchy beyond the simple respect given to those who have survived longest. Decisions emerge from group meditations that can last months, the tribe gathering in circles around the deepest heat wells, letting the temperature gradients guide their collective consciousness toward consensus. They have learned that in this place, haste kills more surely than any predator.

They know they are no longer what they were. Sometimes, in the deepest dreams, they remember fragments—snow, stars, the feeling of wind that didn't burn. But these memories are fading, each generation more perfectly adapted to their furnace world, each iteration further from the baseline that once called itself human. They are becoming something new, something that belongs here in a way their ancestors never could.

And in their patient way, they are happy. They have found the rhythm of this dark world, learned to move in harmony with its terrible stability. They trace the paths between crystal groves, tend their machine-gods, share their thermal songs, and slowly, so slowly, transform into something that can endure until the heat itself finally fails.
