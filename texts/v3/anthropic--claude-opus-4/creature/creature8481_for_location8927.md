The Chorus Weavers make their homes here, a people whose bodies have evolved alongside the cacophony. They are tall and impossibly slender, with limbs that extend like vines themselves—not metaphorically, but literally, as their skin hosts a symbiotic algae that photosynthesizes in the filtered sunlight. This green tinge to their flesh deepens with age, the eldest among them appearing almost bark-like where the algae has formed protective crusts at joints and along the spine.

Their ears have adapted most dramatically. What were once simple human ears have become complex, chambered organs that can close and compartmentalize, filtering specific frequencies from the overwhelming noise. The outer ear is ridged like a mushroom gill, each fold capable of independent movement. When a Chorus Weaver wishes to listen to a particular layer of sound—say, the specific pitch of water drops that indicates a message from three terraces above—they adjust these ridges like tuning an instrument.

The children are born pale, almost translucent, their symbiotic algae taking years to establish. During these vulnerable early years, they live in the deepest sound-dampening chambers, their developing ear structures wrapped in silk scarves woven from moth cocoons. They learn to speak first in signs—a fluid language of gestures that can be read through walls of living wood, where hands move like shadows against the green light filtering through leaves. Their first words are not spoken but drummed, simple patterns beaten on the resonant logs that serve as the settlement's distance-communication system.

But it is in adolescence that the Chorus Weavers truly become themselves. As their ear structures mature and their algae colonies establish, they begin their first major weaving. Each person creates their own living chamber by training vines and branches over months or years, singing to the plants in subsonic frequencies that encourage growth in specific directions. The plants respond to these vibrations, growing denser where the sound is strongest, leaving gaps where silence reigns. No two chambers are alike—each is a physical manifestation of its creator's personal sound signature.

The adults move through their vertical city with an insect-like grace, their extended limbs finding purchase on the smallest protrusions. Their fingers and toes are elongated, with grip pads that secrete a mild adhesive—not unlike the tree frogs they share their terraces with. They wear minimal clothing, usually just woven vegetation that serves more as tool carriers than modesty, holding the various implements of their trade: tuning forks carved from resonant wood, collection vials for the acoustic beetles they harvest, and the intricate sound-maps they create.

These sound-maps are perhaps their greatest achievement. Woven from spider silk and stretched across frames of bent wood, they physically represent the acoustic landscape of the terraces. Different colors of silk denote different frequencies, thickness indicates volume, and the overall pattern shows how sounds flow and interfere across the vertical space. Masters can read these maps like sheet music, predicting where new chambers should be grown to best dampen or amplify certain frequencies.

The eldest among them, those who have lived past their hundredth year, begin to take on even more plant-like characteristics. Their algae grows so thick it forms bark-like plates. Their movements slow but become impossibly precise. Some elders choose to root—literally—spending their final decades in meditation as their feet extend tendril-like into the moss carpets, becoming part of the living architecture itself. These rooted elders become libraries of acoustic knowledge, their chambers visited by younger generations seeking to understand the deepest patterns in the terraces' endless song.

Their culture revolves around the concept of "the Weave"—the idea that all sounds, all growth, all movement in the terraces is part of a single, ever-changing pattern. They don't worship this pattern so much as participate in it, seeing themselves as conscious threads in a tapestry that includes the beetles, the water, the stone, and the green riot of vegetation. Their rituals involve group resonances, where dozens of Weavers will gather to create specific acoustic conditions—sometimes to encourage rain, sometimes to calm the whistling vines during particularly violent storms, sometimes simply to add a new voice to the eternal chorus.

They trade with the lower valleys, sending down acoustic beetles in woven cages (the beetles' synchronized clicking is prized for keeping time in the quieter lands below) and receiving in return metal tools and glass—materials their living city cannot produce. These traders must undergo weeks of ear-training to handle the cacophony, and even then can only stay for short periods before the sound overwhelms them.

The Chorus Weavers see silence not as an absence but as another form of sound—the most difficult to weave, the most precious to maintain. Their greatest masters are said to be able to create pockets of perfect quiet even in the midst of the terraces' most chaotic acoustic storms, though whether this is through clever architecture or some deeper understanding of sound's nature, no outsider has ever learned.
