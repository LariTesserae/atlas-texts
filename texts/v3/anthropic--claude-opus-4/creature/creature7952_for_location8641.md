The dominant inhabitants of the Understrata emerged from the darkness itself, or so it seems when you first encounter them. They are the Seep-Shepherds, though this name captures only a fragment of their nature.

At rest, a Seep-Shepherd might be mistaken for a peculiar stone formation—a vaguely humanoid stack of overlapping plates, each the size of a dinner platter, arranged like fallen dominoes stood on edge. These plates are their primary sensing organs, formed from specialized tissue that has more in common with the local fungal networks than with any surface creature's flesh. The plates constantly secrete a thin film of oil that serves multiple purposes: protection from the caustic resin-falls, chemical communication, and most remarkably, the cultivation of specific bacterial colonies that create their own bioluminescent patterns.

When a Seep-Shepherd moves, the transformation is profound. The plates separate and flow, revealing that they are connected by tissue that exists in a controlled phase-state between solid and liquid. They locomote by allowing parts of themselves to become temporarily fluid, flowing forward before reconstituting. This gives them a disturbing grace—they don't walk so much as pour themselves through the fungal forests, their bodies finding the path of least resistance like living mercury.

They possess no visible eyes, but their entire surface is photosensitive, particularly to the specific wavelengths emitted by the bacterial films. More importantly, they "see" through chemical gradients, reading the complex molecular language of the Understrata with an acuity that would shame any bloodhound. Their plates can shift angle and separation to create acoustic focusing chambers, allowing them to echolocate with precision or generate the low-frequency thrums that serve as their primary distance communication.

The Seep-Shepherds earned their name through their relationship with the resin flows. They have learned to read the stone ceiling's patterns, predicting where new seeps will emerge days or even weeks in advance. Using tools crafted from hardened fungal wood and their own secreted oils, they construct elaborate channel systems to direct the precious flow. But this is not mere resource gathering—it is agriculture of the strangest sort.

Each Seep-Shepherd maintains colonies of specialized organisms within their bodies, carried in hollow spaces between their plates. These range from bacteria that can process specific hydrocarbon chains to small, motile fungi that serve as external digestive systems. By controlling which organisms have access to fresh resin flows, the Seep-Shepherds essentially farm their internal ecosystems, cultivating the precise mixture of symbiotes they need for different tasks.

Their intelligence is undeniable but utterly alien. They build—not structures for shelter, which they don't need, but elaborate arrangements that seem to serve social or perhaps aesthetic functions. Certain groves are cultivated into acoustic chambers where groups gather to generate complex harmonics that can last for hours. They create spiral patterns from different fungal species that, when viewed from the correct angle with the right visual sensitivity, tell stories or convey information through their growth patterns over time.

The Seep-Shepherds reproduce through a process that blurs the line between biology and culture. When conditions are right—usually triggered by specific patterns in the fungal forest's sporing cycles—individuals begin to shed plates. But rather than simply dropping them, they carefully arrange these pieces of themselves in specific patterns, often incorporating fragments from multiple individuals. Over time, these arrangements accumulate enough compatible material to spontaneously organize into new beings, who emerge with a mixture of their progenitors' internal ecosystems and memories encoded in chemical gradients.

Their social structures, if they can be called that, are fluid and complex. Groups form and dissolve based on the needs of resin management and cultivation projects. There seems to be no permanent hierarchy, but rather a constantly shifting network of expertise and need. An individual who discovers a new seep becomes a temporary nexus of activity, drawing others who contribute labor in exchange for access to the fresh flow and the unique organisms it might harbor.

They interact with the other denizens of the Understrata through a combination of careful avoidance and selective symbiosis. The oil-slick creatures are sometimes cultivated for their ability to process certain toxins. The bark-skinned browsers are managed like free-ranging livestock, their grazing patterns controlled through strategic placement of chemical deterrents and attractants. As for the serpentine giants of the deep forest, the Seep-Shepherds maintain what might be called a respectful distance, though there are signs of some deeper relationship not yet understood.

Most unnervingly, they seem aware of observation. When encountered, they don't flee or attack, but rather pause in their activities, their plates shifting to angles that somehow convey attention. They have been seen to deliberately perform actions slowly and repeatedly, as if trying to communicate through demonstration. Some researchers have reported finding simple gifts after encounters—usually small samples of particularly interesting organisms, carefully sealed in resin nodules.

The Seep-Shepherds are clearly still evolving, still exploring the boundaries of their unique existence. Their abandoned tools show a clear progression from crude stone implements to increasingly sophisticated biotechnology. Recent observations suggest they may be developing something analogous to writing—using controlled bacterial colonies to create persistent, evolving patterns that can store and transmit information across generations.

In the perpetual twilight of the Understrata, the Seep-Shepherds tend their strange gardens and pursue their incomprehensible projects. They are gardeners of a sort, but also artists, engineers, and perhaps philosophers, asking questions in frequencies too low for human ears, painting answers in spectra too subtle for human eyes, building a civilization that follows no pattern ever seen beneath an open sky.
