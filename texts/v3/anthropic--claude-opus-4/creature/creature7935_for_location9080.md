The Resonance Shepherds move across the plateau in slow, deliberate migrations, their forms difficult to distinguish from the standing stones until they shift. They are tall—taller than any human—and thin as reeds, with bodies that have evolved to minimize resistance to the constant vibration. Their skin has the texture and translucency of mica, showing faint mineral veins beneath that pulse with sympathetic frequencies. When still, they appear to be natural formations, their bodies unconsciously mimicking the crystalline angles of the ancient monoliths.

They have no ears as such. Instead, their skulls are crowned with branching antlers of living bone, each tine a tuning fork that separates the overwhelming sound into its component frequencies. These antlers grow throughout their lives, becoming more complex and sensitive with age, until the eldest among them carry veritable forests of resonating bone that capture even the subtlest harmonics. They read the plateau's moisture map through these vibrations, knowing where each garden blooms by the way it dampens and colors the eternal roar.

Their eyes have adapted to the brilliant, burning sky by developing multiple translucent lids that filter light in layers. When they blink—a slow, deliberate action—it creates a shuttering effect, like watching the world through wings of dragonflies. These eyes see best in the strange middle distance of the plateau, that emptiness between near and far where sound becomes visible as heat shimmer and moisture gradients paint the air.

The Shepherds tend to the acoustic gardens with a patience that borders on the geological. They understand the relationship between each standing stone and its dependent ecosystem, knowing how to adjust the monoliths with gentle pressure to retune them when centuries of vibration have shifted their frequency. Their long, articulated fingers end in pads that can feel individual sound waves, allowing them to shape resonance like potters shape clay. When they work, they sing—though singing is the wrong word for the subharmonics they produce, sounds felt in the bones rather than heard.

They live in communities that might be mistaken for additional ruins—arrangements of tuned stones that create pockets of relative quiet within the overwhelming noise. Their dwellings are more like acoustic sculptures than houses, spaces defined by sound rather than walls. In these nodes of engineered silence, they rest, pressing their bodies against the cool stone to discharge the day's accumulated vibration. Their young are raised in these quiet spaces, slowly introduced to greater intensities of sound as their resonance organs develop.

Their most remarkable adaptation is their ability to store water within their bodies in specialized chambers that vibrate at specific frequencies. They can survive the long walks between vegetation islands by internally condensing moisture from their own breath and blood, their bodies becoming living versions of the plateau's sound-to-water system. When they reach a garden that needs tending, they can release this stored water in precise amounts, their touch bringing temporary lushness to struggling plants.

The Shepherds communicate through vibration patterns pressed into the stone. Their language is a thing of texture and rhythm, conversations that can stretch across miles of plateau as messages propagate through the bedrock. They leave markers for each other—subtle adjustments to the standing stones that create linguistic interference patterns, readable only to those who know how to parse the standing waves.

They seem to be the only intelligent life that truly thrives here, though whether they evolved from something else or arrived long ago is unknown. They show no interest in the world beyond the plateau's edges, viewing the silence of the lower lands with something approaching fear or perhaps pity. Their entire culture, their whole way of being, is built around the eternal sound. They are its children, its servants, and perhaps its only true understanders.

In their migrations, they follow patterns that repeat across centuries, visiting each acoustic garden in sequences that ensure none fall into silence. They know each standing stone by its voice, each crack in the plateau by its particular note. They are librarians of resonance, gardeners of sound, shepherds of an ecosystem that exists nowhere else—creatures perfectly adapted to a landscape that would drive others mad with its ceaseless voice.

Sometimes, at the junction points where multiple migration routes cross, they gather in larger groups. These convocations appear almost accidental—dozens of Shepherds simply happening to arrive at the same acoustic node at the same time. But there is purpose here. They engage in communal maintenance of particularly complex stone arrangements, their movements synchronized like a dance, their combined vibrations creating temporary architectures of sound that reshape local weather patterns and redistribute moisture across vast areas.

The eldest among them, those whose antlers have grown into magnificent lattices of bone, eventually stop migrating altogether. They choose a particularly active acoustic node and root themselves there, their bodies slowly mineralizing as they merge with the plateau itself. These Elder Stones, still faintly alive, become new focal points for the sound-garden ecosystem, their antlers continuing to grow even as their bodies transform, creating ever more complex instruments for capturing and redirecting the plateau's voice. The younger Shepherds tend to these elders with special reverence, knowing that they too will one day choose their place of transformation.

This is their cycle—motion becoming stillness, flesh becoming stone, always in service to the great resonance that defines their world.
