The Dwellers move through their inherited city with the unhurried purpose of those who have never known urgency. They are neither the builders of this place nor their descendants, but something that emerged from the marriage of fungal patience and salamander dream, shaped by millennia of darkness into forms that make mockery of taxonomy.

Their bodies flow more than walk—translucent flesh that shifts its density as needed, sometimes firm as cartilage to push through narrow passages, sometimes spreading thin as membrane to filter nutrients from the perpetual mist. They have no fixed number of limbs; appendages emerge and retract according to need, each one capable of becoming hand or foot or sensory organ as the moment requires. Their skin weeps the same patient moisture as the stone ceiling, making them difficult to distinguish from the wet walls when they rest.

They see without eyes, or rather, their entire surface serves as one vast eye sensitive to the faintest phosphorescence. When they gather—and they do gather, following cycles tied to the fungi's slow pulsing—their bodies give off their own light, a bioluminescence that paints the ancient streets in colors for which surface languages have no names. These gatherings aren't social in any sense humans would recognize. They pool their substance at the old market squares, dozens of individuals flowing together into temporary collective beings that process information, share nutrients, and make decisions through chemical consensus before separating again.

Memory, for them, is architectural. Each Dweller carries within its cells the mineral taste of every passage it has ever traversed, building internal maps that span centuries. The young learn by consuming shed tissue from their elders, inheriting not just nutrients but navigation, understanding, purpose. They know every tool-mark on every wall, not through study but through accumulated osmosis. When they encounter the bronze chisels and stone hammers of the original builders, they incorporate the metal's taste into themselves, becoming living museums of the city's deep history.

Their cultivation of the giant fungi transcends mere agriculture. They sing to the mushrooms—not with voices but with chemical releases, complex pheromone symphonies that encourage growth in specific patterns. The fungi respond, forming living architecture that complements the carved stone. Dwelling spaces grow from spore and intention. Transit tubes of mycelial threads connect distant districts, allowing the Dwellers to travel as information, their consciousness flowing through fungal networks faster than their bodies could ever move.

They have domesticated the fauna through similar chemical persuasion. The horse-sized salamanders serve as living architecture, their bodies forming bridges across waterways, their translucent bulk filtering light for smaller organisms. The centipedes process detritus, their passage leaving trails of enriched substrate. Even the formless things in the flooded districts serve purposes in the Dwellers' patient economy, though what those purposes might be remains opaque to any intelligence that measures time in heartbeats rather than centuries.

The Dwellers understand their city as a single vast organism of which they are merely specialized cells. They maintain its circulation, ensure the even distribution of nutrients, repair damage not to the stone—that work belongs to water and time—but to the living systems that have colonized it. When a district begins to fail, they orchestrate its controlled decay, guiding the process so that death becomes resource, absence becomes opportunity for new growth.

Their young gestate in the moss gardens, beginning as deposits of concentrated tissue no larger than pearls. The parent—if such a term applies—visits daily to add layers, building the new being slowly from the outside in. The process takes years. The offspring emerges already knowing the taste of its home district, already carrying the chemical memory of a thousand generations of navigation, already fluent in the pheromone languages that bind their society.

Death, when it comes, is equally patient. A Dweller approaching its end seeks out the deepest pools, where the luminescent algae grow thickest. There it dissolves, not in any violent decomposition but in a gradual releasing, its substance feeding the algae, its memories mineralizing into the pool's sediment. Centuries later, other Dwellers will taste these pools and know something of who dissolved there—not personality, for they have no concept analogous to human individuality, but purpose, knowledge, the shape of a life lived in harmony with stone and water.

They fear only dryness and speed. When the rare surface-dweller stumbles into their realm, the Dwellers retreat not from hostility but from incomprehension. The intruders move too fast, speak in vibrations too quick to parse, burn with metabolisms too hot for comfort. Better to wait in the walls until the disturbance passes, as all disturbances pass, leaving only the eternal drip of water and the patient accumulation of centuries.

In the chambers where time eddies, the Dwellers linger longest. They can perceive these temporal anomalies in ways no linear being could, existing simultaneously in multiple moments, their consciousness spread across decades. Here they commune with the city's deepest nature, learning secrets that can only be understood by those who think in geological time. They see the Sunken Quarter not as it was or will be, but as it eternally is—a perfect system of circulation and renewal, as far beyond decay as it is beyond growth, complete in its incompletion.

Their language has a thousand words for different qualities of moisture, but none for drought. They have concepts for every possible gradient of light from total darkness to mere dimness, but none for true illumination. Their mathematics can calculate the curves of erosion over millennia but cannot conceive of right angles. They are what this place has made them, and they have made this place what it is—a civilization that moves like water, thinks like stone, and dreams in the slow explosions of spores.
