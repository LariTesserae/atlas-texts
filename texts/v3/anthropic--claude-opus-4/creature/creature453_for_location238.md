The Keepers arrived three generations after the drowning, drawn by rumors of intact pre-collapse technology. They came expecting to scavenge and leave. Instead, they found purpose in the purposeless.

They live in the cooling towers now, those few dozen souls who chose the mineral purgatory over the dying world beyond. Their shelters cling to the inner walls like barnacles: hammocks of beetle-silk strung between corroded struts, catchment systems for condensing the perpetual steam into something almost drinkable. They've learned to read the salt's moods—when a geyser will erupt, when the beetles will swarm, when the brine glows brightest with its toxic luminescence.

The eldest among them is Salt Mother, though she was young when she first descended the tower stairs. Her skin has taken on the hexagonal patterning of the flats themselves, mineral deposits forming along the lines of old scars. She speaks in whispers now, her throat scarred by decades of sulfur-laden air. The beetles recognize her, parting before her like a biblical sea. Some say she's learned their language—that the clicking of her filed teeth mimics their mandible-song. Others think the prolonged exposure to heavy metals has simply driven her past human.

Her children—not by birth but by teaching—wear suits of beetle carapaces, each shell carefully harvested from the rare units that malfunction and die. The suits click and whisper as they move, making their wearers half-ghost, half-machine. They call themselves the Shepherds, for they've learned to guide the swarms, to direct the endless recycling toward purpose. Under their guidance, the beetles have begun sorting their reclaimed materials: copper here, titanium there, rare earths in precise taxonomies that would make a metallurgist weep.

The Shepherds tend vast gardens of crystal growth, seeding the brine pools with electrical current to coax minerals into unnatural formations. These gardens serve as both art and calendar—each formation marking another year in the towers, another generation of beetles, another meter of salt accumulated over the drowned city. The formations sing in the heat, expanding and contracting with microscopic music, and the Shepherds have learned to interpret these songs as weather, as warning, as worship.

Then there are the Listeners, who lower themselves on beetle-silk ropes into the thermal channels. They press modified stethoscopes against the salt crust, ears tuned to the ghost-turbines below. They claim the city still dreams—that in the interplay of pressure and heat and mechanical momentum, something like consciousness persists. The Listeners map these dreams in salt-drawings on the tower floors, vast mandalas that the beetles inevitably erase and rebuild, each iteration slightly different, like a game of telephone played across centuries.

The youngest Keepers barely remember the world beyond the flats. They race across the crystalline surface on skimmers made from salvaged solar panels and beetle-wing membranes, chasing the heat mirages for glimpses of the city below. They dive in the deeper brine pools, their young lungs adapted to hold breath against the chemical burn, retrieving artifacts that bubble up from the drowned streets: a child's toy, its plastic forever pristine in the preserving salt; a hard drive, its data as dead as the hands that stored it; bones, sometimes, though these are quickly claimed by the beetles, processed into calcium for their ever-growing hives.

These youth speak a creole of human language and beetle-click, and they've begun to dream in hexagons. Some mornings they wake with salt crystals in their tear ducts, their bodies beginning the slow mineralization that marks all long-term residents. They don't fear this transformation—they see it as becoming one with their inheritance, this monument to humanity's industrial apotheosis.

The Keepers maintain the ritual of the Evening Count, when they gather at the highest tower platforms to watch the beetles return to their hives. The swarms create rivers of iridescent motion across the flats, converging on structures that grow more elaborate each year—spires and arches and impossible geometries that seem to serve no function but beauty. The Keepers count the swarms, not in numbers but in patterns, reading omens in their formations like ancient priests reading flight of birds.

They've developed their own mythology: that the beetles are processing the city into something new, that given enough time—millions of years perhaps—they'll transform this industrial graveyard into something unimaginable. The Keepers see themselves as witnesses to this transformation, their purpose to remember what was and document what becomes. They carve their observations into salt tablets that dissolve and reform with each rain of condensation, ensuring their testament remains as perpetual and purposeless as the city itself.

Some nights, when the brine glows brightest and the steam columns dance like spirits, the Keepers perform the Turbine Dance. They move in mechanical precision, mimicking the motion of the ghost machines below, their beetle-shell suits clicking in rhythm. They dance until exhaustion, until the distinction between human and machine movement blurs, until they feel themselves part of the great grinding wheel of reclamation that defines this place.

They are not survivors—survival implies an endpoint, a return to something better. They are not quite human anymore, nor quite machine. They are witnesses, they are shepherds, they are the bridge between what died and what persists. In their towers they keep vigil over a world that needs no keeping, perfect in its post-human equilibrium, terrible in its beauty.

And sometimes, in the deepest part of the night when the steam settles and the beetles rest, they swear they can hear it: the city's metal heartbeat, the pulse of turbines that cannot die, the song of a place that has moved beyond death into something stranger—a mechanical moksha, a synthetic samsara, turning and turning in the widening gyre of its own infinity.
