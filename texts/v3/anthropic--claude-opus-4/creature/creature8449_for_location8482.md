The people of the Terrace Cities call themselves the Vaith-Arun, which in their clicking tongue means "Those Who Breathe Between." They are human still, but generations of immersion have worked changes both subtle and profound upon their flesh.

Their skin has taken on a peculiar quality—not merely pale but somehow translucent, as if the water has begun to claim them layer by layer. In certain lights, you can see the blue tracery of veins beneath, the shadow of bone through finger and palm. This transparency is most pronounced in the eldest, whose bodies have become windows to their own workings. The young are born opaque and darken through childhood, only beginning their fade toward clarity in their third decade.

They have developed a secondary eyelid—a nictitating membrane that flicks across the eye with practiced ease, clearing away the constant accumulation of silt and algae. Their true eyelids they rarely close, having lost much of the reflex to blink in the unchanging medium of their world. This gives them an unnerving quality to surface dwellers, a wide-eyed intensity that speaks of constant vigilance.

Their hair grows in strange patterns. The constant immersion has made it thick and rope-like, naturally forming into locks that resist tangling in the water. Most wear it long, using it as an additional sensory organ—the way it moves in the water tells them of currents and approaching bodies. Children learn to read these subtle movements before they master swimming. The hair grows slowly and is never cut, only bound with copper rings that mark significant life events: birth of a child, completion of a deep dive, survival of the seasonal convections.

The Vaith-Arun have evolved a peculiar relationship with their own breathing. Where land-dwellers breathe without thought, they have made it an art form. Their lungs have grown larger, their ribs more flexible. They practice breath-holding from infancy, playing games where children compete to see who can remain on the lowest terraces longest. By adulthood, most can hold their breath for fifteen minutes without strain, the most accomplished for nearly an hour.

But more interesting than their lung capacity is what they do between breaths. They have learned to store air in small pockets throughout their bodies—in the hollows of their collarbones, in specialized pouches that have developed in their cheeks, even in the spaces between their fingers where the webbing has grown thick. They can release these reserves in tiny sips, extending their time below, or share them with others in moments of intimacy—a mother breathing into her child's mouth, lovers exchanging their last bubbles in the deep gardens.

Their bones have grown dense to counteract buoyancy, giving them a natural weight that lets them walk along the terrace paths with a curious, dreamlike gait. This density comes at a cost—their skeletons are brittle, prone to fracture if they move too quickly or strike against stone. The healing arts of the Vaith-Arun focus heavily on bone-setting, using splints of cultivated coral that gradually fuse with the skeleton itself. The eldest among them are more coral than bone, their bodies becoming part of the city's living architecture.

They organize themselves around the rhythm of breathing. Families synchronize their trips to the air-filled chambers carved into the upper terraces, moving together in practiced formations. These chambers—called "Lunghomes"—are the only truly dry spaces in the city, kept clear of water by careful engineering and constant maintenance. Here, the Vaith-Arun gather for their most important rituals: births (for children must take their first breath in air), naming ceremonies, and the preparation of the dead.

Death among the Vaith-Arun is a gradual return to the city itself. Bodies are not removed but rather installed—placed in specially prepared niches in the terrace walls where the coral is encouraged to grow over and through them. Over years, the deceased become part of the structure, their bones providing framework for new growth. The most honored dead are placed near the thermal vents, where their transformation happens more quickly, their minerals returning to the water that sustained them in life.

Their diet consists primarily of the kelp gardens, supplemented by the strange organisms that grow near the thermal vents—sulfur-feeding bacteria that form mats of yellow and orange, tube worms whose flesh tastes of copper, and the occasional fish that ventures too close to the city's fish-traps. They have developed a cuisine based on fermentation, using the warm waters to break down tough plant fibers in ceramic vessels that bubble constantly with bacterial activity. The resulting foods are pungent, an acquired taste that marks the difference between visitor and resident.

Children are raised communally in the middle terraces, where the pressure is moderate and the kelp gardens provide natural playgrounds. They learn to swim before they walk, to hold their breath before they speak. Their games involve complex three-dimensional movements through the water—tag played across multiple terraces, races through the kelp forests, diving competitions judged not on form but on grace of movement and conservation of energy.

The Vaith-Arun mark time not by days but by breaths—the personal count each individual keeps from birth to death. Important events are remembered by their breath-count: "I met my partner at my forty-thousand-and-third breath," someone might tap against the stone wall. This creates a deeply personal relationship with time, where each person's history is literally measured by their moments of air.

Their governance is likewise tied to breathing. Leadership positions are earned through diving prowess—the ability to work longest at the deep vents, to travel furthest on a single breath. The council meets in the deepest accessible chamber, where the pressure makes speech impossible and decisions must be made through gesture alone. This enforces brevity in governance, a practicality that has evolved into philosophy: if a thing cannot be said in the movements of hands and body, perhaps it need not be said at all.

They dress in minimal clothing woven from processed kelp fibers—garments that cling close to the body to reduce drag, dyed in patterns that indicate profession and family allegiance. Metalworkers wear copper-green, garden-tenders wear browns and gold, the deep-divers wear pure black that helps them disappear into the darkness below. Jewelry is functional: weighted anklets for those who work the bottom terraces, breathing-bladders worn as necklaces, tools incorporated into belts and wrist-guards.

The Vaith-Arun have no concept of privacy as surface dwellers understand it. In a world where everyone can see everyone through the clear water, where sound carries strangely and secrets are impossible to keep, they have developed a culture of radical openness. Emotions are expressed through whole-body movements, conflicts resolved through ritualized swimming patterns that allow aggression to dissipate in the water's resistance.

Yet for all their adaptations, they remain recognizably human in their essential nature. They love, grieve, celebrate, and dream. Their art takes the form of coral sculptures grown over decades, kelp gardens arranged in meaningful patterns, and most importantly, the dance-like movements they perform in groups—synchronized swimming that tells stories of their history, their myths of the time before the waters came, their dreams of what might lie beyond the caldera's rim.

They are a people caught between elements, neither fully aquatic nor able to return to land. The few who have tried to leave, climbing the long coral ladders to the rim and the air beyond, report a terrible heaviness, a burning in lungs too adapted to measured breath, skin that cracks and bleeds in the dry air. Most return within days, diving gratefully back into their vertical city, their translucent world of weighted grace and patient cultivation.

In their mythology, they speak of a time when the mountain will wake again, when the waters will boil away and they will either adapt once more or perish. But this is a distant concern, something for the breath-counts of future generations. For now, they continue their slow life in the terraces, tending their gardens, raising their children, becoming ever more like the coral they cultivate—patient, enduring, somewhere between alive and architecture, breathing between the elements in their warm and silent world.
