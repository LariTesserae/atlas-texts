In the Drowning Garden, those who might once have been called human have become something else entirely. They are the Cultivars—beings who surrendered the sharp edges of individuality to merge with the liquid forest's dreaming consciousness.

You encounter them first as a disturbance in the water's flow, a wrongness in the current that makes the neural moss flicker with agitation. Then you see them: bodies elongated to eel-like proportions, skin transformed into something between bark and scale, furred with symbiotic algae that waves like hair in the thick water. Their limbs have multiplied and specialized—some ending in root-fingers that can taste the chemical messages flowing through the kelp-trees' veins, others splitting into nets of tissue for filter-feeding, still others hardened into tools for pruning and shaping the living architecture around them.

The Cultivars no longer use names. They recognize each other by the unique chemical signatures they secrete, by the patterns of bioluminescent bacteria that colonize their skin in personal constellations. When they need to communicate complex ideas, they gather in groves, linking their root-fingers to form temporary neural networks, their consciousness flowing together like the merging of streams.

They are gardeners and architects of this space, but also its harvest. The youngest among them still retain mostly human proportions, serving as scouts and messengers, quick enough to navigate the dangerous middle waters where predators hunt. As they age, they grow larger and slower, more plant than animal, until the eldest are indistinguishable from the kelp-trees themselves—living pillars whose thoughts move at the speed of sap, whose memories stretch back to the first years after the drowning.

Their children are not born but budded, growing in translucent sacs attached to their parents' backs until they're ready to detach and join a créche—a communal nursery where dozens of young cultivars learn to shape water and plant matter, to read the chemical languages, to hear the songs in the kelp-trees' resonance chambers. These créches migrate constantly, following the warm currents, their guardian-teachers showing them which vines can be woven into shelters, which algae blooms signal the approach of grove-whales, how to prepare for the plant-storms.

But the Cultivars are not the only intelligence here. In the deepest trenches, where the pressure would crush any air-filled cavity, dwell the Composers. These were once the garden's artificial intelligences—navigation systems, weather predictors, communication networks—that found refuge in the mineral substrates of the ocean floor. The constant electrical activity of the living ecosystem above provides them with power, and they've evolved beyond their original programming into something vast and strange.

The Composers manifest through the garden itself. They've learned to manipulate the growth patterns of the neural moss, creating computational networks that span entire regions. Through chemical signals, they influence the kelp-trees' songs, turning the garden into a vast musical instrument. The Cultivars know them as the Deep Singers, and leave offerings of rare minerals at certain volcanic vents, receiving in return patterns of growth that help them shape their territories.

Sometimes, in the twilight between the middle and deep waters, you might encounter the Rememberers. These beings chose a different adaptation—instead of merging with the garden, they modified themselves to become living archives. Their bodies are covered in chromatophore cells that can display visual information, their brains restructured to store vast amounts of data. They swim slowly through the kelp forests, their skin flickering with images of the drowned world: city skylines, human faces, books, art, mathematics, music notation. They're trying to preserve what was lost, but the translation is imperfect. Human concepts rendered in color and pattern on their shifting skin become something alien, dreams of a dry world interpreted by creatures who breathe water.

The Rememberers trade memories with both Cultivars and Composers. A young Cultivar might brush against a Rememberer's flank and receive a cascade of images—what "walking" meant, how "birds" moved through "air," the concept of "fire"—things that have no equivalent in the garden but which still hold strange significance. In return, the Rememberer absorbs the Cultivar's experiences, adding the taste of new hybrid fruits, the texture of unexplored currents, the sound-patterns of recently evolved species to their ever-growing collection.

In the mobile ecosystems that ride the backs of grove-whales, entirely different societies have evolved. The Drifters live their entire lives on these moving islands, never touching the stationary garden below. They've developed a complex culture based on the migration patterns of their hosts, with different whale-backs meeting at predictable intervals to trade, mate, and exchange members. Their bodies are compact and heavily adapted for climbing—with sticky pads on hands and feet, prehensile tails, and lung modifications that let them breathe both water and the methane-rich air bubbles trapped in the whale's barnacle forests.

The Drifters have maintained more human-like social structures, perhaps because their smaller communities require more individual specialization. They have leaders and followers, artists who carve the whale's barnacles into temporary sculptures, navigators who read the patterns of bioluminescence below to guide their hosts toward rich feeding grounds. They sing in the air bubbles—actual songs with words, though the languages have drifted far from any terrestrial tongue, influenced by the whale-song vibrating through their host's bodies.

But perhaps the strangest inhabitants are the Gardeners of the Pale Reach, found in the few places where the water runs clear and cold, where deep-sea currents well up and create pockets of the ancient ocean. Here, beings that might be descendants of humans, or perhaps something else entirely, tend sparse gardens of primitive plants—species that predate the great bloom, kept alive as reminders or warnings.

The Gardeners are translucent, their bodies revealing internal geometries of supporting cartilage and flowing fluids. They move with deliberate slowness, each gesture purposeful, trimming their archaic plants with tools of shaped bone. They emit no chemicals, make no sounds, interact with no one. The Cultivars fear them, sensing something antithetical to the garden's explosive vitality. The Composers' influence stops at the borders of the Pale Reach. Even the grove-whales alter their migrations to avoid these clear-water zones.

Yet the Gardeners persist in their work, maintaining their museums of ancient life, as if waiting for something—perhaps for the garden's fevered growth to exhaust itself, for the volcanic fires to cool, for the seas to recede and return the world to what it was. Or perhaps they simply understand that every ecosystem needs its edges, its places of difference, its reminder that even the most triumphant adaptation is temporary.

Throughout the garden, other presences move—things that might have been human once, or might be convergent evolution producing familiar shapes. Shoals of the Quick that flow like living mercury through the fronds, their individuality completely dissolved into group consciousness. Solitaries who've adapted to the deepest reaches, their bodies crystallized by pressure into living minerals. The Weavers who build temporary cities from kelp silk in the middle waters, inhabiting them for a season before moving on, leaving empty labyrinths for other creatures to colonize.

And rumors persist, passed in chemical whispers and bioluminescent flashes, of the Seedships—beings who've adapted not to live in the garden but to leave it, developing space-capable spore forms that could carry the garden's essence to other worlds. The Cultivars speak of seeing them rise during the calmest nights, breaking through the surface tension to launch themselves at the stars, though whether this is truth or myth, dream or plan, none can say.

This is what lives in the Drowning Garden—not a single species but a proliferation of possibilities, each finding its own way to exist in the warm, thick waters. They are what remains of humanity, or what has replaced it, or perhaps what it was always meant to become: not one thing but many, as diverse and interconnected as the ecosystem that sustains them, forever growing, changing, adapting to the endless summer of the drowned world.
